# MOTIVATING USER-GENERATED CONTENT: THE UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES OF INCENTIVE THRESHOLDS<sup>1</sup>

Liuyi He, Jifeng Luo, and Yisong Tang Antai College of Economics & Management, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Shanghai, CHINA {heliuyi1996@sjtu.edu.cn} {luojf@sjtu.edu.cn} {tangyisong@sjtu.edu.cn}

Zhiyan Wu School of Management, Shanghai University of International Business and Economics. Shanghai, CHINA {wuzhiyan@suibe.edu.cn}

Han Zhang Scheller College of Business, Georgia Institute of Technology. Atlanta, GA, U.S.A. {han.zhang@scheller.gatech.edu}

While monetary rewards have been widely used by online platforms to motivate user-generated content (UGC) contributions, users may not always demonstrate the expected behaviors. Unintended consequences of reward policies, exemplified by unchanged or reduced UGC contributions, may occur. Through two natural experiments, this study investigates the implications of providing users with an incentive structure that rewards users’ continued contribution according to the volume of UGC. Using a unique data set on two completion-contingent incentive programs from a popular online aesthetic medicine platform, we examine user responses to reward thresholds. We found that after users reach a threshold to obtain a monetary reward, they are less likely to continue contributing UGC, suggesting a minimal-effort effect. Our findings indicate that social approval from peers helps to mitigate unintended user responses to monetary reward policies. We also observed that monetary rewards primarily improve the quality and website traffic of low- to moderate-quality contributions.

Keywords: Monetary rewards, incentive thresholds, unintended consequences, online aesthetic medicine community, user-generated content, natural experiment

## Introduction

User-generated content (UGC), such as reviews, posts, and blogs, has become ubiquitous in online marketplaces. Every day, 300 million photos are uploaded to Facebook, 140 million tweets are posted on Twitter, and more than 14 million articles are pinned on Pinterest (Georgiev, 2021). As a prosocial behavior, the creation of UGC in online marketplaces helps inform users about the quality of goods and providers through peers’ experiences, thereby mitigating the “lemon” problem in markets (Akerlof, 1970; Pavlou & Dimoka, 2006). For online platforms whose businesses directly or indirectly build on UGC, their success largely hinges on the provision of high-quality UGC.

However, UGC often faces an undersupply problem (e.g., Chen et al., 2011) primarily because of its property as a public good. In addition, it is hard for users to sustain UGC contributions over a prolonged period (Gallus, 2017). Therefore, online platforms have been experimenting with different incentives to entice more UGC. For example, Amazon offers “incentivized reviews” in its Vine program, where sellers send free or discounted products to reviewers in exchange for product reviews (Imbler, 2018; Qiao et al., 2020). Epinions.com encourages user interactions by allowing one user to subscribe to another to gather more product reviews (Goes et al., 2014). Dianping.com in China pays consumers reward points for writing online product reviews (Wu et al., 2015).

One fundamental difficulty in designing effective incentive mechanisms is how to incentivize users without crowding out their intrinsic motivations. A common way to stimulate user contributions is by providing monetary rewards (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2017; Wirtz & Chew, 2002), with the unintended consequence that providing monetary rewards to foster prosocial behavior may sometimes lead to unchanged or reduced total contributions. This crowding out of intrinsic motivations through extrinsic monetary incentives has been widely observed in a variety of situations, especially when extrinsic motivations are low (e.g., Deci, 1975; Deci & Ryan, 1985; Gneezy et al., 2011; Qiao et al., 2020).

Prior studies have mainly studied monetary rewards that are provided to users once they contribute (Burtch et al., 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018). Few studies have documented how users change their behavior to qualify for a monetary incentive structure that rewards users for continued contributions after their cumulative number of contributions reaches certain thresholds. Generally, the first contribution (e.g., a product review) reveals a user’s initial product experience. In contrast, continued contributions can provide a long-term evaluation of a product’s actual performance for potential consumers. Continued contributions build on existing review(s), allowing users to reflect more deeply on the product; thus, they reveal much more information. Many e-commerce platforms, such as Amazon and Taobao in China, have incorporated a “continued contribution” feature for product reviews. Therefore, it is essential to examine the effect of monetary rewards on continued contribution. To do so, we used unique data from an online aesthetic medicine platform, which offered large threshold-based monetary incentives to reward users for writing diary entries about their aesthetic treatments. Monetary rewards were introduced to encourage users to contribute but users receive a reward only after the number of diary entries exceeded certain thresholds. In examining whether there are any unintended consequences of introducing thresholds in monetary incentive programs, our first objective was to examine whether threshold-based monetary incentives are associated with unintended responses to the thresholds.

Another key question is how to best structure incentives to support programmatic goals while mitigating unintended consequences. Ample research has explored the direct effect of social interaction and feedback on UGC production (Huang et al., 2017; Zhang & Zhu, 2011), but little is known about whether social interaction mitigates unintended responses. In one exception, Burtch et al. (2018) explored the stimulating effects of combining monetary incentives and social norms on the volume and length of reviews. They found that providing both led to greater review volume and length. Social norms improved UGC contribution but potentially made tasks less enjoyable (Corgnet et al., 2021). We propose that the presence of social approval from peers can reinforce users’ intrinsic motivations and mitigate the unintended consequences induced by threshold-based monetary incentives. Therefore, our second objective was to explore how social approval (i.e., comments received from peers) interacts with thresholdbased monetary incentives and mitigates unintended user responses.

We conducted difference-in-differences (DiD) analyses of two natural experiments to identify the causal effect of monetary rewards. Compared to the control group, we found that users’ willingness to continue posting diary entries decreased once they reached the reward threshold. We further confirmed the important role of social approval in alleviating the unintended consequences of incentive programs. Our results show that social interactions from peers in previous diary entries can help to mitigate unintended user responses to thresholds. We found that users with social approval are more willing to continue posting UGC after reaching a rewards threshold.

This study makes several significant contributions to the literature. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to study user responses to monetary rewards that are received only after users’ cumulative contributions exceed specific thresholds. Our results demonstrate that there are unintended consequences associated with this monetary reward policy design. While prior research has revealed similar findings regarding monetary incentives earned after the first contribution (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018), none of them have examined continued contribution designs with thresholdbased monetary incentives. Moreover, we found that social interaction can serve as a moderator for mitigating users’ unintended responses to common threshold-based incentive mechanisms. We complement the findings in the existing literature (e.g., Huang et al., 2017; Zhang & Zhu, 2011) and extend them to a new setting. Finally, from a practical perspective, although we found unintended user responses to threshold-based monetary incentives, our results revealed no reduction in UGC and showed that such incentive schemes led to improvements in diary entry quality and online diary entry traffic. This suggests that threshold-based incentive designs may benefit UGC contributions if carefully implemented.

## Related Literature

## Monetary Incentives

Organizations often employ monetary incentives. Based on the assumption that rational individuals seek to maximize personal utility, it is generally believed that monetary incentives will improve performance (Prendergast, 1999).

Previous studies have examined how payments impact performance in different prosocial contribution contexts. For example, in an open source software context, paid developers were found to engage in more testing and debugging activities than their unpaid counterparts (Roberts et al., 2006). Liu et al. (2014) found that monetary incentives enhanced the quality of the tasks performed on Taskcn. Ashraf et al. (2014) revealed that extrinsic rewards effectively improved public service delivery performance. Chen et al. (2019) discovered that a reward program that paid contributors \$10 per 1,000 page views of their stock opinion articles increased output and attracted more interest from the community.

However, evidence suggests that there are circumstances in which caution is necessary. The negative effects of monetary incentives are especially relevant when intrinsic motivations are the primary driver of behavior and incentives are not large enough (Gneezy et al., 2011). Sun et al. (2017) found that users with high social connectedness were less willing to write reviews after they were provided with monetary rewards for posting reviews. Garnefeld et al. (2012) demonstrated that monetary incentives increased active and passive community members’ participation in the short term but weakened it in the long run. Qiao et al. (2020) showed that after writing reviews for financial incentives, reviewers’ subsequent unincentivized reviews were shorter and involved less linguistic effort.

The monetary incentive structures on online review platforms can be roughly classified into two types: completion-contingent incentives and performance-contingent incentives (Khern-amnuai et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2022). Completion-contingent incentives provide users with rewards as long as they finish the required tasks. In an experiment on Amazon MTurk, Wang et al. (2012) observed no significant differences in review quality between paid and unpaid reviewers in a completion-contingent incentive context where eligible reviewers were paid \$1 for posting a review. Khern-am-nuai et al. (2018) leveraged a natural experiment to investigate the impacts of a monetary incentive program that provided reviewers with 25 loyalty points for every review they authored, up to eight reviews per year. They found an increase in the number of new reviewers and found that the reviews became more positive but at the cost of quality. Through two randomized experiments, Burtch et al. (2018) discovered that providing participants with financial rewards for writing reviews motivated consumers to contribute more but not necessarily longer reviews. Wang et al. (2022) revealed that the launch of a paid feature on a knowledgesharing platform led to more answers from long-time hosts but fewer answers from hosts with shorter tenures on the site without significant changes in the quality of answers. Woolley and Sharif (2021) confirmed the positive influence of incentives on consumers’ linguistic expressions of positivity in reviews.

Performance-contingent incentives reward reviewers based on the performance of their reviews, e.g., reaching a standard of merit or exceeding a specified performance criterion. For example, Wang et al. (2012) found that providing a bonus of \$0.25 for each additional helpful vote, in addition to completion-contingent monetary rewards, improved the quality of paid reviews. Yu et al. (2022) exploited a quasiexperimental setting to examine the effect of reviewers’ receiving a “Review of the Day” recognition on a restaurant review platform in Asia. They found that receiving performance-contingent rewards resulted in more and betterquality reviews. Liu and Feng (2021) theoretically identified two crowding-out effects of monetary incentives that rewarded contributors for the audience attracted by the content. The motivation crowding-out effect arises when monetary incentives reduce contribution motivations that are not driven by monetary rewards. The competition crowdingout effect occurs when less-effective contributors reduce their effort given the intensified competition brought on by increased monetary rewards. The motivation crowding-out effect dominates when monetary incentives are small, but the competition crowding-out effect emerges when they are large.

Different monetary incentive structures imply different contractual arrangements, which incentivize users differently. The incentive structure examined in our research is similar to the completion-contingent monetary incentives studied in prior work (Burtch et al., 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Qiao et al., 2020) but differs in that eligible users are rewarded only after their cumulative number of contributions exceeds a certain threshold. Thus, we were able to observe users’ subsequent behaviors even after their contributions reached the threshold(s). While most prior literature has focused on review volume at the platform level, we examine the probability of subsequent contribution at the reviewer level.

## Social Incentives

Another way to incentivize user contributions relies on social context or social incentives (Ashraf & Bandiera, 2018; Bandiera et al., 2010; Corgnet et al., 2021). A fundamental feature of social life is social interaction, or how people act and react socially. In social interactions, people want to be admired by others. These emotions and expressive activities can be represented as a preference for approval (Hollander, 1990).

Prior literature has suggested that social factors significantly influence users’ authorship of online contributions (e.g., Barasch & Berger, 2014; Huang et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2017; Muchnik et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2018; Zhang & Zhu, 2011). For example, Zhang and Zhu (2011) illustrated that a larger audience size triggered users to contribute more due to the potential social benefits. Jabr et al. (2014) showed that recognition mechanisms, especially feedback-based mechanisms, motivated individuals to contribute more to public goods. In the online review context, Goes et al. (2014) found the mere presence of followers led to more reviews and more objective reviews. Huang et al. (2017) examined the impacts of social network integration on the characteristics of reviews on online review platforms. They discovered that social network integration with Facebook increased review volume and positive emotion in the review text. Burtch et al. (2018) demonstrated that social norms (the contribution behaviors of the peer) led to more and lengthier reviews. Moreover, they found that if both financial incentives and social norms were applied, consumers provided larger volumes of helpful reviews, compared to when only one of them was applied.

Our study is also related to prior literature on increasing users’ UGC contributions through goal setting. Research has shown that “specific and challenging goals” can improve performance (Lunenburg, 2011) because goals lead individuals to focus and orient their efforts toward goal-related activities, improving perseverance, and activating task-related knowledge (Locke & Latham, 2002). Many studies of online communities have focused on the presence of goals in the form of badges, which are related to social context, finding that users increase their contributions to online communities in goal-oriented settings (Cavusoglu et al., 2021; Khansa et al., 2015), especially when they are close to earning a badge (Goes et al., 2016; Qiao et al., 2021). Goes et al.’s (2016) study is more relevant to our research; they found that adopting a status hierarchy reduces users’ effort levels upon goal attainment. Their study focused on the satisfaction or complacency effect of achieving certain prominently displayed status symbols. Our study explores the impacts of threshold-based monetary incentives on user contributions and examines users’ unintended responses to threshold changes.

Most previous studies on this topic have investigated the direct stimulating effects of social incentives (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018; Goes et al., 2014; Huang et al., 2017). It is plausible that social incentives can alleviate the negative effect of monetary incentives on intrinsic motivation. In this vein, Burtch et al. (2018) studied the interaction effects of financial incentives and social norms, manifested on the platform as the number of reviewers in the previous month. However, they mainly focused on social norms, which are typically defined as informal rules and standards that guide or constrain behaviors in groups and societies (Cialdini & Trost, 1998). Our study builds on the aforementioned literature and considers social approval from peers as a mechanism that alleviates the minimal-effort effect. Social approval from peers (i.e., peer comments) differs fundamentally from social norms promoted by platforms, such as performance feedback mechanisms. Peer recognition comes from other users rather than from platforms. Therefore, peer recognition is less controlling and unlikely to threaten autonomy. Users can actively select what they prefer or agree with to attain conformity with others. ## Hypothesis Development

## Monetary Rewards and UGC Contributions

Monetary rewards are a common extrinsic motivation that affects the extent of investment in UGC contributions, which are considered a public good, in two ways: the direct price effect and the indirect psychological effect (Gneezy et al., 2011). On the one hand, users are incentivized to contribute more to achieve monetary rewards (direct price effect). Consistent with the direct price effect, several studies have found that monetary incentives can stimulate UGC contributions (Burtch et al., 2018; Cabral & Li, 2015; Khernam-nuai et al., 2018). On the other hand, because of the crowding-out effect (the indirect psychological effect), monetary incentives can decrease contributions among users who are more intrinsically motivated because the presence of monetary incentives may spoil the reputational (or selfimage) motivation to perform good deeds (Bénabou & Tirole, 2006) and cast doubt on the extent to which their actions are performed for altruistic reasons (Gneezy et al., 2011). As users are heterogeneous in their motivations, the overall effect of monetary rewards depends on the user composition on the platform. But as we discuss below, thresholds play a prominent role.

In certain online aesthetic medicine communities, users devote time and effort into writing diary entries detailing the cosmetic results of aesthetic treatments over time. Writing diary entries to share personal experiences, which benefits others at reviewers’ own costs, is akin to intrinsically motivated contributions to a public good (Reinholt et al., 2011). The quantity and quality of diary entries are functions of users’ skill and level of effort. Gneezy and Rustichini (2000) demonstrated that in such cases, the direct price effect is more significant than the indirect psychological effect if monetary incentives are large enough. In our context, the online aesthetic medicine platform provides a reward when certain thresholds are met, and the reward is usually much larger than typical contribution rewards. Therefore, extrinsic monetary rewards may incentivize users to continue performing the task. Moreover, the thresholds function like goal setting and are designed to motivate and guide users toward a goal. Therefore, thresholds can lead individuals to focus on and orient their efforts toward goal-related activities and can increase perseverance (Locke & Latham, 2002).

## The Unintended Consequences of Thresholds

Recent empirical work has demonstrated utility-maximizing behaviors related to policies both offline and online. For example, Mayer et al. (2014) investigated whether homeowners responded strategically to news about potential loan modifications for seriously delinquent borrowers. They found that such news motivated homeowners to default on their mortgages, and the effect was most considerable among borrowers least likely to default otherwise. Behr et al. (2020) investigated the implication of a nonlinear compensation scheme that penalized poor performance and rewarded loan volume. Their results suggested that loan officers responded to these financial incentives rationally by reacting well before surpassing the default-rate penalization threshold. Burtch et al. (2018) found that when offered minimal compensation, users were more willing to write reviews but expended minimal effort, demonstrated by the relative brevity of their reviews.

One drawback of monetary incentives is that the crowdingout effect can undermine intrinsic motivations. A decrease in intrinsic motivations can lead to reduced contribution efforts. The threshold-based monetary incentive scheme requires participants to achieve specific goals to obtain monetary rewards. Once the requirements are met, the marginal financial payoff of expending more effort and writing additional diary entries zeroes out. If users are largely driven by extrinsic rewards, they will likely exert minimal effort by only satisfying the required number of entries. Therefore, we expect that threshold-based monetary rewards will incentivize users to expend effort only up until the threshold determined by the monetary reward policy. After the threshold is reached, it will weaken users’ motivations to contribute further because the price effect associated with the monetary reward is gone.

H2: Threshold-based monetary rewards lead users to decrease their effort in making contributions once the threshold for the required number of contributions is reached.

## The Moderating Role of Social Approval

Social approval captures positive appraisal and the acceptance of someone or something (a behavior, trait, attribute, or the like) by a social group (MacDonald et al., 2003). Social approval has signaling value, as subjects can build their reputation and signal their abilities to others. Meanwhile, people generally directly value what others think (and say) about them. Users can obtain approval from others with whom they interact (Hollander, 1990). Such approval is more effective when it comes from peers (Lerner & Tirole, 2002). The same is true online but on a larger scale (Çakir, 2015).

In this study, we propose that social approval can ease the crowding-out effect that interferes with intrinsic motivation. Burtch et al. (2018) found that social norms can undermine the crowding-out effect associated with monetary incentives by rationalizing users’ decisions to write reviews based on specific intrinsic motivations rather than merely for monetary incentives. We focus on approval from other users, which is commonly available in online communities. Since social interactions increase perceptions of relatedness to other users, we expect peer interactions and recognition to help restore users’ intrinsic motivation (Burtch et al., 2018) and anticipate that the presence of a social frame can even help them to internalize certain incentives to contribute (Goes et al., 2014) We, therefore, expect a decline in unintended responses to thresholds in the presence of social approval from peers in the audience.

H3: Users who receive feedback from peers are more likely to continue contributing after meeting the monetary reward threshold.

## Research Methodology

## Research Context

Our empirical setting is one of the largest aesthetic medicine e-commerce websites in China, which was established in 2013 and had over 8.5 million monthly active users as of 2020. Aesthetic medicine improves a patient’s physical appearance and satisfaction through minimally invasive or noninvasive procedures (Transparency Market Research, 2021). The global aesthetic medicine market size was valued at 99.1 billion USD in 2021 and is anticipated to expand at a compound annual growth rate of 14.5% worldwide and 10.9% in the Asia-Pacific region from 2020 to 2030 (Grand View Research, 2022).

The focal website in our study provides information about aesthetic medicine hospitals, physicians, and products (aesthetic treatments). Users can search on the focal website and have conversations with physicians or hospital agents. If they want to book a treatment online, users must pay a deposit that is usually 20% of the total price. <sup>2</sup> Because information asymmetry is prevalent in the aesthetic medicine industry, it is difficult for users to choose the right physicians. UGC is a typical method used on many aesthetic medicine sites to alleviate information asymmetry because UGC from users who have received treatments tends to be seen as highly credible. The website provides a post-treatment module that allows users to document their healing process and experience after treatment by posting one or more diary entries in a treatment “notebook.” Posttreatment diary entries in the same notebook include users’ longterm recovery experience based on the same aesthetic treatment. A user can at most have one notebook per aesthetic treatment. Within a notebook, several diary entries describe the effect of the same aesthetic treatment at different times.

Users who wish to post diary entries about a treatment can create a notebook in two ways. For users who have booked an aesthetic treatment online, a highlighted “notebook to be written” button will appear on their personal page after the appointment is confirmed so that they can directly post diary entries for each confirmed treatment. In this case, a notebook with a link to the product on the platform can be seen. Alternatively, users can create a notebook from the platform’s homepage. In this case, they must manually add treatment information (including the project, treatment date, price, hospital link, and doctor link) and cannot link their notebooks to specific products available on the platform. In such cases, a notebook without a product link can be seen. Figure 1 provides screenshots of the two case scenarios. Panel A of Figure 1 shows a notebook with a product link, and Panel B lists a notebook without a product link. These two methods of posting diary entries enabled us to identify treatment and control groups in the empirical design, which we discuss in detail in the Identification Strategy subsection. Starting in October 2014, the platform introduced a series of monetary reward policies to encourage more diary entries on aesthetic treatments. Only users who had made treatment bookings online through the platform were eligible for rewards. The policy change provided us with a unique opportunity to infer the causal effect of the monetary reward policy on users’ sharing behaviors. Figure 2 depicts the timeline of the first two policies up to April 30, 2015.

The first policy was in effect from October 15, 2014, to January 31, 2015. Under the first policy, users were required to post at least three entries to qualify for the reward. The policy also required that the entries reflected the changes before and after the aesthetic treatment. The second policy was rolled out on February 1, 2015, and was valid until April 30, 2015. There are several major differences between the first and second policies. First, the second policy set two thresholds corresponding to two ranges of diary entry volume. Second, the payment was not a fixed amount but proportional to the deposit paid through the platform. Finally, the policy set a reward cap of 1,000 RMB. The exact structure of the payments is complicated but is essentially a discrete completion-contingent plan. Both policies applied to the treatment date rather than the notebook creation or diary entry posting dates. Details of the two policies are shown in Table 2.

## Data and Measures

We developed a Python crawler to retrieve visible notebooks on the website in April 2019. We first compiled a complete list of hospitals on the platform and collected all corresponding notebook links on each hospital page. Overall, we obtained 7,838 hospital links and 410,952 notebook links. For each notebook, we collected the user’s personal information from their profile, including their age, gender, and city. We further collected detailed medical aesthetics treatment information from each page, including the project description, treatment date, hospital link, physician link, product link, price, and pretreatment photos. We then checked every diary entry created within the notebook, including its creation time, text, post-treatment photo links, and comments (text and time stamp) under the entry. For each diary entry, we also collected the aggregate number of browses and likes of the entry up to our collection date. The price information was available if the notebook was created using the “notebook to be written” button. The prices had to be manually uploaded by users when they created a notebook using the homepage. Unfortunately, not all users reported the costs of their treatments. In this case, we proxied with the price of a similar product on the website. We collected information about the type of the hospital (public vs. private) and the title of the physician (hospital director, chief physician, associate chief physician, attending physician, and unknown). There were 5,638 notebooks with 15,402 diary entries about treatments performed before May 1, 2015 (covering the first and second policies); of these, group accounts (e.g., those created by hospitals) had created 2,240 notebooks. In our main analysis, we removed the group notebooks and only retained the 3,398 notebooks created by individual users.<sup>3</sup>

To check whether users eligible for the monetary reward had unintended responses to the threshold, we constructed a variable Threshold1<sub>it</sub>, which is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the number of entries in notebook i at the beginning of period t equals 3. For the second policy, we defined AboveThreshold1<sub>it</sub> and AboveThreshold2<sub>it</sub> as dummy variables indicating whether the number of diary entries at the beginning of t is in the interval of [3, 14] or [15, +∞].<sup>5</sup> To check for the moderation role of social approval, we used comments from other users under each entry of notebook i at the beginning of period t. We created a dummy variable PrevComments<sub>it</sub>, which equals 1 if notebook i received at least one comment in the previous period. We did not use the number of visits and likes to measure social approval because we did not have the time stamps for each visit but only the aggregate data up to our collection date. We defined CumDiaries<sub>it</sub> as the cumulative number of entries in notebook i by the beginning of period t. We also measured the number of periods elapsed between notebook i’s first post and period t (Periods<sub>it</sub>). The main variables and their descriptive statistics are shown in Table 3. The correlation matrix is reported in Table 4.

## Identification Strategy

Our econometric identification depends on the natural experiments related to the policy changes. We used a differencein-differences (DiD) specification to identify the effects of monetary reward policies on the amount of continued UGC contributions by users. According to the policy, only notebooks for treatments booked through the platform were eligible for monetary rewards. We therefore identified the treatment group as those notebooks of treatments booked online and the control group as the notebooks of treatments that were not booked online. Notebooks with specific product links were created from personal pages by users who had made online appointments in advance. Users who went to hospitals without online appointments could only create notebooks without product links from the platform’s homepage. Accordingly, we selected notebooks with specific product links as our treatment group and notebooks without product links as our control group.<sup>6</sup>

the homepage is hidden in the drop-down menu in the upper right corner of the homepage. Such users would also have to manually upload project information. It is unlikely that users who booked online would choose this way of creating notebooks rather than using the automatic notebook creation function. If the control group contained some notebooks that should have been in the treatment group, the impacts of the monetary reward policy would tend to be underestimated. Our results would only be strengthened if these notebooks were excluded from the control group.

A key assumption underlying the DiD specification is the parallel trend assumption, which would assume that the trends of treatment and control groups are parallel prior to policy changes. Figure 3 shows the distributions of diary entries per notebook and the number of photos in each entry for treatment and control groups during the three periods (no policy, first policy, and second policy). The parallel trend is likely to have been satisfied as we observed no significant differences in diary entry and photo counts between the treatment group and the control group before the launch of the first policy. After monetary rewards were introduced (Policy 1), we observed an increase in the number of notebooks in the treatment group that included three diary entries or photos. Also, we observed an increase in notebooks that included 15 entries or 3 photos in our treatment group after Policy 2 was introduced. These patterns are consistent with the requirements of each policy. We did not observe any unusual patterns in our control group before or after implementing the monetary reward policies, suggesting that notebooks without product links were not affected by the monetary reward policies. To further check for the parallel trend assumption, we adopted a relative time framework.<sup>7</sup> The results in Table 5 show no anticipatory effects.

It is possible that the reward policy may have changed the composition of users, as some users may have been incentivized to sign up for treatments in order to take advantage of the reward. Since the treatment price and other relevant costs (e.g., recovery time and transportation costs) generally surpassed the reward provided by the platform, users would have been unlikely to book treatments merely to receive the reward. However, monetary rewards may have incentivized more users to post notebooks, which could have shifted the composition of the treatment group (e.g., monetarily incentivized users may have been more likely to post). Further, whether users booked online using the platform or not may have been endogenous as there may have been differences between the users who booked online vs. offline.

To address these problems, we ran a matching algorithm to improve the comparison between these groups. The whole matching process contained three greedy matching steps using the shortest distance, as shown in Figure 4. We first matched pre-policy and post-policy notebooks into matched pairs, separately for treatment and control groups and then matched treatment and control pairs into matched quadruples. The distance between every two notebooks was measured by Euclidean distance (Imbens & Rubin, 2015). Matching variables included user-level (age and gender) and treatmentlevel information (standardized price, type of treatment, type of hospital, and physician title). Table 6 shows the comparison before and after the matching. We observed that the differences between treatment and control notebooks were no longer statistically significant across the observable characteristics. We then applied the DiD approach to the matched sample.

## Empirical Analysis and Results

## User-Level Analysis: Impacts on Continued Contributions

We first analyzed the impacts of monetary incentives on the probability of subsequent contributions at the user level. Using a discrete choice model, we explicitly examined how policy-induced changes in users’ incentives influenced their contribution decisions. For our tests of monetary incentive effects (H1), we examined users’ contribution choices in a DiD specification:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & L o g i t (D i a r y _ {i t}) = \beta_ {1} T r e a t _ {i} + \beta_ {2} T r e a t _ {i} \times P o s t _ {t} + \\ & \delta D i a r y _ {i, t - 1} + C o n t r o l _ {i t} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where Diary<sub>it</sub> is the dummy variable indicating whether a user of notebook i will post an entry released at period t. Treat<sub>i</sub> is an indicator variable for whether notebook i is in the treatment group. Post<sub>t</sub> is a binary variable indicating whether the current period is within the validity period of the first policy. β<sub>2</sub> captures the average effect of monetary policy on a user’s posting decision. We included the lagged dependent variable as a control to capture a possible carry-over effect. Other control variables included personal information (i.e., Age<sub>i</sub> and Gender<sub>i</sub>), treatment information (i.e., Price<sub>i</sub> and Surgical<sub>i</sub>), the cumulative number of entries in notebook i by the beginning of period t (CumDiaries<sub>it</sub>), and the number of periods elapsed between notebook i’s first post and period t (Periods<sub>it</sub>). μ<sub>t</sub> captures the time fixed effects. For a robustness check, we used an alternative dependent variable DiaryCount<sub>it</sub>, which is the number of diary entries released in period t in notebook i. A Poisson model was applied in this case.

For our main tests of unintended responses, we focused on the policy implications after users met the quantity threshold of the first policy (i.e., posting three diary entries). Threshold1<sub>it</sub> is a dummy variable indicating whether notebook i met the minimum quantity requirement by the beginning of period t. In Equation (2), $\beta _ { 2 } + \beta _ { 3 }$ represents the effect when there were three diary entries.

## Monetary Incentive’s Impact on Continued UGC Contribution

Table 7 summarizes the results of the DiD estimation of the first natural experiment. The left-hand panel of Table 7 (Columns 1-4) lists the estimates and the related statistics for the logistic models of $D i a r y _ { i t }$ using the full sample.

To investigate the average effect of monetary rewards, we examined the change in the likelihood of continuously posting entries between the treatment and control notebooks after implementing the first policy. As shown in Column 1 in Table 7, the coefficient (β<sub>2</sub>) before Treat<sub>i</sub>×Post<sub>t</sub> is 0.09, which is statistically insignificant, indicating that there were no significant differences in the treatment group vs. the control group in the probability of posting subsequent entries. In other words, monetary rewards did not lead to changes in the continued contributions provided by users. Therefore, H1 is not supported by our results. In contrast to the effect on the first contribution (Burtch et al., 2018; Cabral & Li, 2015;

Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018), monetary incentives seemed to have no significant impact on the user’s continued contributions on the same medical aesthetics treatment.

## Incentives and Minimal Effort

To test for the minimal-effort hypothesis, we ran a regression of Equation (2). Column 2 in Table 7 shows the results. We observed a significant drop $( \beta _ { 3 } = - 1 . 1 3 5 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ } = 0 . 0 0 3 )$ in the probability of posting subsequent entries right after the quantity threshold was met. Our findings indicate that users did notice the threshold and changed their level of effort, seeking to increase their chances of being rewarded while minimizing their efforts by barely meeting the minimum threshold requirements. These results suggest that setting a monetary reward threshold causes users to decrease their effort once the threshold is met, supporting H2.

## Incentive and Social Approval

To verify the moderating effect of feedback, we ran Equation (2) on two subsets. Columns 3 and 4 in Table 7 present the results. When a user of notebook i received at least one comment in the previous period (Column 4), we observed no significant drop $( \beta _ { 3 } = - 0 . 0 3 4 p = 0 . 9 5 5 )$ in contributions after the reward threshold was met. This indicates that users are less likely to decrease their level of contribution after a reward threshold is met if they receive feedback from others. In contrast, when no comments were received (Column 3), we observed a significant and negative coefficient $( \beta _ { 3 } = -$ 1.789, $p = 0 . 0 0 3 )$ before Threshold1<sub>it</sub>×Treat<sub>i</sub>×Post<sub>t</sub>. These results indicate that users are more likely to exert minimal effort to obtain a reward and reduce their effort once the reward threshold is met when no feedback is received. We conclude that receiving feedback can help mitigate reductions in users’ contribution levels after a reward threshold is met, which supports H3.

## Sensitivity Analyses

The applied regression and matching method can yield unbiased estimates subject to the conditional independence assumption (CIA). That is, conditional on observable characteristics, receiving treatment is independent of potential outcomes. Because the matching algorithm we used can only match samples on observable variables, we were unable to address potential selection issues resulting from unobservable confounding factors. To assess the underlying CIA, we conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, we conducted the data-driven sensitivity test for unobservable selection proposed by Cerulli (2019). The idea behind this test is that if the baseline matching estimates are insensitive to the addition of unobservables, they should also be insensitive to their removal. Accordingly, the data-driven approach performs a bootstrap over different subsets of matching variables and simulates various matching estimates to be compared with the baseline matching estimates. If the new matching estimates are not significantly different from the baseline matching estimates, the baseline estimates are unlikely to be sensitive to hidden confounding factors. Figure 5 shows the results. For the number of selected matching variables, varying from one to five, the minimal-effort results and the results of the moderating role of social approval did not lose significance, indicating the robustness of our estimates and the validity of the CIA

Second, we conducted the sensitivity test proposed by Ichino et al. (2008). This approach follows Rosenbaum (1987, 2002) but in a simulation, which applies to our matching algorithm. This approach assumes that the confounding unobservables can be summarized by a binary variable U. It is assumed that the CIA is not satisfied but would be satisfied if it were conditional on the additional binary variable U. The method simulates this binary confounder in the data. A comparison of the estimates obtained with and without matching on U reveals the extent to which the estimator is robust to this specific source of failure of the CIA. We specified the values of the parameters $\scriptstyle p _ { i j } = P r o b ( U = 1 /$ $T r e a t { = } i , \quad Y { = } j )$ with $i , \ j \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ , which characterize the distribution of U by giving the probabilities of U = 1 in four cases defined by the treatment status and the potential outcome Y. Then we simulated a value of the confounding variable for each user and reestimated the models after including the simulated U in matching variables. By changing the parameters characterizing the distribution of U, we were able to examine the sensitivity of the matching results under different kinds of deviations from CIA. Table 8 presents the results. After taking the simulated unobservable into account, the estimation results remain consistent with our main results.

## Falsification Test

We conducted several falsification tests to ensure that the estimation results were driven by the monetary incentive policy. ## Impacts of the Second Policy

Compared with the first reward policy, the second policy offered two levels of rewards: users could get a reward equal to 50% of the deposit if they posted three or more entries and a reward equal to 100% if they posted more than 14 entries. Thus, three and 15 entries are cutoff points for the second policy. The main purpose of examining the second policy is to provide results to further validate the existence of the minimal-effort effect when another threshold is added. We expected to observe a drop in contributions when users posted 15 entries in the second policy (compared to notebooks under the first policy). Here, the control (treatment) group refers to notebooks without (with) a specific product link reviewing aesthetic treatments performed between October 15, 2014, and April 30, 2015. Notice that we are comparing the impacts of the second policy relative to the first policy. ## Content-Level Analysis: Impacts on Diary Entry Quality and Traffic

Our analysis has thus far focused on changes in users’ posting strategies in response to monetary rewards. We extended our findings by investigating the impacts of monetary reward policies on diary entry quality (entry lengths and topics) and online marketplace performance (the amount of online traffic<sup>9</sup>). For this analysis, we examined the performance of diary entries in a DiD specification with each observation corresponding to a diary entry:

$$
D V _ {i j} = \beta_ {1} T r e a t _ {i} + \beta_ {2} T r e a t _ {i} \times P o s t _ {t} + C o n t r o l _ {i j} + \mu_ {t} + \varepsilon_ {j t}\tag{3}
$$

where $D V _ { i j }$ is the dependent variable of interest, including DiaryLength<sub>ij</sub>, DiaryTopic<sub>ij</sub>, and DailyBrowse<sub>ij</sub>. Treat<sub>i</sub> is an indicator variable for whether the notebook i to which the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ entry belongs is in the treatment group. Post<sub>t</sub> is a binary variable indicating whether the aesthetic treatment described in notebook i was performed after the launch of the first policy. $\mu _ { t }$ controls for the day effects of release time of the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ diary entry in the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ notebook. $\beta _ { 2 }$ captures the impacts of monetary policy on a diary entry’s quality and traffic performance. Age<sub>i</sub>, Gender<sub>i</sub>, Price<sub>i</sub>, Surgical<sub>i</sub>, the number of diary entries of the same aesthetic treatment available by the release date of the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ diary entry of notebook i (SimilarDiaries<sub>ij</sub>), the number of entries posted before entry j (CumDiaries<sub>ij</sub>), and the days elapsed from the treatment date through the posting time of entry j (DaysAfterTreat<sub>ij</sub>) are included as controls.

Besides the effect of a monetary reward policy on the average outcome, we are interested in the entire distribution of outcomes. In particular, we employed a quantile regression approach (Angrist & Pischke, 2009) to see whether the reward policy affected the quality distribution. Quantile regression is a powerful tool to estimate how the distribution of an outcome variable changes with the explanatory variable.

## Review Length

Previous work (Burtch et al., 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2022) has found that review length is a good predictor of review quality. From the perspective of both consumers and the platform, diary entries containing more information would be more beneficial. Therefore, we examined the impacts of monetary incentives on the length of diary entries, DiaryLength<sub>ij</sub>, measured by the natural log of 1 plus the number of words in the $j ^ { \mathrm { t h } }$ entry. The upper panel of Table 14 shows the results. The DiD results suggest that, on average, monetary rewards lead on an increase of 43.9% in text length. The effect is more prominent in lower quantiles than in higher quantiles. The result could be partially attributed to the requirement specified in the reward policy. As shown in Table 14, the reward policy on the platform specifies not only the number of entries but also the number of photos required. Accordingly, an increase in words is likely to accompany an increase in photos.

As shown in Figure 7, 2 is the optimal number of topics when maximizing log-likelihood, 3 is the optimal choice for minimizing the SVD KL divergence value, and the optimal number of topics in the other two criteria is much larger.

After inspecting the distribution of keywords, we finally set the number of topics as 3, which provides an intuitively meaningful distribution of keywords. The first topic contains words such as “injection,” “skin,” and “hyaluronic acid,” indicating that the topic was more about the treatment itself. The second topic contains words such as “feeling,” “effect,” “recovery,” and “swelling,” suggesting that the topic is strongly related to the recovery process following an aesthetic treatment. Both of these topics are treatment-related topics. The top words in the third topic are related to daily life, such as “pepper,” “drink,” and “vegetables.” Table 15 shows the top 20 words for each topic.

For each diary entry, we obtained the probability vector $( P _ { I } , P _ { 2 } ,$ P<sub>3</sub>), in which $P _ { i }$ is the probability that the focal diary entry belongs to topic i. We measured the treatment-related information in a diary entry, DiaryTopic<sub>ij</sub>, by the probability that the diary entry was related to the first two topics $( P _ { I } + P _ { 2 } ) .$ We then estimated a DiD analysis with DiaryTopic<sub>ij</sub> as the dependent variable. The results are reported in the middle panel of Table 14. Overall, our findings suggest that a monetary reward policy increases the probability that the focal diary entry contains treatment-related information by 0.050.

Again, the effect was more prominent in the lower quantiles. The diary entry length and topic results can be seen as users responses to the reward policy, which required diary entries to reflect real changes following the treatment because diary entries that are too short or unrelated to treatment cannot fully capture changes. Furthermore, the more prominent effect in the lower quantiles suggests a minimal-effort effect in content. Users generally provided only enough content to meet the standard of reflecting real changes after treatment. Accordingly, we see a shift to the right of the entire distribution of quality, but the shift mainly comes from the left tail and only marginal improvements are found on the right tail (upper decile).

## Traffic

Intuitively, higher-quality entries will be viewed more times. We measured online traffic using the natural log of 1 plus the number of DailyBrowse<sub>ij</sub>. We regressed online traffic on Treat<sub>i</sub>×Post<sub>t</sub> to see whether monetary rewards increased diary entry traffic. The results are reported in the lower panel of Table 14. The results indicate that monetary rewards increased online traffic by 20.0%, on average.

According to the quantile regression results, the reward policy raised the median traffic by 11.7%. In contrast, there were no changes in the lower and upper deciles before and after the policy implementations. Our results suggest that monetary incentives likely affect quality distribution differently: i.e., they increase the traffic for low- to moderate-quality entries. These results may be partially attributed to improvements in diary length and topic relevance due to the requirement specified in the reward policy. Although positive, no evidence suggests that the traffic of diary entries eligible for monetary rewards was significantly higher than those in the control group in the upper decile.

## Economic Benefit Analysis

Our findings indicate that the reward policy did not increase the number of continued contributions by each user but incentivized users to post higher-quality diary entries, which then brought more traffic to the platform. On average, the policy increased the daily browsing of each diary entry by

1.34 units.<sup>10</sup> Given that the average number of diary entries in each notebook before the policy was implemented was 3.62, the policy increased the daily browsing of each notebook by 4.85 units. Among all the post-policy treated notebooks, on average, 52.78% reached the threshold. Thus, the platform had to pay 200\*52.78% = 105.56 RMB for each notebook. The average price of aesthetic treatments on the platform was

12,680 RMB, and the platform generally takes a 10% commission. Thus, given a break-even conversion rate 105.56/(4.85\*30\*1268) = 0.06%, the platform would benefit from the monetary reward policy within 30 days. Of course, the platform could have benefitted more from increased traffic (e.g., investment) than from pure purchase, which is, however, beyond the scope of this study.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted several robustness checks to ensure the robustness of our results. First, assuming that aesthetic treatments are typically booked in advance, some randomness exists in the reward qualification for users who booked treatments on the platform shortly before and after the policy change. Meanwhile, given that the policy is an exogenous change to users, it is unlikely that the users who booked a treatment shortly before the policy was implemented and those who booked a treatment shortly after were systematically different. This provided us with a neutral setting to test the robustness of our results. Therefore, we restricted the sample to treated notebooks on treatments done within 10 days before and after the policy launch and ran the following specifications. <sup>11</sup> Table 16 shows the estimation results. The coefficients of Post<sub>i</sub>×Threshold<sub>it</sub> are significantly negative, indicating the existence of minimal-effort behavior.

$$
L o g i t (D i a r y _ {i t}) = \beta_ {1} P o s t _ {i} + \delta D i a r y _ {i, t - 1} + C o n t r o l _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{r l} L o g i t (D i a r y _ {i t}) = & \beta_ {1} P o s t _ {i} + \beta_ {2} T h r e s h o l d 1 _ {i t} \\ & + \beta_ {3} P o s t _ {i} \times T h r e s h o l d 1 _ {i t} + \delta D i a r y _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + C o n t r o l _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t} \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

Second, we adopted a count data model to further validate the results on the number of subsequent contributions. We defined a new dependent variable DiaryCount as the number of entries released in period t in notebook i and ran a series of Poisson regressions. As shown in Table 17, estimates from the Poisson models are consistent with our main results. Third, in the main analysis, we removed post-policy observations of notebooks on treatments performed before the policy’s launch to avoid possible spillover effects of the reward policy. Retaining these observations provided consistent results, as shown in Table 18. Fourth, to test H3, we partitioned the data into two subsets based on the value of PrevComments<sub>it</sub> and ran the regressions on each subset. As a robustness test, we introduced a four-way interaction term in regression by interacting Threshold1<sub>it</sub>×Treat<sub>i</sub>×Post<sub>t</sub> with PrevComments<sub>it</sub>. The results are reported in Table 19. The results are qualitatively consistent. Fifth, we pooled observations of both policies and ran a DiD regression using Post1<sub>t</sub> to indicate the implementation period of the first policy and Post2<sub>t</sub> to indicate the implementation period of the second policy. Table 20 shows the estimation results. We observed a drop in the probability of posting subsequent entries at the first threshold of three entries in Policy 1. However, in Policy 2, the drop at the first threshold was smaller and less significant due to the second threshold in Policy 2—some users were incentivized to achieve the second threshold to obtain a higher reward. These results are consistent with our prior estimations. Finally, for the content-level analysis, we reestimated the effect of the reward policy on diary entry length, topic, and traffic using a matched sample with quantile regressions. The results are shown in Table 21. Consistent with our main results, we found that the reward policy positively affected the length of diary entries, topic relevance, and the traffic on diary entries in the lower quantiles.

## Conclusion and Implications

In this study, we explored how users’ posting behaviors change before and after reaching the thresholds outlined in monetary reward policies. An explicit goal of the focal monetary incentive policy was to encourage more highquality UGC. By basing rewards on thresholds, however, the policy could change users’ motivations, causing unintended user responses.

Through two natural experiments, we demonstrate that monetary rewards indeed alter users’ behaviors without obviously enhancing their contributions. Our results reveal that users tend to respond to monetary reward policies by exerting minimal effort. Users are more likely to reduce the contribution levels after meeting reward thresholds.

Moreover, we found that peer feedback mitigates unintended user responses. Social approval seems to be a powerful motivator when monetary incentives produce unintended consequences by crowding out users’ intrinsic motivations. One possible explanation for this is that social approval motivates users to gain recognition from community members.

The research context of our study is unique in that it takes some time for individuals to determine and share the effects of aesthetic treatments. This uniqueness allowed us to observe users’ behaviors in contributing UGC over time. With the increasing penetration of the internet in the mainstream business of high involvement products or services, motivating users to continue writing reviews on one product or service will become more prevalent. Moreover, goal setting is a widely adopted way to motivate individuals. We believe that threshold-based monetary incentives are likely to be adopted in other online contexts. Therefore, our results regarding the unintended consequences of threshold-based monetary incentives and the role of social approval from peers can likely be generalized to online communities in general and industries focused on experience or credence goods, in particular (e.g., online fitness and online medical communities).

## Theoretical Contributions

Our work makes several significant contributions to the literature on monetary incentives, UGC, and users’ unintended responses. First, although completion-contingent monetary incentives have been considered in the prior literature (Burtch et al., 2018; Khern-am-nuai et al., 2018), the reward policies that have been previously examined only require users to take action (e.g., write a product review) to obtain a reward. Our research focuses on reward policies demanding that users meet several requirements. We expand the findings in the literature by revealing users’ unintended responses to completion-contingent components in reward policies. Unlike previous literature (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018), where the dependent variable is whether a user is more likely to post UGC in the context of financial incentives, we focus on whether a user will continue to post subsequent entries. Compared with the effect of offering rewards for the first contribution, it is more difficult to obtain monetary rewards in the context of review volume thresholds. These thresholds may entice individual reviewers to focus on reaching a goal but may eliminate the intrinsic motivation after the task is completed. We observed a minimal-effort effect, where some users reduced their contribution after reaching the reward threshold compared with those in the control group. Our work not only expands on the above studies but also complements the literature on user motivation.

Second, our research builds on past work addressing the effects of feedback on individual behavior (e.g., Burtch et al., 2018; Huang et al., 2019; Jabr et al., 2014; Zhang & Zhu, 2011). Most information systems studies examining the influence of social interaction on UGC have focused on the direct effect of social factors on user behaviors. We considered whether social interactions serve as a mechanism that moderates the minimal-effort effect. Monetary incentives can shift a situation from a social to a monetary frame. But the presence of a social frame may cause users to worry that their money-seeking behavior might be observed, which could hurt their social image. Therefore, social interactions can preserve the social frame and thereby dissuade users from exerting minimal effort. Burtch et al. (2018) found that social norms can reduce the minimal-effort effect, but our research is conducted in a different context. In their study, users were rewarded if they contributed, whereas we focus on users unintended responses to threshold-based monetary incentives involving subsequent contributions. In contrast to social norms proxied as the number of reviewers in the previous month (Burtch et al., 2018), social approval from other users in our study was less intrusive and controlling. Our results suggest that social approval is also crucial in alleviating the crowding-out effect related to monetary incentives and can be easily implemented in online communities. Moreover, our work is based on natural experiments, which provide empirical results with better external validity. Therefore, our results contribute broadly to recent work in information systems that has examined the influence of social interactions in online UGC contexts.

Third, our analyses demonstrate that, on average, diary entries’ quality and traffic improved. The results are similar to those of Yu et al.’s (2022) study of performance-contingent monetary rewards. Complementing Yu et al. (2022), our results suggest that the quality improvements are seen primarily with the low and middle deciles, with users trying to improve the quality of their entries to meet the requirements of the reward policy. Only a slight improvement was found in the upper decile. Moreover, there was no change in terms of traffic in the upper decile. Our results suggest that users relying on extrinsic motivation (monetary rewards) will generally post entries that satisfy the minimum requirements.

## Managerial Implications

Incentive design is more important than ever in our intensely competitive world. This study offers several interesting managerial implications. First, it provides insights for online communities interested in designing and developing reward systems for UGC. Monetary reward policies are often implemented without much consideration regarding unintended consequences. However, our study suggests that such policies should be implemented with caution. We show that users are sensitive and responsive to threshold-based monetary incentives. Although such monetary reward policies are intended to stimulate UGC contributions, using threshold-based standards to determine eligibility could have unintended consequences. Users may weigh their efforts and their chances of being rewarded, consequently choosing to only to fulfill the minimum requirements. This shift in attention from the desired outcome to measurable outputs is evidenced in our study on threshold-based monetary incentive programs. Managers would do well to carefully design reward thresholds while taking potential unintended effects on user contributions into account.

Second, the effectiveness of using incentives to encourage contributions depends on whether those incentives affect the trust relationship between the involved parties. Our results regarding the second policy suggest that users indeed respond in an unintended way to requirement changes related to different policies. Firms must be alert to the possibility that frequent changes in incentive policies can signal distrust to users (Gneezy et al. 2011).

Third, our results also provide implications for functional designs for online platforms. We found that users respond positively when peers provide feedback on their diary entries. Gallus (2017) revealed that purely symbolic awards had a sizeable effect on newcomer retention, which persisted over the four quarters following the initial intervention. Complementing Gallus (2017), our results suggest that facilitating readers to express social approval is a plausible way of inducing prosocial behavior. Our findings indicate that the unintended consequences of threshold-based incentives are less likely to occur when a social frame is present; thus, online platforms should consider incorporating feedback mechanisms. For instance, platforms could facilitate users in searching for highquality diary entries or encourage more social interactions between reviewers and users. Fostering a sense of community among users can also help to restore the social frame.

## Limitations and Further Research

Our study is subject to several limitations that present opportunities for further research. First, we investigated the unintended consequences of policy requirements regarding UGC quantity in a monetary incentive program. The focal online community of this study required not only a minimum number of entries but subsequently rolled out an incentive program requiring a minimum number of words for each diary entry. Policies with UGC quality requirements could also produce unintended user responses. Therefore, future investigations are warranted to understand the impacts of the quantity and quality requirements of incentive programs designed to improve UGC contributions. Second, we studied the moderation effect of comments received from peers on users’ behavior. Future work is needed to explore how different social mechanisms (e.g., social connectedness) can attenuate the salience of unintended user responses. Moreover, whether and how platforms should explicitly utilize these mechanisms remain open empirical questions. Third, the reward payment to qualified users in the monetary incentive policies in our study was relatively large. We empirically found that increasing the size of the reward could prompt users to respond in unintended ways. For example, we found that in the second policy, those who received the full reward cap of 1,000 RMB after reaching the first threshold were less likely to exert further effort to attain the second threshold. While we did not specifically examine the differences between large and small monetary rewards, this is a promising future research direction. Finally, we chose the online aesthetic medicine industry as our research setting. Although we believe that our results showing that unintended responses can be induced by incentive programs hold in general, it would be worthwhile to examine the magnitude of the incentive-contribution gradient across different settings.
