# Organizational buyers’ assimilation of B2B platforms: Efects of ITenabled service functionality

A.K.M. Najmul Islam<sup>a,⁎</sup>, Ronald Cenfetelli<sup>b</sup>, Izak Benbasat<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Turku, Finland

<sup>b</sup> Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia, Canada

## ARTICLE INFO

Keywords: B2B platforms Service functionality Benefits Platform assimilation costs Platform assimilation

## A B S T R A C T

This paper investigates service functionality in the domain of B2B platform assimilation from the buyer’s perspective. Using a customer service life cycle framework, we identified five dimensions of service functionality, namely, information search, negotiation, acquisition, ownership, and retirement. We theorize that the importance of these dimensions is contingent upon current level of B2B platform assimilation. Furthermore, building on an enabler-inhibitor perspective, we theorize that the benefits and top management support are the enablers, whereas assimilation costs, managerial complexity, and demand uncertainty are the inhibitors of a firm’s future decision to assimilate a B2B platform. Using a two-staged field survey, we tested our theory on a sample of 191 professionals. The results indicate that the importance of service functionality dimensions varies depending on the current level of service assimilation, namely, the importance of information search functionalities decreases while the importance of ownership and retirement functionalities increases as the firms move from the awareness stage to the general deployment stage. Furthermore, our results indicate that benefits and top management support enable future platform assimilation irrespective of the assimilation stage a firm is in currently. Assimilation costs were found to have negative impact on future platform assimilation among the companies who had a low level of current assimilation. However, the efect became non-significant for the companies with a higher level of current assimilation. Our paper contributes to the theory de velopment by (i) showing that the importance of diferent IT mediated services is contingent upon current level of assimilation; and (ii) showing which factors retain their importance throughout the assimilation stages. We describe management implications for B2B platform owners and buyer organizations.

## Introduction

Business-to-Business (B2B) e-commerce platforms are a virtual and technology-enabled meeting spaces in which multiple buvers and suppliers are able to interact and transact without the need for physical or even synchronous contact (Yoo et al., 2011; Matook, 2013). According to a recent report by Forrester.¹ the US B2B e-commerce market will reach 1.2 trillion USD by 2021. Much of that growth is expected to come from “many-to-many” platforms, with large numbers of both buyers and sellers, such as the Amazon Business section of Amazon.com and Alibaba.com. Thus, businesses are increasingly moving their supply spending to B2B e-markets, reflecting a shift away from long-term contractual agreements with a subset of vendors. A report (B2BECNews, 2015) suggests that 34% of B2B companies search for products on platforms before making purchases. Taken together, B2B platforms continue to be a vibrant and important topic for information systems (IS) researchers and practitioners (Tan and Ludwig, 2016; Penttinen et al., 2018; Gorla et al., 2017).

The term assimilation refers to the extent to which a B2B platform has difused across organizational processes and is routinized in its use for purchasing activities (Fichman, 2001; Rai et al., 2009). Organizations may not fully realize expected benefits from a technology without its deep assimilation (Cooper & Zmud, 1990) and thus scholars have noted a troubling assimilation gap between rates of initial adoption and full assimilation (Fichman & Kemerer, 1999; Rai et al., 2009; Wright et al., 2017). Assimilation of a B2B platform is important for buyer organizations. For example, a report by Forrester<sup>2</sup> found that 90% of firms agreed that implementing B2B e-commerce led to an increase in attracting new customers, as well as keeping the existing customers satisfied. Furthermore, 92% firms believed that B2B e-commerce helped them in lowering costs, and 89% agreed that it helped them gain a competitive advantage.

Prior research (see Appendix A) investigating diferent stages of assimilation of B2B platforms for purchasing, or electronic procurement in general, have utilized institutional theory (Son and Benbasat, 2007; Ke et al., 2009), transaction-cost theory (Son and Benbasat. 2007). structuration theory of technology assimilation (Rai et al., 2009). technology-organization-environment (Mishra et al., 2007; Teo et al., 2009; Sila, 2013), resource-based view (Soares-Aguiar and Palma-Dos-Reis, 2008; Zhang and Dhaliwal, 2009), and innovation difusion theory (Chan and Chong, 2012). Two research gaps are observed in prior research. First, there has been an absence of research into the IT-enabled service functionalities that likely influence B2B platform assimilation for purchasing. This is surprising especially in light of the calls that such IT design attributes should be a focal area for IS research (Orlikowski and Iacono, 2001: Benbasat and Zmud. 2003: Benbasat and Barki. 2007), Second, by focusing on only one stage of the assimilation life cycle, such as the decision to adopt or use, most past studies have overlooked the fact that assimilation in an organization is an ongoing process. Mangalaraj et al. (2017) based on their meta-analysis suggest that organizations will benefit from attention to diferent factors at diferent stages of assimilation. For example, it would help organizations to navigate the adoption and use of B2B platforms for procurement purposes by including appropriate responses at various stages of assimilation. However, prior empirical research on using B2B platforms for purchasing falls short on explaining which factors retain their importance throughout the assimilation stages.

Towards addressing the above noted limitations, we investigate the role of IT-enabled services, namely service functionality, in the context of a buyer firm’s assimilation of platforms for its purchasing needs. We define service functionality as the array of ITenabled functionalities offered by a B2B platform that assists member firms in accomplishing their purchasing goals. B2B platforms are increasingly focusing on augmenting services, such as spend management, distribution and tracking, order management, etc. Emarkets are not only enabling firms to do more than just purchase materials, but also acting as an outsourcing partner for a number of supply chain management issues. The question of “what services should be provided” has been studied empirically in the context of B2C e-commerce (Cenfetelli et al., 2008) and in e-government contexts (Tan et al., 2013). however, in both instances the focus has been on individuals receiving IT-based services in an online context. Yet, surprisingly little research has investigated the role of service functionality in the B2B sphere even though a large volume of online transactions are B2B in nature. This is an important omission since we do not believe that the implications of the findings of service functionality studies conducted in the individual domain can be directly applied to the organizational services domain, and furthermore the theoretical context in which organizational versus individual service functionalities should be evaluated are significantly diferent, as we will describe next.

The services that are ofered in B2C (Cenfetelli et al., 2008) or e-government (Tan and Benbasat, 2009; Tan et al., 2013) contexts may not be fully appropriate in, and directly applicable to, the B2B context. One key reason is that the complexity of organizationa purchasing decision making is greater than that of the consumer or e-government context. In B2B context, prices are highly variable, volumes are much higher, and of a much wider range. Thus, negotiation between buyers and sellers may be more typical in the B2B context than the B2C (Malone et al., 1987). More flexible shipping and logistics solution may be required in the B2B context. Marketing and client educational initiatives are more complex in B2B, as clients are likely to be more demanding to understand how products work and interact with other products they already have or are considering for purchase. Thus, ownership and retirement services (i.e. after-sales services) may have more importance in the B2B context as compared to the B2C context (Cenfetelli et al., 2008). The platform policies (security, privacy guarantee, etc.) may impact sales highly in the context of B2B. Consequently, providing information related to these policies may be more important in B2B context as compared to B2C

Also, the importance of IT-mediated service functionalities may vary across organizations depending on their current level of service assimilation. Tan et al. (2013) shed some light on this phenomenon by finding that the more frequently citizens perform egovernment transactions, the greater will be their appreciation of ownership-oriented functionalities that assists customers to get the most benefit out of owned products or purchased services following their acquisition. However, beyond ownership-oriented services in the context of e-government, the importance of other functionalities – especially in the B2B context – is unknown. We describe next why this is very important particularly in B2B context.

From a theoretical standpoint, stakeholder theory suggests that certain stakeholders, because of their potential to satisfy critica organizational needs, will be more important than others (Jawahar and Mclaughlin, 2001). Thus, an organization needs to identify its important stakeholders and decide on a strategy to deal with each stakeholder depending on its importance. In order to set a strategy to deal with diferent stakeholders, such as buyer organizations with a low versus high level of current service assimilation, the platform owners need to know which service functionalities are critical for these stakeholders. Consequently, research that addresses the importance of diferent IT-mediated service functionalities depending on the current level of assimilation may bring practica benefits for platform owners, such as, a customized intervention plans depending on the buyer group (low versus high level of current service assimilation). In other words, diferent types of technology designs may be needed to retain diferent types of organizationa buyers. Despite its practical relevance, no research to our knowledge has investigated how the importance of IT-mediated service functionalities may vary across buyer organizations depending on their current level of service assimilation. Taken together, research investigating service functionality in the domain of B2B is highly relevant for both research and practice

The aim of our study is threefold: (1) to understand, identify and evaluate the IT-enabled service functionality provided in an online B2B context; (2) to understand the relative importance of these IT-mediated service functionalities contingent upon the current level of service assimilation; and (3) to fine grained understanding of the factors that influence a buying firm’s future assimilation of a platform throughout the assimilation stages.

We will first derive the dimensions of service functionality from the customer service life cycle (CSLC) framework (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). We use this framework to explicate a wide array of service functions that can be deployed through B2B platforms. Next. we validate this framework, as well as identify a list of possible enablers and inhibitors associated with B2B platform assimilation, based on a study of 35 respondents. We then develop a model of the role that this array of service functionalities can play in influencing the decision-makers in the organization to assimilate these platforms, and test this model using a sample of 191 respondents. We conclude the paper with a discussion of the results and their implications for research and practice with a focus on ITenabled functionality.

## IT-enabled service functionalities for B2B platforms

The first goal of this paper is to identify a list of IT-enabled service functionalities that can be provided by B2B platforms. ITenabled service functionalities refer to the use of IT to provide an array of online services which support business customers beyond the core products or services being bought or sold (Lovelock, 1994; Cenfetelli et al., 2008). The functionalities of a platform are a means of augmenting core products or services by helping buyers reach their overall purchasing and transactional goals, such as product search, product ordering, delivery tracking, and doing so through the use of IT. An example of applying this concept to the B2B context is Alibaba.com’s use of collaborative filtering technology to make individual product recommendations. This IT functionality provides specific product recommendations based upon the buyers’ characteristics, such as business type and geographica location of the product vendor. Consequently, the array of IT service functionalities can be viewed as object-based beliefs (see Wixom and Todd, 2005) that assist in the firm’s achievement of their purchasing goals.

In order to identify a list of IT-enabled service functionalities in the B2B context, we adopted and adapted the CSLC framework (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). The CSLC describes the various interactions a buyer has with a vendor before, during, and after the purchase of a product or service. The interaction between buyer and vendor begins with requirements specification and acquisition of the product, and then continues with the ownership and the retirement of the product. Each of these four phases, in turn, are augmented by a series of buyer-centric service functions that can generate added value for buyers (Ives and Learmonth, 1984). Table 1 summarizes the CSLC model.

According to Piccoli et al. (2001), the CSLC serves as a generic framework that can be used to study buyer-seller relationships in both B2B and B2C contexts. Therefore, prior research applied the CSLC model in e-commerce and e-government domains in order to identify website design principles that meet individual consumers’ transactional expectations. For example, Cenfetelli et al. (2008) applied the CSLC to the B2C context and subsequently derived dimensions of website service content functions. Tan et al. (2013) adapted the CSLC to the e-government context in order to investigate service content quality of various government websites for individual citizens. Both studies found that service functionality was an important design attribute towards attracting and engaging online clients and/or citizens.

## Information search

One of the main reasons for buvers and suppliers to join a platform is to reduce search costs (Malone et al.. 1987: Kaplan and Sawhney, 2000; Standing et al., 2010). Obtaining and processing information about suppliers, product oferings, and price (Bakos, 1998; Standing et al., 2010) is a cumbersome and time consuming task that a platform could perform or support as a core technologyenabled service to its client firms. In a B2B platform context, this is the phase where a buyer searches for a business partner to transact with, a partner’s product oferings, information about the platform provider, and general information about trades. Consequently, we propose the following three sub-dimensions related to these activities that can be enabled by IT.

Search for products is a part of the information search phase of the business customer service life cycle. One of the main roles of a platform is to aggregate the suppliers’ product oferings (Standing et al., 2010). Therefore, IT can be used to assist in product search is an online product catalogue (Kumar and Raheja, 2012) and/or pages for describing the details of products, to include product visualizations or even virtual product demonstrations. During this phase, the platform uses IT to help buyers search, refine the search and/or compare the products based on the date posted, availability and lead time, and price in some cases.

Search for supplier is another sub-dimension of the information search phase. Generally, a supplier can create awareness through advertising in online newspapers or journals, which can reach a large audience (e.g., economist.com). However, in the B2B platform context, becoming a member can increase a supplier’s chances to become known by potential buyers. In addition to being a medium for creating awareness, a B2B platform provides a medium to suppliers for posting “selling leads”. IT helps buyers search, and/or refine their search based on supplier categories and/or the geographical region of the supplier. The B2B platform can provide a supplier’s detailed information, and the typical products and services that supplier sells.

Dahlman (1979) argued policing and enforcement related costs can be avoided by contractual stipulations or by declining to trade with suppliers who would be known to avoid fulfilling their obligations, if there is suficient foreknowledge. Therefore, platforms may include information related to transaction policies and enforcement. Following this, we propose a third sub-dimension of information search, namely B2B platform search. In order to support this sub-dimension, the platforms may share general information about privacy, guarantees, security policies, B2B trade news (e.g. trade shows), and other items of general interest to marketplace members. Additionally, because the transactions in a B2B platform happen virtually with minimal face-to-face interactions, considerable uncertainty may arise among buyers and suppliers. Grewal et al. (2010) argued that platform efectiveness may depend on manifestations of uncertainty about the behaviors of the market maker and market participants. To reduce this uncertainty, B2B platforms may disclose information, such as, the composition of its senior management team, its partners and afiliates, or it may provide links to third-party assurances. Links to “about us” and/or “customer testimonials” are common examples of IT functionalities to support this sub-dimension. Moreover, the platform may use IT to provide “customer service” and/or “frequently asked questions” links and a “forum” for its client firms to discuss issues freely. Such IT functionality services are expected to improve a B2B platform's reputation and integrity (Grewal et al., 2010).

## Negotiation

Negotiation, defined as the process through which a buyer and a seller establish the terms of a purchase agreement, is a fundamental phenomenon in inter-firm exchange behavior in industrial markets (Perdue and Summers, 1991). Due to its pragmatic importance, much research has been conducted on negotiation since the 1950s (Alderson, 1957; Perdue and Summers, 1991; Peterson and Lucas, 2001). Among these, a stream of research has indicated the importance of pre-negotiation planning on negotiation success (Rognes, 1995; Lewicki et al., 1997; Peterson and Lucas, 2001) and distinguished between pre-negotiation and actua negotiation activities in a negotiation process (Peterson and Shepherd, 2010). Building on this, we also argue that B2B platforms may utilize IT for supporting both pre-negotiation (i.e. preparation for negotiation), and subsequent negotiation activities. In the preparation for negotiation phase, the B2B platform may assist a potential buyer by providing information about the trading rules of countries that the suppliers belong to, their currencies, as well as demonstrate how to negotiate and if available, how to use negotiation services. In order to support the actual negotiation phase, B2B platforms may utilize IT to provide functionality to support online negotiation, thus potentially saving time and costs (Johnson and Cooper, 2009). In addition, design specific functions, such as dynamic tools can be used to support diferent kinds of negotiation possibilities, such as auctions or online bidding (Ordanini, 2006). If the supplier and its business customer agree on negotiation terms, the phase concludes with a contract (Archer and Yuan, 2000). Agreement on quantity and price of product, product design, delivery, warranty and other post-sale services are the major details of the contract. The platform can help alert both buyer and supplier parties of these contractual needs and incorporate those needs into an automated contract production tool.

## Acquisition

In the acquisition phase, the supplier fulfills its business customer’s contract requirements in order to satisfy the customer. The acquisition sub-dimensions are order, payment, shipment, and delivery. Order and payment describe those sub-dimensions where a buyer communicates what and how much of a product is desired (Cenfetelli et al., 2008) and the buyer subsequently makes payment. The B2B platform may use design-specific functions, such as, electronic purchase orders, add/change/delete items to the shopping cart, provision of email order confirmation, fast checkout for repeat customers, cancel/change orders, secured online payment processing, and/or alternate forms of online payments. The shipment and delivery phases are the sub-dimensions of acquisition that are typically the responsibilities of the suppliers. However, a B2B platform may also provide design specific functions, such as, diferent shipment and delivery options with estimated costs, and online order status tracking. B2B platforms that provide delivery and logistics function may form partnerships with third parties who specialize in these core activities, such as FedEx (Dai and Kauffman 2002).

## Ownership

The ownership sub-dimensions are training, monitor, and maintenance. During the training phase, a platform may use IT to assist in training its business customers on how to use a product most efectively for example through video materials. This is potentially useful for buyers especially when the B2B platform operates in a single industry, since the product lines provided by the suppliers may be limited. The monitor phase involves support for the buyer to track activities related with the products that a buyer has already purchased towards exploiting a product to its fullest capabilities. For example, the B2B platform may use IT to inform buyers of a need for replacement parts/supplies based upon the monitoring and analyzing of past purchase behavior. Furthermore, B2B platforms can provide tools and reports that leverage the large quantity of data generated from online transactions to inform its customers about the trends of industry-specific procurement knowledge for future decision-making (Fauska et al., 2013). In the maintenance phase, a B2B platform may help the buyers get a product repaired or keep it in good working condition. The platform could make announcements about upgrades available to the product purchased by buyers, such as additional modules for purchased software packages.

## Retirement

According to the CSLC (Ives and Learmonth, 1984), the retirement dimension consists of four services: replace, evaluate, return/ resale, and account. Among these, replace and return/resale are the functions that are mostly managed by the suppliers and buyers with minimal efort from platforms. As a result, platform may provide services only during account for and evaluate phases. Account for enables buyers to track their spending portfolio as done through the B2B platform. For example, IT can be used to help buyers track their spending history such as the suppliers negotiated with, transactions made, or their history of payments. In the evaluate phase, a buyer evaluates both the platform and the supplier. Therefore, a B2B platform may provide tools and services (e.g., ratings, feedback forms) enabling buyers to provide feedback about the suppliers and the platform. Both account for and evaluate may be particularly important for managing suppliers' opportunism (Grewal et al., 2010).

Taken together, we identified five classes of service functionalities in B2B context: (i) information search functionalities, such as product search, supplier search, and B2B search; (ii) negotiation functionalities, such as preparing for negotiation, and actual negotiation; (iii) acquisition functionalities, such as order, payment, delivery and shipment; (iv) ownership functionalities such as train, monitor, maintenance, and upgrade; (v) retirement services, such as account for and evaluate. These functionalities correspond to the five procurement stages described in CSLC. Therefore, we note that any new IT-mediated service identified in the future can be placed under one of the five classes of service functionalities identified in this paper. This implies that our conceptualization of the five classes of service dimensions would remain valid irrespective of new services identified in the future. These five services represent moderately complementary services that help buyer organizations in their procurement activities. The adoption and use of a certain function does not guarantee the adoption and use of other functions. For example, a buyer organization may search for products in a B2B platform to make a price comparison, however, they may decide to purchase via other channels and refrain from using other platform functions.

## Conceptualizing functionality assimilation

There are seven stages of assimilation, namely, awareness, interest, evaluation/trial, commitment, limited deployment, partial deployment, and general deployment. Awareness is the stage when the key decision makers are aware of the technologies. At the interest stage, the organization is committed to actively learn more about the technologies for their B2B purchases. Evaluation/trial is the stage when the organization has acquired the technologies and has initiated evaluation or trial usage. Commitment is the stage when the organization has committed to use the technologies in a significant way. Next, at the limited deployment stage, the organization has established a program of regular, but limited use (e.g., 5 percent to 25 percent) of the technologies for B2B purchasing. At the partial deployment stage, the organization increases its use of the technologies (e.g., 25 percent to 50 percent). Finally, at the general deployment stage, the organization has reached a state, where the technologies are used on a majority of B2B purchases.

In comparison to the decision to adopt or use a B2B platform, which has been one of the main dependent variables in the prior literature, assimilation instead ofers a more comprehensive assessment of a firm’s degree of use, from whether there is no B2B platform usage at all, through to initial adoption and continued use of a B2B platform. As we described above, assimilation captures a continuum ranging from mere awareness and interest through to partial and general deployment. As one of the goals in this paper is to identify the generalizable factors that influence a buying firm’s future use of a platform throughout the assimilation stages, the comprehensive assessment of assimilation using the seven stages described earlier is deemed more suitable than the decision to adopt or use.

Assimilation of a B2B platform can be conceptualized and measured in two ways. First. it can be conceptualized as to what extent the organization uses the B2B platform as a whole. The second approach is known as aggregated approach, where assimilation can be conceptualized and measured as to what extent the organization uses individual service functionalities and then aggregating them together. Prior research suggested adopting an aggregated approach to assess assimilation in a buyer firm because aggregated measures across complementary functions enhance predictive validity (see Fichman, 2001; Rai et al., 2009). As described earlier, the five service functionalities identified in this paper are complementary functions that are provided by a B2B platform. Therefore, the aggregate measure across these services helps us to capture a richer assessment of B2B platform assimilation. Furthermore, the aggregated approach has been suggested to be more preferable as it improves measurement reliability (Rai et al., 2009). Consequently, we adopt aggregated conceptualization and define current or future B2B service assimilation as the extent to which the five service functionalities (i.e., information search, negotiation, acquisition, ownership, and retirement) have difused or will difuse across a buyer firm’s procurement process (Rai et al., 2009).

Taken together, our conceptualization provides a comprehensive assessment of B2B platform utilization for transactions by capturing seven assimilation stages ranging from awareness to general deployment as well as aggregating the assimilation of individual service functionalities

## Study 1: Validating the dimensions of b2b functionality

To validate the array of service functionalities described in Table 2, we recruited a panel of B2B purchasing experts from the business community. The panel was recruited as follows. First, we contacted 23 business managers with expertise in B2B purchasing activities. Participants were recruited through colleagues and the authors' own professional contacts. We invited these business managers to participate in the electronic survey and applied a snowball sampling technique by asking each panelist to recruit his/her own contacts who also have expertise in B2B purchasing activities. This led to an additional 12 panelists, for a total of 35 participants. 20 panelists (57.1%) were male. The panelists reported their job titles such as managers (42.9%), procurement executives/consultants (34.2%), and directors (22.9%). The panelists had experience in organizational purchasing with an average of 9 vears. We asked panel members to evaluate the array of service functionalities in Table 2 to determine whether or not all of the important service functionalities were included. We also asked the panel to report what other functionalities should be added or removed and why using an open-ended survey question.

Furthermore, we asked the panel members to help us develop a possible list of important factors that act as enablers and inhibitors of transacting via a B2B marketplace. Prior literature (see Appendix A) provided us a long list of factors that might enable or inhibit transaction via a B2B marketplace. We first presented the list of enablers in a randomized order and asked the panel members to select those factors that they think would facilitate them transacting via a B2B platform. The panel members could select as many factors as they desired. Furthermore, they were also asked if other enablers should be added using an open-ended survey question. Next, we provided the panel members all the inhibitor factors found in prior literature in a randomized order. We then asked the panel to select those factors they think would prevent them from transacting via a B2B platform. We also asked the panelists for any other possible inhibitors that should be added using an open-ended survey question.

All 35 panelists indicated that all of the important functionalities were included in the dimensions and no other additions or deletions were suggested. 5 panelists attempted to answer the open-ended questions. ## The theoretical framework

The theoretical model developed in this section explains the role of the service functionalities available in the B2B platforms (described in the previous section) in influencing the decision-makers to assimilate these platforms in their organizations’ purchasing activities. Our overall theoretical model is illustrated in Fig. 2.

As described earlier, the left side of the model showing IT-mediated service functionalities has been developed based on the CSLC and studies on B2B electronic procurement life cycle (Archer & Yuan, 2000). In the right-hand side of the model, we adopted the engbler-inhibitor perspective (Luftman et al., 1999: Cenfetelli. 2004: Kannabrian and Dharmalingam. 2012). The enablers are the factors that either encourage or discourage usage, dependent on valence (Cenfetelli, 2004). Enablers are positively oriented and they are suggested as positively associated with use. For example, top management support may lead to the use of a B2B platform, whereas, lack of it may discourage usage. Cenfetelli (2004) also argues that there exists some factors that are uniquely negative, and thus discourage use and vet are not simply the opposing valence of an enabler, In contrast to enablers, inhibitors act solely to discourage use. For example, if an online purchase is completed without any incident, it will be favorably perceived. If the organizational decision makers do not have the inhibitor perception (e.g., transaction risk), it is unlikely to have any impact on the use of B2B platform. The presence of enablers and inhibitors is also supported by the two-factor theory of job satisfaction (Herzberg et al., 1966), which suggests that satisfaction is not the opposite of dissatisfaction, rather than they are separate factors. The same logic applies to trust and distrust (Lewicki et al., 1998). According to the two-factor theory, the motivator factors cause job satisfaction, but their absence may contribute little to job dissatisfaction. Similarly, the hygiene factors cause job dissatisfaction. but their absence do not cause job satisfaction. This implies that factors that discourage B2B assimilation may be unipolar in nature, which also supports Cenfetelli’s (2004) view on the presence of uniquely negative factors (i.e. inhibitors).

We adopted the enabler-inhibitor perspective due to the following four reasons. First, prior IS research focused mostly on enablers and put limited attention on inhibitors. However, inhibitors can exist independently from enablers, as well as behave diferently than enablers in influencing adoption or rejection (Cenfetelli & Schwarz, 2011). Second, according to Baumeister et al. (2001), “bad is stronger than good” in that negative events are often more salient and diagnostic as compared to positive events. Therefore, an organizational use of a technology may depend as much or more on the presence of inhibitors as the presence of enablers. Third, inhibitors may attract faster attention as they are negative in nature. Therefore, the inhibitors may drown out the enablers, biasing the decision to use a technology based on the inhibitors. Finally, by investigating both enablers and inhibitors together, we investigate the key unique factors that influence B2B platform assimilation across stages. This allows us not only to identify manageria implications that encourage use of B2B platform, but also implications that may discourage their use.

The decision of a firm to participate and assimilate B2B platforms is often based upon an assessment of the benefits and costs of doing so. Although the reasons for using a B2B platform are usually complex and multifaceted as reported by Son and Benbasat (2007), our panelist study found that benefits are the most prominent enabler for transacting via a B2B platform (Table 3). On the inhibitors side, both managerial complexity and transaction risks were dominant reasons that may prevent firms from transacting via a B2B platform. According to Zhu et al. (2006), managerial complexity and transaction risks can be considered as the costs for transacting via a platform. Thus, we include benefits (i.e. direct benefits and indirect benefits) and costs (i.e. managerial complexity and transaction risks) in our research model. In addition, from the results of study 1. we suggest that two other enablers, organizational readiness and top management support facilitate an organization’s future assimilation of a B2B platform, whereas one other inhibitor, demand uncertainty makes it dificult for an organization to assimilate a B2B platform. Finally, the relationship between ITenabled functionalities and benefits is supported by prior literature (e.g. Cenfetelli et al., 2008; Wixom & Todd, 2005), which we discuss further in the following sub-section.

## Influence of IT-enabled functionalities on benefits

By leveraging IT, a B2B platform can support a core product or service transaction to help buyers reach their online purchasing goals. We argue that a B2B platform’s technological platform in supporting a transactional task will lead to organizations being subjected to advantages. Depending on the degree of service functionalities of a B2B platform, an organizational buyer benefits from transactional eficiencies in terms of paper-processing cost reduction, order accuracy, and error reduction in purchase orders (Eng, 2004; Chang and Wong, 2010).

We earlier defined B2B platform functionality as the array of IT-enabled services provided by a platform to its client firms for accomplishing their purchasing goals. As noted earlier, the IT enabled service functionalities can be viewed as object based beliefs, whereas, benefits can be viewed as behavioral beliefs. According to Wixom and Todd (2005) object-based beliefs are concerned about the characteristics of the technology, whereas, behavioral beliefs are concerned about the consequences of the technology usage. Wixom and Todd (2005) argued that obiect-based beliefs might have indirect effects on adoption and use via behavioral beliefs. They further argued that obiect-based beliefs would have effects on behavioral beliefs via obiect-based attitude. However, subsequent studies empirically validated the direct effect of obiect-based beliefs on behavioral beliefs (Islam. 2012: Cenfetelli et al., 2008) Furthermore, prior research has empirically verified the relationship between IT-enabled service dimensions and service functionality in both B2C (Cenfetelli et al., 2008) and e-Government (Tan et al., 2013) contexts.

As represented in the theoretical model in Fig. 1, the benefits of a platform increases when the IT-enabled functionalities provided by the platform increases. We modeled the benefits of platform as being influenced by five aggregate dimensions of (1) information search: product search, supplier search, and B2B search, (2) negotiation: negotiation preparation and negotiation, (3) acquisition: order, payment, delivery and shipment, (4) ownership: train, monitor, maintenance, and (5) retirement: account for and evaluate. The focus on these five major dimensions as the antecedents of platform benefits also implies that these are enduring service dimensions and that as other lower-level services are potentially made available in the future through advances in technology, the model still remains valid by focusing on these five major dimensions with the possible inclusion of additional services under each.

While the service functionalities are designed to encourage proactive participation on the part of the clients, the nature of certain transactions may not necessitate prolonged involvement (Tan et al., 2013). For example, organizations that have made severa trading activities using the platform may be able to identify additional products from the same supplier and thus transact with them. The buyer organizations may prefer to build a closer cooperation with the sellers (Malone et al., 1987) after performing some trading activities. They may realize that the identified sellers are perhaps the right options for fulfilling their needs. Thus, they may prefer a mechanism that is somewhat similar to electronic integration instead of market (Malone et al., 1987). However, they may still remain in the platform and keep an eye on the market to make sure that their preference is justified. As a result, their membership to the platform is still important as it helps to avoid the risks of opportunistic trading partners in terms of prices. However, information search and negotiation related functionalities might be deemed less important for these organizations than the organizations that are less frequent in trading activities. It implies that the current level of service assimilation may impose an attenuating effect on the relationships between benefits and information search as well as benefits and negotiation.

On the other hand, the less frequently buyers perform a transaction, the greater will be their appreciation of information search and negotiation functions. These buyers would prefer to check the marketplace carefully to identify new products and prices as they transact occasionally and better products and prices may be available since their last purchase. Thus, information search and ne gotiation services would be highly valuable for these buyers. Consequently, we propose the following hypotheses.

H1. The efect of information search on benefits is moderated by current level of service assimilation, such that the efect will be stronger among companies with low level of service assimilation.

H2. The efect of negotiation on benefits is moderated by current level of service assimilation, such that the efect will be stronger among companies with low level of service assimilation.

Piccoli et al. (2001) suggested that customers might find certain CSLC phases present particular challenges for them. Therefore, seller firms need to adjust their oferings based on these challenges. In particular, Piccoli et al. (2001) suggested that in a fiercely competitive industry the real potential might lie in the firm’s ability to diferentiate its ofer by dealing with unresolved customer problems such as the need to efectively account for the total cost of ownership or use of its product or service. This implies that acquisition, ownership, and retirement services are critical as well as diferentiating factors that retain buyer organizations who have made several trading activities using the B2B platform.

The B2B services make it easy for the customers to do business with the sellers. The services related to acquisition, ownership, and retirement usually make this particularly easy for customers who purchase frequently, as certain elements of subsequent orders (e.g., shipping information, payment methods) typically are repeated from the first (Piccoli et al., 2001). The B2B platform builds a detailed customer history to guide the buyers for new purchase as well as providing necessary aftersales services. Therefore, it increases switching costs in the form of reconfiguring new purchasing channels if the buyer decides not to purchase via the B2B platform. This implies that the services related to acquisition, ownership, and retirement will be valued higher by the firms who have a higher leve of service assimilation.

The above notion is also supported by Archer & Yuan (2000). According to Archer and Yuan (2000), during the acquisition, ownership, and retirement phases, the emphasis shifts to building loyalty, trust, and commitment that will result in a lasting relationship. As we have explained earlier, once a buyer organization is satisfied with transacting with a seller, it may prefer a closer cooperation with the seller. Providing good support during acquisition, ownership, and retirement stages are the basis to build long lasting relationship. This implies that the acquisition, ownership, and retirement services will be mostly valued by the organizations that have made several trading activities using the platform (i.e. organizations with a high current level of IT-enabled service assimilation). Furthermore, the more an organization purchases via the B2B platform, the better it may realize the cost-savings on acquisition, ownership and retirement services. Consequently, these IT-mediated services may be deemed more important for organizations that have a high current level of IT-enabled service assimilation. In contrast, buvers with low level of assimilation may not prioritize to build a closer cooperation, instead they may just want to take advantage of the platform to make sure that the prices of the products they want to purchase are competitive. Thus, they do not fully realize the cost-savings on acquisition, ownership, and retirement services. Son & Benbasat (2007) suggest that for infrequent buyers, the benefits of platform are not fully realized. Indeed, these services would be important for them in general. However, in compare to the organizations with high level of assimilation, these organizations may emphasize less on these services as the main benefits of the platform. Furthermore, the switching costs in terms of establishing new purchasing channels for these buyers would be less. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses.

H3. The efect of acquisition on benefits is moderated by current level of service assimilation, such that the efect will be stronger among companies with high level of service assimilation.

H4. The efect of ownership on benefits is moderated by current level of service assimilation, such that the efect will be stronger among companies with high level of service assimilation.

H5. The efect of retirement on benefits is moderated by current level of service assimilation, such that the efect will be stronger among companies with high level of service assimilation.

The efects of enablers on B2B platform assimilation

## Benefits

Among other things, firms often take the benefits of a platform into account when they decide upon future use of a platform. Benefits can be of two types: direct benefits and indirect benefits. Direct benefits refer to the benefits, including cost savings and other internal eficiencies arising from, for example, reduced paperwork, reduced data entry, and reduced error rate (Iacovou et al., 1995). On the other hand, indirect benefits are opportunities that emerge from the use of a B2B platform, such as improved relationship with suppliers and the potential for process reengineering (Kuan and Chau, 2001).

The prior literature supports that firms use inter-organizational information systems (IOS) as these systems provide potentia benefits such as cost reductions, and productivity gain (Barrett and Konsynski, 1982; Choudhury et al., 1998). For example, Saunders and Clark (1992) demonstrated the impact of benefits of IOS on intention to adopt Electronic Data Interchange (EDI). Iacovou et al. (1995) investigated the role of benefits in adoption and integration of EDI in the small organizations context. Quaddus and Hofmeyer (2007) found benefits as significant in predicting intention to adopt B2B. Similarly, Wang et al. (2010) also found that benefits had significant influence on intention to increase B2B integration sophistication. Therefore, we posit that the degree of benefits will enhance a firm’s assimilation of B2B platform and propose the following hypothesis.

H6. The degree of benefits will have a positive impact on B2B platform assimilation.

## Organizational readines

Organizational readiness can be viewed as the resources through which goals are accomplished and power is exercised (Orlikowski & Robey, 1991). Organization readiness measures whether a firm has suficient IT sophistication and financial resources to undertake the assimilation of a B2B platform (Iacovou et al., 1995: Rai et al., 2009). IT sophistication refers to the aggregate signal of the desirability and importance of IT-mediated innovations for core organizational processes, such as B2B platform assimilation (Rai et al., 2009). Firms with a high degree of IT sophistication possess superior corporate data resources, information management practices, skilled stafs, and resources for the organizational integration of IT innovations (Rai et al., 2009). This means that firms with high IT sophistication usually have suficient resources and capabilities to transform procurement using B2B platforms. Suficient levels of resources and capabilities have been suggested as the pre-requisite of success in prior literature (Broadbent et al., 1999; Han et al., 2008). On the other hand, financial resources refer to those monetary resources available to be invested in organizationa investment and innovation, such as B2B platform assimilation. The availability of financial resources that can be used for online procurement purposes also legitimizes and supports actions related to installation, integration with processes, enhancements, and ongoing expenses during B2B platform assimilation (Chwelos et al., 2001; Rai et al., 2009). Taken together, we expect that a high degree of organizational readiness related to IT sophistication and financial resources will impact B2B platform assimilation. Prior research reported significant positive relationship with readiness and EDI adoption (Iacovou et al., 1995; Chwelos et al., 2001). As well, Rai et al. (2009) reported a positive relationship between organizational readiness and electronic procurement assimilation. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

H7. The degree of organizational readiness will have a positive impact on B2B platform assimilation.

## Top management support

A key requirement of successful assimilation of a B2B platform is top management support. Drawing on structuration theory (Orlikowski, 1992), Rai et al. (2009) argued that top management provides the strategic rationale and can legitimize assimilation of a technology by demonstrating their commitment and political support through participation in deployment initiatives. Actions by top management can modify existing structures and introduce complementary structures that facilitate B2B platform assimilation. Without strong backing from senior management, it becomes dificult, if not impossible, for an organization to successfully assimilate a B2B platform in the procurement process. For instance, the procurement team may realize procurement via a B2B platform is a strategic necessity for reducing costs and business survival, but they need to seek commitment and support from the top management to prioritize and assign resources in relation to online procurement. Thus, we suggest that top management support can impact B2B platform assimilation. Several prior studies have investigated and supported the relationship between top management support and online procurement decisions. For example, Premkumar and Ramamurthy (1995) reported a positive causal relationship between top management support and EDI adoption. Rai et al. (2009) found top management support as the significant predictor of electronic procurement assimilation. Chan and Chong (2012) also reported a positive causal relationship between top management support and IOS adoption. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

H8. The degree of top management support will have a positive impact on B2B platform assimilation.

## The efects of inhibitors on B2B platform assimilation

## Assimilation costs

In study 1, we identified two factors (i.e. managerial complexity and transactional risk) that can be regarded as costs (Zhu et al., 2006). First, managerial complexity is defined as the level of complexity and attendant risk associated with making process changes and organizational adjustments necessary to accommodate the B2B platform for online purchase (Zhu et al., 2006). Manageria complexity is regarded as one of the major sources of costs as it adds significant implementation risk. Second, transactional risk is defined as risk and security concerns about transactions conducted in the B2B platform (Zhu et al., 2006). B2B platforms bring unique issues with respect to data security in online transactions as compared to offline transactions. Online transactions take place over what may be insecure channels or otherwise subject to cybersecurity threats, thus leading to financial loss, stolen information, or other security risks. Together, managerial complexity and transactional risks may entail additional costs to transact via a B2B platform. We hypothesize that these costs can be a barrier of B2B platform assimilation as follows:

H9. The degree of assimilation costs will have a negative impact on B2B platform assimilation.

## Demand uncertainty

According to the transaction cost theory (Rindfleisch and Heide, 1997; Williamson, 1993), uncertainty, along with asset specificity, is a key factor influencing an organization’s choice of governance structure. Based on this, we suggest that uncertainty is an important factor that can influence an organizational decision to assimilate a B2B platform for online purchasing. There are two specific types of demand uncertainty that may influence organizational use of B2B platform: (1) the extent to which the frequency of purchase of a product is predictable (frequency uncertainty of demand), and (2) the extent to which the volume of purchases of a product is predictable (volume uncertainty of demand). Son and Benbasat (2007) proposed a negative impact of demand uncertaint on the organization's use of a B2B platform due to the two reasons. First, when high level of demand uncertainty exists. B2B platforms could be a less viable option. In fact, buyers and suppliers may choose to cope with the uncertainty jointly by developing seamless integration of cross-organizational business processes though electronic hierarchies. Second, due to a high level of demand uncertainty, a firm might not easily quantify the benefits of engaging in B2B platforms. Thus, the firm cannot easily justify its investment on B2B platform. Based on this, we propose the following hypothesis.

H10. The degree of demand uncertainty will have a negative impact on B2B platform assimilation.

## Study 2: Testing the theoretical framework

## Methodology

In Study 2, we tested our research model as shown in Fig. 2. We tested the model using a field survey methodology. We gathered data from business professionals who have experience in organizational purchasing and have knowledge about B2B platforms and their services.

## Instrument development for survey

Where available, the questionnaire items were adapted from previous literature; otherwise, new items were developed. In addition to the items adapted from Cenfetelli et al. (2008), we initially generated 60 items to attempt to tap into the various service functionality dimensions. To verify the construct validity of the service function dimensions, we employed a card-sorting technique (Moore and Benbasat, 1991). We recruited nine judges who were MBA or PhD students familiar with B2B e-commerce. The judges were asked to assign each item to one of the functional dimensions or to an “ambiguous” category if they were unsure about the meaning of the item or about the placement of the item. Two dimensions – delivery and shipment – showed some conceptual overlap as many of the judges placed items from these dimensions into the same category. Given the prima facie similarity in these dimensions, we combined shipment with delivery. This resulted in an average “hit ratio” of 83.3% across the resulting 13 dimensions. The Cohen’s Kappa was 0.81, well above the acceptable level of 0.70 (Boudreau et al., 2001).<sup>3</sup> The instrument then went through the first round of pilot testing with 35 professionals who had purchasing experience with B2B platforms. Based on alpha values, item-item correlations, and item-to-total correlations, many items were deleted. At the end this process, 26 items were retained to measure the array of services. Thereafter, another round of pilot testing was conducted with 95 professionals who had purchasing experience with B2B platforms. No item was removed at this stage.

The wording for the measurement items for direct benefits, top management support, IT sophistication, financial resources, managerial complexity, transaction risk, volume uncertainty, and frequency uncertainty was derived from the earlier literature on B2B (Kaun and Chau, 2001; Zhu et al., 2006; Quaddus and Hofmeyer, 2007; Son and Benbasat, 2007; Rai et al., 2009). Finally, current and future service assimilation was measured using a Guttman scale (Guttman 1950) with 7 increasing stages of assimilation: awareness, interest, evaluation/trial, commitment, limited deployment, partial deployment, and general deployment. This scale derives directly from Rai et al. (2009).

The measurement items for all constructs are provided in Appendix B.

At the first stage, we sent email invitations to an international marketing research company’s panel to participate in the electronic field survey. ## Results

## Non response bias

To investigate possible non-respondent bias, we ran a series of independent sample t-tests. The respondents were first divided into two groups based on the median date of completion of the survey, and their responses on the constructs were then compared (Armstrong and Overton, 1977). The tests did not reveal any statistically significant diferences between the early and late responses, supporting the notion that non-response bias was not a major concern in our study.

## Common method bias

One of the maior concerns with self-report data is common method bias (CMB). Taking the suggestion of Podsakoff et al. (2003) into account, we used concise and clear items, employing a mix of Likert, Guttman, and ratio scales to measure independent and dependent variables, and controlling for scale length. After collecting data, we evaluated the residual CMB risk using two techniques. First, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and applied a one-factor extraction test (Harman, 1967). The EFA with al items revealed that no single factor explained the majority of variance. supporting that common method bias is not a threat. Second. we evaluated the common method bias using the marker variable approach (Lindell and Whitney, 2001) in a post hoc fashion (Malhotra et al., 2006). Lindell and Whitney (2001) suggested using the second-smallest positive correlation as a conservative estimate of common method bias. From the correlation matrix (Appendix C). one can see that the second-smallest positive correlation is 0.02. In marker variable approach, the method factor is assumed to have constant correlation with all of the measured items (Malhotra et al., 2006). Thus, CMB-adjusted correlations between the variables under investigation are calculated by partialling out the estimate of CMB from the original correlations using the following equation (Malhotra et al.. 2006);

$$
r _ {A} = \frac {r _ {U} - r _ {M}}{1 - r _ {M}}
$$

where $r _ { A }$ is the CMB-adjusted correlation, $r _ { M }$ is the CMB estimate, and $r _ { U }$ is the original uncorrected correlation.

The estimate of CMB (i.e. 0.02) is so low that the diferences between the original and CMB-adjusted correlations are expected to be very low. In other words, none of the original correlations are expected to be significantly diferent from their CMB-adjusted counterparts. To formally compare original and CMB-adjusted correlations, we converted both correlations into a z-score using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. Then, these z-scores were compared and corresponding p value was calculated (Cohen and Cohen, 1983). As expected, none of the pairs of correlations were significantly diferent, implying that common method bias is unlikely to distort our interpretations.

## Modeling IT-mediated functionalities

Cenfetelli et al. (2008) modeled service functionality as an aggregate factor by aggregating all individual IT- mediated service dimensions in the B2C context. In contrast, our objective in this paper is to investigate how the importance of the five broader categories of B2B IT mediated services vary depending on the current level of assimilation. Therefore, consistent with the broader categories identified in the CSLC (Ives and Learmonth, 1984), we classified each individual dimension into one of the five broader categories. We modeled these five categories as second-order constructs. As a result, each category is the aggregation of a weighted sum of their respective individual IT-enabled services.

## Model testing

To ofer an accurate analysis of our theoretical model, we used a combined analysis of measurement and structural model as suggested by Gefen et al. (2000). We used the partial least squares (PLS) approach to assess measurement and structural properties of our theoretical model (Chin, 1998). PLS has been suggested as a powerful and convenient technique for testing complex research models (Goodhue et al., 2012). A rule of thumb for the required sample size in PLS is that the sample should be at least ten times the number of independent variables in the most complicated multiple regression in the model (Hair et al., 2011). The sample size in this study meets this minimum sample size requirement.

## Measurement model assessment

To assess the measurement model, we tested internal consistency, convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs. All the indicator items of our research model (Fig. 1) loaded highly on their intended constructs (> 0.70, Hair et al., 1998). An indicator intended to measure the IT sophistication construct, and another indictor intended to measure the frequency uncertainty construct did not load well on the construct. The loadings were lower than the suggested acceptable value of 0.70 (Hair et al., 1998) and consequently the items were dropped from further analysis. All item loadings are shown in Appendix B.

Internal consistency of model constructs was assessed through composite reliability (≥0.70) and average variance extracted (AVE) (≥0.50, Fornell and Larcker, 1981) values. The internal consistencies of all constructs were in excess of the recommended thresholds (Appendix B), thus supporting convergent validity. We evaluated discriminant validity by comparing the square root of AVE with the correlations between constructs (Appendix C). The square root of AVE for a construct should be greater than its correlations with any other construct (Fornell and Larcker. 1981). For the latent variables of the model, all bivariate correlations met this test. As another test for discriminant and convergent validity, we checked item loadings on their associated latent constructs and cross-loadings with other constructs in the model (Gefen and Straub, 2005). All items loaded on their associated constructs close to 0.70 level and those loadings were higher than any cross-loadings on any construct, thus supporting discriminant and convergent validity (Gefen and Straub, 2005).<sup>5</sup>

We observed a few correlations among the IT-mediated services that were above 0.7. As the IT-mediated services are complementary, high correlations are expected. However, high correlation might be an indicator of multicollinearity. Therefore, we investigated the VIF among the IT-mediated services and found values ranged that from 5.6 to 9.8. The cutof value of multicollinearity suggested in prior literature is 10 (Hair et al., 1998; Mathieson et al., 2001). Therefore, we conclude that multicollinearit is not a concern in this study.

## Structural model assessment

We followed a two-stage approach (Agarwal and Karahanna, 2000) for structural model assessment. First, the latent variable scores are initially estimated without the second-order construct present. The latent variable scores are stored and subsequently used as indicants in a separate higher-order structural model analysis. All the first order constructs were supported as significant contributors to their associated second-order aggregate constructs (Appendix D). The results of the higher-order structural model analysis, including standardized path coeficients and their statistical significance, and the amount of variances explained (R<sup>2</sup>) are listed in Table 5.

The interaction term of information search and current service assimilation had a negative efect on benefits, supporting H1. In contrast to our hypotheses, the interaction term of negotiation and current service assimilation had a non-significant efect on benefits. Thus, H2 was not supported. H3 was also not supported as the interaction term of acquisition and current service assimilation had a non-significant effect on benefits. As hypothesized in H4, the current service assimilation was found to positively moderate the relationship between ownership services and benefits. Finally, H5 was also supported as the interaction term of retirement services and current service assimilation had a positive impact on benefits. Taken together, the predictors of benefits explained 34% variances in benefits.

Furthermore, based on the significance of the path coeficients in the structural model, benefits and top management support had significant positive efects on future B2B assimilation. Thus, H6, and H8 were supported. Organizational readiness, demand uncertainty, and assimilation costs had non-significant efects on future B2B assimilation. Hence, H7, H9, and H10 were not supported. The joint efect of benefits, organizational readiness, top management support, assimilation costs, and demand uncertainty explained 40% variance of future B2B assimilation.

In order to investigate if the results vary among industry groups, we divided the sample into three groups (Information industry, Wholesale, retail & manufacturing industry, and Other industry) and tested three separate models. The results are shown in Appendix E. The direct efect of negotiation on benefits were non-significant (consistent with the overall sample) among both Information and the Wholesale, retail, & manufacturing samples. However, it was significant (p < 0.05) for “other” organizations. The interaction term of current assimilation and retirement was significant (consistent with the overall sample) in both Information and the Wholesale, retail, & manufacturing samples, whereas, it was non-significant for the “other” organization sample. Taken together, the results largely remained consistent across industry groups.

## Discussion of results

## Key findings and insights

The findings of our empirical investigation raise two interesting points. First, our analysis revealed that the current level of B2B service assimilation moderates the relationship between three IT-mediated services (i.e. information search, ownership, and retire ment) and benefits. These moderating efects suggest that the information search functions become less important while the ownership and retirement functions become more important as the companies move from the awareness stage to general deployment stage in assimilating a B2B platform. Consistent with our theory, we find that the relative importance of IT-mediated service functionalities is contingent upon the degree of a firm’s current assimilation of the platform.

Second, benefits and top management support are the key enablers of future B2B assimilation. In fact, these two factors retain its importance throughout the assimilation stages in predicting future B2B assimilation. In contrast, assimilation costs is found to have negative impact on future B2B assimilation when the firm’s current stage of assimilation is at low level. However, the efect wears out for the firms with higher level of current assimilation. Finally, we observe that organizational readiness, and demand uncertainty had non-significant influence on future B2B assimilation during all three aggregate stages (low, medium, and high) of current service assimilation.

## Contributions to theory development

The first aim of the study was to understand, identify, and evaluate the IT-enabled service functionality provided by a B2B platform. Building on the CSLC, this study identified a comprehensive set of IT-enabled services for B2B platforms. We were able to empirically demonstrate how this array of services influences a firm’s assessment of a B2B platform’s benefits. We believe that depicting B2B platform functionality in a granular structure (i.e. 13 IT-enabled services, and five aggregate constructs) provides more meaningful implications for researchers and B2B platform owners. Our study found additional functions (e.g. B2B search, preparing for negotiation, and negotiation) that are more typical and appropriate in the B2B context. Although Cenfetelli et al. (2008) found retirement services (account for and evaluate) had no importance in B2C context, our results highlighted the importance of these services in B2B context. The identified13 IT-enabled functions point out the key areas of B2B platform service design where IT can be leveraged to enhance customer service and ultimately increased level of technological assimilation

In relation to the second aim of this study, we empirically show that the relative importance of the IT-mediated services is contingent upon a firm’s current level of assimilation. Our study is the first of its kind to test the moderating efects of the current level of service assimilation on the relationships between the IT-mediated functionalities and benefits. We found that as firms move from the awareness stage to the general deployment stage of assimilation, information search functions become less important, whereas ownership and retirement functions become more important.

The third aim of the study was to gain a fine-grained understanding of the factors that influence a buying firm’s future assimilation of a B2B platform throughout the assimilation stages. In relation to this objective, our study contributes to the past research on B2B platform adoption and use (e.g. Son and Benbasat. 2007: Wang et al., 2010), as it is one of the very few studies to capture orga: nizational assimilation stages of B2B platforms. Most studies have focused on one stage of assimilation life cycle, such as decision to adopt or use. Thus, our study more holistically captures the dependent variable and identifies the important factors that afect across assimilation stages. In our Study 1, a subset of the most important enablers and inhibitors from the prior literature was found and subsequently used to construct the research model. By testing these enablers and inhibitors simultaneously in a single model, this study contributes to the literature by providing a comparative evaluation of diferent factors, previously found to be significant in past studies, in influencing future B2B platform assimilation among buyer organizations for purchasing purposes.

Our study provides empirical support that mainly benefits and top management support are the key enablers of future B2B platform assimilation. Our analysis further suggested that these two enablers retain their importance in predicting future B2B platform assimilation irrespective of what assimilation stage a firm is currently in. Thus, our findings extend and validate the findings of earlier studies. For example, several prior studies reported significant positive relationship between benefits and intention to adopt or use (Quaddus and Hofmeyer, 2007; Mishra and Agarwal, 2010; Wang et al., 2010). Consistent with the findings of Rai et al. (2009) in the context of electronic procurement innovation assimilation, our results suggest that top management support is an important enabler of B2B platform assimilation.

Assimilation costs had a negative significant influence on future B2B platform assimilation when the firm was at low level of service assimilation. However, the effect wears out as the firm moves to higher level of assimilation. One explanation for the nonsignificant influence of assimilation costs is that organizations with a medium or high level of current assimilation have most likely incorporated the process changes and organizational adjustments necessary to accommodate the B2B platform for online purchase. In addition, these organizations – at these more advanced assimilation stages – have most likely reduced transactional risk by building trust with the suppliers over time. Thus, assimilation costs may be less likely to hinder the purchasing via a B2B platform. Our results also show that demand uncertainty had a non-significant efect on future B2B platform assimilation irrespective of at which assimilation stage the firm is currently in. This finding contrasts the claim of earlier studies (e.g. Choudhury et al., 1998; Son and Benbasat, 2007) that reported uncertainty in volume and frequency hinder the adoption and use of a platform.

## Managerial implications

The findings of this study also have important implications for both B2B platform owners, and buyer organizations that plan to assimilate a B2B platform. First, our study found a list of important IT-mediated services for B2B platforms by validating the practica values of these functions among mature B2B platforms. The IT-enabled services identified in this paper provide a prescription to platform owners about how to best leverage IT to support their clients’ purchasing processes. The array of service functionalities can be viewed as a collection of actionable IT levers that can be exploited by B2B platform owners towards enhancing the service functionality of that market. Furthermore, the relative importance of IT-mediated functions in shaping benefits from platform serves as a guide to mangers towards identifying the most critical actionable IT design levers.

Second, our findings clearly show the necessity of group-specific IT-mediated service oferings. For example, the moderating efects of current service assimilation suggest that firms with a high level of service assimilation require diferent service oferings as compared to those firms that have a low level of service assimilation. By leveraging diferent services for diferent groups, platforms can serve existing clients better. Based on our results, we suggest platform owners put more emphasis on services related to ownership (e.g., training on the product, monitoring the activities on the product, maintenance and upgrading the product), and retirement (account for and evaluate) to serve the companies with high level of service assimilation better. On the other hand, in order to serve the companies with a low level of service assimilation, we suggest platforms put more efort on information search related service functionalities.

Third, our results demonstrate that benefits (both direct and indirect benefits) have efects on platform assimilation. Therefore, we suggest managers of buyer organizations to identify the potential benefits that they can get from a B2B platform based on their needs and the oferings of the platform. It is also vital for platform owners to support their users whether they actively engage in transactions, or they are simply members of the trading network. The latter support would increase the likelihood of firms to realize the benefits that such potential users are seeking. We advise platform owners to carefully plan how to communicate the direct benefits (e.g. reduction of costs, staf, paper work, clerical errors, etc.) as well as the indirect benefits of being part of the network of the platform such as improving company image and gaining competitive advantages. The platform owners are advised to play an active role in coordinating the communications regarding these benefits to the trading parties. In addition, our study clearly indicates that by ofering 13 IT-mediated service functionalities, platform owners can boost the overall benefits of their platform.

Fourth, our results demonstrate that the support of top management plays an important role in the B2B platform assimilation. Therefore, senior managers of the buying organizations are advised to show their support by providing essential financial resources that can leverage B2B platform assimilation. Senior managers should also align their e-commerce strategy with their overall business plan. This will enable their firms to perform efective cost-benefit assessments of their investments in a platform, as well as the justifications behind it. It is important for platform owners to ensure that their marketing eforts are targeted towards senior IT management executives and top management in general. This will in turn, assist senior IT managers and top management to legitimize the use of B2B platforms in place of traditional approaches by signifying how it maps to their firm’s overall procurement strategy.

Finally, our results show that the negative efect of assimilation costs in the form of managerial complexity and transactional risks on future B2B platform assimilation diminishes as the firms move from a low to a high level of current assimilation. Therefore, we suggest managers of buyer organizations to incorporate the required process changes that are necessary for purchasing via a B2B platform at their early stage of assimilation. This will in turn reduce the negative impact of assimilation costs on future B2B platform assimilation, and perhaps push the assimilation to a higher level. Once a firm’s assimilation level is higher, it will also reduce transactional risk.

## Limitations and future research

There are several limitations in this study that deserve considerations. Some of these limitations may serve as the avenue for future research. First, the sole focus of our study was organizational buyers. Indeed, the seller’s perspective is also important for designing B2B platforms. Participation by both buyers and sellers is the key to success of any B2B platform (Son and Benbasat, 2007). Thus, we recommend future research towards extending our current study by refining our research model with the seller perspective in mind. Second, our sample is tended toward small and medium sized organizations. Thus, we recommend future research toward validating the findings of our study for larger organizations. Third, we linked the IT-mediated services directly with benefits. According to Wixom and Todd (2005), object-based attitudes mediate the relationships between object-based beliefs and behaviora beliefs. Therefore, future research may incorporate the relevant object-based attitudes to investigate their mediating efects. Fourth, future research could identify and conceptualize diferent design objects (Faulkner & Runde, 2019) and empirically investigate their efects on service functionality dimensions in order to evaluate to what extent these design objects enable them. Finally, the B2B platforms often rely on ofline activities (e.g., small-scale exhibitions and visits by sales representatives) in addition to the available IT-mediated service functionalities in order to attract new customers and retain existing ones. Therefore, we recommend future research toward investigating to what extent such ofline activities encourage future B2B platform assimilation for online purchasing.
