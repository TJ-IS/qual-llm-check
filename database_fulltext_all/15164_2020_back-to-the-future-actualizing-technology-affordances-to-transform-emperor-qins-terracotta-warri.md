# Back to the future: Actualizing technology afordances to transform Emperor Qin’s terracotta warriors Museum

Yenni Tim<sup>a</sup>, Taohua Ouyang<sup>b</sup>, Delin Zeng<sup>c,</sup>\*

<sup>a</sup> School of Information Systems and Technology Management, UNSW Business School, UNSW Sydney, Room 2085, West Wing, Quadrangle, 2052, Australia <sup>b</sup> School of Economics and Management, Beihang University, Room 952, New Main Building, 37 College Road, Haidian District, Beijing, China <sup>c</sup> School of Economics and Management, Beijing Jiaotong University, Science and Technology Venture Building, Room 979, No.3 ShangYuanCun, Haidian District, Beijing China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Technology affordances Technology constraints Digital transformation Emerging technologies Case study

## A B S T R A C T

Digital transformation brings new opportunities and challenges to organizations as they make radical changes to their operation, business and value creation processes. This paper presents an in-depth case study of the Empero Qin Shi Huang’s Mausoleum Site Museum in China to discuss the promises and challenges that arise during a digital transformation. Our findings uncover several afordances of emerging technologies that were actualized for the museum’s transformation, as well as several unintended consequences that arose during the process. Our conceptualization of the enabling and constraining mechanisms serve to inspire further investigations into the transformational changes in organizations and their paradoxical implications.

## 1. Introduction

Advancement in social, mobile, analytics, cloud technologies, and the Internet of Things is driving profound disruptions that shift consumer behaviors, expectations, and competitive landscape. Companies across all industries are responding to the disruptions with varying digital transformation strategies. Digital transformation encompasses the leveraging of information, computing, communication, and connectivity technologies to create major changes in business operations, business and value creation processes [1]. Unlike the more widely studied IT-enabled organizational changes, digital transformation is a process that has profound implications in terms of scale, scope, and speed [1]. With such a significance, digital transformation is inevitably a challenging process for most organizations, especially for pre-digital firms that have long-established models and processes, as well as organizational structure and capital that cannot be easily reconfigured [2].

Museums are a case in point. Museums have long served as the gatekeeper of our valuable cultural objects and knowledge. Since their formation in the 18th century, museums have largely been insulated from technological disruptions [3]. It was not until the most recent decade that one of the most significant technology-related transformations of museums occurred [4,5]. Advanced technologies have not only made tasks such as collecting, tracking, and preserving artifacts more efective and eficient [6], but have also shifted the ways in which museums perform cultural dissemination and engage with audiences [7]. Museums today are able to establish a digital presence that transcends geographical boundaries and physical constraints. For example, digital surrogates of extremely high precision can be created, and made widely available, for even the most exclusive of cultural artifacts. With the support of mobile devices and social platforms, museum audiences are also given a more powerful role in shaping the cultural experience museums have to ofer. Such a transformation not only presents museums with unprecedented possibilities, but also challenges that need to be overcome.

While digital transformation is gaining significance in the museum space, we know very little about how museums can efectively manage such a disruptive process. At present, museums remain an underrepresented sector in our research space [7]. This study represents an early attempt to investigate the transformation process shifting the museum space in the past decade. Specifically, this paper aims to address the research question: How does a museum actualize emerging technologies for digital transformation? To answer this research question, we conducted an in-depth case study of a world-renowned museum – Emperor Qin Shi Huang’s Mausoleum Site Museum in the city of Xi’an, China. In order to conceptualize the process of digital transformation, we adopt the perspective of technology afordances as our theoretical lens. Digital transformation is a complicated process that involves not just an addition of technologies, but a reconfiguration of legacy systems, processes, and even culture [1,2]. The technology afordance perspective sensitizes researchers to the important potentials of a technology and to understand how organizational actors can actualize these potentials to achieve desirable organizational outcomes [8,9]. It is therefore a particularly appropriate theoretical lens to adopt in order to unpack the process of digital transformation.

The findings developed from this research make two contributions. First, based on our theoretical lens of technology afordances, we develop in-depth insights on how a museum can seize the opportunities ofered by emerging technologies, and reconfigure the necessary components of the organization to embrace a digital transformation. The conceptualization of afordance-actualization developed in this paper could inform future research that aims to investigate the what and how of digital transformation, especially for pre-digital firms with well-established processes and structure. Second, this research highlights the competing demands and tensions that arise during a digital transformation. Sensitized by the technology afordances perspective, our research uncovers several constraints that arise when emerging technologies are actualized for transformational changes in organizations. Existing research has pointed out how emerging technologies often present “both game-changing opportunities and existential threats to big old companies” ([2], p. 197). Conceptualizing how specific constraints can emerge during a transformation process answers the calls for research on the paradoxes and competing demands of digital transformation in organizations [10], and inspires future research to investigate not just the positive but also the undesirable outcomes of digital transformation.

## 1.1. Literature Review: Digital transformation of museums

In this research, we adopt a consolidated definition of digital transformation developed by Vial [1] – digital transformation is “a process that aims to improve an entity by triggering significant changes to its properties through combinations of information, computing, communication and connectivity technologies” ([1], p. 118). Consistent with this definition, we see digital transformation as an evolution of the “IT-enabled transformations” that were examined more frequently in existing literature. A digital transformation is often triggered by disruptive changes in the environment, and occurs at a more significant scale, scope, and speed compared to the IT-enabled, incremental transformations often initiated by organizational decisions (for ex ample, an ERP implementation) [1].

The rise of transformational technologies in the most recent decade has served as both a trigger and an enabler for digital transformation. An increasing amount of research has been conducted to understand how technologies alter traditional ways of doing business. Existing research on digital transformation focuses on two main themes: (1) the nature of digital technologies and their roles in triggering transformational changes in organizations [11], and (2) the impact of such transformation at an organizational level [12,13]. Digital transformation empowers organizations to create new value by enabling, for example, disintermediation [14], hybridization [15] and the reduction of slack resources [16]. Recent research has also discussed how transfor mative technologies such as big data analytics are adopted by organizations to realize a renewal of existing processes [17,18], structures and even culture [19].

Nevertheless, while digital transformation ofers tremendous possibilities for organizations, executing such a significant change is not an easy process. A handful of existing research has pointed out how digital transformation demands significant commitment from organizations, including the development of new capabilities [20], organizational restructuring [21], changing organizational culture [12], and over coming employee inertia and resistance [22,23].

Understanding “what it takes” for an organization to digitally transform is therefore the objective of this research. In this paper, we focus on the transformation of museums – our cultural organizations that are currently underrepresented in the Information Systems (IS)

research space. Museums are a place of culture, education, and experience [24]. The core responsibility of a museum has always been to collect, preserve, and study its core assets, i.e., the collection of artifacts and extensive cultural knowledge associated with the artifacts [25]. Constructed as a formal institution that entails expertise, capital, and exclusivity [26], it is not surprising that museums have been relatively conservative in adopting technologies [3].

Nevertheless, technological advancement in the past decade has brought forth digital transformation in the museum industry [5]. We define the digital transformation of museums as the process that triggers significant changes to a museum’s organizational system (i.e., how a museum is run) and long-held identity (i.e., how a museum is keeping with its goal) through combinations of information, computing, communication, and connectivity technologies. Unlike the more common, incremental digitalization (for example, the use of mainframe systems to store descriptive information of museum artifacts since the early 1960s [27]), we are interested in the transformational changes undergoing in the museum industry in the most recent decade. Appendix A summarizes some of these changes with illustrative examples.

A key part of museums’ digital transformation involves the use of technologies to fundamentally renew the approach with which museums manage their precious artifacts. For example, wireless radio frequency (RF) technologies, which forms the basis of the Internet of Things (IoT), are being used to transform the time-consuming processes involved in researching and updating inventory records in museums [28]. Advanced 3D scanning and photography technologies are also being used to empower the preservation of historically important sites and objects through the creation of blueprints and high-precision replicas [6]. Ubiquitous technologies have also allowed museums to create “living exhibitions” that are highly interactive and collaborative, creating unprecedented cultural experience that re-ignite interest in our cultural institutions [29]. Renowned museums such as the Louvre and the British Museum have taken the lead in revitalizing the traditional approach of presenting cultural artifacts by creating exclusive online galleries and virtual tours to better engage visitors [30].

Nevertheless, scattered insights from a handful of studies also suggest how complexities and uncertainties arise when museums are pushed to embrace the digital landscape. Transforming for the digital era requires museums to leapfrog from being a conservative gatekeeper to a technology-oriented institution. Museum professionals face the challenge of needing to cultivate new capabilities in the process, as information resources are becoming technically complex and customers demands are becoming increasingly sophisticated [31]. A lack of resources, funding support and actionable guidelines have also been found to further exaggerate the challenge [5]. Reports from practitioners have also consistently pointed out how digitally enabled changes in the environment have made it necessary for museums to embrace a virtual presence, which is often an identity-challenging endeavor for the cultural gatekeeper [32].

Taken together, insights drawn from allied studies and practitioners reports have indicated how digital transformation remains as a potentially attractive, yet identity-challenging option for the museum industry. In the present research, we investigate this topic through a theoretical lens of technology afordances.

1.2. Theoretical background: Technology afordances, actualization, and constraints

The concept of afordances was first developed by Gibson [33] to explain the possible actions enabled by an object. This concept was then adopted by IS scholars to capture the action potentials of technological objects [34,35]. Technology afordances is a relational concept; it takes into account how actors interact with technology and how these interactions are shaped by the actors’ context [36,37], including “expertise, organizational processes and procedures, controls, boundaryspanning approaches, and other social capacities present in the

A handful of existing studies have also made explicit that “afordances are not only enabling, but also constraining” ([9], p. 55). Majchrzak and Markus [34] proposed a framework of technology affordances and constraints theory (hereafter TACT) to guide the theorization of both the enabling and constraining aspects of afordances. Technology constraints, according to TACT, refer to “ways in which an individual or organization can be held back from accomplishing a particular goal when using a technology” ([34], p. 1). In other words, a technological object favors, and at the same time constrains, a specific set of actions [8,38]. Defined in this way, a technology afordances perspective provides a useful ground for scholars to examine what a technological artifact ofers, both for good and for ill [19,34,39].

Recently, several scholars have called for a theorization on affordance-actualization (e.g., Burton-Jones and Volkof [36],Strong et al. [9],Tim et al. [19]). Afordance-actualization captures the process where actors realize the specific potential of technological artifacts to facilitate a goal-oriented action [9]. Given that afordances are “po tentialities that only exists when leveraged within a specific domain and set of actions” ([35], p. 39), conceptualizing afordance-actualiza tion becomes essential in capturing the real “moments” where afordances are realized, as well as the concrete outcomes of the actualization [9,36]. Understanding actualized afordances that were enacted through the symbiosis of technological and organizational features, along with the actors’ goal-directed actions, enables a rich theorization of the mechanisms of organizational change [9].

Taken together, describing digital transformation from a technology afordances perspective provides several opportunities for theory development. First, the technology afordances perspective provides a language to explain the mutuality of organizational features and technological capabilities in enabling a particular change or outcome [9,38]. Such a perspective therefore allows us to unpack the process of how technologies are being used by organizational actors to enable specific transformative outcomes. Second, the technology afordances perspective also allows an investigation of the unintended consequences of technologies [19,40]. Understanding both intended and unintended uses of technologies is essential for the theorization of technology-associated phenomena [19,40]. Being able to conceptualize such paradoxical implications contributes important insights to enrich the digital transformation literature [1].

## 2. Research Methodology

## 2.1. Case description

We started this research with the aim of developing an understanding of the phenomenon of digital transformation in the museum sphere. Recognizing the nascent stage of knowledge on this evolving phenomenon, an interpretive approach was adopted [41,42]. An interpretive approach allows us to gain deep insights and develop a rich theoretical understanding from empirical data [43]. Consistent with existing guidelines on conducting interpretive case study [41,43], we conducted an in-depth case study to find answers to our “how” research question [43,44]. The case study of the Emperor Qin Shi Huang’s Mausoleum Site Museum (hereafter the Qin Museum) presents a unique opportunity to study a museum in transformation, growing and evol ving both operationally and culturally.

We secured access to perform a case study at the Qin Museum in the city of Xi’an, China, to understand how digital transformation manifests in a traditional cultural institution. The Qin Museum is one of the largest mausoleum museums in the world, covering the mausoleum of the first emperor of China in a total area of two million square meters. The construction of the museum began in 1975, after an astonishing chance discovery made in 1974. In the early spring of 1974, farmers digging a well outside a village in east of Xi’an stumbled upon fragments of sculpted heads, rusty crossbow, and arrowheads made with terracotta, buried under the soil. Intrigued, archeologists performed further excavation, only to reveal one of the greatest archeological finds in the history – a 2200 year old underground army of life-size terracotta warriors – constructed under the order of the first Emperor of China (Emperor Qin Shi Huang) as the guardian of his mausoleum. The ambitious construction of the mausoleum started as early as 246 BCE, when Qin was only 13 years old. This underground empire presents a largely untold story of one of the most important times in the Chinese history: The Qin Dynasty (221-206BCE) when Emperor Qin united seven warring states, built a legacy of a bureaucratic nation named China, and took the name of Qin Shi Huang (which translates to “First Emperor”).

The Qin Museum was first opened to public on 1 October 1979. The museum’s architecture mirrors the urban plan of the city in the Qin dynasty, with several exhibition halls and auxiliary buildings built over the mausoleum and excavation pits. At present, the museum has three excavation pits serving as the main exhibition sections (see Fig. 1). In total, the museum is estimated to contain as many as 8000 uniquely crafted, life-sized terracotta warriors and a total of 50,911 artifacts. The Qin Museum attracts more than five million visitors each year, and has 22 departments devoted to preserve, research, and exhibit the mausoleum site and its associated archeological finds. However, even after 40 years of concentrated eforts from museum professionals, much of the embedded historical legacies of the palatial tomb remain unexplored as thousands of terracotta warriors are yet to be unearthed.

In 2014, the Qin Museum initiated a digital transformation plan in response to the changing demands of customers in digital age and the need for operational improvement. The transformation initiative is a large-scale, disruptive initiative which focuses on leveraging emerging technologies, including Augmented Reality (AR), Internet of Things (IoT), mobile technologies, and social media, to satisfy the sophisticated operational requirements and new expectations arising in today’s digital environment. Such an initiative involves not only a renewal of the museum’s preservation, restoration, and exhibition practices, but also a revolution of customer engagement and cultural communication strategies. To investigate how the Qin Museum leverages emerging tech nologies for its transformation, we approached key stakeholders and initiated our data collection.

## 2.2. Data collection

In this study, we relied on interview data and published evidence as our primary data sources. Prior to conducting the interviews, we had developed an initial understanding of the case institution and its digital transformation initiative based on publicly available materials, in cluding the museum’s website, social media account, and public articles. Our first few interviews were conducted onsite with managementlevel personnel at the Qin Museum, such as the curator of the museum and directors from various departments. These interviews (which in total span several hours) were used to develop an initial understanding of the museum’s digital transformation initiative and to identify key issues to explore further. Being able to interview the management team also allowed us to obtain an understanding on the vision of the museum’s transformation efort and their strategies moving forward. Based on this understanding, we then identified, following the snowballing technique suggested by Myers and Newman [45], specific employee who were deeply involved with the museum’s digital transformation and conducted further interviews. In order to account for possible differences in interpretations, we selected interviewees across diferent functional areas [41]. Combining various perspectives also allowed for a comprehensive reconstruction of the transformational journey.

All interviews were semi-structured and composed of open-ended questions. The interviewees were first asked to provide an overview of the digital transformation initiative and then elaborate in detail on the relevant facts and key events. Guided by our research aim and theoretical lens, we focused on asking questions related to interviewees roles and involvement in the transformation process, and how they made use of technologies in their role. Notwithstanding, we also had non-directive conversations and always remained flexible to make necessary adjustments (e.g., adding new interview questions to clarify on emerging themes) [45]. Whenever possible, we also cross-checked information gathered from interviewees against publicly available materials as a means of controlling for retrospective bias. The interviews ranged from 45−90 minutes in length and were digitally recorded, transcribed, and translated for analysis. In total, our in-depth interviews provided us with 370 minutes of interview data. Appendix B provides a complete list of our interviewees.

Next, we collected a range of published evidence surrounding the case to capture contextual complexity and to facilitate more in-depth understanding of the phenomenon. First, we reviewed the large amount of information available on the Qin Museum’s oficial website to reconstruct details of its digital transformation journey. As a Chinese public institution, the Qin Museum has a responsibility to produce timely reports on important events and to publicize them on its oficial website. We took advantage of these frequent and highly detailed up dates to “observe” how the transformation unfolded and to develop insights into the timeline of events. Second, we studied and collected data from the museum’s oficial WeChat account. The museum’s WeChat account serves as a new channel for public engagement. The WeChat page publishes frequent updates and short articles that introduce Qin Museum’s latest digital ventures.

Furthermore, we explored all of the mobile and web applications developed by the Qin Museum as part of its digital transformation initiative. We saved relevant screenshots of these applications and created notes about the features of these applications and how they were used. These extensive notes, photos taken, and observations during our field visit (including a guided museum tour) served as valuable data for triangulation purposes. In addition to this primary data, a range of archival data from published reports, articles, and books were also collected for triangulation to ensure the reliability of our findings [41]. Overall, our rich primary data and secondary archival data sources enabled a triangulated understanding of digital transformation at the Qin Museum. We summarized our data sources and their uses in data analysis in Appendix C, and provide a summary of relevant articles collected from the museum’s oficial channels in Appendix D.

## 2.3. Data analysis

We approached our analysis from an interpretive perspective, focusing on understanding the Qin Museum’s eforts in actualizing emerging technologies for digital transformation. An interpretive approach allows researchers to take into consideration the contextual factors and therefore to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon [46]. Throughout our data analysis process, we adopted the perspective of technology afordances as the “sensitizing device” ([41], p. 75) to provide us with the theoretical sensitivity to make sense of our rich data. Data analysis is interspersed with data collection in interpretive research [44] and thus, we started our data analysis as we collected our data. To begin, we developed narratives to delineate the digital transformation journey at the Qin Museum. We made use of data from a variety of sources to reconstruct narratives of the phenomenon, which expressed key themes and stories of the transformation in the language of our interviewees. Converging on multiple sources of data and perspectives allow for data triangulation, which add breadth and depth to our analysis [47]. As is typical for interpretive research, we worked back and forth between our data, literature, and the emerging insights to construct an understanding of the phenomenon. The theoretical lens of technology afordances provided us with the theoretical sensitivity to organize our data into themes, including both the enabling and constraining efects of emer ging technologies in digital transformation.

Through further studies of the data and literature, our understanding towards the phenomenon was sharpened and we were able to organize the emerging themes to form a more meaningful comprehension of the phenomenon. Specifically, through an interpretive text analysis, we made sense of our data and developed narratives on how our interviewees ascribe meaning to the phenomenon [48,49]. Further text analysis allowed us to identify themes or invariants that emerged from the text, and more importantly, to develop an interpretation for the phenomenon. At this stage of our analysis, we identified six actualized afordances and the associated actualization processes, as guided by our theoretical lens. More intriguingly, we also identified several unintended consequences of technology usage during the ana lysis process. Through further dialogue with existing literature and the emerging interpretations, we were able to conceptualize these unintended consequences as constraints that emerged when actors actualize technologies to achieve certain transformation goals.

Next, we continued our text analysis to abstract the “essence” from the text [49]. In order to develop a more elaborated conceptualization of the phenomenon, we performed more readings of data and theory. We continuously assessed the emergent findings with probing questions informed by our theoretical lens (e.g., how were these afordances actualized? what are some of the enablers of afordance-actualization? what are the outcomes of actualizations and why do they occur?) to identify the underlying connection between the emerging themes. During this process, we relied on our rich data collected from multiple sources to verify the significance of identified concepts and to refine our interpretations. Nevertheless, we also retained “a considerable degree of openness to the field data, and a willingness to modify initial as sumptions and theories” ([43], p. 76) to ensure that we did not constrain ourselves from identifying new concepts and insights from our data. This final step of data analysis allowed us to develop a rich con ceptual interpretation of the phenomenon. Overall, our interpretive text analysis allowed us to understand the museum professionals’ perceived intentions, the decision-making processes, and consequences of the processes, which together developed into a comprehensive conceptualization of the phenomenon. We present this conceptualization and a theoretical framework in the following sections of the paper.

## 3. Case Analysis

Over the past few decades, museum professionals at the Qin Museum have faced significant operational challenges in managing the enormous mausoleum site. A huge number of unearthed terracotta figures are broken and require a substantial combination of efort and time to reassemble. Considerable resources are also needed for the preservation and management of the increasingly large collections of recovered artifacts. However, like most institutions established in the pre-digital era, the Qin Museum has been operating on a largely manual and time- and resource-intensive operational model. As elaborated by our interviewees, even the most basic operations, such as collection management, have been incredibly challenging:

“All our collection management is done manually. To catalogue our collections, we need to sort through every single artifact… which becomes overwhelming given the huge collection we have. We might need a whole year to perform a full inventory check. Actually, now that I think about it… even a year is not enough…” – Deputy Director of the Conservation Department

Beyond safeguarding its artifacts, the Qin Museum also faces a growing challenge in maintaining its relevance amidst an increasingly digital environment. As a gatekeeper for over two millennia worth of artifacts, the Qin Museum is continuously pressured to find new ways to communicate the historical importance of its artifacts to a modern audience. The Director of the IS Services Department provides a vivid summary of this challenge:

“Museums are also undergoing changes. Our role is no longer simply just to display and exhibit of our artifacts. We also need to ensure that we efectively communicate and pass on the embedded stories and historical value.”

With the notable technological advancements that have happened in the most recent decade, museum professionals soon recognized the potentials in embracing technologies to resolve the long-standing operational challenges and to revitalize the museum’s age-old identity. They accepted that the move towards a digitally enabled museum is not just an opportunity, but a necessity:

“Right after our department was established, our main responsibility was to do all we could to catch up with technology development… but do we just follow what others have done? How can we be better? This is why we have started to adopt IoT technologies… to catch up with changes in the environment, we began to embrace social media, such as WeChat and Weibo. Although we are still unsure about the intricate details… but we know we must be front-footed.” – Director of the IS Services Department

Consequently, building on the strong drive to harness the power of technologies and a need to fulfill sophisticated digital expectations, the Qin Museum established concrete ideas for its first digital transformation initiative in 2014. As explained by the museum curator, this transformation efort is unlike any previous digitalization task. The digital transformation process involves a series of significant changes, from renewing the museum’s age-old practices to developing a new identity:

“We used to take photos and videos of our artifacts and store them… but the actual transformation has only started in recent years. This is a fundamental transformation… Today, we have an entirely different vision and expectation on digital transformation."

In the following, we present our theorization on how organizational actors at the Qin Museum actualized several afordances of emerging technologies to digitally transform. Through the theoretical lens of technology afordances, our theorization also unpacks the unintended consequences that arise during the process and how these undesirable outcomes are constraining further progress in the transformation.

## 3.1. Actualizing techno-capability for organizational renewal

The Qin Museum actualized several afordances of technologies, which we collectively categorized as techno-capability, to transform its long-established organizational processes. One of the most impactful examples was the actualization of IoT technologies to revolutionize the museum’s collection management. IoT technologies refer to an ecosystem of technologies (e.g., smart objects and RF technologies) that are used to monitor physical objects and capture meaningful data of and around the objects. The Deputy Director of the Collection Management Department describes how they actualized the afordances of IoT technologies to improve existing processes:

“Ever since our RFID-based system was put in place, we know exactly where an artifact has been stored or transferred to… something we couldn’t do in the past. Keeping track of such a huge collection used to be a daunting task; taking up a lot of time. If we were to still follow our traditional approach, we wouldn’t be able to reach this level of detail, especially at this magnitude.”

The next afordance-actualization that is evident from our analysis was the use of technologies to renew the museum’s preservation and restoration processes. The terracotta warriors and mausoleum site at the Qin Museum serve as important archeological evidence that reveal stories about the founding father of China, whose enormous achievements range from establishing a standardized writing system to the unification of China. Despite the legacy of Emperor Qin, there are limited historical resources that provide insights into this intriguing part of Chinese history. To date, a large part of the Qin’s mausoleum has remained unexplored and therefore require careful conservation. As emphasized by the Deputy Director of the Conservation Department, preserving the artifacts and mausoleum site will also remain a foremost responsibility for the Qin Museum:

“When it comes to digitalization, for us, the most important task is still the preservation of our artifacts. If our artifacts are gone, what are we to do? Nothing will matter then.”

Emerging technologies allowed for a revitalization of the museum’s meticulous restoration tasks. For example, 3D imaging technologies have aforded the possibility for museum experts to create highly precise digital surrogates for fragile and fragmented artifacts. The creation of digital surrogates allows museum experts to examine cultural artifacts in incredible detail during the research and restoration process. Working with digital copies also prevents possible damage to the original artifacts. This is particularly important for the Qin Museum as the terracotta relics are extremely fragile and could crumble through exposure to air and light. The use of 3D imaging technology has thus transformed the handling and restoration of the fragile terracotta fig ures, which used to be time-consuming and heavily reliant on the judgment and expertise of a small group of experts:

“When it came to the restoration of our terracotta warriors, in the past, we used to rely solely on our experts’ judgment. Now with the help of technologies, the precision and eficiency of our restoration process has been improved” – Museum Curator

In order to shed light on the process of transformation, we focus on analyzing how the actualization of technology afordances is possible. Sensitized by our theoretical lens, our analysis revealed insights on how specific organizational arrangements are required for the actors to actualize an afordance. For example, while emerging technologies provide several action potentials, without the experiences and tacit knowledge of museum professionals and a common goal of revitalizing the museum’s operations, the technology afordances would not have been perceived and actualized. The Director of the Collection Management Department provides an eloquent account on how the process of affordance-actualization unfolds:

“Actually when it comes to technology, it’s all about your awareness. It doesn’t matter what advanced technology you have, it’s all about what you want to do with it. Today, we have so many technological options. It’s about whether you have a need and an awareness.”

Despite the powerful features ofered by the technology, during our interviews, museum professionals have consistently highlighted the importance of having experts’ judgments and interventions in transforming existing practices digitally. The director of IS service department provides an excellent explanation that illustrates the nuances of technology use during the transformation journey:

“When it comes to the restoration of our artifacts, we still emphasize on the importance of experts’ judgment. Technologies work as a support, but the integration of our expertise and the technologies is the key. For example, some of our terracotta figures have been destroyed over time, they are missing some really crucial parts, which makes the identification of its origins and information, such as the year, shape etc., very challenging. These judgments can only be made by the experts based on years of experience. Then, the 3D technologies come into play. It allows us to simulate the restoration process, to create a highly precise digital copy which we will use in the restoration”

## 3.2. Actualizing techno-influence for identity revitalization

Museums today function as more than a repository of objects – they are institutions that create social and cultural experiences for their visitors. As technology advances and consumers’ demands become increasingly sophisticated, museums have been pushed to find new ways to engage with the public. At the Qin Museum, the pressing need for societal relevancy is one of the key triggers for digital transformation:

“There is a new demand from society put forward for museums. Museums are no longer a place solely for knowledge and history, they are also a place to provide entertainment and to engage and interact with visitors… we need to find an engaging and fun way to allow everyone to understand history, and eventually, achieve our goal of preserving the history, preserving the Chinese culture.”– Curator Hou in a public interview [50]

Emerging technologies ofer new opportunities to liven up museums. Our analysis reveals how the Oin Museum actualized several afordances of AR technologies, social media, and mobile technologies to revitalize its identity. First, the afordances of AR technologies were actualized to create groundbreaking digital museum experiences. As the guardian for a huge mausoleum site with innumerable artifacts, the Qin Museum has been facing challenges in creating interactive experiences for its visitors. For example, to prevent weathering of the artifacts, around 5000 terracotta warriors were left entombed within several pits, and many unearthed relics were stored away, pending restoration be fore they are available for public view. To date, only around 2000 restored terracotta warriors are on display for public view. The museum professionals recognized the potential of AR technologies in overcoming this challenge.

Our analysis suggests that AR technologies ofer several action po tentials in enabling an interactive and immersive museum experience. For example, AR technologies allow the creation of an “augmented reality”, where virtual objects can be overlaid on top of the real environment, creating new possibilities to convey information. In our case study, we see how the exhibition experts in the Qin Museum make use of this technology to make history come alive. An AR-enabled appli cation has been developed to allow visitors to have an extremely close and detailed view of the museum site and its objects. This application combines no less than a thousand high-definition photos of the museum site from several angles into a 20-billion pixel panorama photo that features a 360-degree view of the museum. It showcases both excavation pit 1 and 3 and is accessible on both desktop and mobile devices, allowing visitors to appreciate the grandeur of terracotta warriors in incredible detail (see Fig. 2).

The museum professionals also leveraged social media and mobile technologies to help better convey the often-embedded historical value of cultural artifacts. One of the most successful initiatives as part of this transformation plan was the launch of an integrated mobile service platform on WeChat – China’s largest social media site. The mobile service platform, oficially named “The Emperor Qin Shi Huang’s Mausoleum Site Museum’s Intelligent Internet Plus Service Platform”, was developed to promote visitors’ engagement:

“We launched these initiatives with the hope of attracting younger visitors. The younger generation now focuses more on the experience. Digital experience is the best experience for them” – Curator Hou in a public interview [50].

Our analysis also unveiled important insights that allowed us to further unpack the afordance-actualization process. In our case study, we saw that for the organizational actors to efectively actualize technologies for transformative outcomes, having access to powerful tech nologies was just the first step. Specific organizational features were required for the actors to be able to perceive the afordances and effectively actualize them. For example, we observed that museum professionals at the Qin Museum had a deep understanding of the historical significance of the museum’s collection and a common goal in aspiring to renew the identity of the museum. It was with their experiences in working with the museum’s collections and their strong drive to promote the cultural significance of the museum that allowed them to commit to exploring and experimenting with technologies. The Deputy Director of the Conversation Department provided a great summary for this insight on afordance-actualization:

“Adopting advanced technologies definitely brings tremendous opportunities to the museum. Exactly how to efectively apply those technologies is still an ongoing exploration for us. Given the same technology, using it in diferent contexts will bring forth diferent [ways of] applications. Having access to a particular technology does not mean that we are able to efectively apply the technology in a specific context.”

Along the same line, the Director of the Exhibition Department also pointed out the importance of deep contextual knowledge in afordance-actualization. When asked about how the museum was working on overcoming the challenges of digital transformation, the director pointed out that it will be an ongoing process of experimentation, and the process will have to be led by museum professionals who have a deep understanding of both the existing practices, and the vision and mission of the museum:

“We have tried to collaborate with some IT companies, but they often only explain and demonstrate available technologies to us. They do not really know how to integrate those technologies into our museum.”

## 3.3. Unintended consequences of techno-stress

While analyzing the afordance-actualization process, our analysis also revealed several undesirable outcomes of digital transformation. We observed that techno-stress arose as the Qin Museum underwent significant changes during the transformation process. Transforming age-old museum practices incurs great complexity and a multiplication of goals. From our case study, we saw that museum professionals were overwhelmed and intimidated by the complexities that arose during the process. For example, the Director of the Exhibition Department noted on such tremendous pressure:

“We are not familiar with many of the required technical skills. We need to learn. At the same time, we are still restricted by our professional role. I think, slowly, our department needs to become a ‘know-it-all’. We need to know everything.”

Digital transformation is particularly challenging for pre-digital organizations as the organizational actors often do not have suficient technological competency to manage technology uses. In the Qin Museum, this lack of technological competency constrains the museum professionals from further actualizing the potentials ofered by tech nologies. There is a significant gap in capabilities which many museum professionals highlighted during our interview process. For example, the Deputy Director of the Conservation Department expressed how the mismatch of capabilities serves a constraint for the museum’s transformation:

“Our background is in archeology. Some of us may even have a background in the arts. However, that just means that we as a collective totally lack knowledge and expertise in IT-related areas. However, we have no other option but to rely on ourselves [to learn].”

While the museum professionals are eager to embrace the digitally fueled future, overcoming such challenges has proven to be dificult. For example, the Qin Museum even finds it dificult to recruit digital talent who can fit into the organization:

“First, our people will need to have a rich background knowledge in history and literature. They need to have a deep understanding of and passion towards the history and cultural background of our museum. Only with this foundation can they then make the best use of technologies to convey the historical value [of our artifacts]. But, right now, we don’t have any of these [people]. There is a huge gap.” – Director of the IS Services Department

Beyond the skill gap, some of the museum professionals also high lighted their hesitation on digital transformation, as they are unsure of how to cater for the ever-changing expectations without running the risk of jeopardizing the academic and gatekeeping mission of a mu seum. The Deputy Director of the Conservation Department summarized this dilemma succinctly:

“If we do not adopt technologies, we will be deemed as boring and outdated. Or people will simply see us as ‘a place for the old’… but, well, not surprisingly we have very few technology experts here. Most of us are old employees, who still see ourselves as the ‘guardian of the key’.”

Taken together, our analysis paints a realistic picture of digital transformation based on the perspective of technology afordances. The first part of our findings provide a rich description on how several affordances of emerging technologies can be actualized when specific organizational arrangements are in place. When we dive deep into understanding the digital transformation process, our analysis also unveils how museum professionals find themselves under mounting pressure to make sure that they are not missing out on the promises ofered by emerging technologies. While the deep cultural knowledge and experience of museum professionals has helped to better enact the value of emerging technologies, the demand for technical expertise and openness to change has incurred a great deal of tension and stress. Such undesirable outcomes have constrained their ability to further enact the potentials of technologies and hence constrained the progress of digital transformation. ## 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Actualizing technology afordances for digital transformation

First, this research unveils a set of afordances of various emerging technologies in the context of a museum’s digital transformation. Each afordance, as presented in Table 1, is named as a gerund associated with the actions that were taken to actualize that afordance (following Majchrzak and Markus [34] and Strong et al. [9]). We categorize the identified afordances into two categories: techno-capability and techno-influence. Techno-capability includes technology afordances that provide possibilities for improved operational outcomes. In our case study, for example, this includes the action potentials for museum professionals to perform collection management digitally and to monitor the status of physical objects in real time. Techno-influence, then, includes technology afordances that provide possibilities for new value propositions. One key example in our case study is the potential ofered by the ubiquitous mobile and social media technologies – technologies which are “fundamentally changing the way we communicate, collaborate, consume and create” ([51], p. 3). These technologies ofer unprecedented ways for museums to engage with their audiences and to convey cultural significance in ways never before possible. In the context of museums, these two categories of afordances ofer not incremental changes, but paradigm shifts that involve a redefinition of existing practices and organizational identity. For example, the possibility for museums to work with digital copies of extremely valuable yet fragile cultural objects opens up immense opportunities for research and dissemination. Having access to interactive technologies also enables museums to create visibility in the digital space and facilitate a renewal of interests.

To answer our “how” research question, the next part of our find ings goes beyond identifying the afordances of technologies to conceptualize the actualization of each afordance (as summarized in Table 2). In our conceptualization, we make explicit both the technological and organizational features that are central to the afordanceactualization. Specifically, our analysis highlights that the mere existence of technologies (i.e., the action potentials) will not directly lead to successful actualizations, unless the specific organizational features that facilitate the actualization process are in place. For example, the presence of a common goal among the museum professionals to revitalize the museum’s operations, and their long-established expertise in collection management, served as important organizational features that drove the actualization of techno-capabilities for improved processes. Also, a strong drive and need for the museum professionals to catch-up with the new expectations in the digital environment and their deep appreciation of the cultural significance of the museum’s artifacts drive the actualization of techno-influence for an identity renewal. With this conceptualization of afordance-actualization, we aim to inspire scholars to open up the black box of digital transformation to consider the intertwining of technological and organizational features, the goaloriented actions, and the organizational outcomes.

## 4.2. Transforming for the digital age and the unintended consequences

The technology afordances perspective provides a lens for us to understand not only the enabling power of technologies, but also the constraints. From our analysis, we have uncovered several undesirable outcomes of digital transformation, which we capture under the notion of techno-stress. Techno-stress relates to the stress experienced by individuals in organizations as a result of their use of technology [52]. In the context of digital transformation, we see techno-stress as a salient unintended consequence that arises from a struggle to keep up with constantly evolving technology. Our study unveils two groups of stressors, i.e., techno-complexity and techno-uncertainty.

First, embracing digital transformation necessitates the use of complex technologies. However, investing in technologies generates significant techno-complexity, especially in the context of age-old organizations where difusion of technologies has been relatively limited. Techno-complexity describes the complexities associated with technology uses that necessitate a greater investment of time and efort [53]. As new technologies bring forth sophisticated and unfamiliar requirements, the process of learning and mastering them is often intimidating and stressful (D’Arcy et al. [54]). In the context of our study, the techno-stress induced by techno-complexity is particularly salient as most of the museum professionals lack the technological competence to quickly master advanced technologies. The presence of techno-stress constrains the organizational actors from further actualizing the afordances and hinders the progress of digital transformation.

Second, our analysis unveils the rise of techno-uncertainty when museum professionals are faced with an overwhelming number of technological choices. Our use of the notion of techno-uncertainty captures the negative consequences that arise when continuing technological changes unsettle the professionals and create anxiety [52,55]. Techno-uncertainty induces stress especially when technological development is so frequent that it inhibits professionals from accumulating experience [52] and injects fear into the individual that their existing expertise may become obsolete [53]. When professionals are faced with a potentially attractive yet identity-challenging technological opportunity, they are often uncertain about whether to embark on the risky endeavor or to bypass the opportunities. Techno-uncertainty may give rise to decision paralysis, or a tendency to maintain the status quo over the unfamiliar [56]. In other words, techno-uncertainty fosters resistance towards digital transformation.

Taken together, our findings highlight how unintended consequences can hinder the afordance-actualization process and thus inhibit the achievement of organizational outcomes (as illustrated in Fig. 3). This understanding suggests that while technologies may possess several action potentials, in the presence of constraints (such as techno-stress), actors may fail to perceive the afordances as opportunities for action, or simply be unable to put in place the necessary organizational arrangements to actualize the afordances. Technology constraints are still a relatively under-theorized concept in the existing literature [9,19,39]. In identifying the intriguing feedback efect of constraints on afordance-actualization, we spoke to relevant research on technology afordances and more generally, the dark side of technologies. ## 5. Implications and conclusion

This study addresses the research question of how emerging technologies could be actualized to facilitate digital transformation in a museum. The proposed findings inform future research in several areas, as summarized in Table 2. First, this study represents an early attempt to develop a theoretical understanding on the digital transformation of our museums. Museums are among the many mature institutions that currently find themselves at a crossroads of digital transformation. By presenting a revelatory case of a museum’s digital transformation, we encourage scholars to take a closer view at the paradoxical implications of digital transformation undergoing in diferent industries, especially pre-digital ones. In line with allied studies in the existing literature (e.g., [57]; [58]), we aim to encourage more explorations on how emerging technologies could be enacted by institutions to revive “endangered” industries in the digital era.

Second, our findings extend existing understanding on technology afordances by contributing a conceptualization on both the process of afordance-actualization and the technology constraints. Our conceptualization unveils the process in which afordances of varying emerging technologies are actualized for the attainment of digital transformation goals. Focusing on afordance-actualization is essential as it provides a closer view on the roles of actors and uses of technologies, and allows scholars to paint a more complete picture on digital transformation [9,19]. Providing an understanding on the action potentials of emerging technologies and how those potentials can be ac tualized to enable transformational changes in organizations can also help practitioners better leverage technologies for major business improvements.

Furthermore, by uncovering the unintended consequences of technology use, our findings provide a realistic understanding on a digital transformation process that is inherently complex and uncertain. Existing research on digital transformation has highlighted the need to understand how organizations navigate through resistance and inertia during the disruptive transformation process [57]. Making explicit the unintended consequences helps to deepen our understanding on the uses and implications of technologies as it helps enrich existing perspectives that mostly foreground the positive aspects of technology uses [40].

In closing, we are hopeful that our findings will open new paths of inquiry in examining the multifaceted nature of digital transformation. As technology advancements bring increasing digitalization to organi zational processes, products, and services [59], organizational change today have inevitably become strongly entangled with technologies [60]. IS research has great potential in informing the role and uses of technologies in the process of digital transformation ([61]; [62]). Based on the technology afordances perspective, this study presents a rich conceptualization of how a digital transformation unfolds in the context of a museum. Using our findings as a point of departure, we seek to encourage further investigations into the potentials of emerging technologies in facilitating digital transformation, and more specifically, to invite scholars to dive into the potentially paradoxical impacts of technologies in the transformation of pre-digital institutions.

Yenni Tim is a Lecturer at the School of Information Systems and Technology Management. UNSW Business School. Yenni has research interests in digitally enabled phenomena and the unintended consequences of technology, and currently specializes in the fields of cybersecurity and data analytics. Her research on cybersecurity focuses on the human factors, from individuals’ awareness to organizational culture and education. She works with practitioners to create data-driven understanding and measures on cybersecurity awareness and behaviors. In her research, she also drives changes through behavioral interventions and the design of proofs-of-concepts, and develops metrics to assess the impact of such changes on relevant organizational outcomes
