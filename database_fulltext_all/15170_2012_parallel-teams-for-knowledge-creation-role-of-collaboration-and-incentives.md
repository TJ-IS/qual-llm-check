# Parallel teams for knowledge creation: Role of collaboration and incentives

Shankar Sundaresan <sup>a,1</sup>, Zuopeng Zhang <sup>b,</sup>⁎

<sup>a</sup> Rutgers School of Business-Camden, Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, 227 Penn Street, Camden, NJ 08102, United State

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Keywords: Parallel teams Incentives Collaboration Knowledge creation

## a b s t r a c t

Parallel team strategy, in which multiple teams simultaneously pursue project goals, has been widely adopted by high-tech industries for knowledge creation. In this study, we investigate the design of organizational incentives, including a <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment and an additional reward structure, for effective management of the parallel team strategy. We consider two main variants of parallel teams—collaborative and non-collaborative teams. Proposing and investigating three types of organizational reward policies, individual, aggregate, and contingent, we analyze the viability and characteristics of these policies. We show that individual reward policy performs better than aggregate policy, and that collaboration in parallel teams is vital. When parallel teams work noncollaboratively or when aggregate reward policy is used for collaborative teams, the <sup>fi</sup>rm achieves optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by only offering a share of the knowledge creation bene<sup>fi</sup>t as the reward. Under some conditions for collaborative teams, we demonstrate that individual and contingent reward policies can achieve maximal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (<sup>fi</sup>rst-best) for the organization. This research provides valuable insights for <sup>fi</sup>rms in employing parallel team strategy for knowledge creation.

## 1. Introduction

Teams serve as effective organizational structures for pursuing innovation in <sup>fi</sup>rms. In a ground-breaking study of the practices of General Motors in 1943, Peter Drucker pointed out the effectiveness of teambased structures in organizations. As evidenced by numerous successful cases, team structures have been employed in many new projects. For example, Microsoft launched its gaming platform Xbox within a short period of time by employing the team strategy. At Google, various teams are assembled to work on different projects, such as Google Documents, Google Health, and Google Checkout.

Past research provides insightful perspectives on team composition. For instance, Drucker [9] identi<sup>fi</sup>es three kinds of teams as baseball, football, and tennis doubles teams. Cohen and Bailey [6] categorize teams in organizations into four types: work, parallel, project, and management teams. Katzenback and Smith [20] suggest three types of teams: teams that—recommend, do, or run things. These papers share some similar notions on teams, for instance, the parallel team categorization of Cohen and Bailey [6] is the same as the football team identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by Drucker [9].

Taking into account the increasingly complex nature of technologies, recent IS research on teams has considered virtual teams, for instance, the application of group support systems in supporting them [18] and the social factors for their creation and effectiveness [23]. Not only do <sup>fi</sup>rms have to adopt appropriate team structures, but also assemble multiple teams to collectively engage in a single project. For example, Microsoft organized more than 200 programmers into several teams in developing the Windows95 operating system which contains more than 11 million lines of code [8]. Firms are continuously seeking cost-effective strategies to manage and coordinate teams on innovative projects.

Different team strategies have been employed by industries for innovation and research. Concurrent team strategy is widely adopted to shorten the development time of new products. For instance, project managers at Microsoft usually divide a project into modules and different teams then work simultaneously on their modules but they synchronize with each other and debug daily [7]. In contrast, in the parallel team strategy, many teams work on the same research project simultaneously so as to maximize the overall success rate of the project. We cite a broad range of applications where parallel team strategy has been successful. It is frequently applied in research where single-team strategy results in high failure rates. For example, Nelson [29] documents the adoption of parallel strategy in R&D by the U.S. Air force. A similar parallel team approach was also used at National Institute of Health (NIH) to develop a malaria vaccine, instead of the traditional process of malaria vaccine development, where if one approach fails, the entire effort dies with it as well [32]. Arditti and Levy [2] report the use of parallel path strategy in the development of aircraft engines and MIG <sup>fi</sup>ghters, and also in the electronics industry for color TV development. Siegal and Chang [34] describe Samsung's use of parallel product development teams—teams in California and Korea working simultaneously pursuing different approaches in DRAM chip development, but cooperating as well. Wiklund [38] advocates the use of parallel drug development in which several candidate compounds are evaluated simultaneously to bring in productivity gains and shorter development times. In this paper, we model how to combine the parallel team strategy with incentives for effective knowledge creation.

We highlight this paper's contribution by considering two related important dimensions of team functioning: (i) whether the teams work in parallel or not, and (ii) whether the teams collaborate or not. Most prior research has addressed primarily non-parallel teams focusing on issues such as team structure, team motivation, and social and organizational factors impacting team performance [19]. Some papers incorporating collaboration within and between non-parallel teams investigate how collaboration improves team performance [1]. Among the very few papers that have analyzed the parallel team strategy, Arditti and Levy [2] study how to choose the best number of parallel teams in new product development; but their context is limited to non-collaborative teams. In contrast, our paper primarily focuses on the so-far-unaddressed parallel and collaborative teams and highlights the role of collaboration by comparing it to the case when it is absent. Considering the parallel teams strategy, we study how the design of incentives and collaboration affect the success rate of innovation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we address the following research questions in this paper.

First, how does the design of teams differ between collaborative and non-collaborative parallel teams? Multiple parallel teams can be formed as either non-collaborative team or collaborative team similar to the differentiation between working groups and teams by Katzenback and Smith [19]: non-collaborative teams are loosely bound together for some common goals, whereas collaborative teams coalesce because of the collaboration among them. In particular, non-collaborative teams work independently without learning from or sharing with other teams, whereas collaborative teams work closely together so as to effectively increase the success rate of the research project. We study whether and how the presence of collaboration in parallel teams helps a <sup>fi</sup>rm improve knowledge creation.

Second, how should a firm design incentive structures for parallel research teams? The incentive structure for a team in this research consists of a <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment and an additional reward policy. All the teams get the <sup>fi</sup>xed payment irrespective of their individual success and they will be rewarded additionally if their research project succeeds. Three types of reward policies (individual, aggregate, and contingent) are proposed in this paper and we analyze how these rewards should be designed so that the <sup>fi</sup>rm may achieve maximal pro<sup>fi</sup>t.

Finally, how should the firm match the reward policy appropriately for different team structures? We examine whether different reward policies can achieve the maximal pro<sup>fi</sup>t (<sup>fi</sup>rst-best) for the two team structures. We show the conditions under which these reward structures are effective.

Our paper makes several signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions. While it is true that collaboration can be expected to improve team performance, we demonstrate that the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution can be achieved even when workers voluntarily choose their effort levels. Similarly, although rewards can generally improve performance, we characterize the structure of the reward itself. Moreover, we explore the characteristics of various reward policies—individual, aggregate, and contingent policies, including the appropriate number of teams. We show that individual reward policy performs better than aggregate policy, and that collaboration in parallel teams is vital. When parallel teams work non-collaboratively or when aggregate reward policy is used for collaborative teams, the <sup>fi</sup>rm achieves optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>ts by only offering a share of the knowledge creation bene<sup>fi</sup>t as the reward. In spite of the negative complementarity between the effort level and the total number of teams, we demonstrate that individual and contingent reward policies can achieve maximal bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (<sup>fi</sup>rst-best) for the organization. Therefore, managers can employ our results to design reward structures to improve knowledge creation outcome.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 brie<sup>fl</sup>y reviews prior re lated research, Section 3 outlines our model, Section 4 presents the detailed analysis and discussion, and Section 5 provides managerial insights and concludes the paper.

## 2. Literature review

We brie<sup>fl</sup>y review prior research on the theory of knowledge creation, team incentives, tournaments, collaboration and team approach for knowledge creation, and contrast our work from these studies. The theory of knowledge creation pioneered by Nonaka [30] proposes four patterns of interactions between tacit and explicit knowledge in organizations. Against this backdrop, Fong [11] develops a conceptual model of knowledge creation in the context of multidisciplinary project teams by synthesizing various relevant knowledge creation processes. Li and Kettinger [22] outline an iterative process of knowledge creation in which new knowledge is generated through applying existing knowledge in solving problems. Malhotra and Majchrzak [25] describe different types of IT support to enable knowledge sharing across multiple far-<sup>fl</sup>ung teams so as to facilitate knowledge creation. Our research does not focus on what speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge creation process is used, but rather on the complementary aspect of incentives and collaboration that facilitate knowledge creation in parallel teams.

A rich body of research exists on incentives, teams, and tournaments in the economics literature. Classical principal–agent models explore the optimal incentive structure under incomplete information and the relationship between <sup>fi</sup>rst-best and second-best solutions. For example, Holmstrom [16] and Grossman and Hart [13] study the moral hazard problem within the context of a single principal and a single agent. Spence [35] introduces the important idea of signaling in a model where employees signal their productivity levels via educational levels in the job market. These early studies provide signi<sup>fi</sup>cant insights into how the principal can use incentives to induce agents to exert optimal effort. Building on the principal–agent models, the theory of teams literature [26] introduces important ideas for team-based incentive systems. Two common incentive systems – the paid worker and pro<sup>fi</sup>t sharing incentive structures – may be used to align team members' interests with that of an organization [14]. However, in a team with multiple agents, group incentive cannot achieve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency without breaking the budget-balancing constraint [17]. Under certain conditions, a contract linear in output can be optimal for a team and monitoring does not improve the team output [27]. But none of these papers consider parallel team structures, whereas our research focuses on the design of incentives for parallel teams with or without collaboration.

We present a brief overview of the related stream of literature on tournaments and contrast our model. In tournaments, compensation is based on relative ranking rather than on output level. Tournament format is often used when the absolute value of the output is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to measure, whereas relative ranking is easy to establish. Examples abound in employee promotion policies and sports. Considering a <sup>fi</sup>rm with two players, and external shocks, Lazear and Rosen [21] show that tournament prize schemes can achieve the same optimal resource allocation induced by output based contracts when agents are risk neutral. Green and Stokey [12] further analyze tournaments with many risk averse agents, and demonstrate when tournaments perform better than independent contracts—without common shock, independent contracts fare better while tournaments dominate when the shock is diffused. In the context of idea generation, Morgan and Wang [28] explore the suitability of different types of tournament formats and features (such as winner-take-all, multiround tournaments, handicapping) to the nature of participants (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous abilities) and tasks (evolutionary and revolutionary). While our model bears some similarity to tournaments, there are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences as well. Similar to tournaments, in our model, the compensation is not based on output level, but on achieving project success. Unlike tournaments, where there is always a winner, we permit a no-winner outcome. Moreover, in tournaments, output level truly does not matter, only the ordinal ranking; we require the output to cross the success threshold, making it closer to the “meeting standards” approach to designing incentives. Tournament prizes are usually <sup>fi</sup>xed, whereas we analyze a range of reward policies that involve more complex interactions. Tournament analysis does not usually permit interdependency of participant efforts (due to complexity), whereas our stylized model allows for detailed exploration of collaborative teams.

IS researchers have recently touched upon incentive issues for knowledge workers. Incentives have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as an important dimension in designing knowledge management systems to facilitate knowledge sharing and learning [3,5]. Incentives related to knowledgemanagement efforts are essential in creating a knowledge-sharing culture [36]. Ba, Stallaert, and Whinston [4] demonstrate how a Grove-Clarke like incentive mechanism can be employed in an internal knowledge market. However, neither incentives for parallel teams nor collaboration among teams is considered in these studies.

In the context of non-parallel teams, prior research has studied collaboration within and across teams. For instance, Gupta et al. [15] propose a mechanism to facilitate collaboration between teams in different locations so as to effectively distribute tasks across multiple teams and coordinate their effort to complete a project. Ramesh and Tiwana [31] develop a prototype system to address the problems of managing tacit and explicit knowledge among collaborative teams. From a socio-technical perspective, Thomas and Bostrom [37] investigate the important role of team leaders and their knowledge of information technology for team collaboration on information system development projects. Lin et al. [24] identify team collaboration patterns by using information retrieval and data mining techniques. A few studies have modeled and analyzed various aspects of knowledge creation using teams. Sharda et al. [33] propose the concept of knowledge network, describe its architecture, and implement the structure in two concurrent teams for new product development. Dutta and Prasad [10] explore a multi-period model in which workers can decide whether to participate in research in each period and once a researcher succeeds, her discovery will be disseminated automatically to the whole <sup>fi</sup>rm. Arditti and Levy [2] investigate how to optimally choose the number of parallel R&D teams in developing a new product. We synthesize elements of some of these studies to provide the basis for our analytical model. For example, our model considerably extends the simple construction by Arditti and Levy [2] to a more sophisticated framework: (1) we design and analyze different types of organizational incentives to motivate individual teams; (2) we model and investigate the critical role of collaboration among parallel teams.

## 3. Model of parallel teams for knowledge creation

In this section, we present our model for knowledge creation using parallel teams, in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm designs and announces incentive structures. Beginning with the analysis of an individual team's decision problem under the given reward policy, we then consider the organizational decision problem. Finally, we simplify the analysis of the model by an innovative approach.

We consider a <sup>fi</sup>rm that wishes to employ parallel teams to engage in a research project for knowledge creation. In the parallel team approach, multiple teams work simultaneously, pursuing the same research goals, the primary motivation being improved success rate. Each team i exerts a certain level of effort $e _ { i } ,$ generating a success probability $\rho ^ { I } ( e _ { i } )$ . We assume that a team's success rate concavely increases in its effort, i.e., when team members collectively exert more effort, the team is more likely to succeed in the project; however, at higher effort levels, additional effort brings lower improvements to the project success rate. In our model, the team is considered as the unit of analysis and we assume all the teams are homogeneous with respect to their abilities in research. In this research, we do not focus on the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of how the collective effort is distributed within the team or the team composition.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm wishes to maximize the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the project and it can choose the reward structure and the number of parallel teams (M). Increasing the number of teams will improve the overall project success rate, while incurring a higher compensation cost. Choosing an appropriate reward structure can in<sup>fl</sup>uence effort levels of the teams. We consider a payment structure that consists of two parts: a <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment w and an additional reward r. We <sup>fi</sup>rst consider the individual reward policy, in which each successful team receives the same incentive reward. The payment for successful teams will be w+r and unsuccessful teams will be w.

A team decides how much effort to exert based on the announced reward policy and its own cost of effort. We assume that the cost of effort c(e ) increases convexly in the amount of effort. Given the <sup>fi</sup>xed payment w and the reward r for the individual reward policy, team i maximizes its expected net payoff:

$$
\pi_ {i} \left(e _ {i} | w, r\right) = w - c \left(e _ {i}\right) + \rho^ {I} \left(e _ {i}, M\right) \cdot r,\tag{1}
$$

where $\rho ^ { I } ( e _ { i } , M )$ is the individual team's probability of success. The difference in $\rho ^ { I } ( e _ { i } , M )$ when the teams function collaboratively or noncollaboratively is discussed in detail later on in Sections 4.1 and 4.2. If team i chooses its effort level $e _ { i }$ such that $e _ { i } { \in } a r g m a x \left\{ \pi _ { i } \Big ( e _ { i } ^ { ' } | w , r ) \right\}$ then the incentive-compatibility constraint for team i is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Let $\rho ^ { G } ( E , M )$ represent the group success rate of the project when the team effort vector is given by E and let B denote the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the research project. Then the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net payoff, the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>t less the expected cost, is:

$$
\pi (w, M, r) = \rho^ {G} (E, M) B - M \cdot w - \sum_ {i} ^ {M} \rho^ {I} (e _ {i}, M) \cdot r.\tag{2}
$$

The <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem [P] is to choose the optimal payment structure and the number of teams, while incentivizing the teams to choose the optimal effort level voluntarily (incentive compatibility) and yielding a positive net bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the teams (individual rationality). We denote by $E ^ { * }$ the set of effort levels for all the teams when each individual team i's incentive-compatible constraint is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. We assume that reservation utility for teams to be zero. Formally, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem is:

$$
\max _ {w, M, r} \quad \pi (w, M, r),\tag{3}
$$

subject to:

$$
\begin{array}{l} E \in E ^ {*}, \\ \pi_ {i} \geq 0, \\ w \geq 0, \end{array}
$$

where the <sup>fi</sup>rst constraint is the incentive-compatibility constraint (IC) for all teams, the second constraint is the individual-rationality constraint (IR), and the last constraint ensures a positive wage payment (see Table 1 for the complete list of notations).

We conduct our analysis in the next section for different scenarios: (1) for individual, aggregate, or contingent reward policy; (2) for noncollaborative and collaborative parallel teams.

We consider three types of reward policies in this research. Under the individual reward policy, all successful teams receive the same reward r. When the aggregate reward policy is applied, all the teams (both successful and unsuccessful teams) will equally share an aggregate reward R when the project succeeds. With the contingent reward policy, only successful teams will be rewarded and the reward r they receive is related to the number of successful teams (j).

The second dimension of our analysis focuses on two types of teams. For a team in a non-collaborative setup, individual team success rate is independent of the total number of teams. Hence, the individual team success rate is $\rho ^ { I } ( e , M ) = \rho ( e )$ . ## 4. Analysis and discussion

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst solve the <sup>fi</sup>rm's optimal solution for noncollaborative teams with the individual reward policy. Second, for the collaborative team setting, we introduce the notion of team elasticity of collaboration, which describes the change of collaboration level when one more team is added and explore various conditions regarding teams' effort levels and the number of teams. Third, we consider the aggregate team reward policy and derive the optimal effort levels. Finally, we propose a contingent reward policy that outperforms both individual and aggregate reward policies for the symmetric equilibrium.

## 4.1. Non-collaborative teams

We begin with the investigation of non-collaborative teams. As is common in economic models, we consider the benchmark of <sup>fi</sup>rstbest solution (solution to problem [P]′ without constraint in Eq. (5)) wherein the <sup>fi</sup>rm is able to mandate the optimal effort levels and the optimal number of teams to maximize organizational net bene<sup>fi</sup>t (while individual team utilities are zero). We compute the equilibrium under the conditions when the teams voluntarily choose their effort levels and need to be appropriately incentivized, and compare our equilibrium solution with the benchmark solution. For this setting, we show in the next proposition that the maximum bene<sup>fi</sup>t is achieved by the <sup>fi</sup>rm (referred commonly as the second-best solution) by not offering any <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payments to teams.

## 2. the firm cannot achieve the first-best solution.

When the teams voluntarily choose individual effort levels, they trade-off the cost of the effort with the increased likelihood of success that additional effort will bring, <sup>fi</sup>nding a balance which maximizes their expected net utility. This effort level is invariably lower than the organizational optimal effort level (<sup>fi</sup>rst-best). Since the teams do not collaborate and only successful teams get a reward, there is no bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the individual team from the failure or success of other teams. In addition, as teams are not willing to share the risk of project failure by paying the deposit (negative wage payment) to the <sup>fi</sup>rm, they would rather work at a lower (second-best) effort level to obtain the same expected utility. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution. The <sup>fi</sup>rm is forced to maintain a positive wage payment preventing the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution. The best number $M ^ { * }$ of teams in the second-best case is larger than that in the first-base case, As teams exert less effort in the secondbest scenario, the <sup>fi</sup>rm needs to assemble more teams to increase the success rate of the project. In summary, with the individual reward policy,<sup>2</sup> in a group of non-collaborative teams, the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rstbest effort levels. The second-best effort levels can be obtained by paying teams a proportion of the research bene<sup>fi</sup>t as reward for success under certain conditions.

## 4.2. Collaborative teams

After discussing non-collaborative teams, we next turn our attention to the incentive design for collaborative teams. We explore the effects of three organizational reward polices for parallel teams in the next subsections.

## 4.2.1. Individual reward policy

In this subsection, we demonstrate that for collaborative teams, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution under some conditions. We <sup>fi</sup>rst propose the concept of team elasticity of collaboration and examine its relationship with project success rate, then de<sup>fi</sup>ne and investigate the complementarity between the effort level e and the total number of teams M, and <sup>fi</sup>nally characterize the conditions to obtain the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best and second-best solutions.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne the term ${ \psi } { = } M q ^ { \prime } ( M ) / q ( M )$ as the team elasticity of collaboration which measures how the degree of collaboration changes when one more team is added. Notice that when $\psi = 1$ $q ( M ) = \kappa ^ { * } M ,$ where κ is a constant, meaning that the degree of collaboration $q ( M )$ among teams is a linear function of the number of teams. In addition, this team elasticity can be negative if the collaboration strength starts to decrease when more teams are in the group. The project success rate is related to the team elasticity of collaboration. Our next proposition shows a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient condition for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to obtain an optimal solution, where ε represents the Euler's number.

Proposition 2. For collaborative teams, the following relationship at the stationary point $( e ^ { * } , M ^ { * } )$ between the team elasticity of collaboration and the project success rate serves as the prerequisite to the existence of the firm's solution (Hessian Matrix being negative definite):

$$
\begin{array}{l} 1. \text {when} 0 \leq \rho^ {G} (e ^ {*}, M ^ {*}) \leq 1 - 1 / \varepsilon , \psi \geq 1, \text {and} \\ 2. \text {when} 1 - 1 / \varepsilon <   \rho^ {G} (e ^ {*}, M ^ {*}) \leq 1, 1 \leq \psi <   \frac {1}{1 + 2 l n (1 - \rho^ {G})}. \end{array}
$$

Proposition 2 demonstrates the ranges of the team elasticity of collaboration for different project success rates so that the <sup>fi</sup>rm may achieve the maximal bene<sup>fi</sup>t. There exists a threshold level of the project success rate, $1 - 1 / \varepsilon .$ . When the project success rate is lower than this threshold, the team elasticity of collaboration always exceeds 1 without an upper bound, implying that collaboration is always necessary. In contrast, when the project success rate is relatively high (higher than the threshold), collaboration is still required (but must lie below an upper bound) in order for the <sup>fi</sup>rm to obtain the possible optimal bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

We next turn to discuss the implication of the conditions in Proposition 2 with respect to the complementarity between the effort level e and the number of teams M. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned $\partial ^ { 2 } \pi / \partial e \partial M$ as the complementarity between the effort level e and the number of teams M, which measures how they jointly affect the rate of the change of the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, $\mathrm { i f } \partial ^ { 2 } \pi / \partial e \partial M { = } 0 ,$ , there exists no complementarity between e and M, which means that individual team's effort level is independent of the total number of teams; if $\partial ^ { 2 } \pi / \partial e \partial M { > } 0 ,$ , there exists positive complementarity between e and M, implying that the effort level increases when there are more teams; and if $\bar { \partial ^ { 2 } } \pi / \partial e \partial M { < } 0$ , there exists negative complementarity between e and M so that the effort level decreases when more teams function in parallel. Based on Proposition $^ { 2 , }$ we further illustrate the role of complementarity between e and M at the optimal stationary point in the following corollary.

Corollary 1 indicates that positive complementarity between effort levels and the number of teams requires a higher team elasticity of collaboration than zero or negative complementarity. In other words, if the elasticity of collaboration for teams is high enough in the range of $\scriptstyle \left( { \frac { 1 } { 1 + l n ( 1 - \rho ^ { G } ) } } , { \frac { 1 } { 1 + 2 l n ( 1 - \rho ^ { G } ) } } \right]$ , an individual team will exert <sup>þ ð Þ þ ð Þ</sup>more effort when more teams work in parallel. If the project success rate $\rho ^ { G }$ is higher than $1 - 1 / \varepsilon ,$ teams will always exert less effort when more teams join the group; if the project success rate is lower than $1 - 1 / \varepsilon ,$ , then negative complementarity will exist when the team elasticity of collaboration is lower than $\frac { 1 } { 1 + l n ( 1 - \rho ^ { G } ) } .$

Proposition 3. For collaborative teams under the individual reward policy, the necessary and sufficient condition for both the firm's optimal profit and the fixed wage payment to be positive is:

$$

and the teams' optimal effort level can be characterized as:

\frac {c ^ {'} (e ^ {*})}{\rho^ {'} (e ^ {*})} = \frac {q (M ^ {*})}{M ^ {*}} \left(1 - \rho (e ^ {*})\right) ^ {q (M ^ {*}) - 1} B,
$$

Which can be induced by offering the optimal reward as:

$$
r ^ {*} = \frac {c ^ {\prime} (e ^ {*})}{\rho_ {e} ^ {I} (e ^ {*} , M ^ {*})} = \frac {(1 - \rho (e ^ {*})) ^ {q (M ^ {*})}}{(1 - \rho (e ^ {*})) ^ {\frac {q (M ^ {*})}{M ^ {*}}}} B.

Proposition 3 characterizes the conditions that enable the <sup>fi</sup>rm to offer a positive <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment and still maintain a positive optimal pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Under the individual reward policy, the existence of negative complementarity between e and M is a prerequisite for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t as well as the <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment to be positive and achieving the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution. In other words, when the <sup>fi</sup>rm achieves the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution, the total number of teams in the group should negatively complement the teams' effort level, i.e., when more teams join in the group for research, each team shall bene<sup>fi</sup>t and exert less effort. Thus, the reason that the <sup>fi</sup>rm can achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution only with collaborative teams but not non-collaborative teams lies in the facts that (1) collaboration enables teams to achieve a higher project success rate and (2) collaboration lets teams exert less effort so the <sup>fi</sup>rm incurs lower cost by making less payments.

When the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution cannot be obtained, the <sup>fi</sup>rm may still achieve the second-best solution, the condition for which is presented in the next proposition.

Proposition 4. For collaborative teams under the individual reward policy, the firm can only achieve the second-best solution by paying a zero wage payment if:

$$
\psi <   \frac {M \rho^ {I}}{- (1 - \rho^ {I}) l n (1 - \rho^ {G})},

and the effort level and reward in this case can be determined from:

\begin{array}{c} {r = \frac {c ^ {'} (e)}{\rho_ {e} ^ {I} (e , M)},} \\ {c (e) = \rho^ {I} (e, M) r.} \end{array}
$$

## 4.2.3. Contingent reward policy

Following our discussion of the differences between the individual and aggregate reward policies, we propose a contingent reward policy which moderates the negative effects of both policies. Under contingent reward policy, the <sup>fi</sup>rm <sup>fi</sup>rst determines the total number of teams M and then announces the reward policy which is a vector containing the amount of reward (for each successful team) corresponding to the total number of successful teams, which may range from 1 to M. Hence, a team will be rewarded only if it succeeds and the reward is related to the total number of successful teams: when more teams succeed, each successful team will get a higher reward. Therefore, a team will voluntarily collaborate with other teams to “help” them succeed, thereby enhancing team collaboration.

We next investigate the structure of this contingent reward policy. The next lemma demonstrates that the contingent reward policy can induce teams to exert <sup>fi</sup>rst-best efforts under the symmetric equilibrium, in which $r _ { j }$ is the amount of reward for each successful team when j number of teams succeed, $\forall j = 1 , . . . , M .$

Proposition 6. Under the contingent reward policy for collaborative teams, the reward policy $r _ { j } , \ \forall j = 1 , . . . , M ,$ , that satisfies the following condition:

$$
\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {M} C _ {M - 1} ^ {j - 1} \left(\rho^ {I} (e, M)\right) ^ {j} \left(1 - \rho^ {I} (e, M)\right) ^ {M - j} r _ {j} = \frac {\rho_ {e} ^ {G} (e , M)}{M \rho_ {e} ^ {I} (e , M)},\tag{8}
$$

can induce teams to exert the first-best efforts under the symmetric equilibrium.

## 5. Managerial implications and conclusion

The increasingly competitive market has forced companies to seek more cost-effective ways to engage in knowledge creation. Recent trends in knowledge creation clearly indicate that companies are constantly searching for a better strategy to not only lower costs but also improve the quality of knowledge discovery.

In this paper, we presented a model of parallel teams in which a <sup>fi</sup>rm employs multiple teams and designs incentives to motivate these teams to exert their best effort. We derive important managerial insights about the parallel team strategy and our analysis provides valuable guidelines for managers in deploying parallel teams as discussed below.

First, incentivizing teams to effectively engage in knowledge creation is essential for <sup>fi</sup>rms to improve their productivity and overall performance. We show how to design incentives by choosing the combination of a <sup>fi</sup>xed wage payment and rewards to induce workers' best efforts in knowledge innovation, enhancing the success rate of research projects for potential knowledge discovery.

Second, when parallel teams work non-collaboratively due to organizational constraints, the <sup>fi</sup>rm should not offer any <sup>fi</sup>xed payment to the teams, but only offer a share of the knowledge creation bene<sup>fi</sup>t as the reward.

Third, under a collaborative structure, we demonstrate that the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution can be achieved under certain conditions. Successful teams can be rewarded individually or all teams collectively when the project succeeds. Under the individual reward policy, although it is possible to achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best effort levels, the incentive to motivate collaboration may not be so strong because only successful teams get the reward. In contrast, under the aggregate reward policy, teams will equally share the total reward as long as a team succeeds, so the teams will collaborate voluntarily. However, the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution cannot be achieved. To take the advantages of the positive features of both the individual and aggregate reward policies, we propose the contingent reward policy that rewards only successful teams and the amount of reward increases with the total number of successful teams. This contingent policy can be implemented to achieve the <sup>fi</sup>rst-best solution and also facilitate the collaboration among teams.

This study sheds light on how incentives and collaboration among teams affect organizational decisions on employing parallel teams in knowledge creation. Future research may explore the competition among parallel teams and how to exploit it for organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>t, study the uncertainty of innovation bene<sup>fi</sup>t with potential knowledge discovery, and investigate the impacts of information technology on team collaboration. In conclusion, our paper provides valuable insights for managers to determine the structure and level of rewards in managing parallel teams and achieving optimal organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Dr. Shankar Sundaresan is an Assistant Professor of Management in the School of Business at Rutgers, the State University of New Jersey, Camden. His research interests span both technology and management aspects of information systems covering the areas of economics of information systems, pricing of information goods and services, knowledge management, and automated database design. His research has won awards in 1995, 96, 97 and 2002 at various premier IS conferences. Dr. Sundaresan was awarded the IBM Faculty Fellowship in 2003. His papers have appeared in ACM Transactions on Database Systems, Journal of Management Information Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, International Journal of Electronic Commerce, Data & Knowledge Engineering, Information System Frontiers and other journals. He has served as an ad-hoc referee for many journals including Management Science, Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly and Journal of Management Information Systems. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Rochester, and was a member of the faculty at Pennsylvania State University before joining Rutgers.
