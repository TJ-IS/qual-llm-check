# Impact of network effects and diffusion channels on home computer adoption

Gang Peng <sup>a</sup>, Ming Fan <sup>b,</sup>⁎, Debabrata Dey <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Williamson College of Business Administration, Youngstown State University, Youngstown, OH 44555, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 11 June 2010 Received in revised form 25 December 2010 Accepted 20 January 2011 Available online 27 January 2011

Keywords: Household computer adoption Network effects Social learning Diffusion channels Repeat purchases

## a b s t r a c t

Researchers have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed network effects as one of the major drivers for the adoption and diffusion of household technologies. However, the mechanisms and social contexts through which network effects induce technology adoption are unclear. In this paper, we investigate the adoption of household computers using the dataset from the 1989–2003 Computer and Internet Supplement to the Current Population Surveys (CPS). We argue that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning play a dominant role in inducing the adoption of home computers, and workplaces and schools are important channels through which network effects take place. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that recent adopters have a stronger impact than distant adopters on future computer adoptions. When the adoption rate is low, channels play a more important role, but their effects diminish as the adoption rate grows. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that diffusion channels are more effective for <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption than for repeat purchases. Overall, our study provides important theoretical, policy, and managerial implications.

## 1. Introduction

The advent of computers and the Internet has completely changed the way businesses are conducted [37]. Technology adoption plays an important role in economic growth and development [13]. In the labor market, studies <sup>fi</sup>nd that skill-biased technology change has shifted labor demand towards more skilled workers [7]. Technologically-skilled individuals are, therefore, better prepared to participate in economic, political, and social activities, and to engage in complex and technology-oriented dimensions of today's economy [57]. However, although policy makers and researchers have long recognized the importance of technology, and government agencies have implemented a series of programs to make it more accessible,<sup>1</sup> the “digital divide” still exists today. According to the U.S. Census Bureau [56], for example, 38% of the households in the U.S. did not have computers at home in 2005, and the number quickly rises to 55% for Black or Hispanic households. In a recent national survey, Parks Associates found 18% of U.S. households still do not have Internet access.<sup>2</sup> In a global sense, the digital divide is even wider—in Japan and Norway, household computer adoption is over 80%, whereas it is below 10% in many parts of the world such as Philippines, Sri Lanka,

Paraguay, and Uganda [58]. Statistics of the International Telecommunication Union show that only 5.34% individuals in Africa had access to the Internet in 2007, compared to a world-wide average of 22.01%.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, how to bridge the “digital divide” remains on the top of the agenda for both researchers and practitioners [3,40,63].

Information systems (IS) literature has studied technology adoption mainly from users' perception of technology [2,21]. But increasingly, researchers have come to realize that social structure and interactions among individuals also play important roles in affecting attitude towards technology [3,5,47,48]. For technology adoption by households, the importance of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence is re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the model of adoption of technology in households (MATH model), which proposes that attitudinal belief and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence are important factors affecting household computer adoption [15,60].

One of the most important and powerful social in<sup>fl</sup>uences is that of network effects. The notion of network effects is that, as individuals are increasingly exposed to others' adoption, they are more likely to adopt the same technology [48,55,62]. However, current literature on network effects faces several challenges. First and foremost, although there are several possible mechanisms underlying network effects, it is unclear which speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms affect the adoption decision [1,26,55]. Second, though the aggregate impact of network effects is well known in literature [25,34], few have examined the speci<sup>fi</sup>c effect of diffusion channels on technology adoption. In addition, whether these diffusion channels are as effective on repeat purchases as they are on <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption is largely under-explored. In this study, we examine the above research issues in the context of household computer adoption.

Examining the impact of network effects and diffusion channels on the adoption of home computers also bear important practical implications. Although home computers are widely adopted today in the U.S., the adoption levels are still very low in many developing countries. Therefore, analyzing how network effects and diffusion channels have induced the proliferation of home computers in the U. S. will provide valuable experience and insights for those countries in their efforts to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of household technologies [60]. In addition, as suggested in prior literature, network effects can be powerful for the adoption of many technologies [50]. However, network effects can possibly arise from several mechanisms [1], which bear distinct policy implications [26]. Therefore, in order to take advantage of the network effects, we need a better understanding of their underlying mechanisms. This study attempts to meet this need and makes the following contributions:

1. We develop competing hypotheses to investigate the underlying mechanisms of network effects. We argue and provide empirical evidence that social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning play a dominant role in the adoption of household computers.

2. We further examine the diffusion channels through which social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning take place and affect potential adopters. In particular, we analyze the effect of computer uses at workplaces and schools on home computer adoption.

3. We contend that it is important to distinguish between computer adoption (<sup>fi</sup>rst-time purchases) and repeat purchases when examining the effect of diffusion channels, and argue that the impact of diffusion channels is stronger for <sup>fi</sup>rst-time purchases than for repeat purchases.

4. We analyze a large publicly available dataset from the 1989–2003 Computer and Internet Supplements to the Current Population Surveys (CPS). By addressing the heterogeneity across adopters and causal ambiguity, we are able to tease out the relationship between social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and the adoption of home computers in a much more nuanced way than before.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we discuss the theoretical background and develop testable hypotheses. Data and methodology are presented in Section 3, followed by empirical results in Section 4. Contribution, implications, and limitations are discussed in Section 5. Section 6 concludes the paper and offers direction for future research.

## 2. Theory and hypotheses

Research on technology adoption and diffusion has received attention from various disciplines including economics, sociology, information systems (IS), organizational science, and marketing [21,64]. Diffusion and adoption of information technologies, both at the individual level [21] and at the organizational level [24,62], have been widely studied in IS. In a survey paper, Banker and Kauffman [9] identify technology adoption as one of the main IS research themes.

The innovation adoption and diffusion theory postulates that people adopt new technologies when the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the adoption exceed the costs [36,49]. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technology adoption can be derived from both technology-side factors (such as technology attributes) and adopter-side factors (such as income and preferences). The decision to adopt a technology can be regarded as a utility maximization process where a potential adopter compares the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of adoption and decides whether to adopt the technology or not [27]. We follow and extend this approach for our study. Although it differs from the social psychology approach [4,21], there is a growing common perspective that integrates the two views [6,10,67].

In this research, we focus on the impact of network effects and diffusion channels on household computer adoption. Network effects have been well recognized as an important factor in technology adoption; yet, the speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms and social contexts through which network effects function are unclear. We <sup>fi</sup>rst analyze the various mechanisms underlying the network effects and how they can potentially be distinguished.

## 2.1. Network effects

Network effects describe the social phenomenon that an increase in the number of adopters of a technology will further fuel future adoption of the same technology. Several underlying mechanisms have been proposed [1]. First, from the economics perspective, the decision to adopt a technology depends on the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the adoption. As more people use a particular technology, users can derive higher net bene<sup>fi</sup>t [23,55,62]. In the case of home computer adoption, network effects manifest themselves in the following way: Higher adoption drives down software prices by distributing developers' costs across a larger user base. Also, when more households are using computers, it becomes easier for users to share information and obtain technical help.

Second, from a social perspective, network effects can arise from social in<sup>fl</sup>uence [52] and learning [35]. Quite often, early adopters create a “bandwagon” effect so that potential users would like to follow earlier adopters and become users of the same technology [52]. Sometimes, it is the information and status of the adopters that generate a social pressure, causing more potential adopters to adopt, thereby reinforcing the bandwagon pressure. Network effects can also be realized through association and learning from members within the network [35]. Increased adoption of home computers will create more opportunities and contact points for potential adopters to overcome adoption barriers and to learn and gain computer-related knowledge, thereby facilitating further adoption.

Despite the differences between the economics and social perspectives on network effects, prior research is often not clear about which mechanism dominates home computer adoption [26]. Here, we argue that there are some subtle, but important, differences in how these two mechanisms in<sup>fl</sup>uence a potential adopter and that the social perspective can complement the economic analysis by providing a richer understanding of how network effects take place. First, from the economics perspective, the impact of network effects on technology adoption is typically modeled as a utility maximization problem, and the utility to adopt a technology is decomposed into two components:

$$
U = a + b x\tag{1}
$$

where a is the network independent utility, b is the bene<sup>fi</sup>t coef<sup>fi</sup>cient, x is the installed base, and, therefore, bx is the network-generated bene<sup>fi</sup>t [23,32]. As shown in Eq. (1), in the presence of network effects, an increased size of a network will bene<sup>fi</sup>t incumbent users and becomes more attractive to potential adopters as well. Therefore, when potential adopters consider possible adoption of home computers, a larger installed base of current adopters are more attractive to them since they might potentially bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a larger market or network of incumbent users, which will further facilitate the exchange of <sup>fi</sup>les and tips and may encourage software developers to devote resources towards developing more and better software. One of the important properties of the economic network effects is that, as evident in Eq. (1), each adopter in the installed base x, no matter when the adoption occurred, will have the same impact on a future adoption. In other words, if we could divide the current adopters into “recent adopters” and “distant adopters” based on the timing of their adoptions—recent adopters are those who adopted the technology in a more recent time, while distant adopters did so in an earlier time—both groups of adopters will generate the same amount of utility for potential adopters. Thus, from the economics perspective of network effects, we expect:

Hypothesis H1a. Recent and distant computer adoptions will have an equal positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on future adoption of household computers.

The economics perspective provides simple explanations for the adoption behavior by treating each individual as an abstract utilitymaximizing agent [19,33]. However, this approach largely ignores the social contexts of the interactions among the different adopters. Therefore, there is a need to complement the economics approach and study the adoption behavior by considering different types of adopters (e.g., recent vs. distant adopters) and the different social environment (e.g., different channels) where social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning can take place.

Network effects, when viewed from social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning perspective, provide an alternative explanation and lead to a conclusion that is very different from Hypothesis H1a. Social theory argues that potential adopters contemplating future adoption make their adoption decisions primarily based on information and knowledge obtained from other users in the network [1]. Interacting with current adopters, potential adopters gain access to new information, which is used to update their belief about the technology [49]. Through this process, potential adopters can vicariously learn from the adopters and adjust their assessment of the technology accordingly [17]. Viewed from this angle, recent and distant adopters may actually affect potential adopters quite differently. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we argue that, if social learning is the primary mechanism for household computer adoption, recent adoptions will have a stronger impact on future adoption than distant adoptions. First, for social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning to occur, the information and knowledge received has to be perceived as powerful or valuable. Often, a more recent adoption signals that the technology is increasingly gaining acceptance and legitimacy [42], and a recent adoption reveals the latest information about the technology [8]. Therefore, recent adoptions are more salient and relevant, and have a stronger impact on potential adopters [12,22,46,65]. Second, potential adopters are likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd recent adopters to be more similar to themselves in socio-economic status, when compared to distant adopters [60]. This similarity could further strengthen the impact of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning from recent adopters [47]. Therefore, from the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning perspective, we would expect the following:

Hypothesis H1b. Recent adoptions will have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence than distant adoptions on future adoption of household computers.

## 2.2. Diffusion channels

At the heart of the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning process of technology adoption is the modeling and imitation by potential adopters of their near peers [49]. Individuals decide whether to adopt a technology based on the communicated experience of others who have already adopted the technology [48]. The places that facilitate these communication and interaction processes are the diffusion channels [45,49]. A study of diffusion channels may reveal interesting social contexts within which network effects actually take place. For example, Lesnick [38] <sup>fi</sup>nds that the diffusion of photovoltaics in the Dominican Republic was largely facilitated by an organized association. Coleman et al. [20] <sup>fi</sup>nd that diffusion of a new medical drug among physicians is strongly associated with whether the doctor is af<sup>fi</sup>liated with a hospital as a regular staff member and whether the doctor frequently attends hospital staff meetings. Studies have also demonstrated that network of professional organizations is the single most in<sup>fl</sup>uential variable in determining adoption of new technologies by <sup>fi</sup>rms [18,51].

Diffusion channels can be formal or informal, of different qualities, and may come in a variety of forms [49]. Among them, two channels are the most common: workplaces and schools, both of which are important conduits for social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning to take place [26]. Household members who use computers at these two places, in fact, play the role of change agents, who help facilitate and champion the adoption of home computers [49]. Although computers have been used by companies since the 1950s [7], household computer use is a much more recent phenomenon. This is analogous to the adoption of cellular telephones—they were <sup>fi</sup>rst adopted at workplaces in the early 1980s before penetrating into the mass consumer market [49]. Therefore, we expect a workplace to be an important venue where employees can learn to use computers. As workers use computers at their workplaces, they usually acquire computer knowledge and skills and become more likely to adopt computers for home use. Another primary source for acquiring computer knowledge is schools. Schools usually have a high level of computer adoption and, when household members go to schools, they learn important computer skills and often use computers to <sup>fi</sup>nish their school-related tasks such as assignments. These people are then more likely to desire computers at home. Thus, we have:

Hypothesis H2a. Household computer adoption is more likely if the household members use computers at work.

Hypothesis H2b. Household computer adoption is more likely if the household members use computers at school.

## 2.3. Adoption rate and diffusion channels

Technology adoption and diffusion take on several stages over time [49]. At different stages of technology adoption, a diffusion channel would have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different impact in providing information and learning opportunities to potential adopters [60]. Thus, it is important to examine not just the overall effects of diffusion channels, but their effects at different adoption rates, as well. A high installed base can create a dense network of existing adopters that facilitates information dissemination and norm creation [19], so the effects of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning may play a more important role. On the other hand, diffusion channels serve as conduits of learning at the dyadic level [49], which could be more important when adoption rate is relatively low.

Here, the question is whether an individual is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced relatively more from the diffusion channels when she is embedded in a community with a low level of adoption rate. We argue that channels are particularly important when a technology is <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced (i.e., when the installed base is small). From the learning perspective, when the adoption level is low, individuals usually have little resource in terms of help and suggestions in using a new technology. Thus, personal experience from various channels comprises the primary resources for getting the related knowledge [29]. This is similar to the <sup>fi</sup>nding that mass communication media substitute for interpersonal communication when inducing behavior changes [59]. The above argument is more evident when we examine the communication process within a local network: for a given individual, the number of colleagues at work with whom one could communicate should remain the same. Therefore, when the installed base for a technology is low, the relative channel effect derived from learning opportunities with work associates is more important compared to the case when the installed base is high. Similar argument applies to the second channel, computer use at school. The effect of the channels, however, should attenuate gradually as the adoption rate grows and the technology becomes well-known. As more people adopt a technology, potential adopters can receive an increasing amount of social information or in<sup>fl</sup>uence, which subsequently increases their chance of adopting the same technology [35,49]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis H3a. The impact of computer use at work on home computer adoption is stronger when the adoption rate is lower.

Hypothesis H3b. The impact of computer use at school on home computer adoption is stronger when the adoption rate is lower.

## 2.4. First-time adoption versus repeat purchases

In a given year, computer purchases can be classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into two categories: (i) <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption by households without any computers, and (ii) repeat purchases by those who already have computer(s) at home. So far, our analysis has been restricted to <sup>fi</sup>rsttime adoption only. We next investigate the differential effects of diffusion channels on <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption and repeat purchases. This would enable us to compare the relative impact of the two channels on potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers versus existing owners.

Brown and Venkatesh [14] propose that early and late home computer adopters might react differently to different factors such as hedonic outcome, utilitarian outcome, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence, and various barriers. We expect that diffusion channels would also work differently for <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers when compared to existing owners. From the social in<sup>fl</sup>uence perspective, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence from others on behavioral intention attenuates after individuals have gained direct experience with the technology, as their attention shifts more toward outcome expectations rather than peer expectation [11,31]. One could reach the same conclusion from the social learning perspective of network effects, as well. Knowledge and expertise learnt through various channels represent second-hand information, and they are particularly useful for potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers. However, as individuals gain more experience from their prior consumption, they increasingly rely on their own experience, and the impact of diffusion channels diminishes [30,54,61]. Thus, diffusion channels are more helpful to potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers in acquiring computer skills and knowledge, reducing skepticism, and facilitating adoption. This leads to the following two hypotheses:

Hypothesis H4a. The impact of computer use at work is stronger on <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers.

Hypothesis H4b. The impact of computer use at school is stronger on <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers.

## 3. Data and methodology

## 3.1. Data

The data used in this study are primarily obtained from the Current Population Surveys (CPS) administered by the U.S. Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics. These surveys have been conducted monthly for over 50 years and constitute the primary source of of<sup>fi</sup>cial government statistics on the labor force characteristics of the U.S. population. They include basic monthly surveys and supplementary surveys on various topics. A basic monthly survey gathers direct measurement of employment status for a random sample of the U.S. population. They provide a comprehensive source of monthly estimates of total employment, wage and income levels, number of working hours, and information on occupations and industries. This rich dataset has been widely used by researchers, policymakers, and legislators for evaluating and planning government programs [3,39].

The supplementary surveys of the CPS are designed to meet a variety of additional requirements and are conducted as needed to gather in-depth information on speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspects of the labor force. In October 1989, October 1993, October 1997, September 2001, and

October 2003, <sup>fi</sup>ve supplementary surveys related to computer and Internet use were conducted. They include questions on computer ownership and general use of computers and the Internet. These surveys further identify the householder in each family. From these surveys, we obtain the data used in this study. The dataset contains information about 96,821 households in total; 56,425 of them did not own any computers at the beginning of the survey years and are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as potential new adopters or potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers.

There are two other sources of data used in this study. We use the consumer price index (CPI) from the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) to de<sup>fl</sup>ate the household incomes.<sup>4</sup> Also, we use the computer price index from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) to control for declining computer prices.<sup>5</sup>

## 3.3. Estimation models

We use a logit model [27] to examine household computer adoption. As discussed earlier, our analysis consists of two parts: <sup>fi</sup>rsttime adoption and repeat purchases. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the analysis, to avoid causal ambiguity, we restrict the sample to those households that did not have a computer prior to the survey year, i.e., $F _ { i j } = 1$ . The probability that household i decides to purchase or adopt a new home computer in the survey year is expressed as:

$$
\operatorname{prob} \left[ Y _ {i j} = 1 \mid \mathbf {X} _ {i j} \right] = \operatorname{prob} \left[ \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha C _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j} > 0 \right],\tag{2}
$$

where $Y _ { i j } = 1$ indicates that household i in MSA j adopts home computers in the survey year, $C _ { j }$ is the percent of computer ownership in MSA j, and $\mathbf { X } _ { i j }$ is a vector of variables such as computer use at work and school, income, computer price, education, race, and the presence of school-going kids; $\varepsilon _ { i j }$ is the error term. For the sake of notational convenience, we rewrite Eq. (2), as well as all the subsequent models, using latent variables [27]:

$$
Y _ {i j} ^ {*} = \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha C _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}; Y _ {i j} = 1 \left[ Y _ {i j} ^ {*} > 0 \right],
$$

where $Y _ { i j } ^ { * }$ is the latent variable for $Y _ { i j \cdot }$

Potentially, there are other MSA-level variables that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the adoption of home computers [41]. These variables include density of computer stores, use of marketing campaigns, and the presence of high-tech industries or companies. Estimation results without controlling for these confounding factors tend to be biased [27]. Since these MSA-level variables are not available from our dataset, we estimate a group effects model:

$$
Y _ {i j} ^ {*} = \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha C _ {j} + u _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}; Y _ {i j} = 1 \left[ Y _ {i j} ^ {*} > 0 \right],\tag{3}
$$

where $u _ { j }$ stands for unobserved MSA-level variables in MSA j affecting home computer adoption. In Eq. (3), a positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant α would indicate the existence of network effects. To test Hypotheses H1a and H1b, we estimate a similar model, but instead of using $C _ { j } ,$ we use the percentage of recent ownership (CR ) and the percentage of distant ownership (CD ):

$$
Y _ {i j} ^ {*} = \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha_ {1} C R _ {j} + \alpha_ {2} C D _ {j} + u _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}; Y _ {i j} = 1 \left[ Y _ {i j} ^ {*} > 0 \right].\tag{4}
$$

In $\operatorname { E q . }$ (4), if α and α are both positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, and they are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different, then Hypothesis H1a will be supported. If $\alpha _ { 1 }$ and $\alpha _ { 2 }$ are positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but $\alpha _ { 1 }$ is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger than $\alpha _ { 2 } ,$ then Hypothesis H1b will be supported. In addition, the results from Eqs. (3) and (4) are used to test Hypotheses H2a and H2b since vector $\mathbf { X } _ { i j }$ contains the two diffusion channel variables, $W _ { i j }$ and $S _ { i j } .$ We test the following model for H3a and H3b:

$$
Y _ {i j} ^ {*} = \gamma_ {1} C _ {j} \cdot W _ {i j} + \gamma_ {2} C _ {j} \cdot S _ {i j} + \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha C _ {j} + u _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}; Y _ {i j} = 1 \left[ Y _ {i j} ^ {*} > 0 \right],\tag{5}
$$

where $C _ { j } \cdot W _ { i j }$ and $C _ { j } \cdot S _ { i j }$ are the interaction terms. Negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $\gamma _ { 1 }$ and $\gamma _ { 2 }$ would provide support for Hypotheses H3a and H3b, respectively.

In the second part of our analysis, we compare the magnitude of the channel effect on <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption versus repeat purchases, and test Hypotheses H4a and H4b using the following model:

$$
D _ {i j} ^ {*} = \gamma_ {3} F _ {i j} + \gamma_ {4} F _ {i j} \cdot W _ {i j} + \gamma_ {5} F _ {i j} \cdot S _ {i j} + \mathbf {X} _ {i j} ^ {\prime} \boldsymbol {\beta} + \alpha C _ {j} + u _ {j} + \varepsilon_ {i}; D _ {i j} = 1 [ D _ {i j} ^ {*} > 0 ],\tag{6}
$$

where $F _ { i j } \cdot W _ { i j }$ and $F _ { i j } \cdot S _ { i j }$ are the interaction terms. Positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant values of $\gamma _ { 4 }$ and $\gamma _ { 5 }$ would provide support for these two hypotheses. As discussed in Section 3.2, $D _ { i j }$ represents <sup>fi</sup>rst-time as well as repeat purchases of computers during the survey year. Hence, the sample for Eq. (6) consists of all the households (96,821) included in the dataset, regardless of whether they owned computers before the survey years. All the models are estimated using Stata 9.2.

## 5. Discussions

Computer adoption, along with technology adoption at large, has been a major research topic in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld for the last two decades, and one of the most robust <sup>fi</sup>ndings is that of network effects on adoption and diffusion of technologies [34]. However, the underlying mechanisms of network effects for technology adoption are often not clear [26,55]. In addition, few have examined the speci<sup>fi</sup>c effects of diffusion channels on technology adoption. This paper examines the mechanisms of network effects and identi<sup>fi</sup>es diffusion channels on the household computer adoption in the U.S. It contributes to technology adoption literature and provides important policy and managerial implications.

## 5.1. Contributions

This paper makes several contributions to the literature. First, we propose an approach that can potentially detect the mechanisms underlying the adoption of home computers. As mentioned earlier, a major challenge towards understanding network effects is often our inability to distinguish the underlying mechanisms; it is unclear whether the network effects manifest themselves from an economic perspective or from a social perspective [26,55]. The empirical strategy typically adopted in prior studies is to include a single variable of installed base of current adopters and test its impact on future adoption. In this study, we argue that although both economic network effects and social learning lead to the same eventual result, they differ in a subtle, but important way—the timing of their impact.

Economic network effects derive utility from all current adopters irrespective of the timing of their adoption. In contrast, social learning from recent adopters is stronger than that from distant adopters. Correspondingly, we propose a method to detect the existence and magnitude of these two mechanisms. Our results indicate that, in the case of household computer adoption, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning are much more powerful than economic network effects.

Second, we study two important diffusion channels for home computer adoption: workplaces and schools. While considerable studies have been conducted on network effects, few have examined how network effects take place, i.e., the speci<sup>fi</sup>c channels through which technologies diffuse. Our study not only identi<sup>fi</sup>es the two key diffusion channels, but also shows that both diffusion channels play a more important role on computer adoption at early stages, when the level of computer adoption is low and the installed base is small. Once the adoption process gathers momentum, however, it becomes selfsustainable, and the effects of the two diffusion channels diminish. Therefore, it is important that we examine network effects and diffusion channels together, rather than separately, in order to understand their roles in the technology adoption life cycle.

Third, our study compares <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption with repeat purchases. While both are related to technology adoption, a <sup>fi</sup>rsttime computer adoption is particularly important since it represents the breadth of technology adoption and a possible narrowing of the digital divide. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that diffusion channels, such as workplaces and schools, have a stronger impact on potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers. Workplaces have the closest tie with households' economic wellbeing. Prior studies have shown that computer use at work is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly associated with a higher wage level [7]. As a household member uses computer at work, she not only gains valuable computer knowledge and skills, but may also become economically more prepared (with possible wage increases) to obtain a computer at home. The dynamics of computer use at work, quality of job, and household computer adoption is an interesting future research direction.

Finally, our work contributes to the existing models on household computer adoption. Over time, a number of theories have been developed in IS to explain individual technology adoption and acceptance, such as the theory of reasoned action, TRA [4], and technology acceptance model, TAM [21]. Particularly relevant to our study is the model of adoption of technology in households, MATH [15,60], which identi<sup>fi</sup>es attitudinal beliefs as major determinants of purchase behavior, and <sup>fi</sup>nds that in<sup>fl</sup>uences from friends and family members signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect adoption [60]. However, one limitation of these studies is that the results cannot be readily applied to social programs or marketing campaigns that aim to bridge the digital divide, since constructs based on psychological dimensions of consumers are often not observable. In comparison, our model is based on commonly observable consumer and household characteristics, and the results are more applicable to practice.

## 5.2. Policy and managerial implications

Several useful insights can be obtained from our study. Prior research suggests that policy implications of economic network effects and social learning are largely different. For example, economic network effects would justify government subsidy towards those that are most responsive or that generate the largest bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, while social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning can potentially be sustained by private compensation between the two parties involved in the learning process [26]. However, because it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to distinguish the effects of these two mechanisms, practitioners are often not sure about which policies to adopt to bridge the digital divide. Probably the most important implication from our <sup>fi</sup>nding is that, for home computer adoption, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and learning are much more powerful than economic network effects, with a clear policy implication towards the design of social programs that can more effectively facilitate social learning and help the diffusion of computer skills and knowledge.

Network effects are powerful economic and social forces in technology adoption. While network effects in general facilitate technology adoption, we should be cautious in deploying them for the following reasons. As a large percentage of people and households adopt computer technology in certain communities and population groups, network effects would add an extra momentum in the adoption process in those communities and population groups. As shown in Table 2, there are many aspects of the digital divide, along the lines of income, education, and race. There are possible disparities in technology adoption in urban versus rural areas as well. Thus, network effects could leave some under-privileged communities or population groups further behind, thereby potentially widening the digital divide.

Our results reinforce the well-accepted notion that diffusion channels have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect on computer adoption and should be one of the primary targets for government initiatives to reduce the digital divide. More importantly, our results reveal that the impact of a diffusion channel is not uniform; it is stronger when the overall adoption rate is low. Therefore, public and private programs that target communities and population groups with a low computer adoption rate may be more effective in addressing this issue. An effective way of utilizing the diffusion channels would be to provide more education and employment opportunities. This point of view is further supported by our <sup>fi</sup>nding that the channels are more effective for <sup>fi</sup>rst-time adoption, which represents the breadth of technology adoption and a reduction of the digital divide. This <sup>fi</sup>nding is particularly useful in designing campaigns to close the digital divide. Such a campaign is likely to be more effective if it can potentially identify and target the potential <sup>fi</sup>rst-time buyers. When it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to do so, targeting communities or groups with lower adoption rates is probably a viable alternative.

For many businesses, such as e-commerce companies and computer hardware and software vendors, the success of their business relies on the penetration of computer technology into a large population base. While this, by itself, may not provide suf<sup>fi</sup>cient incentive for a business to attempt to close the digital divide, carefully planned government programs and incentives can complement this and induce businesses to lower the cost for computer adoption and create broader channels to promote their products. Our empirical results provide further evidence that supporting technology programs for schools could be a mutually bene<sup>fi</sup>cial proposition for many businesses as well as communities. This could help more people acquire computer skills and, at the same time, enable companies to develop markets for their products. Meanwhile, governments may have to provide incentives for businesses to proactively create jobs that require the use of computers in order to target markets with low adoption. As businesses demand employees with computer skills, the demand for technology products would grow as well. Governments may also work with technology companies to develop innovative technology solutions that enable traditional industries, such as agriculture and mining, to adopt new technologies. As computer use becomes pervasive in more businesses, the demand for computers at the household level would grow more steadily.

## 5.3. Limitations and directions for future research

This study uses relatively well-measured socio-economic variables and focuses on social effects on household computer adoption. Although it departs from earlier technology adoption models such as MATH in using a different type of dataset (secondary data versus survey data) and applies a different methodology (logit model versus structured equation model), it complements extant literature. We believe that there are exciting possibilities for future research by integrating the two approaches.

Our study <sup>fi</sup>nds that network effects and diffusion channels are important in technology adoption. However, how those factors affect individual attitudes is not clear. Future research can study the effects of social in<sup>fl</sup>uences on various MATH belief constructs. For example, it would be interesting to investigate whether network effects elevate attitudinal beliefs toward personal computers and reinforce social norms. Since workplaces and schools are considered to be conduits for knowledge diffusion, one can examine whether those channels reduce the behavior barriers in technology adoption.

It is also possible to integrate psychological factors with rational consumer choice model such as the one we use in this study. While the purpose of rational choice models is to predict individual behavior by focusing on systematic, invariant aspects of choice behavior by using reasonably well-measured socio-economic data, theories in behavioral psychology aim to understand the nature of the decision elements and how they are established by deconstructing individual decision process [10]. Despite these differences, there is a growing common perspective that integrates the views from both economics and psychology [28,67]. Researchers have extended rational choice model by including attitudinal and other latent variables [6] or integrate economic model with psychological decision models [28,67].

## 6. Conclusions

Computer adoption is a rich area for IS research, and this paper examines the impact of network effects and diffusion channels on household computer adoption, with particular interest on the mechanisms underlying network effects and the contingencies of channel effects. Our study opens many areas for future research. First, as discussed in the previous section, integrating socio-economic and psychological models would be an interesting area worthy of exploration. Second, one could examine the effects of various factors on home computer adoption dynamically, by considering the effects of social in<sup>fl</sup>uence over time. Third, social networks involving friends and relatives are likely to play an important role in shaping an individual's attitude. Future studies could analyze those networks and utilize detailed social network measures to study their in<sup>fl</sup>uence.
