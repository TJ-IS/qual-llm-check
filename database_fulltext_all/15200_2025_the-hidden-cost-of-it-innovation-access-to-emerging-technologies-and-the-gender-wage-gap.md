# THE HIDDEN COST OF IT INNOVATION: ACCESS TO EMERGING TECHNOLOGIES AND THE GENDER WAGE GAP<sup>1</sup>

Prasanna B. Tambe The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {tambe@wharton.upenn.edu}

Tiantian Yang

The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {yangtt@wharton.upenn.edu}

Although an extensive information systems literature has explored the economic benefits of information technology (IT) investments, how these benefits are distributed between male and female IT workers has not been as closely examined. This paper bridges this gap by addressing two questions: (1) Do women and men have similar opportunities to acquire skills related to new IT innovations? (2) How do these differences affect the gender pay gap? We argue that women are underrepresented in roles that use emerging technologies due to two interrelated processes: demand-side labor market conditions and supply-side job sorting. By analyzing two independent datasets that provide insights into wages, career trajectories, and skill prerequisites for IT roles, we found that women are less likely to apply for jobs requiring expertise with emerging technologies. Such positions often require extended work hours, frequent job mobility, and geographic relocation—which can conflict with the family responsibilities typically assumed by women. Yet because these positions tend to offer higher wages, women’s underrepresentation in these roles exacerbates the gender pay gap in the IT sector. Our findings stress the importance of creating more flexible job structures and enhancing women’s access to emerging technology roles as critical for achieving gender equity in the IT industry.

Keywords: Women in IT, Information systems, IT investment, human capital investment, management of IT human resources

## Introduction

The role of information technology (IT) investments in driving economic growth and wealth creation has been extensively documented over the past half-century in the information systems (IS) literature (Atasoy et al., 2021; Chang & Gurbaxani, 2012; Tambe & Hitt, 2014; Zolas et al., 2020). While significant emphasis has been placed on the economic benefits to firms (profits) and consumers (surplus), the distribution of these benefits among the IT workers at the forefront of these innovations remains underexplored (Allen, 2001; Fichman & Kemerer, 1997; Mithas & Lucas, 2010; Tambe et al., 2020). As employees engage with cutting-edge technology, they gain valuable skills, potentially influencing wage structures and disparities. But inequitable access to these career-defining technologies could exacerbate pay gaps and wage imbalances (Ahuja, 2002; Trauth & Connolly, 2021; Trauth et al., 2009).

Our research explores gender disparities in access to emerging technologies and their effects on wages. Specifically, we ask: Do men and women have equitable opportunities to engage with emerging technologies? And how might disparities in access shape earnings for women versus men? Anchoring our investigation in the foundational literatures on IS and gender studies, we propose that women are underrepresented in roles linked to emerging technologies due to the interrelated processes of demand-side labor market conditions and supplyside job sorting. On the demand side, IT careers that involve emerging technologies are structured to require frequent skill upgrades and job mobility. The constant evolution of such roles necessitates ongoing skill upgrades, often leading to prolonged working hours (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Joseph et al., 2012). Maximizing the financial payoff of newly acquired IT skills requires frequent job shifts and relocation due to the agglomeration of frontier IT jobs. Those with mobility constraints might be disadvantaged when building a career hinging on these technologies. On the supply side, women may face greater familial constraints in pursuing their careers: Their primary childcare responsibilities and support of their spouses’ careers often limit their potential to pursue jobs demanding extensive work hours and requiring frequent relocations. Together, these conditions imply that fewer women will seek jobs involving emerging technologies due to an aversion to tasks intensifying work-family conflict arising from demanding work hours or geographic relocation that would disadvantage them in gaining relevant experience.

Further, we evaluate three pathways through which differences in access to emerging technologies might drive a gender pay disparity in the IT workforce: (1) interfirm mobility, (2) wage bargaining, and (3) firm-specific wage premiums. We argue that because much of the wage trajectory of IT employees requires capitalizing on the scarcity of new, rare technical skills in the labor market through bargaining and interfirm mobility (Joseph et al., 2015), women’s limited access to emerging technologies affects not only their current pay but also their mobility into higher-paying jobs and their ability to negotiate for higher pay with future employers.

We leveraged two distinct and detailed datasets to empirically examine these relationships. Our first dataset documents men’s and women’s applications to full-time tech positions over the last decade, alongside the corresponding skill prerequisites. The second dataset, sourced from a leading U.S. jobs board, provides insights into the wages and career trajectories of a cross-section of IT professionals. Collectively, these datasets enabled our analysis of both intrafirm and interfirm gender wage disparities rooted in differential technological exposure. The confluence of data on worker remuneration, gender, career progression, and access to diverse technologies enabled our contribution to this literature.

Our analyses reveal that women are less inclined than men to seek positions demanding proficiency in emerging technologies. This imbalance, our study shows, is largely driven by job characterizations that disproportionately associate emerging tech roles with the need for ongoing skill development and frequent geographic relocation, which often conflicts with women’s familial responsibilities. These findings remained consistent even after controlling for job title heterogeneity and employing matched samples of men and women. Furthermore, we found that women’s limited engagement with emerging technologies also constrains their interfirm mobility, impacting their negotiation abilities with potential employers and hindering their chances of securing higher pay. These disadvantages ultimately contribute to a pay gap for women in the IT sector. Consequently, the wage competition to attract workers with emerging technical skills disproportionately benefits men.

Our study’s key contribution demonstrates that gendered sorting across jobs leads to inequalities in exposure to emerging technologies, which drives pay disparities. Our findings contribute to the literature on IT and the STEM labor market and labor supply and offer substantive policy implications that are discussed at the end of the paper.

## Theoretical Background

Work is a foundational component of society, influencing individual identity, facilitating interpersonal connections, and determining placement in the socioeconomic system. Moreover, work creates a link between societal contributions and monetary rewards. Therefore, the fairness with which society rewards an individual’s work carries profound implications for equity (Goldin, 1990; Kalleberg & Dunn, 2016). From the worker’s perspective, pay equity reflects the ethical and moral standing of managerial conduct. Thus, organizations that reward employees fairly are more likely to create a culture that values and uses the talents of all wouldbe members (Aguilera et al., 2007; Cropanzano et al., 2007). Yet, despite the substantial social and economic benefits of pay equity, the gender pay gap—the empirical regularity with which women earn less than men with equivalent qualifications in the same domain—remains one of the most stubborn forms of social inequality (Altonji & Blank, 1999; Blau & Kahn, 2017; Goldin et al., 2017).

A large body of research in sociology, psychology, management, and labor economics has sought to understand and address the gender pay gap, identifying both demand-side and supply-side factors as explanations (Ahuja, 2002; Bertrand, 2011; Blank & Goldin, 1992). On the demand side, research has identified processes that perpetuate gender inequality, such as queuing and discrimination (England, 2010; Nelson & Bridges, 1999). These factors produce different career opportunities for men and women, causing vertical and horizontal gender differences in occupations (Langer et al., 2020; Trauth et al., 2009). Conversely, supplyside research has emphasized men’s and women’s divergent career decisions and self-selection into jobs, leading to disparate career outcomes (Charles, 2011; Correll, 2004; England, 2010; Niederle & Vesterlund, 2007; Reskin, 2003). For example, women might avoid male-dominated occupations due to a hostile or unfavorable cultural climate (Adya & Kaiser, 2005; Wright, 1997) or exposure to biased environments, unfavorable peer comparisons, or substandard pedagogy (Bapna et al., 2021; Fisher & Margolis, 2002).

Although these explanations are useful in addressing gender pay gaps in the broader labor market, they do not fully account for the salient contextual conditions of IT labor markets and how a gender pay gap could arise in this context. Workers in the IT sector face unique challenges imposed by the need for constant skill updating—much of it acquired on the job—that is required to support emerging technologies. As firms invest in new technologies, hiring and training decisions for the most advanced technologies can significantly affect workers’ earnings and pay growth. Workers who acquire skills through working with new technologies often gain the largest benefit for long-term wage growth and are most likely to transition to higher-paying jobs via interfirm mobility (Freedman, 2008; Joseph et al., 2012). However, prior research has not fully explored the impact of these emerging technologies on shaping IT wages. Questions remain about whether women are less likely to have access to emerging technologies and how limitations to access might contribute to their relatively lower pay in the IT sector.

## Defining Characteristics of the IT Labor Market

To identify the distinct role of emerging technologies in contributing to the gender pay gap, we start by characterizing the defining features of IT labor markets (Annabi & Lebovitz, 2018; Trauth & Connolly, 2021). We draw on the IS literature and other reference disciplines to depict two key features of the IT labor market: (1) rapid skill updating and (2) frequent labor reallocation.

The first defining feature of the IT labor market is the rapid waxing and waning of skill demand (Bapna et al., 2013; Langer et al., 2020; Tambe & Hitt, 2014). A constantly changing technological landscape impacts the labor market in two ways. First, the accelerated development and transformation of technologies prompts employers to compete aggressively for high-tech talent. Firms often view skilled IT personnel as a key driver of performance and innovation. Consequently, workers’ ability to acquire new technological skills has become a significant factor in their IT career success (Barley & Kunda, 2006; Fitch & Tilley, 2020; Hutchens & Grace-Martin, 2006; O’Mahony & Bechky, 2006). Second, IT employees carry greater on-the-job responsibility to maintain and enhance their skills relative to those in other occupations. Thus, IT workers are likely to prefer employers that offer opportunities to update their skills by working with new technologies (Tambe et al., 2020). Workers capture significant value when firms invest in new IT systems, forming new and transferable human capital (Atasoy et al., 2021). From a human capital perspective, IT skill acquisition enables workers to utilize these skills in subsequent career stages and across various employers. Additionally, acquiring cuttingedge IT skills enables workers to perform existing tasks in new ways and expands the variety of tasks they can perform (Iyengar et al., 2015). Hence, firms that invest in emerging technologies attract more workers and hold a competitive edge in the IT labor market (Atasoy et al., 2021; Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). In short, access to emerging technologies and employers’ willingness to invest in workers’ skills are likely significant drivers of wage growth because acquiring new IT skills is vital for IT career success.

Another defining feature of the IT labor market is the rapid pace of labor reallocation. High-skill jobs in the IT sector tend to have narrowly defined work content, providing limited exposure to the broader organization (Joseph et al., 2012). Inhouse career paths are often limited, and despite the broad skill requirements, the task content of job titles in the IT labor market exhibits little variation (Cappelli, 2000). Furthermore, compensation for these jobs does not rely heavily on training or preparation for future roles (Reich & Kaarst-Brown, 1999). Consequently, relative to recruitment for other high-skill occupations, HR managers filling IT roles devote more time to sourcing talent rather than training or development, leading to the characterization of IT careers as boundaryless (Arthur & Rousseau, 1996; Joseph et al., 2012): interfirm labor mobility is a prevalent and critical feature of career progression in the IT sector (Freedman, 2008). For employers, an adaptable IT workforce is beneficial in addressing the need to adapt continuously to a shifting technological landscape, which necessitates constant workforce reskilling. For workers, job transitions in this industry are often linked to productivity benefits and wage growth (Freedman, 2008; Joseph et al., 2015). Thus, differences in career progression through job transitions, especially in the external labor market, might account for more wage disparity in IT work than in professions where internal ladders are critical for career development (Ang & Slaughter, 2004).

## The Gender Pay Gap Among IT Workers: The Role of Emerging Technologies

Because of these two defining features of the IT labor market—rapid skill updates and frequent labor reallocation— workers’ experience with emerging technologies is a key driver of wage growth for IT workers. However, women may face disadvantages when gaining on-the-job experience with emerging technologies.

The IS literature and gender research have suggested that conditions that intensify work-family conflict can deter women from jobs that offer opportunities to engage with emerging technologies. Work-family conflict, a notable source of jobrelated stress, arises when individuals foresee or face clashes between work and family responsibilities, pushing them toward careers that attenuate this conflict (Higgins et al., 1992; Nomaguchi & Johnson, 2009). Theoretical models of workfamily conflict highlight its roots in the competing requirements of work and family roles, negatively impacting both domains (Edwards & Rothbard, 2000; Greenhaus & Beutell, 1985). Although job demands that heighten this conflict affect both men and women, women often anticipate and/or experience this conflict more intensely because of their roles as caregivers and spousal supporters (Ahuja, 2002; Higgins et al., 1992). Specifically, for IT workers, the IS literature has suggested two structural conditions that conflict with women’s familial responsibilities, inhibiting their career progression in the IT sector: the need for longer working hours and the need for frequent geographic relocation.

## Demanding Work Hours

Although IT jobs generally impose stringent work conditions, roles involving emerging technologies are particularly demanding. These roles necessitate rapid skill updating, often leading to extended work hours, on-call duties, and regular travel (Ahuja, 2002; Eccles, 1994; Seymour & Hewitt, 1997; Vitores & Gil-Juárez, 2016). The fast pace of technological advancements in these fields require continuous learning and adaptation, making the burden of skill updating significantly heavier. This need for constant upskilling may clash with women’s family commitments, causing many to avoid such roles. For many women, their early professional years align with pivotal personal events, such as marriage and starting a family (Frome et al., 2006). During these life phases, women experience the multidimensional challenge of juggling career ambitions, household responsibilities, and childcare (Hochschild & Machung, 2012).

Additionally, despite the prevalence of dual-earner families, organizational structures seldom provide sufficient flexibility for both members of dual-career marriages (Glass, 2004; Higgins et al., 1992). As a result, couples with children or other care responsibilities tend to maximize family utility through specialization, with the woman prioritizing caregiving and the man prioritizing career pursuit (Goldin, 2021). Thus, a lack of work flexibility might disproportionately affect women, who often make career sacrifices to assume more domestic duties. In the IT sector, this incompatibility between demanding work hours and care responsibilities means that women will be less likely than men to pursue the most time-demanding jobs, such as those requiring longer work hours. Because excessive work hours are particularly pertinent to jobs requiring emerging technical skills, such jobs are particularly incompatible with the disproportionate family responsibilities that women often assume.

## Frequent Geographic Relocation

The IT career structure favors individuals who can leverage their skills through frequent job transitions across firms (Ahuja, 2002; Joseph et al., 2012). However, this frequent labor reallocation is especially critical for roles in emerging technologies due to the rapid pace of innovation and the need for continual skill acquisition. Technological innovation— applying new knowledge to production—predominantly happens in urban areas, where knowledge, skills, and access to production and consumption markets are concentrated. Lucas (1988) argued that cities serve as growth engines because they aggregate knowledge and skills alongside production and consumption, providing superior access to the factors and markets essential for adapting new technologies. Supporting this viewpoint, studies have found that workers engaged in novel tasks are more commonly found in cities initially dense in college graduates and industry variety (Lin, 2011). This pattern is consistent with the benefits derived from the geographic concentration of resources and markets that facilitate technological adaptation (Acemoglu, 2009; Autor et al., 2024; Lin, 2011). In the IT sector, Tambe (2014) demonstrated that employment involving newer, emerging technologies is largely concentrated in labor markets where IT investments by a sizable number of firms have cultivated a group of workers skilled in these new technologies.

Such geographical relocation is often at odds with constraints faced by women in dual-career marriages. For example, women are less flexible in choosing work locations (Rivera, 2017; Sorenson & Dahl, 2016), and recent research has found that the relaxation of relocation requirements ushered in by COVID-era remote work options increased the number of women applying for technical jobs (Hsu & Tambe, 2024). Nonetheless, when relocation is necessary to increase pay, women are more likely than men to forgo lucrative job opportunities to support their spouse’s career (Cha & Weeden, 2014; Duxbury et al., 1992). Specifically, studies have shown that to balance family needs, women often prioritize family stability over higher salaries (Fanning Madden, 1981; Hanson & Johnston, 1985; Le Barbanchon et al., 2020) and might opt for lesser-paying roles or avoid relocation to avoid disrupting their spouse’s career (Mason et al., 2009; Rivera, 2017).

The constraints faced by women in dual-career marriages make it particularly challenging for female IT workers to pursue jobs involving emerging technologies. The rapid pace of technological advancement means that workers need to frequently transition between jobs, projects, and even companies to stay relevant and competitive. Furthermore, this mobility is often concentrated in urban areas where technological ecosystems thrive, further necessitating geographic relocation. Women, who often bear the brunt of family caregiving responsibilities, may find it difficult to relocate frequently without disrupting their spouse’s career or their children’s stability. Consequently, women in the IT sector might choose to avoid roles in emerging technologies, anticipating the difficulty of managing frequent relocations and the resultant family disruptions.

In sum, the structural conditions leading to competing career and family demands may deter women from pursuing roles involving emerging technologies. We present this argument as the foundation for the following hypothesis:

H1: Women are less likely than men to pursue positions in the IT workforce that involve emerging technologies.

## Key Pathways: Interfirm Mobility, Wage Bargaining, and Firm-Specific Premiums

We delineate three specific pathways through which limited experience with emerging technologies might contribute to lower pay in IT occupations in the short and long term.

## Interfirm Mobility

Emerging IT skills significantly impact career trajectories in the tech sector by shaping interfirm mobility (Zucker et al., 1998). Tech professionals often seek roles that enable them to develop new technical skills because these competencies facilitate career advancement across organizations (Freedman, 2008). For instance, to achieve wage growth in the tech industry, workers typically prioritize early skill development, complemented by subsequent strategic job transitions. Companies place a premium on employees who are well-versed in the newest IT systems because they recognize the value of hands-on experience—particularly in the early stages of technology adoption when the supply of complementary skills is scarce. Skills developed in one company are frequently transferred to another via employee mobility (Tambe & Hitt,

2014). Firms unable to provide internal training might try to lure talent from rivals by offering attractive compensation packages. This competitive dynamic increases salaries for workers who are adept in the latest information technologies.

## Bargaining with Future Employers

Upon receiving job offers, an individual’s potential to optimize their earnings from IT skills largely depends on negotiation (Babcock & Laschever, 2003; Bowles et al., 2007; Dannals et al., 2021). Negotiation is particularly effective in increasing compensation in specialized fields such as IT (Engel et al., 2023). In the IT labor market, the rapidly evolving landscape of emerging technologies and companies’ heavy reliance on those technologies for innovations and productivity mean that workers with these scarce, advanced skills are highly valued by employers (Fanning Madden, 1981; Hanson & Johnston, 1985). Possessing advanced IT skills enhances bargaining power, leading to greater salary advancement when transitioning between firms. Consequently, female IT workers’ limited access to advanced IT skills can limit their ability to negotiate with potential employers.

## Firm-Specific Premiums

Firms differ substantially in their ability to invest in emerging technologies, and access to these systems has become a key differentiator driving productivity and innovation (Ang & Slaughter, 2004; Tambe et al., 2020). Adopting firms often pay competitive salaries to attract skilled labor. Firms offering more opportunities for learning emerging technologies might also pay wage premiums, and such firm-specific pay premiums (given their superior financial positions) might contribute to higher pay for those who use these technologies (Card et al., 2016). ## Data

## Overview

To analyze the relationship between emerging technologies, gender, and pay, we analyzed datasets from two job boards. The first captures applications sent to technical job listings posted by high-tech startups from 2015-2022, which enabled an analysis of how men and women differ in their interest in jobs with different characteristics. The second dataset is from 2010 and includes employment histories from a sample of U.S.-based IT workers employed primarily at large firms. This dataset contains information on their employers, education, experience, gender, most recent wage, and target wage, which we connected with measures of whether they were using an emerging technology at their most recent job. These two data sources were collected at different times from job boards that serve different U.S. employers, so they captured relationships for IT workers facing different labor market conditions at different times. However, the analysis of two distinct datasets provides evidence that the trends we document generalize beyond a single wave of investment and beyond a small set of employers. Details of these data sources are provided in the following sections.

## IT Job Applications

To study job applications for technology-related roles, we used data provided by a job portal capturing labor market activity for U.S. high-tech startups. Employers on this platform range from companies with only one or two employees to larger startups with thousands of employees. The platform is a destination for employers seeking to hire talent by posting job openings. It captures fields such as location, title, salary, and skills sought for these openings, as well as gender, location, and other information. Therefore, we could observe the gender composition of the applicant pool for a large sample of technical jobs that are heterogeneous in their technology skill demands.

We analyzed job listings that appeared in this dataset in 2015-2022. We restricted the analysis to full-time jobs in the

U.S. that fall into the “Information Technology” job category. In this sample, the most common jobs are Software Engineer and Full-Stack Engineer.<sup>2</sup> The sample we analyzed included applications submitted to job openings posted by thousands of high-tech startups.

## IT Employment and Wages

Our second dataset tracks wages and movements within and across firms for a sample of IT workers, allowing us to analyze how access to emerging technologies is related to mobility and wages. This type of measurement is not possible with common employer-employee matched datasets, such as the Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) data compiled by the U.S. Census Bureau, because they do not capture the technologies workers use.<sup>3</sup> We obtained these employment histories from one of the largest internet job boards focused on the U.S. labor market at the time of data collection.<sup>4</sup> Millions of participants have used this job board, although our analysis only included about 5,000 workers in IT occupations<sup>5</sup> who reported the technologies they use in their job descriptions and posted to this jobs board in the years shortly before and after the dot-com bust (1997-2005). We further limited the sample to public firms to attach it to external databases with industry information. In addition to the information entered during a job search, the website contains employment history data for each participant. The final database on careers includes each worker’s occupation, location, education, experience, gender, and employment history.

These job seekers likely differ from the broader IT workforce along some dimensions.<sup>6</sup> Workers on this jobs board are more focused on external labor market opportunities than the average worker. About 22% of the sample is female—a lower figure than the Bureau of Labor Statistics estimate of 25% to 26% for the percentage of female workers in computing professions.<sup>7,8</sup> This difference could arise from the fact that the sample is drawn from a job-search site. If men are more active on job-search sites, female IT workers will be underrepresented in samples drawn from such sites.<sup>9</sup>

Workers on this site self-report their wages and the target wages they expect from future employers. We cannot verify that wages were truthfully reported, although this concern applies to most wage data used in the literature, including the census and other data collected through administrative sources (Blau & Kahn, 2017; Budig & England, 2001; Ruppert et al., 2009). One concern with an evaluation of gender wage comparisons using self-reported wage data is that men, in particular, might inflate self-reported wages (Erat & Gneezy, 2011). This could be especially important on job search platforms if workers have incentives to falsify wages with an expectation of higher wage offers from prospective employers. Such a difference could bias gender wage gap estimates because the data would indicate that men earn higher wages than women of comparable skill, even in the absence of a genuine wage difference. Such reporting errors in the dependent variable can be challenging to uncover because administrative data with information on gender, including those used in much of the prior work in this area, are also self-reported (e.g., Blau & Kahn, 2017), so these biases could also persist there.<sup>10</sup> However, in the analysis, we illustrate that the gender wage gap estimated using our data is smaller than that estimated when using the Current Population Survey wage data, indicating that any systematic wage-reporting errors by men are not significantly worse than in administrative data.<sup>11</sup>

Target wages (i.e., wages sought from prospective employers) exist only in workers’ minds and thus cannot be verified. Methods commonly used to elicit data on target wages, such as surveys, suffer from known measurement problems. Workers can report aspirational wages, misinterpret the question, or inflate their wages to justify setting a higher target wage. In this dataset, however, the market context of the job board presents an advantage because there are repercussions if future employers discover that a candidate has misreported their wages. Moreover, the intention of eliciting target wages is to provide employers with a sense of which candidates fall in the wage range they plan to offer. In a market context, employers might discount candidates who set their target wages too high, and candidates who set their target wages too low could incur a penalty because this figure is the first step in the bargaining process. Therefore, the reporting of these target wages in a market context increases the likelihood that these targets are reported accurately and is one advantage over a setting where workers are asked to provide these figures in a hypothetical context.

## Identifying Emerging Technologies

On-the-job measurement of technology usage is rare in large samples. Firm-level proxy measures are common in large empirical studies but have the disadvantage of obscuring worker-level activity. We are not aware, for either level of analysis, of standardized taxonomies that categorize technologies by whether they are new or “emerging.” For this study, across the two datasets, we used a single approach to identify whether a technology that appears in a job listing or employee resume is an emerging technology.

We classified a technology as emerging if it appeared for the first time in the 10-year period preceding the posting of the job listing or resume and if it experienced rapid growth (top 5% among skills) in that time, which is approximately consistent with industry analysts’ classifications of emerging technology (Appendix A). Because the technical skills needed for a job are explicitly listed in the postings, we identified a job listing as requiring an emerging technology if any such skill is listed in the job opening. In the job application data, this definition yields a list of approximately 90 technologies,<sup>12</sup> with 34% of listings using at least one emerging technology. The average intensity of emerging technology skills across all skills in all listings is lower (about 9%).

For the employment history data, we text-mined resumes to extract the technologies workers used at their current job. In our sample, we retained workers who listed at least one recognizable technical skill in their current job description, and we classified workers as using an emerging technology if the newest technology they listed in their job description met the qualifications described earlier (i.e., less than 10 years old and experienced rapid growth). In this sample, 15.6% of women and 17.4% of men worked with an emerging technology.<sup>13</sup> The difference between these figures and the higher figure in the job listing data (34%) could be attributable to employers listing more skills in openings than workers do in their employment descriptions. In Appendix A, we assess this definition of “emerging,” compare it with benchmarks from industry and the literature, and probe the sensitivity of our estimates to using different window lengths.<sup>14</sup>

## Assignment of Skills to Categories

Some of our supplementary analyses and control variables required the classification of skills in our database into other categories, such as being a “technical” skill or “database” skill. For this classification task, we assigned labels to the skill dataset using a large language model (ChatGPT-4).<sup>15</sup>

## Model Results

We next utilized these data to investigate the relationship between gender, technological maturity, and wages in a multivariate framework. First, we used the job application data to establish evidence of women’s limited uptake of emerging technologies. Second, we analyzed the employment history and wage data to substantiate the link between limited access to emerging technologies and lower pay, exploring three pathways: job mobility, wage bargaining, and firm-specific premiums.

## Gender Differences in Job Applications

We first explored whether listings containing emerging technologies attract less interest from women, holding other job characteristics constant. Table 2 reports results from a negative binomial regression with applications from women as the dependent variable (FEMALE):

$$
\begin{array}{r} F E M A L E _ {i} = E M E R _ {i} + T E C H _ {i} + \mathrm{Log(Apps)} _ {i} \\ + \mathrm{Log(Skills)} _ {i} + \gamma_ {i} + \epsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

EMER measures whether the job posting lists an emerging skill and i indexes the job listing. Control variables include whether the listing contains any technical skill (TECH), the logged number of applications for the job listed, the logged number of skills in the listing, and a vector of fixed effects that can include job title, year, city, and whether the job is remote. In all columns, estimates are clustered on the employer posting the job listing.

The estimates in Column 1 of Table 2 indicate that a job listing receives fewer female applications if it contains an emerging skill, after controlling for whether the job requires technical skills and other factors. The magnitude of the coefficient is -0.0611, which when converted to average marginal effects, implies almost two fewer female applicants (-1.91) when an emerging skill appears in a job listing, holding constant the total number of applications to the listing. This is a substantial effect economically, because the aggregate difference in means in numbers of female applicants between listings with and without emerging skills is 10.3 and the difference in medians is 4.

Column 2 adds fixed effects for job title and Column 3 adds additional fixed effects for whether the position allows remote work and for the city noted in the listing. The coefficient on EMER is negative and statistically significant across all models (H1), although the magnitude of the coefficient drops by one third after we add fixed effects for job title in Column 2, meaning that differences in jobs account for some but not all of this heterogeneity. The coefficient on TECH is positive in Column 1 but is no longer significantly different from zero after adding job title fixed effects in Column 2 which suggests that whether a job contained a technical skill did not affect the number of female applicants in this sample.

In Column 4, we added measures for the salary and equity offered with each position, which reduced the sample size because these data were not available for all listings. Adding these measures did not alter the magnitude or direction of the correlation between EMER and applications received from women. With these additional control variables, the implied average marginal effect of EMER is 1.35 fewer female applicants to a job listing.

Columns 5 and 6 further narrow the sample to control for heterogeneity among the diverse set of jobs in the sample. Column 5 limits the sample to listings with “Engineer” in the title, which includes titles ranging from Software Engineer to Director of Engineering. Column 6 further limits the sample to four common technical titles: “Senior Software Engineer,” “Software Engineer,” “Data Engineer,” and “Machine Learning Engineer.” Neither restriction removes the negative correlation between EMER and applications from women.

## Intercity Job Mobility

These findings document a robust, negative correlation between EMER and applications from women after controlling for job characteristics, time trends, and other factors. We argued earlier that one reason we might observe this pattern is that women could be less likely to seek roles requiring the use of these technologies because a financial payoff depends on periodic transitions requiring frequent job mobility and relocations. Therefore, we explored how the need for geographic relocation (i.e., intercity moves) affected the relationship between job vacancies and applicant gender.

Table 5 shifts the focus from the job listing to the application level. These are logistic regression results, and the dependent variable is whether a submitted application is from a woman (FEMALE). Column 1 indicates a negative relationship between EMER and FEMALE, after accounting for job title. Women are less likely to apply to a job listing requiring an emerging technology, consistent with our earlier findings. Because both the applicant and the job have geographic locations in these data, Column 2 adds an indicator for whether the applicant and job are in different cities. Our findings suggest that women are less likely to submit applications to jobs in other cities. Column 3 adds an interaction term between EMER and whether a job is in a different city and indicates that women are less likely to submit applications to jobs in other cities, particularly for jobs requiring emerging skills. For job listings in the same city, the correlation between EMER and FEMALE is not significantly different than zero, suggesting that the lower propensities women exhibit to submit applications for jobs in other cities relate to the costs of moving and/or commuting. Column 4 shows that adding compensation does not materially alter the coefficients.

## Emerging Technologies and Wage Earnings: Three Pathways

The analyses discussed above indicate that women may receive less exposure to emerging technologies but this can only drive wage disparities if there is a wage premium for working with these technologies. We turn to the employment and wage data to analyze if and why these technologies generate a wage premium. First, however, Column 1 in Table 6 uses the employment and wage data to confirm that a gender pay gap exists in this sample. This analysis indicates that women’s wages are 6% lower than men’s in this sample and the estimate remains similar in Column 2 after including employer fixed effects.<sup>18,19</sup> Column 3 shows that a positive relationship exists between emerging technology use and wages. Together, these two columns support the assertion that differences in exposure to emerging technologies may explain some of the gender pay gap in this sector. The remainder of Table 6 evaluates the three pathways that we proposed through which the use of these technologies could drive wage disparities: (1) interfirm mobility, (2) wage bargaining, and (3) firm-specific premiums.

Interfirm mobility: Column 4 uses the employment history data to examine correlations between EMER and cross-firm movement. We used ordinary least squares (OLS) models to evaluate an employee’s tendency to switch to other employers, measured by the log of the employment duration with the current employer before conducting an external search.<sup>20</sup> IT workers using emerging technologies stay with an employer for a shorter time before searching for external opportunities. The estimated magnitude implies a 7% shorter period before searching for a new job for IT workers who use emerging technologies on the job. Column 5 estimates a Poisson model that uses the total number of employers as the dependent variable. This model controls for total labor market years, so a higher employer count indicates more frequent employer changes up to that point in a worker’s career. IT workers who use emerging technologies in their current employment tend to have switched employers more frequently up to that point in their careers.

Wage bargaining: Prior research has emphasized the significance of the asking wage in shaping final wage agreements (Hall & Krueger, 2012). We therefore analyzed asking wages (target wage) during job search, a critical first stage in the bargaining process. These data are useful because few data sources capture the wages employees ask when switching employers, and it allowed us to gauge the extent to which gender disparities at this job-search stage might contribute to a broader gender pay gap. The target wages in our data indicate whether women ask for lower wages than men when exploring new job opportunities, potentially signaling less bargaining power.

Column 6 reports the wage increment employees ask for when searching for their next employer. We found that IT workers who use emerging technologies ask for 2% higher wages from their next employer after controlling for current wage levels, education, experience levels, and employer fixed effects. However, this estimate is imprecise because of the small sample size (t = 1.73).<sup>21</sup> Nonetheless, a 2% difference would be substantial, particularly if it compounds each time a worker searches for a new job. Column 7 provides corroborating evidence that the wage returns of emerging technologies are higher for workers who have more frequently switched employers.

Firm-specific premiums: Another explanation for the wage premium for emerging technologies is that workers are paid more when using these technologies because employers with the resources to invest in these technologies pay higher salaries. A comparison of Columns 3 and 8 tests this argument. Column 3 shows that wages are 6% higher for workers who use emerging technologies. In Column 8, we include employer fixed effects, which removes the effects of general differences in wage levels across employers. The number of employers in this wage sample for which we have multiple workers is not very large, limiting the power of this analysis. Yet, after including employer fixed effects, we found that although R-squared values increase substantially, meaning that a significant amount of IT wage variation can be attributed to between-firm variation, the estimated coefficient on EMER changes very little, which means that general wage differences across employers do not explain the premium paid to workers who use emerging technologies. We found similar results when controlling for more worker and employer characteristics (see Appendix B).

## Conclusion

Our study illuminates a critical but overlooked gap in the IS literature: the impact of gender disparities in access to emerging technologies on IT wage structures. We examined how labor market conditions influence job-seeking behaviors, subsequently restricting women’s engagement with emerging technologies. We explored three mechanisms by which access limitations impact women’s earnings in the short and long term: (1) interfirm mobility, (2) wage bargaining, and (3) firm-driven wage premiums.

Our findings indicate that women are less likely than men to work with emerging technologies. This disparity is largely attributable to demand-side labor market conditions and supplyside job-seeking behaviors. Specifically, the dynamic nature of tech roles, combined with the degree of job mobility needed for financial advancement, poses challenges for individuals balancing competing demands related to their family responsibilities (Ahuja & Thatcher, 2005; Joseph et al., 2012). These dynamics collectively steer women away from IT jobs requiring proficiency with emerging technologies. The results on gender differences when applying for jobs involving emerging technologies remain robust when using matched samples between male and female IT workers. Although prior research suggests that gendered job-seeking behaviors are attributable to gender stereotypes in jobs (e.g., technological skills versus interpersonal skills), we found evidence that women’s hesitation to submit job applications for IT jobs requiring emerging technologies is largely attributable to the costs of moving and/or commuting. This finding highlights an additional challenge for women pursuing careers in the IT sector.

We also provide evidence for how disparate access to emerging technologies influences gender disparity in wage outcomes in the short and long term. We found that interfirm mobility and bargaining power contribute to higher wages for workers skilled with emerging technologies. We did not find support for a firmspecific wage premium attached to investment in these technologies. Workers who use emerging technologies in their jobs earn more but not because their firms happen to pay better.

We acknowledge several limitations of our analysis. First, our wage data are from a job board, potentially introducing the issue of self-reporting bias common in much of the wage data used in the literature, including the census and other data from administrative sources (Blau & Kahn, 2017; Budig & England, 2001; Ruppert et al., 2009). Given our focus on the gender wage gap, this bias may skew the results because men often overreport wages and women tend to underreport them. Second, our wage data were collected in 2006-2010, limiting the generalizability of our findings to the subsequent decade, when the technology landscape was changing rapidly. The relative significance of the three mechanisms we examine may have evolved in the interim. For instance, as large companies are increasingly dominating the tech sector, firm-driven wage premiums might have become more salient. Future research should extend the temporal scope to elucidate the shifting dynamics that affect the IT gender pay gap. Moreover, the rapid evolution of IT systems implies unique features for each period, necessitating that future research continually update its focus on emerging technologies.

Despite its limitations, our research provides insights into the IT sector’s unique challenges and opportunities. First, rapid technological progress has made skill obsolescence a critical issue. Future studies might examine how demand is changing in this fast-paced industry and focus on the growing importance of artificial intelligence and automation. Our research suggests that pay disparities, often studied in the context of discrimination and other structural issues, can arise from technological change. Furthermore, the IT sector’s global nature adds complexity, making cross-border and cultural factors important areas for further research.

Second, our research suggests that managers could counter gender stereotyping norms through targeted initiatives. Instituting formal training programs that foster an understanding of gender-neutral skill requirements is crucial. Such initiatives can significantly improve women’s access to and participation in roles that rely on emerging technologies. Importantly, we found that women’s lower rate of entry into these roles has far-reaching implications, particularly for their future wage growth. From an equity perspective, women’s lower rate of entry into roles involving emerging technologies necessitates a restructuring of workplace norms and cultures. Our work also illuminates the unintended consequences of work-family conflict as organizations invest in emerging technologies. To address this challenge, organizations could consider implementing childcare services or flexible work hours, aiming to create a more equitable work environment. Nontraditional job structures, such as job sharing, could provide further flexibility. The goal is to create environments that facilitate work-family balance for jobs using cutting-edge technologies, empowering women to choose these roles more freely.

Third, another critical concern is IT market volatility, exacerbated by external factors such as the COVID-19 pandemic or changes in interest rates. Our research suggests that future studies could focus on how such shocks, by increasing or decreasing firms’ incentives to invest in emerging technologies, differentially affect the career opportunities and resources available to men and women. For instance, examining whether women’s roles are more or less vulnerable during economic downturns could be valuable. The rise of the gig economy in IT and its gendered implications also warrant close attention (Czymara et al., 2021).

These findings help identify policy levers to improve opportunities for women in the IT labor force. For instance, policies offsetting asymmetric costs for IT workers who are transitioning between jobs could be of value. One example is benefits portability, which would permit workers to transfer health care insurance and other benefits from one employer to another (Gruber & Madrian, 1995). Moreover, as the IT sector is integral to discussions about work-from-home policies, assessing their impact on gender equality is vital (Barrero et al., 2021). Future policy discussions should consider whether telecommuting exacerbates or alleviates existing gender imbalances given that women often face greater responsibilities at home.

## Appendix A

## Measurement of Emerging Technologies

This appendix evaluates the sensitivity of our wage results to altering the window in which a technology is considered “emerging.” One challenge associated with analyzing the effects of emerging technologies is the absence of a standardized definition or taxonomy for defining which technologies are emerging. Technologies are often denoted as emerging because they are new or growing or because new applications are emerging from the technology. In this study, we used technological age as the primary indicator of whether a technology is emerging.

In our main analysis, we classified technologies that were new or experiencing particularly rapid growth as emerging. This classification fits with industry perspectives such as the “Gartner Hype Cycle” which tracks the emergence and maturation of technological innovation. Although this method does not place technologies into categories, Figure A1 shows that most technologies on either side of the emerging technology peak are less than 10 years old. Some technologies continue along the emergence path after 10 years, but a 10-year window seems to most accurately capture the technologies that this index denotes as emerging.

The literature on R&D, innovation, and obsolescence is also relevant. This literature (e.g., as shown in Figure A2) examines how the knowledge embodied in patents diffuses (e.g., Jaffe & Trajtenberg 1996). Figure A2 shows that patent-based knowledge peaks in its diffusion rate between three and five years but continues to diffuse for many years thereafter.

Although we chose a 10-year window to classify technologies as emerging, we can evaluate the sensitivity of our wage results to different windows. Table A1 demonstrates that our results relating emerging technology use to wages change predictably as we alter this window. The table shows that the returns to working with a technology fade as it ages. In other words, the wage premium for working with emerging technologies is largest when the technology is new and the supply of workers who can use the technology is scarce.

Table B3 reports results from wage regressions on emerging technology use that add additional controls to the baseline regression. As in the main paper, all regressions in the table include experience and education.
