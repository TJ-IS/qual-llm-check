# The influence of governance equilibrium on ERP project success<sup>B</sup>

Eric T.G. Wang<sup>\*</sup>, Jessica H.F. Chen

Department of Information Management, School of Management, National Central University, Chung-Li, 32054, Taiwan, ROC

Available online 11 November 2004

## Abstract

As the ERP adopter and ERP consultancy combine their efforts and resources to achieve mutually desirable goals, the problem of governance, which has been mainly identified as an intrafirm problem, is recognized to be an interfirm problem. To investigate the relationship between various governance mechanisms and their capacity to relieve project hazards, we propose a covariance model of ERP governance. The constructed model analyzes the complementary contributions of different governance mechanisms on ERP project success. Four governance mechanisms are studied, including explicit contracts, implicit contracts, reputation, and trust. This study holds that the virtue of one mechanism will grow into the gap of another, forming an equilibrium of governance structure. Then, the governance equilibrium plays a mediating role between ERP project hazards and ERP project success. Empirical analysis based on 122 ERP implementation projects shows a significantly positive relationship between governance equilibrium and ERP project success. Further, in support of transaction cost theory (TCT), the results indicate that governance equilibrium tends to be aligned with exchange hazards, specifically those associated with specialized investments and uncertainty. The empirical evidence suggests that governance equilibrium, when treated as a multidimensional construct, can provide a holistic representation of complex interfirm governance and allow researchers to match broad predictors with broad outcomes, leading to greater explanatory power of governance mechanisms on ERP projec success. Implications, limitations, and future research directions of the study are discussed. <sup>D</sup> 2004 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: ERP, Implementation; Transaction cost theory; Governance

## 1. Introduction

Since a full adoption of ERP system spans all functional silos, the hazards of implementation uncertainty are usually salient. Many firms thus bring outside help to accomplish the ERP implementation effort. Unfortunately, implementation difficulties still make approximately 90% of ERP projects late or over budget, encounter many technical problems, or fail to realize expected benefits. In order to relieve the ERP project hazards, business managers should attend to the governance issues in an ERP project so as to increase the likelihood of project success.

Governance is a recurrent theme in economic and social systems. Traditionally, the literature on governance has paid much attention to identifying the most efficient mode of isolated governance within a particular context. Studies applying the reductionist approach have focused mainly on how a single governance mechanism affects transaction efficiency (e.g., Ref. [9]) and/or how pairs of governance mechanisms (formal vs. social) explain performance in a bivariate view (e.g., Refs. [10,43]). Such reductionism treats the anatomy of interfirm governance as being decomposable into components that can be examined independently, and the knowledge gained from each component can then be aggregated to understand the overall interfirm cooperation. However, a complicated ERP project is embedded in a complex matrix of economic, social, and cultural dimensions. Any reductionist or bivariate analysis therefore is likely to result in internal inconsistency among multiple pairwise contingencies [54], and the managerial implications of each contingency are unlikely to be the same and often in conflict [13]. For example, an implicit agreement by itself may not lead to more cost-efficient cooperation. The same may also hold true for the reputation mechanism. However, if an interfirm relationship relies on both mechanisms, the mechanisms may function as complements, thus reinforcing each other [27].

Therefore, rather than viewing interfirm governance through an overly simplistic lens, a systems approach is adopted here to explore governance adaptability and completeness in ERP implementation projects. This approach provides more insights into interfirm governance than studies based on individual mechanisms because of the potentially greater effectiveness of collective mechanisms [16]. This paper aims at exploring the relationships among various governance mechanisms and examining whether more comprehensive governance can mitigate ERP project hazards more effectively so as to achieve higher project success. Fig. 1 depicts the conceptual model of this study. The concept <sup>b</sup>governance equilibrium<sup>Q</sup> is represented as a jigsaw puzzle—each piece represents a governance mechanism with its own competence (protrusion) and deficiency (indentation). The deficiency of one governance mechanism is covered by another, and in the same way, the virtue of one mechanism will <sup>b</sup>grow into the gap<sup>Q</sup> of another [16].

In what follows, we begin by reviewing the literature on ERP project success and interfirm governance. Each individual governance mechanism is examined in terms of its transaction cost and competence in dealing with exchange hazards. From the literatures, a comprehensive approach is adopted to examine the relationships among different governance mechanisms and their combined effects on ERP project outcomes. Then the relationship between the degree of project hazards and the governance mechanisms demanded is investigated. After describing the methods and data collected, the results of data analysis are presented. Finally, the findings, limitations and suggestions for future research are discussed in the conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical development and hypotheses

## 2.1. ERP project success

Due to the firm-wide impact of ERP systems, practitioners and theorists are still grappling with the question of which constructs best represent ERP success. Markus and Tanis [36] indicate that different measures are needed at different stages in the system lifecycle and a minimum set of ERP success metrics should include project metrics, early operational metrics, and long-term business results. In the implementation stage, business managers usually target project metrics such as shortened implementation time frames, because anything that takes a longer period costs more and <sup>b</sup>sooner-is-better<sup>Q</sup> equation is accessing the payback from the ERP system. So most ERP projects start with a basic management-driven impetus to target at a faster implementation and more cost-effective project.

Mabert et al. [33] report that firms over budget for ERP implementation tend to view the system as less successful in meeting desired company goals. Their results implicate that process success (e.g., budget and schedule control) manifests the cost-effectiveness of a project. Their findings then indicate that firms whose ERP implementation is under/on budget tend to rate the system value (goal success and business case attainment) higher than the overbudget firms. Such a result seems reasonable; a project overrun in cost and time discloses a potentially troubled venture and the likelihood of unfulfilled promises. The study of Mabert et al. [33] also suggests that the under/on budget group of firms not only manage their ERP implementation better but also do a better job of running their business. This viewpoint is supported by our field interviews with a number of ERP adopters. According to a consultant we interviewed, <sup>b</sup>from the consultants’ viewpoint, the definition of [ERP] success is an on-time installation<sup>Q</sup>. Consequently, we define ERP project success from the project implementation perspective and focus on the process indexes of projects.

For the purpose of managing a cost-effective project, governance, a tenet of transaction costs theory (TCT), matters. While TCT has placed more emphasis on the need to view governance mechanisms from the perspective of unavoidable incompleteness, we assert that multiple governance mechanisms should act collectively as a more complete governance structure to economize the limited information capacity of people and to reduce the incompleteness of each mechanism in question.

## 2.2. Governance mechanisms

Williamson [60] has pointed out clearly that governance <sup>b</sup>is the means by which order is accomplished in a relation in which potential conflict threatens to undo or upset opportunities to realize mutual gains<sup>Q</sup> [p. 12, emphasis in original]. The primary concern of governance is the <sup>b</sup>means<sup>Q</sup> aspect of the means-end relation and its objective is to effect good order through different governance mechanisms [60]. However, due to imperfect foresight and information asymmetry, each governance mechanism has its own costs and limits in dealing with conflict and making good order. Hence, the questions need to be resolved are <sup>b</sup>what governance mechanisms should be used in an exchange and how?<sup>Q b</sup>What happens if various mechanisms act together?<sup>Q b</sup>What are the relationships among different mechanisms?<sup>Q</sup> To explore the above issues, a comprehensive approach is adopted to examine the relationships among different governance mechanisms and their combined effects on ERP project outcomes.

Certain governance mechanisms of an ERP project appear to be particularly important in determining how the project might be executed. They are: explicit contracts, implicit contracts, reputation, and trust. Some other mechanisms such as pecuniary bonding, short-term contracting, and hostage are not common in ERP projects, and thereby are not examined in this research. In the following, the characteristics and associated supporting literatures of each governance mechanism are discussed.

## 2.2.1. Explicit contracts

Formal contracts are binding in an ex ante clearly defined sense. They provide customized approaches, mutually agreed upon policies, and procedures for dealing with necessary adaptations in an exchange [59]. Contract provisions are enforced by the third party and the relevant information is assumed to be verifiable by the courts [16]. Theoretically, the more precise the contract specifications, the less likely that the specific contracted terms will be violated [11]. If one party does violate the contracted terms, the other has the right to go to the court to apply for corrective actions.

Explicit contracts, however, have their own drawbacks. First, strictly contractual control may set off a vicious cycle of suspicion and retaliation via restrictions that may stifle a relationship. Further, it reduces the flexibility and may work against joint development since at the outset of a relationship firms cannot define expectations or demands exactly [42]. Contract enforcement may be technically infeasible, and it also matters whether a partner can observe performance (output) or effort (input) [1].

## 2.2.2. Implicit contracts

Implicit contracts are the shared expectations that parties have concerning the relationship. They aim to respond to the incompleteness of explicit contracts that arise because agreements are not, or at least not perfectly, enforceable. They are constructed in an unartificial manner, and thus, are usually treated as self-enforcement contracts [39]. Consequently, implicit contracts cover norms of behavior rather than risk sharing [16].

## 2.2.3. Reputation

Reputation is the credibility of a firm and is determined by contrasting what a firm says it will do and what it actually does [24]. The way one firm behaves in a particular transaction, or a series of transactions, will color its general business reputation. As Macaulay [34, p. 64] emphasizes: <sup>b</sup>Blacklist can be formal and informal<sup>Q</sup>. Firms want not only to deal with each other again but also to deal with other business parties in the future. Reputation, thus, can remove the incentives for opportunistic behavior by creating a cost that offsets the short-term gains from the opportunistic behavior [39]. Hence, reputation is also considered as a self-enforcing safeguard as implicit contracts.

## 2.2.4. Trust

Drawing on the work of Boon and Holmes ([8], p. 194), trust can be defined as <sup>b</sup>positive expectations about another’s motives with respect to oneself in situations entailing risk<sup>Q</sup>. This definition regards risk as the core of trust, and the trust is a relevant factor only in risky situations [12]. Trust, like any other psychological mechanisms, is an emergent feature of social structure built up through learning and thus creates and reproduces positive expectations through time [53]. In a world of costly and incomplete contracting, trust is crucial for realizing many transactions [39]. Considering the orthodox principal-agent model, when risk aversion is high and when the available information signal on effort has a high variance, extrinsic incentives can result in substantial risk bearing, hampering a principal’s ability to motivate work. Under such circumstances, trust in a contractual relationship may lead to more accurate, comprehensive, and timely information exchange and relaxation of controls on others, resulting in the reduction of behavioral uncertainty [61]. As such, the complexity of reality is reduced more quickly and economically through trust than via prediction, authority, or bargaining [44].

In the next section, we use the concept of governance equilibrium to explain why the aforementioned governance mechanisms should act together.

## 2.3. Governance equilibrium: an adaptive view

As a result of bounded rationality and imperfections found in reality, fabricated governance is incomplete, and thus incurring positive transaction costs is inevitable [57]. The implication is that an incomplete contract must rule in practice and be supplemented by other extralegal guarantee instruments to achieve an equilibrium [16]. Furubotn and Richter [16] have conceptualized the idea of institutional equilibrium in the following way:

Presumably, an institutional equilibrium would mean that an original set of formal rules remains in active use despite the fact that a supplementary set of informal rules and enforcement characteristics has grown up to complete the total structure. Then, an institutional equilibrium may be said to be essentially stable: (1) if it is achieved <sup>b</sup>automatically<sup>Q</sup> in the sense that the informal rules reach some stable endpoint of the new (complete) institutional arrangement without destroying the original formal framework, or (2) if, after a disturbance of an initial institutional equilibrium, a new institutional equilibrium (not necessarily the original) will be reached. The central concern expressed here is with the adaptive capabilities of institutions such as market [22] or hierarchies [6] [p. 23–24, emphasis added].

Based on the above discussion, we define governance equilibrium as: <sup>b</sup>A stable situation among various governance mechanisms aims to adapt to changing transactional circumstances<sup>Q</sup>. A change in one mechanism implies a similar directional change of other mechanisms for keeping governance complete. To understand why governance completeness is important to performance, we explore the relationships among these mechanisms in the next section.

## 2.3.1. Explicit contracts vs. trust

Even though some researchers (e.g., Refs. [35,46]) believe that trust can work as a substitution of formal contract, many others view the relationship between trust and formal contract as mutually supplementary. According to Das and Teng [12, p. 496], <sup>b</sup>a higher trust level does not automatically dictate a lowering of the control level, and vice versa. All it means is more confidence in partner cooperation predicated upon certain levels of trust and control functioning as parallel phenomena<sup>Q</sup>. In the presence of opportunism, the combination of formal and informal safeguards may deliver higher exchange performance than isolated ones. On the one hand, every contract is necessarily incomplete, and thus accompanies moral hazard problems. One party to a transaction may undertake an action that affects transaction value to the other party who is not able to monitor the action perfectly or enforce agreements through a binding contract. In responding to moral hazard problems, firms will only risk entering into a contractual relationship when they trust their trading partners who will adapt to any unexpected situation in a manner that respects a fair division of economic returns. Further, trust as we have discussed above, is built up through social processes. Through such processes, norms of flexibility, solidarity, and information exchange are promoted [43]. Hence, in a world of costly and incomplete contracting, trust may function to mitigate the precise exchange hazards targeted by formal contracts.

On the other hand, a formal contract can also complement trust. Poppo and Zenger [43] have pointed out that formal contracts are usually designed to specify contingencies, adaptive processes, and controls likely to mitigate opportunistic behavior as well as to limit the gains from opportunistic behavior, and thereby support trust. Trust cannot be created instantaneously and is the product of an evolutionary process that takes time [16]. Trust development depends on past interactions and experiences [31]. Failing to contractually specifying exchange elements that can be easily specified merely heightens incentives for short-run cheating and lowers the expectations of cooperation [5]. Consequently, <sup>b</sup>the specification of contractual safeguards promotes expectations that the other party will behave cooperatively and thus complements the informal limits of relational governance. Cooperative behavior in the present then reinforces an expectation of cooperation in the future<sup>Q</sup> ([43], p. 713). Contracting process itself promotes cooperation expectations that are consistent with trust.

## 2.3.2. Explicit contracts vs. implicit contracts

Ideally, detailed contracts can specify each party’s obligations as well as costs and benefits allocation in every conceivable state of nature. However, designing any deliberated governance involves costs [51]. In the real world, relevant information in a deliberated contract may not be verifiable by the third parties, and <sup>b</sup>a breach of contract law suit may settle a particular dispute, but such an action often results in a <sup>d</sup>divorce<sup>T</sup> ending the <sup>d</sup>marriage<sup>T</sup> between the two businesses, since a contract action is likely to carry charges with at least overtones of bad faith<sup>Q</sup> [34, p. 65]. Even the best attempts of drafting an explicit contract cannot be expected to yield perfect or complete governance [16]. However, there is a reason to believe that <sup>b</sup>relatively noncontractual practices<sup>Q</sup> are common [34], and there are various ways to reach trade and other types of agreements, such as implicit contracts.

Implicit contracts act as gentleman agreements serving to set expectations and establish decision processes for dealing with inevitable unforeseen circumstances and avoiding some of the difficulties of writing detailed explicit contracts [39]. However, there are no formal documents and there may never even have been any oral statements of the contracts; implicit contracts are thus in no way enforceable by the courts. The situation is that only the parties involved can determine whether the agreement has been violated, and only they can enforce the performance of promises by means of threat to discontinue the relationship [16, p. 183]. Since implicit contracts are unverifiable, the risk a party facing is agreement noncompliance by another party. To reduce such a risk and gain the effectiveness of implicit agreements, one natural question is: Under what conditions will one party not violate its promises? Or, in other words, in what situations, can we have faith in our trading partner? For this reason, implicit contracts must rely on other enforcement mechanisms. Thus, when a firm wants to rely on implicit contracts, it must search for trading partners who can be effectively governed by other complementary mechanisms.

## 2.3.3. Reputation, trust, and implicit contracts

Another enforcement mechanism is reputation. As Milgrom et al. [40] describes, <sup>b</sup>the system of private judges is designed to promote private resolution of disputes and otherwise to transmit just enough information to the right people in the right circumstances to enable the reputation mechanism to function effectively for enforcement<sup>Q</sup> [p. 3]. Therefore, a firm’s concern of getting a bad reputation, which would reduce its future possibilities for profitable transactions, will limit reneging its promises. This is akin to Telser’s [52, p. 29] argument: <sup>b</sup>someone is honest only if honesty, or the appearance of honesty, pays more than dishonesty<sup>Q</sup>. Thus, concerning with a partner’s reputation is an effective check for ex post opportunism and can overcome the temptation to renege or renegotiate.

To explain the correlation between reputation and implicit contracts, consider Kreps’s [27] game-theoretic model of reputation. The model aims to disclose that the possibility of ex post breach of an implicit contract could discourage a vulnerable party from entering into a contractual relation. The logic of Kreps’s argument is that one party’s verbal promise can nonetheless be ensured by the interest that another party has in maintaining its good reputation in a community of potential trading partners. If an ex post promise breach leads to reputation loss, a party will find it difficult to persuade other partners in the future into cooperation or will have to pay a premium for this cooperation. The basic conclusion is: if the expected future value gaining from honest trade is greater than the one-time gain from reneging on one’s commitments, then a party will have incentives to behave honestly [40]. As such, the problem related to implicit contractual compliance is reduced to an estimate of the costs and benefits of maintaining a good reputation [31].

Unfortunately, it may nevertheless be possible for those involved to decide afterward whether or not the parties have behaved honestly. If deciding what constitutes an honest behavior were easy, then it would also be easy for the courts to settle disputes at a low cost [39]. On the contrary, if one party cannot accurately communicate whether she/he is cheated and communicate it without any cost to other members in the community (potential trading parties), then the reputation mechanism in enforcing implicit contracts will be degraded [60]. In the same vein, Lorenz [31, p. 307] puts it clearly: <sup>b</sup>if these [unambiguous] conditions were not met, it would be impossible for community members to make a judgment as to the honesty of their potential partners. In the case of contractual disputes they would be unable to make clear judgments as to whose claims were bogus. The inability of the wider community to make such judgments would clearly reduce the value to the individuals involved in contractual relations of always acting so as to maintain their good reputation<sup>Q</sup>. Consequently, a nonverifiable contract may still be violated in the case of highly unanticipated and ambiguous contingencies. Reputation effect is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for enforcing implicit contracts [31]. Other governance mechanisms must arise to restore some of the lost effectiveness.

One way to enhance the effectiveness of reputations is to build trust that will provide a set of clear expectations for all parties in a transaction. Trust, like reputation, is built up through a long learning process, but is embedded in a particular relationship rather than in the marketplace. <sup>b</sup>Small risks were followed by larger ones, contingent on the success of cooperation. The importance of time and experience in deciding whether or not to cooperate points to the limits of the argument that reputation alone can account for the success of long-term contractual relations<sup>Q</sup> ([31, p. 309], emphasis added). The past rules the present, hence, history matters [16]. An unpleasant experience lowers one party’s trust and limits the probability of future cooperation with the present partner.

In sum, trust and reputation affect the credible commitments and make the cultivation of a transactional <sup>b</sup>relation<sup>Q</sup> the objective in its own right [16]. Trust and implicit contracts, on the other hand, stress on <sup>b</sup>leaving room<sup>Q</sup> for the rapid adaptation of governance to unforeseen circumstances [16]. There are sufficient flexibility and open ends so that trading parties are able to adapt swiftly and at low cost to new circumstances [16]. Although trust, reputation, and implicit contracts are all self-enforcement mechanisms, they are different in nature. First, the main distinction between trust and implicit contracts is <sup>b</sup>risk<sup>Q</sup>. Trust implies a certain degree of risk undertaking, but implicit contracts, in contrast, do not imply an agent’s willingness to bear risk. Second, when service tips are taken for example, it is an implicit rule that after a meal is served, a customer is expected to leave proper tips even when she/he does not expect to visit the same restaurant again. Under such circumstances, implicit contracts are created without goodwill, trust, or reputation considerations. Third, although in many situations reputation and trust are main drivers behind implicit contracts, they could also be the consequence of implicit contracts. Consider another example. Y Company adopts a Customer Relationship Management (CRM) module, e.g., Relationship Management Solution, from CRM Supplier N. Since this is the first time that Y Company cooperates with Supplier N, low trust exists in the initial relationship. However, over time, Supplier N learns what Y Company needs, how to help Y Company implement CRM, and what service it can provide. Similarly, Y Company knows better the roles on both sides in the implementation project and how to cooperate with Supplier N after the learning process. Implicit contracts emerge during consultation and interaction. More important, while implicit contracts are recognized and complied by both sides, they facilitate trust creation and good reputation of Supplier N. Thus, implicit contracts drive trust and reputation.

The above discussion shows the complex linkages among these four governance mechanisms. Since there is something holistic and ordered about the pattern of different governance mechanisms, an equilibrium of various governance mechanisms would provide useful insights into a powerful concept of equifinality or the feasible set of internally consistent and equally effective configurations [54]. The effect of governance equilibrium in an ERP project is to restrict the possible behaviors of consultancy through different mechanisms and make consultancy do the desirable things to achieve the project goal.

Governance equilibrium plays an important role in ERP projects. In ERP projects, performance ambiguity and system complexity usually render complete contingent contracts impossible. As many outsourcing companies have noted, they often fail to contract in sufficient details and pay inadequate attention to setting up measurement systems to monitor system performance [56]. However, to win the bid of an ERP outsourcing service, consultancies always claim verbally a number of benefits such as <sup>b</sup>quickened information response time<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>improved quality of information<sup>Q</sup>, <sup>b</sup>improved on-time delivery<sup>Q</sup>, and <sup>b</sup>increased interaction across the enterprise<sup>Q</sup>. Since high performance ambiguity makes it impossible to write those promises into an explicit contract, they become implicit contracts between the client and the consultancy to adapt to unforeseen situations during implementation. These implicit agreements act as a mutual understanding that the consultancy will help the ERP adopter in achieving those ERP benefits. There may also be informal agreements that define the responsibilities and roles of each party when additional system customizations and modifications are required. These implicit agreements will be realized if the consultancy values its reputation.

Trust between an ERP adopter and its consultancy is also critical for the construction of implicit agreements and reputation effect to work in a complicated ERP context. Consequently, we maintain that when a higher level of governance equilibrium is reached, a more cost-efficient (on time and within budget implementation) and <sup>b</sup>good order<sup>Q</sup> (archiving expected benefits) project is anticipated in the means-end relation of ERP implementation. Hence, it is proposed that:

H1. The greater the level of governance equilibrium, the higher is the ERP project success.

2.4. Aligning projects hazards with governance equilibrium

All governance mechanisms are costly to manage [12]. Contract selection, development, and implementation can be expensive [50]. Trust and reputation are not free either; trust and reputation building are planned activities, taking considerable resources to build over time. The emphasis here is that not every ERP project needs complete governance. Governance, in other words, should vary with the nature of the implemented project at hand. As concluded by Williamson [58, p. 105], <sup>b</sup>simple governance structures should be used in conjunction with simple contractual relations and complex governance structures reserved for complex relations seems generally sensible. Use of a complex structure to govern a simple relation is apt to incur unneeded costs, and use of a simple structure for a complex transaction invites strain<sup>Q</sup>. Two ERP project hazards, which differentiate simple implementation projects from complex ones, necessitate different governance structures: measurement uncertainty and project-specific investments. Their effects on governance equilibrium are addressed below.

## 2.4.1. Measurement uncertainty

Measurement uncertainty refers to the lack of knowledge about which state of nature will prevail and, therefore, which actions should be taken [7]. In the initial stage of a project, firms usually lack sufficient knowledge to make right decisions. For example, Markus et al. [37] reports that many ERP adopters usually make plans for software modifications early in the project phase when they do not understand the software thoroughly. However, they learned that their customizations were unnecessary and sometimes failed to work well after wrestling with the modifications. Knowledge insufficiency of ERP adopters makes ERP projects more uncertain, and causes greater informational asymmetry between ERP adopters and their consultancies. Under such circumstances, ERP adopters are difficult to determine whether a true breach of promise has occurred. For this reason, a contract may be legally more complicated and correspondingly more difficult to negotiate [27,39]. However, under an imperfect legal system, any problem may be solved practically by adopting a <sup>b</sup>principle<sup>Q</sup> that gives all participants in a relation an idea of how adjustments will be made if unexpected circumstances appear [16]. This is why informal governance mechanisms such as implicit contracts, reputation and trust exist. These informal mechanisms can <sup>b</sup>grow into the gap<sup>Q</sup> in an explicit contract, and the residual hazards in the contract can be covered to some extent.

However, explicit contracts are still necessary because informal mechanisms have their limitations [10]. Trust and implicit contracts could cause ambiguous expectations and misunderstandings, giving rise to higher coordination costs and greater opportunism [10]. Informal mechanisms are neither formally codified nor verified, and thus enough residual opportunism continues to exist [3]. The economic benefits of an explicit contract may outweigh the potential costs of residual opportunism left over by informal mechanisms.

On the contrary, a low level of uncertainty will simplify the control problem, since under more certain circumstances it is relatively easy to specify appropriate acts ex ante and to enforce any agreements [7]. The value of a governance mechanism depends on its potential costs associated with carrying out safeguarding, adaptation, and evaluation processes. Thus, simple governance should be used in more certain ERP implementation contexts for transaction cost economizing. As Rousseau [47, p. 399] puts it: <sup>b</sup>in effect, people do not need to develop trust when their exchange is highly structured and easily monitored<sup>Q</sup>. The demand for balancing between formal and informal governance mechanisms is less in certain situations than in uncertain situations. Although all of these four mechanisms jointly increase correct expectations and confidence of an ERP adopter, all of them are costly to come by. Governance supply therefore should fit the governance demand. In other words, firms should attend to all these mechanisms in a more complete manner in uncertain situations.

H2. Uncertainty inherently in an ERP project is positively associated with the level of governance equilibrium.

## 2.4.2. System-specific investments

System-specific investments (SSI) are those tangible and intangible efforts or resources required for supporting the implementation of a specific ERP system. ERP implementation is different from building a tailor-made system, because the emphasis of ERP is on changing an organization to <sup>b</sup>fit<sup>Q</sup> technology rather than customizing the system to optimize an adopter’s business processes. In order to reengineer business processes in line with those processes embedded in an ERP system, ERP adopters usually commit significant human and financial resources to learn a particular ERP package. When a system is substituted or fails to work properly because of system misfits, the value of investments will be largely lost because of their very low salvage value. For example, Glover et al. [18] have investigated the problems in ERP projects with a case study. In their target company, the consultancy lacked the <sup>b</sup>big picture<sup>Q</sup> perspective of the client’s business operations and was unable to suggest an efficient and effective reengineering plan to facilitate ERP adoption. ERP modules, thus, did not integrate well and some key reports generated by legacy systems could not be generated by the new system. Hence, the client was forced to perform operational queries manually after ERP adoption. Many ERP functions were idle, making much user training and investment wasted.

Moreover, most ERP systems are required to upgrade to new versions periodically. Consultancy may no longer support the older versions, and as a result, an ERP adopter’s skill and knowledge become useless and are demanded to be updated with new versions. Therefore, the SSI represents sunk costs to ERP adopters. Underlying this rationale is a central premise that chosen governance should vary systematically with the level of specific investments represented and will be the one that minimizes project hazards [23].

Monitoring provided for free by the market may alleviate moral hazard problems [39]. A consultancy with good reputation will be willing to forego desirable short-term outcomes obtainable through opportunistic behavior in order to protect its valued reputation and the long-term benefits that the reputation provides. However, reputation can work as a selfenforcement mechanism only when the ERP adopter can clearly observe whether a consultancy actually reneges on its responsibilities. The costs and difficulties in making correct judgments undermine the effectiveness of the reputation system. Legal contracts and trust can help countering the hazards. These mechanisms are complementary to each other for governing project hazards in the presence of specific investments [49]. An ERP adopter may start with an appropriate set of explicit procedural rules with a reputed consultancy for guiding their responses to unanticipated contingencies. Then the ERP adopter and its consultancy can promote the kind of mutual learning that contributes to trust build-up and implicit agreements, which in turn increases the likelihood of a successful ERP implementation. Hence, it is proposed that

H3. Specific investments required from an ERP adopter for the ERP system is positively associated with the level of governance equilibrium.

## 3. Research methods

## 3.1. Data collection

Our sampling frames are the Directories of the 2000 Common Wealth 1000 largest manufacturing firms and Hsinchu Science-Based Industrial Park Business Directory in Taiwan. A total sample of 1120 firms was selected and a mail survey of ERP managers or IT managers was undertaken in 2002. When preparing the questionnaire, five senior ERP implementers (four implementing SAP system and one implementing Oracle system) and one ERP consultant (Origin) were interviewed semistructurally. They were asked to identify any ambiguities in terms, concepts, or issues raised in the preliminary version of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was adjusted based on their comments; at the same time, the standard back-translation procedure for the Chinese version was followed. Further, in order to exclude unreliable responses, respondents were requested to give their background, position, length of time in the company and department, role in ERP implementation, and their confidence level in filling out the questionnaire. After two rounds of reminders, 160 questionnaires were returned, resulting in a response rate of 14.29%. Among the 160 questionnaires, 35 were rejected because they did not install or were in the process of installing the ERP system and thus deemed unusable. Only three fully completed questionnaires were rejected because of the low score of confidence level of respondents (score value=1 or 2 on a five point Likert-type scale), yielding a total 122 complete questionnaires applicable for data analysis.

Nonresponse bias was checked based on the information obtained from the Directory of the 2000 Common Wealth 1000 largest manufacturing firms and the Top 500 largest corporations in Taiwan. From these resources, we were able to compare some firm attributes between responding and nonresponding firms. The mean differences between respondents and nonrespondents with respect to the number of employees and total assets were tested using an unpaired t-test. The results demonstrated that all t statistics were nonsignificant (number of employees, t=1.332, p=0.183; total assets, t=1.709, p=0.088). The industry composition showed a highest representation among firms from electronic industries (43.40%) and information and telecommunication industries (13.01%). Of the 122 respondents, 81 are the CIOs of their company and 59 are the project leaders in ERP implementation (among the CIOs, more than half are also the ERP project leaders).

Since project complexity may have a significant effect on project outcomes, the number of implemented ERP modules, which should reflect to a great extent the project complexity of ERP implementation, was used as the indicator of project complexity to control for its effect on project success. For our respondents, the most frequently implemented modules were Financial Accounting (114 firms adopted, 93.4%), Material Management (112 firms adopted, 91.8%), Controlling (95 firms adopted, 77.9%), Production Planning (90 firms adopted, 73.8%), and Asset Accounting (88 firms adopted, 72.1%). Except for three respondents (two of them install two modules, the other one installed only one module), the remaining 119 ERP adopters installed more than three ERP modules. The respondents installed an average of six ERP modules, representing a fairly wide range of adopted modules.

## 3.2. Variable measurement

Most of the measures were adapted from previous studies except for system-specific investments of an ERP adopter. The items for assessing Measurement uncertainty deal with the extent of measurement ambiguity in cost, scheduling, and performance evaluation for ERP implementation. This scale was adapted from Loh and Venkatraman [30]. The System-specific investments scale refers to the extent to which modifications and adaptations were made by an ERP adopter for satisfying its requirements and was developed by the present study. Explicit contract describes the extent to which conflict resolution, system modification, performance evaluation, and communication management were provided in formal provisions. Empirical studies conducted in IS settings provided constructive guidelines for selecting significant issues that reflect this concept [26]. Implicit contract was measured with a scale developed by Lusch and Brown [32], including five items dealing with the mutual understanding of role, responsibil ities, procedure, and conflict resolution between the ERP adopter and the consultancy. Reputation was measured as the history information and credibility of the ERP consultancy. Four items were generated based on the instrument developed by Nguyen and Leblanc [41]. The five items for measuring Trust were adapted from Aulakh et al. [4], and Lee and Kim [29]. A five-item scale developed by Rai and Al-Hindi [45] was used to assess Project success. These items reflected some desirable characteristics of implementation process such as whether the project was completed on schedule, within budget, and the degree of expected objectives met.

## 4.2. Validation of governance equilibrium

As shown in Table 2, all the four mechanisms are highly correlated (all significant with p<sup>b</sup>0.001); thus treating them as a set of predictors of performance will arise the multicollinearity issue in multiple regression and run the risk of using the coefficients as the indicators of the relative importance of the predictor variables. In addition, general linear models, like regression analysis, miss the internal logic underlying a governance structure, and <sup>b</sup>the regression coefficient may have statistical significance, but may indicate no apparent logical linkage among the various independent variables<sup>Q</sup> ([19, p. 571], emphasis in original). Hence, the ability of such linear models for proposing a pattern of covariance model is doubtful.

Systems approach is used here to respond to the aforementioned problems. It is one of the multivariate analytic approaches for examining the patterns of consistency among various variables. In the past decade, the systems approach has incorporated the general systems theory concept of equifin ality by interpreting fit as feasible sets of equally effective alternative designs, with each design internally consistent in its structural pattern and with each set matched to a configuration of contingencies facing the firm [54]. In this study, we create a new higher order construct—governance equilibrium, which reflects the internal consistency of the four intercorrelated dimensions, and use this new construct in place of its dimensions. The governance equilibrium is a commonality, reflecting the degree of fit among the four governance mechanisms, and therefore, the mechanisms under the latent model have to be correlated. Since <sup>b</sup>governance equilibrium<sup>Q</sup> is a latent multidimensional construct, the confirmatory approach is preferred for modeling the fit as covariance [28]. Also, when a multidimensional construct is treated as a first-order factor and its dimension scores serve as the observed variables in a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model, summing scores could raise the issue of measurement errors and may fail to capture the differences in the relationship between the construct and its dimensions. The first order approach also ignores the relationships between each dimension and its measures [14]. To overcome these limitations, a second-order factor model was adopted for testing the validity of governance equilibrium.

We began with a baseline null model, which hypothesized that each of the four governance dimensions were independent constructs (shown in Appendix C). Then, a second-order model was built to see if the covariance model should be accepted over the baseline as a <sup>b</sup>truer<sup>Q</sup> representation of the model structure. Since the second-order factor model is merely a parsimonious representation of the covariance among the first-order factors, the goodness of fit of the second-order model can never exceed that of the baseline model [38]. To evaluate the efficacy of the second-order model, the Target coefficient (T) proposed by Marsh and Hocevar [38] was used. The result in Appendix C shows that the Target coefficient is very high (T=0.99), suggesting that using the second-order model as a more parsimonious representation of the observed covariance is appropriate, and the second-order model has similar predictive power as the baseline model. Evidence for the validity of a multidimensional construct includes the strength of its relationships with its dimensions and the degree to which it captures the relationships between its dimensions and other theoretically relevant constructs, which can be assessed with the total coefficient of determination or multivariated $R ^ { 2 } .$ . As illustrated in Appendix C, the four first-order governance mechanisms consistently reflect the second-order factor, and all the four coefficients are significant $( { p } ^ { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ providing support for the internal coalignment among the four governance mechanisms. ## 5. Discussion and implications

This study makes a valuable contribution to both the ERP and TCT literatures by focusing directly on the presence of governance equilibrium. In the orthodox economic research, governance is often captured as a single or a small collection of mechanisms that affect transaction costs separately. Such formulations are appealing for their simplicity in research administration and analysis. However, because of the interdependence of governance mechanisms, many interactions among these mechanisms could be hidden in observation (as discussed in Section 2.3). The importance of each governance mechanism may vary depending on the situation. For this reason, we extend the reductionist analysis by exploring the relationships among explicit contracts, implicit contracts, reputation, and trust from a more complete and adaptive perspective, showing how they mitigate project hazards to increase the likelihood of ERP project success. The main premise of this paper is that treating governance equilibrium as a multidimensional construct can provide a more holistic representation of complex interfirm interactions, allowing researchers to match broad predictors with broad outcomes and increasing explained variances. Our findings confirm the contention that governance equilibrium is multidimensional. If this is indeed the case, we can understand why different ERP adopters who hire the same consultancy and are offered the same ERP service provisions failed to achieve similar implementation outcomes. Our results also show that an ERP adopter needs more complete governance when it is locked into a relationship because of idiosyncratic investments. Holistic governance is also necessarily prominent in cases where perfect monitoring and evaluation of consultancy performance are impossible. Several implications can be derived from our findings.

First, governance mechanisms, essentially, are created for either constraining opportunistic behavior or inducing good behaviors that promote the continuance of a given relationship [51]. In a world of incomplete and asymmetric information, different governance mechanisms enable the bounded-rational individuals to economize on the information processing needed for different decisions. Business agents thus need alternative governance mechanisms to gain the diverse information for making their decisions [2]. However, many business agents often prefer one mechanism to the other. For instance, in Chinese society, many business managers choose their clan as their business partners. The assumption is that only people with blood relationship can be trusted. They may not have any protective safeguard to restrict their partners’ behavior, e.g., signing a formal contract. The only way to constrain the opportunistic behavior is by social norms. This gives their partners the loopholes to exploit, in many situations making the exchange break down. As Birnbirg [7, p. 421] emphasizes, <sup>b</sup>it is likely that no one view of the control process is uniformly applicable. Different combinations of these characteristics will lead to a different aspect of the activity being <sup>d</sup>critical<sup>T</sup> for controlling the interfirm relationships. This, in turn, will result in a different aspect of the control process being highlighted<sup>Q</sup>. Therefore, by emphasizing different governance mechanisms concurrently, more complete governance can leave room for the development of <sup>b</sup>private ordering<sup>Q</sup> and for each party to cultivate the business relation over time.

Second, governance equilibrium in terms of the balance between governance supply and governance demand presents a transaction cost-economizing issue. If supply is over demand, then some governance mechanisms are pointless and result in costinefficiency. On the other hand, if governance supply is inadequate, moral hazards problems will be more likely. When business agents employ these mechanisms, costs cannot be ignored. There are considerable amounts of real resources demanded to establish, operate, and adapt governance, and thus, the collective effects of various mechanisms may be less efficient in relatively certain circumstances. In such a situation, a simple contract or trust may be adequate for constraining opportunistic behavior and effecting good order.

Third, client–consultancy relationship governing should be consistently considered and evaluated over the whole implementation process. In the initiation stage, the central governance issue is to select a consultancy that <sup>b</sup>fits<sup>Q</sup> organizational needs. After a consultancy has been chosen, attention should be paid to the issues of contracting and specifying project goals and requirements. During implementation, open communication and interaction between the parties will facilitate trust building and mutual understanding on how to implement the ERP system successfully. Selecting and contracting with a reputed consultancy is only the beginning of ERP implementation; trust building and mutual learning to gain a win-win situation is more challenging to the parties.

## 5.1. Limitation and future research

Some limitations of the present research include data collection and interpretation of our results. The first potential issue is the common method bias. Since all quantitative data were collected with the same survey instrument, the methodological nuisance of common method variance may occur. That is, the strength of the relationships among the constructs might be somewhat inflated. In response to this problem, Harman’s one factor test was performed to examine the extent of systematic errors [20]. Unrotated factor analysis using the eigenvalue-greaterthan-one criterion revealed seven factors, and the first factor explained only 13.38% of the variance in the data. The seven factors totally extracted over 70% of the variance (Appendix A). The existence of multiple factors accounting for a sizable percentage of the variance indicates that the method variance may not be a major concern here [20]. Additionally, to avoid common method biases, questionnaire items were arranged so that the dependent variable followed rather than preceding the independent variables [48].

The second potential limitation is that treating the concept of <sup>b</sup>governance equilibrium<sup>Q</sup> as a multidimensional construct may result in interpretational problems [21]. The utility of multidimensional constructs has generated considerable debates in areas such as organizational behavior. Critics contend that multidimensional constructs are conceptually ambiguous, because variation in a multidimensional construct may imply variation in any or all of its dimensions. Hence, a multidimensional construct may explain less variance than that explained by its dimensions collectively and confound the relationships between its dimensions and other constructs [21,25]. Since different explanations may apply to different dimensions of a multidimensional construct, theories that explain the relationships between the construct and other variables are more difficult to develop [25]. These problems may be relieved by providing a much greater precision in the pattern of logical consistency among the factors and the explication of the underlying logical links among the dimensions [14]. To account for this issue, we maintain a strong theoretical consideration of the nature of the multidimensional construct before modeling the equilibrium construct with the structural equation modeling approach. Third, the research relies only on the responses of ERP adopters. The data thus might provide only an incomplete view of the relationships. Ideally, we would like to collect data from both the client and the consultancy and perform a dyadic analysis. However, data inaccessibility prevented such a possibility.

Finally, having submitted that governance of ERP implementation can be understood as an equilibrium concept, this perspective may be extended to analyze the governance evolution over the ERP system lifecycle (e.g., over the phrases of pre-implementation, implementation, and post-implementation). Since some governance mechanisms like trust and implicit understanding are accumulated with time, the more emphasized dimensions of governance may change from one phase to the next. As the implementation process moves on, the involved parties may also recontract or renegotiate to capture some of the potential gains from the ERP implementation. In order to understand better the demand of governance at different stages of ERP implementation, longitudinal studies are needed to gain and analyze comparative and historical information about governance.

1. Measurement uncertainty (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

Before installation, our firm can be certain abou

1. the requirements specification of ERP.

2. the required costs of ERP implementation.

3. the delivery dates of ERP.

4. the delivery quality of ERP.

5. the implementation performance of ERP.

6. the service quality of ERP consultants.

2. System-specific investments (1=<sup>b</sup>very low<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>very high<sup>Q</sup>)

The degree of modification

1. the organizational structure to fit the new ERP configuration.

2. the data formats to fit the new ERP configuration.

3. the management control procedures to fit the new ERP configuration.

4. the operating procedures to fit the new ERP configuration.

5. the presentation format of the output to fit the new ERP configuration.

6. the presentation contents of output to fit the new ERP configuration.

3. Explicit contract (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

The ERP service contract with consultancy clearly specifies

1. the procedures of modification of the ERP configuration.

2. who can propose the change that requires renegotiation of any aspect of the contract.

3. the mechanisms for dispute resolution.

4. the contact or the liaison persons in both companies with the duty to coordinate and manage the relationship.

5. the test methods and environments to be applied.

6. the basis and rates of payment for additional work.

4. Implicit contract (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

In dealing with the consultancy, we have a mutual understanding of

1. the roles on both sides.

2. the responsibilities on both sides.

3. how each party is to perform.

4. what will happen in the case of events occurring that were not planned.

5. remedies for failure to perform.

5. Reputation (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

The consultancy has

1. a good reputation in the market.

2. a good performance record for serving its prior customers (new scale).

3. a good reputation for being credible.

4. The consultancy has a good reputation for fair dealing with its clients.

6. Trust (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

1. Our business relationship with the consultancy is characterized by high levels of trust.

2. The consultancy and we generally trust that each will stay within the terms of the contract.

3. Consultancy and we generally believe the information provided by the other party.

4. Our consultancy is sincere at all times.

5. We believe the consultancy is honest at all times.

7. ERP project success (1=<sup>b</sup>strongly disagree<sup>Q</sup> to 5=<sup>b</sup>strongly agree<sup>Q</sup>)

1. We took less effort than others for ERP implementation.

2. Implementation was completed within budget.

3. Implementation was completed within schedule.

4. All users have had a complete training of the ERP system when the system has gone live.

5. All ERP functions can meet the expected objectives.
