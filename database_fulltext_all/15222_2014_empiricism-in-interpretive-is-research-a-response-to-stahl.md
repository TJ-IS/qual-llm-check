# Empiricism in interpretive IS research: a response to Stahl

Geoff Walsham

Cambridge University, Judge Business School, Cambridge, UK

Correspondence: Geoff Walsham, Cambridge University, Judge Business School, Trumpington Street, Cambridge, CB2 1AG, U.K. Tel: þ 44 (0) 1223 339606; E-mail: g.walsham@jbs.cam.ac.uk

## Abstract

This short article is a response to that of Stahl (2013) who argued that there is an empiricist ‘bias’ in interpretive IS research, not justified by its philosophical roots in hermeneutics and phenomenology. Stahl calls for a wider range and quantity of non-empirical approaches. I am broadly supportive of this call but, in this response, I also argue for the value of studies based on empirical data. In addition, I think that Stahl overstates his empiricist case somewhat and I identify a range of existing non-empirical genres of publication including theoretical papers, literature reviews, polemics and future-oriented speculations. However, I do like the idea of extending non-empirical approaches and I suggest some ideas for the future including short stories and novels, plays and artistic comparisons. I end with a brief discussion of the feasibility of such ideas bearing in mind the politics of academic publishing. ## Introduction

I enjoyed reading the paper by Stahl (2013) and I agree with his argument that interpretive work in the IS field could be usefully broadened to include a wider range of non-empirical approaches. I accept that my own published work has normally drawn on empirical data, but I refute the charge of being an empiricist. Stahl defines empiricism as the doctrine that experience rather than reason is the source of our knowledge of the world. I would, however, see both experience and reason as being valuable and, indeed, inextricably interlinked. Therefore, why have I, and interpretive IS researchers more generally, not produced more non-empirical pieces? I will explore this issue in the next section through discussion of some historical background on interpretivism in the IS field from the 1980s onwards and how this has influenced published interpretive research. I will then argue the merits of collecting, analysing and using empirical data.

Therefore, the first part of my response can be construed as trying to explain, and to some extent, justify the empirical ‘bias’, which Stahl identifies. However, in the second part of my response, I examine alternative approaches in more detail. First, I identify a range of existing non-empirical work in the interpretivist IS literature, including some of my own. I then discuss a wider range of ideas for the future, from plays to fairy tales and from novels to painting. I end with some short conclusions.

## Some historical background on interpretivism in the IS field

Interpretivism is a relative latecomer in the IS field and was initially not accepted as a valid approach by the dominant positivist school, particularly in U.S-based journals. For example, the following MISQ editorial statement was made in October 1989:

‘A paper in the Theory and Research category should satisfy the traditional criteria for high quality scholarly research. It should be based on a set of well-defined hypotheses, unbiased and reproducible procedures for collecting evidence that supports or refutes the hypotheses, and sound analytical procedures for drawing appropriate conclusions from the evidence.’ (Emery, 1989, p. xi)

Thus, positivism was seen by the chief editor of MISQ at that time as the only acceptable approach. This attitude changed somewhat in the 1990s and the senior editor for theory and research of MISQ wrote the following in 1993:

‘On the empirical side, we welcome research based on positivist, interpretive, or integrated approaches. Traditionally, MIS Quarterly has emphasized positivist research methods. Though we remain strong in our commitment to hypothesis testing and quantitative data analysis, we would like to stress our interest in research that applies interpretive techniques, such as case studies, textual analysis, ethnography, and participant observation.’ (DeSanctis, 1993, p. vii)

Although this shift of attitude can be seen as more welcoming to interpretive studies, it is worth noting a caveat. The interpretive techniques mentioned in the above quotation were all empirically based. There was no mention of any non-empirical approaches as being acceptable. The message to interpretive researchers was clear. To be published in MISQ , your contribution must use empirical data. This is not surprising since the jump from empirically based positivist studies to empirically based interpretive studies is a big one, but the leap to non-empirical interpretive studies would have been even bigger. Although interpretive studies were ‘welcome’, they had to operate under the shadow of the dominant positivist methodology.

What is the position some 20 years later? All the main IS journals have editorial policies, which embrace interpretivism as well as positivism. However, positivism remains dominant in the main U.S-based journals such as MISQ and ISR in terms of the percentages of published papers as it was in the early 1990s (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). In addition, interpretive papers that are accepted by such journals tend to be empirically based. The positivist shadow still persists and can be argued to influence interpretive researchers towards approaches, which are not too far away from the empirically based hypothesis testing of the positivists. For example, even the valuable paper by Klein and Myers (1999) on criteria for good interpretive research has a neo-positivist flavour in its eight criteria for carrying out such work.

I would like to personalise the above historical context to say something about my own attitudes to publishing interpretive work over the past 20 years or so. I have undoubtedly been influenced by the historical context outlined above and my submissions to U.S-based journals such as MISQ and ISR have tended to be empirically based. My first ISR paper (Walsham, 1995) could be considered an exception, although even that paper included an empirical analysis of the IS field itself. I have recently co-authored an MISQ paper (Mingers and Walsham, 2010), which is theoretical in its emphasis on discourse ethics but does include some limited illustrations of the potential applicability of the theory to empirical contexts. My empirical ‘bias’ can partly be explained by context, but also results from my liking for empirical data. I turn now to reasons for this.

## A defence of empirical data

Although I recognise the value of non-empirical accounts and will discuss these later in the article, I wish to defend the value of empirically based approaches. First, I find empirical data essential in contexts that are new or underexplored. For example, Sundeep Sahay and I carried out empirical research in the 1990s on the use of geographical information systems (GIS) in Indian district-level administration (Walsham and Sahay, 1999). At that time, no similar studies existed, and knowledge of the socio-political context of GIS applications in developing countries such as India was very limited. We theorised our findings through various lenses including actor-network theory, but the analysis would not have been possible without access to extensive field data.

A second set of contexts where empirical data is valuable, and perhaps essential, is where access to new situations may challenge old ideas and theories. For example, the politics of IS implementation is a very old topic going back to classic studies such as that by Markus (1983). However, the world has changed since those days and we have new phenomena such as open source software, cloud computing, and social networks. Power and politics are always present in any situation but the particular forms that they may take may differ in the new contexts. I find that first-hand empirical data is valuable in stimulating my thought processes about the old topic in the new context and in gaining a deeper appreciation of what others are writing or saying on the subject.

A further defence of the collection, analysis and use of empirical data is the need to accumulate experience in particular domains, and first-hand engagement is one valuable way of doing this. I have worked on the topic of IS in developing countries for about 30 years and I continue to try to gain experience and understanding by direct field contact. This is not to argue that this is the only, or even the best, way to deepen one’s understanding. However, it is surely one valuable way. Returning to the charge of empiricism discussed early in this response, my view is that it is experience and reason, which can provide sources for our knowledge of the world.

## Non-empirical approaches

I have defended the value of empirical data in the section above but I agree with Stahl that non-empirical approaches can be valuable. However, I think that he underestimates the volume of existing non-empirical publications and, in this section, I discuss examples from the literature under the headings of theoretical papers, literature reviews, polemics and future-oriented speculations. There is some emphasis in what follows on my own published research. This can be justified as part of a response paper to a critique of my work, but it should not be taken to imply that I consider my research to provide the best examples of particular genres.

## Theoretical papers

There is a long tradition of theoretical papers in interpretive IS research. A good example is cited by Stahl himself when referring to Boland (1985). Boland discussed phenomenology and hermeneutics, particularly the work of Gadamer, and argued that they provide a sound theoretical basis for interpretive research in the IS field. Stahl (2013) notes that theoretical pieces such as that of Boland are often ‘supported by examples or vignettes’ and thus have some empirical basis to them. It is true that Boland does briefly mention some ‘attempts at doing phenomenology’, but the thrust and core content of the paper are non-empirical.

In terms of my own work in this category, I would give Walsham (1997) as an example. The purpose of this article was to assess the current and potential future contribution of actor-network theory to IS research. Again, as with the paper by Boland, some examples of the application of the theory to empirical cases are provided. However, the great majority of the paper is concerned with theoretical concepts, their potential applicability to IS, and a critical appraisal of the theory.

## Literature reviews

Literature reviews have an empirical basis in two senses. They survey a body of literature to provide support for their arguments and, indeed, some of the surveyed papers will themselves be based on case studies or other empirical approaches. However, I would argue that a good literature review goes beyond this to provide nonempirical arguments, speculations and conclusions. An example is provided by Jones and Karsten (2008) where they examine the relevance of structuration theory to IS research. The paper reviewed the different ways in which the theory had been utilised by IS researchers to date. It then proposed how structurational IS research might be advanced in the future, providing eight areas for future research.

Literature review papers, such as that of Jones and Karsten, are hard work, and perhaps because of this, my own contributions to this genre are somewhat limited. However, Walsham (2010) provides an example. In this article, I surveyed the literature on the use and potential for ICTs to provide development benefits to the citizens of India. Some of the articles surveyed dealt with empirical cases of course. However, the main thrust of the article is not empirical. The key argument of the article is that ICTs should not be seen as ‘silver bullets’ for development but neither are they irrelevant. They are potentially important contributors towards development in India but only through their integration in wider socio-technical interventions.

## Polemics

Stahl (2013) uses the subtitle of ‘a critical polemic against the empiricist bias in interpretive IS research’ and polemical papers are examples of a third strand of nonempirical interpretive IS research. Stahl says that publications such as his that do not refer directly to empirical research are often labelled ‘opinion pieces’. I have no problem with this label per se. The problem comes when the term ‘opinion piece’ is used in a way that implies hierarchy with empirically based papers at the top and mere opinion pieces at the bottom. U.K. researchers are required to submit their four ‘best’ papers to a research assessment exercise known as REF in its current incarnation. It would be a brave person who submitted an ‘opinion piece’ to the REF if they had other empirically based papers to offer. I will return to the politics of publishing in the conclusions of this response article.

A ‘polemic’ is defined by the Concise Oxford Dictionary as a ‘controversial discussion’. I have published one paper with the word ‘polemic’ in the title, namely, Walsham (2005a). A key premise of the paper was the need to see the term ‘development’ as something of concern for all individuals, groups, organizations and societies, and not something of relevance only to the so-called developing countries. I argued that IS research is needed to address a wide variety of development arenas with context-specific studies, which are broadly critical in nature. Although the term ‘polemic’ is not in their titles, I have also published polemical pieces on the need for more critical IS research (Walsham, 2005b) and taking issue with Friedman’s (2005) notion of the ‘flat world’ brought about by ICTs (Walsham, 2008).

## Future-oriented speculations

Speculations about the future are a further category of non-empirical studies, although they can often be considered also as polemical papers since they tend to be controversial. An excellent example of this is provided by work on the desirable future nature and conduct of the IS field as a whole. This debate has gone on throughout the field’s history but the article by Benbasat and Zmud (2003) provoked a storm of response about a decade ago. Benbasat and Zmud argued the need to focus on a core set of properties, concepts and phenomena in order to define the IS field and tackle its ‘identity crisis’. Many respondents argued against this position or wanted, at least, to modify it. For example, Robey (2003) supported the need for a more coherent identity for the IS field with information technology at its core, but he argued that this identity should be flexible and adaptable in order to cope with continuing environmental changes. Robey also argued against a dominant methodological paradigm, namely, positivism.

I have recently entered into this debate on the future of the IS field myself (Walsham, 2012). My paper argues that it is ironic that the success of ICTs, which could be considered prima facie to be good for the IS field, has led to concerns that the distinct research contribution of the IS field could become lost and that the field may therefore decline. I put forward a skeleton future agenda for the field. This agenda emphasises the need for a focus on ethical goals, increased use of critical approaches, welcoming other disciplines with open arms, widening our field of study to many non-traditional settings, and rejecting a dominant methodological paradigm. Whatever the reader thinks of my ‘prescriptions’, the point for this response article is that Walsham (2012) is not an empirical article.

## Ideas for the future

The previous section has identified a range of nonempirical approaches in the existing interpretive IS literature and has provided a few examples. However, these approaches can be classed as fairly traditional since few would disagree with the desirability of theory or literature review articles, and many would also support polemics and future-oriented speculations about the IS field for example. Stahl (2013) hints at the possibility of less traditional approaches, although he does not develop these in any substantial way. However, for example, he mentions the opportunities which might arise from opening up the boundaries between interpretive IS research and different fields such as literature, arts, and performance. I have thought about some non-traditional approaches myself over the years, although I have not to date followed them through. I will now outline a few of them in this section on ideas for the future.

I liked Stahl’s comparison between empirical interpretive IS research and fairy tales, and I agree that there are considerable similarities. Fairy tales use archetypal subjects who interact in a story, often concerning good and evil, and there is normally a moral message. It is important to entertain so that the message is absorbed. I could imagine a whole set of such stories involving a cast of interpretive and positivist researchers, IS managers, users, journal editors and so on. If the term fairy tale is too much of a radical departure for academics, then an alternative label is short story. If someone sent such a short story to one of our mainstream IS journals now, would they be taken seriously? I think not, but I will return to the issue of the feasibility of radical approaches in the conclusions section.

What about longer stories or novels? I am a fan of Jane Austen and I have often argued that her novels are particularly acute on issues such as social structure, personal motives, interpersonal politics and gender issues. Indeed, her characters often appear more alive than the rather wooden descriptions of people and their interaction, which we publish in our learned academic journals.

Now most of us do not have the literary talent of Jane Austen, but the notion of an IS novel is surely a feasible one for some of our more gifted colleagues.

Turning from the literary arts to those of performance, I had the pleasure a number of years ago to attend an interesting ‘panel’ at an IS conference. I cannot recall the exact year or location but I think it was one of the IFIP wg8.2 conferences. My most negative stereotype of an academic panel is one where the speakers drone on well past their allocated time and there is only time at the end for five minutes of audience interaction. The audience members sink deeper into their seats, perhaps quietly dozing, as the time ebbs away. In contrast, the panel I mentioned above took the form of a play. The panel members acted parts in an IS drama, and the audience was certainly wide awake. It required some bravery on the part of the panellists to try such an approach, and on the part of the conference organisers to accept the contribution. Sadly I have not seen this genre repeated at conferences since, but this may reflect academic conservatism rather than academic value.

My final speculation on ideas for the future concerns links with painting. Painters can be considered as interpretivists. They often use empirical data in the form of artist’s models or scenes from life. However, they also use their imagination and artistry to produce pieces of work designed to stimulate, inspire or shock. The painter Miro´, for example, used his work throughout his career to interpret and comment on the world around him, and his paintings reflected a deep engagement with that world, including the turbulent period of Spanish history through which he lived. It would be interesting, although ambitious, to attempt a comparison between interpretivism in IS research and that of a painter such as Miro´.

## Conclusions

I have responded to Stahl (2013) in two broad ways. First, I have explained and defended the value of interpretive studies based on empirical data. However, second, I have agreed with Stahl that we could fruitfully extend the range and quantity of non-empirical genres of interpretive IS research. With respect to existing work, I think that Stahl overstates his empiricist case and I have illustrated four non-empirical genres of publication: theoretical papers, literature reviews, polemics and futureoriented speculations. I have also suggested some ideas for the future, including novels, plays and artistic comparisons, which are more speculative and radical in their departure from existing norms.

This brings us directly to the politics of publishing. There are undoubtedly problems here for those of us, including Stahl and myself, who would wish to see a wider range of non-empirical approaches. There is likely to be opposition to radically new styles of research and publication among traditionalists in both the positivist and interpretive tradition. Fairy stories, polemics and the like can be labelled as non-scientific. However, there is hope for these new domains. Remember that interpretive research itself was rejected in the late 1980s by the editorial policy of MISQ at that time, but is now an accepted component of the mainstream.
