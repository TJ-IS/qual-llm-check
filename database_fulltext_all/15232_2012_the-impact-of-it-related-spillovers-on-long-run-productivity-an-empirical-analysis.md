# The Impact of IT-Related Spillovers on Long-Run Productivity: An Empirical Analysis

Young Bong Chang

Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology, Ulsan 698-798, Republic of Korea, ybchang01@gmail.com

Vijay Gurbaxani

Center for Research on Information Technology and Organizations, The Paul Merage School of Business, University of California, Irvine, California 92697, vgurbaxa@uci.edu

In contrast, prior research has largely focused on the direct and contemporaneous impacts of IT investments. As a result, we do not fully understand how IT investments are associated with ongoing productivity improvements in future periods and how spillovers influence these gains. In this paper, we examine whether firms receive incremental benefits from IT-related spillovers and whether these spillovers lead to more persistent returns. ## 1. Introduction

The growth rate of the U.S. economy has exceeded that of most other developed countries for the better part of the last two decades (Basu et al. 2004, Van Ark et al. 2008). Although the rate of expansion of the U.S. economy has slowed since 2001, labor productivity in the United States is still among the highest in advanced economies (Van Ark et al. 2008). Further, some U.S. industries with large investments in information technology (IT) have once again achieved levels of labor productivity that are comparable to those in the late 1990s (Holman et al. 2008). Not surprisingly, the impact of IT on productivity has been the focus of considerable attention among academics and policy makers.

Most studies in this domain have focused on the impact of a firm’s own IT investments on its productivity. However, a firm may also accrue spillover (or external) benefits from the IT investments and

IT-related knowledge of its trading partners. For example, a firm may receive spillover benefits when it purchases goods electronically from its suppliers as it learns about their IT management practices through its trading activity. Caselli and Coleman (2001) show that rates of IT adoption and use increase when trading in manufactured goods with technologically advanced suppliers and attribute this to spillovers arising from suppliers’ superior IT know-how. Similarly, the benefits of IT-related knowledge may spill over to a customer firm when it transacts with IT services firms for consulting, systems development, or outsourcing services (Lee 2001, Lin et al. 2005). In particular, IT services firms have invested in R&D activities to develop expertise in technologies and their management in many domains (Bessen and Hunt 2007). Miozzo and Grimshaw (2005) provide examples of spillover benefits such as improvements in management and business processes accruing to client firms from their IT outsourcing providers even when the arrangement is aimed at improving IT service delivery. We believe that it is important to distinguish between the productivity impacts of a firm’s own IT investments and those from IT-related spillovers, and to examine whether spillover benefits are economically significant.

Recent studies have provided both direct and indirect evidence of IT-related spillovers (Mun and Nadiri 2002, Cheng and Nault 2007). At the industry level, Cheng and Nault (2007) find that IT investments made by an upstream industry have significant spillover effects on downstream industry productivity. Studies have also reported that the contributions of IT to output are greater than private IT returns (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1995, Gilchrist et al. 2001, Anderson et al. 2003), which would be the case if IT-related spillovers were economically significant. Accordingly, these findings provide indirect evidence that IT-related spillovers may exist and contribute to productivity growth.

In practice, IT is used as a general-purpose technology whose contributions span a variety of roles, such as coordinating economic activities and enabling new business processes (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg 1995, Dedrick et al. 2003). A critical feature of IT is that, unlike machine tools, which are tangible assets, it also has intangible knowledgelike properties. In particular, IT-related knowledge has nonrival properties so that a firm may apply it repeatedly at lower cost (Becchetti and Adriani 2005).<sup>1</sup> In the R&D context, which is the focus of much of the spillover literature, accumulated knowledge resulting from other firms’ R&D can in turn become the source of long-run productivity growth (Romer 1986, 1994; Coe and Helpman 1995). Analogously, we conjecture that IT-related spillovers have a sustained impact on productivity in the long run if formal economic transactions are accompanied by the transfer of IT-related knowledge. Specifically, when knowledge accompanies trading activity (Keller and Chinta 1990), IT-related spillovers do not become obsolete instantly.<sup>2</sup> In such cases, the incremental benefits that accrue to a firm from the IT and R&D investments of its trading partners may result in sustained effects on productivity growth over a longterm horizon.

The primary objective of this paper is to examine the magnitude and time pattern of the impact of ITrelated spillovers over a long-term horizon. We focus on the spillovers that are transmitted to a firm from its trading partners, especially the IT services industry, and examine whether these benefits lead to larger and more persistent returns. We begin by building an extended firm-level production function that includes spillover indices in addition to the traditional input factors of capital (including IT capital) and labor. To account for the ability of a firm to capture the benefits of IT-related knowledge, we control for the degree of IT intensity. To examine the magnitude of the spillover effects we use the error correction model (ECM), which is designed to capture long-run economic relationships while controlling for short-term adjustments. Then, we use the vector autoregressive (VAR) model to examine dynamic effects.

Overall, we find that spillover effects are statistically significant, with firms with relatively high IT intensity receiving higher spillover benefits. The sum of the output elasticities of the two categories of spillover indices is estimated at 0.026 and 0.017 for firms with high and low IT intensity, respectively (see Table 3 for details). Importantly, the impact of spillovers from the IT services industry is estimated to be larger than that from other industries (around 80% of the total). Moreover, spillover benefits from the IT services industry persist over longer periods and are continuously associated with output growth for high IT intensity firms. However, the impact of spillovers disappears after four years for low IT intensity firms. In sum, our study shows that IT-related spillovers contribute significantly to productivity growth and that these benefits can persist over time.

Our study builds on earlier research and makes important new contributions. We show that IT-related spillovers are economically important in magnitude and duration, and shed light on when and how spillovers improve productivity. We identify transactions with the IT services industry and with other industries as two important sources of IT-related spillovers, and allow for differing IT capabilities in the receiving firms. Accordingly, we are able to develop deeper insights into the transmission mechanism of IT spillovers. We show that the IT services industry is a particularly important source of these spillover benefits and that the ability of a firm to capture these benefits depends on IT intensity.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to examine the long-run productivity effects of ITrelated spillovers. A few studies have examined the spillover effects of IT and the long-run impacts of IT separately, but not together. As discussed above, Cheng and Nault (2007) have found evidence of spillover effects at the industry level. However, their analysis does not examine the possible persistence of benefits due to the knowledge properties of IT-related spillovers. Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2003) examine the long-run impact of IT using the conventional growth accounting approach, but they do not consider the role of spillovers. In this paper, we explicitly include IT-related spillovers as a source of long-run productivity growth, and we separate short-run dynamics and long-run effects within a unified framework. Further, we control for long-run benefits that may arise from unobserved complementary assets by taking firm-specific effects into account. Therefore, our study is able to demonstrate the specific role and impact of IT-related spillovers on long-run productivity growth.

Our paper is organized as follows. In §2, we present a review of the relevant literatures on IT and productivity and on IT-related spillovers focusing on their theoretical underpinnings. Section 3 is devoted to the description of our model in a production function framework. Our data set is discussed in §4. The empirical analysis and results are discussed in §5. Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

This paper draws primarily on two related research streams: IT productivity and IT-related spillovers. We begin by reviewing studies of IT productivity, which highlights the need for incorporating IT-related spillovers in estimating IT returns. Then, we examine the research that considers IT-related spillovers and approaches to modeling these spillovers.

## 2.1. IT and Productivity

Assessments of the numerous studies of IT and productivity have led to the consensus view that IT contributes significantly to output (see Dedrick et al. 2003 for a comprehensive review). These studies generally employ neoclassical assumptions and model IT as a traditional input factor of production. For instance, Brynjolfsson and Hitt (1995) use the Cobb-Douglas production function and estimate the output elasticity of IT capital as being in the range of 0.05 to 0.11. Dewan and Min (1997) jointly estimate output and substitution elasticities using the CES-Translog production function, finding that the output elasticity of IT capital is 0.10 for the median firm. In this framework, IT, as a standard production technology, contributes to output growth through capital deepening. Firms respond to the price decline of IT capital by changing their input mix, substituting IT capital for non-IT capital and for labor. However, input price declines do not guarantee productivity enhancements in the long run, as would be the case with a shift in the production function (Stiroh 1998).

In the framework of neoclassical economics, all capital inputs must be paid their marginal product. Hence, the output elasticity of IT should equal its input factor share (Solow 1956, Barro and Sala-i-Martin 1995) if neoclassical assumptions hold. However, studies have estimated the output elasticity of IT to be substantially higher than its input share and interpreted this finding as excess returns (Gilchrist et al. 2001, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2003). Explanations of excess returns typically focus on omitted factors in analyses that may influence their magnitude. For instance, estimates of IT returns may capture the productivity effects of complementary organizational changes (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000) or unaccountedfor IT-associated risk (Dewan et al. 2007), both of which are valuable explanations. Given the nonrival properties of IT-related knowledge, spillover effects are likely and may provide an alternative or additional important explanation. The literature implicitly views spillovers as a potential source of excess returns to IT investment, and some industry-level studies have demonstrated their positive impact on productivity growth (e.g., Cheng and Nault 2007). However, we are unaware of any studies at the firm level that have directly examined the role and impact of ITrelated spillovers as an explanation for excess returns.

## 2.2. IT-Related Spillovers: Rent and Knowledge

Spillovers are considered to be of two types: rent and knowledge. They differ in the transmission mechanisms by which a firm benefits from investments in technology and in R&D made by other firms in the same or other industries (Griliches 1979). Rent spillovers are external benefits embodied in goods or services when they are purchased from other firms at a price lower than their full quality-adjusted price. Knowledge spillovers are external benefits dissipated to noninnovating firms from innovating firms due to the nonrival property of knowledge. In our context, rent spillovers occur when suppliers are not able to capture all the economic rents from the innovations that result from their IT investments. This implies that their efforts towards quality improvement or cost reduction are not translated into correspondingly higher output prices (Siegel 1997). Accordingly, a substantial part of the benefits are transferred from suppliers to their trading partners as cost reductions, quality improvements, and improved service delivery (Bresnahan 1986, Cheng and Nault 2007). For instance, as suppliers build Internet-based systems that allow their customers to purchase goods electronically, purchasing firms may experience a reduction in the transaction costs of purchasing, a spillover benefit that suppliers cannot capture, usually due to a competitive environment in which suppliers do not have the pricing power to charge for the improved service.

Several studies have reported evidence of rent spillovers in IT. Griliches (1979, p. 104) observes rent spillovers in the computer industry which, he says “has had a tremendous real productivity growth, most of it unmeasured in its official indices, and most of it inappropriated within the industry itself (because of rather intensive competitive pressure).” Bresnahan (1986) finds evidence of downstream spillovers from computer use in the financial industry, which delivers customer service at a lower cost due to computer price declines. At the industry level, a few studies (Mun and Nadiri 2002, Cheng and Nault 2007) show the statistically significant impact of IT spillovers derived from trading with other industries.

In particular, Cheng and Nault (2007) focus on industry-level spillover benefits that result from IT investments made by upstream industries. They construct a spillover index under the assumption that all spillover benefits result from a mismeasured price deflator deriving from unmeasured quality improvements associated with IT investment. The spillover index, measured as aggregate industry-level IT investment weighted by transaction flow based on input-output tables, is incorporated in the production function. They estimate that, on average, a 10.5% increase in suppliers’ IT capital leads to an increase in downstream industry output of \$72.71 M. Their results suggest that the magnitude of spillovers corresponds to that of the mismeasured price deflators in the presence of competition. Mun and Nadiri (2002), using a similar spillover measure of aggregate industry investment in IT but based on the cost function approach, also provide evidence of spillover benefits from the IT investments of trading partner industries. They show that a 1% increase in spillover stock reduces the variable cost of a recipient industry by 0.03%. In particular, the magnitude of IT spillovers is larger for industries with high IT intensity, indicating the importance of the level of IT capital in determining the size of the effect.

Unlike rent spillovers, which are likely to be associated with trade in intermediate goods, knowledge spillovers occur because accumulated knowledge at one firm can be transmitted to other firms due to its public good or nonrival characteristics (Griliches 1979, 1998). Knowledge spillovers can result from learning, observation, and replication of others’ innovations. In our context, knowledge that one firm develops while adopting new IT solutions and management processes may spill over to other firms (Siegel 1997, Pilat et al. 2002, OECD 2004, Brynjolfsson et al. 2007). Brynjolfsson and Hitt (2003) suggest that a firm’s IT-related knowledge can diffuse to rival firms through consulting firms, employee transfer, and educational institutions. One key insight is that

IT-related management practices can evolve into systematic knowledge or learning (Pilat et al. 2002) that can spill over to other firms.

Although the two types of spillovers are conceptually distinct, most studies do not distinguish between them due to empirical challenges (Nadiri 1993, Monjon and Waelbroeck 2005, Wieser 2005). For example, consider the context of interorganizational systems in which a supplier cannot fully appropriate all rents from the deployment of its investment in vendor-managed inventory systems due to competitive pressure, transferring some benefits to its customers. Although this example includes features of rent-associated spillovers, customers may also benefit from knowledge spillovers in the same transactions by learning about supply chain practices from their suppliers. In the case of IT services, firms hire external providers to develop applications, manage processes, implement technologies, and share best practices. In these arrangements, IT-related knowledge can diffuse through formal transactions with trading partners for intermediate goods and services (Mun and Nadiri 2002). In such cases, purchased intermediate goods and services, typically associated with rent spillovers, are also likely associated with knowledge transmission (Van Pottelsberghe and Lichtenberg 2001). Therefore, in empirics, transaction-based measures may capture both rent and knowledge spillovers.

## 2.3. Modeling Spillovers

The economic literature focusing on spillovers is largely focused on R&D. However, economists do not share a common view of how to best model R&D spillovers, in part due to the empirical difficulties in isolating rent from knowledge spillovers. Due to the public good characteristics of R&D as a knowledge generator, some studies assume that a firm can enjoy spillover benefits even in the absence of economic transactions with trading partners. They also typically assume that all investments in R&D made by others are equally relevant to the receiving firm or industry. These studies use unweighted industrywide R&D expenditures either in a firm’s primary industry (Raut 1995) or in other industries that a firm trades with to measure R&D spillovers (Bernstein and Nadiri 1988, 1989; Jacobs et al. 2002).

However, a firm’s spillover benefits are likely to increase with the volume of its transactions with firms that invest in R&D, or when its technology is closer to that of the investing firms (Scherer 1982, Jaffe 1986, Coe and Helpman 1995, Jacobs et al. 2002). In this view, buyers can capture a portion of suppliers’ R&D efforts or related knowledge through the purchase of intermediate goods. At the country level, Coe and Helpman (1995) find that the productivity growth of a country is affected by the foreign

R&D capital of its trading partners where foreign R&D capital is constructed as a weighted sum of their R&D capital based on import shares. At the industry level, Keller (2002) uses a similar approach and finds that both interindustry and intraindustry R&D spillovers have a positive impact on productivity. Although these studies capture rent spillovers by employing economic transactions between trading partners, they do not exclude the possibility that knowledge is frequently transferred with intermediate goods. That is, a firm can also derive spillover benefits from the transmitted knowledge embodied in intermediate goods.

In our context, IT-related knowledge can be transmitted through a firm’s interactions with its trading partners. That is, we conjecture that IT-related knowledge can be embodied in traded goods and services (Caselli and Coleman 2001). For instance, a firm can acquire industry best practices through the purchase of application software that codifies these practices (Keller and Chinta 1990). This suggests that economic transactions with trading partners are an important conduit for knowledge transmission and are also a source of rent spillovers. Because a main focus is on IT-related knowledge and its persistence, we categorize trading partners into two groups: IT services firms that are significant developers of this knowledge, and all others. This allows us to study the spillover effects associated with the knowledge held by IT services firms and those related to the IT management practices of its trading partners, which may be acquired during the exchange of goods and services.<sup>3</sup> In sum, in addition to rent spillovers that can occur with economic transactions, IT-related practices and knowledge are also transmitted with the flow of intermediate goods (Caselli and Coleman 2001, Connolly 2003). Accordingly, the conventional measure of spillovers weighted by economic transactions can capture the effect of rent spillovers while allowing for the transmission of knowledge (Coe and Helpman 1995, Van Pottelsberghe and Lichtenberg 2001).

## 3. Model Description

## 3.1. The Production Function with IT-Related Spillovers

As discussed earlier, we consider trading partners in two different categories: the IT services industry and all other industries. We begin by describing our reasons for employing this categorization scheme.

Prior research in economics has typically grouped channels for spillovers into two categories—own industry (the industry that the receiving firm is in) and all other industries (Bernstein and Nadiri 1988, 1989; Jacobs et al. 2002; Raut 1995). A major factor in this categorization is the role of technology distance. To the extent that the industry a firm is in is a proxy for technology distance, this may be a useful categorization. However, unlike R&D spillovers, our focus is not on technological proximity of the firm’s primary product or service, but rather on IT-related spillovers. Correspondingly, given the focus of the IT services industry on developing and disseminating IT-related knowledge, we identify a firm’s trades with the IT services industry as a particularly important channel for spillovers. Of course, economic transactions with firms other than IT services firms can also contribute to the generation of spillover benefits because IT-related knowledge may be transferred during trading activity. That is, spillover benefits also result from a firm’s trades within its own industry and with other industries. However, we must choose only one of the two due to potential multicollinearity issues between these two types of spillovers. We focus on interindustry transactions because they account for a substantial portion of trading volume. Our choice also makes our study more comparable to earlier work (e.g., Cheng and Nault 2007, Mun and Nadiri 2002) because the majority of them have also focused on interindustry transactions as a channel for spillovers.

We turn our attention next to the traditional production function approach and extend it by introducing IT-related spillover effects. Consistent with the model specification that is widely used in the IT (see Brynjolfsson and Hitt 1995, Dewan and Kraemer 2000, Cheng and Nault 2007) and R&D literatures (Griliches 1979, 1998; Raut 1995; Jacobs et al. 2002), we assume an extended Cobb-Douglas production function that includes index variables to capture potential IT-related spillover effects from the IT services industry and from other industries in addition to ordinary capital, IT capital, and labor. By explicitly including these variables, our approach allows us to directly estimate the impacts of IT-related spillovers on firm productivity. This leads us to use R&D capital of the IT services industry as a proxy for its accumulated IT-related knowledge. We also assume that firms with larger IT scale are more likely to receive larger spillover benefits from the IT services industry (SP1).

However, scale-based arguments do not fully explain trends in the IT services market (Gurbaxani 2007). For instance, studies show that larger firms account for a large share of IT outsourcing arrangements (Ang and Cummings 1997, Poppo and Zenger 2002). This may result from their focus on the strategic benefits of external sourcing (Quinn 1999) rather than only on production costs (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998, Quinn 1999, Kakabadse and Kakabadse 2002, Lee et al. 2003). Levina and Ross (2003) argue that IT services firms are better positioned to deliver strategic and economic gains through their accumulated complementary competencies. As a result, firms with large IT scale often purchase IT services to achieve strategic objectives and may be better able to leverage the skills and capabilities of IT services firms (DiRomualdo and Gurbaxani 1998, Quinn 1999). Moreover, given the likelihood that these firms have more bargaining power and are typically more experienced at writing contracts (Poppo and Zenger 2002), the incidence of external provision of services may be greater in these firms. Finally, the adoption of software and related services depends on the installed base of computer systems (Pohjola 2003). Given that the IT services industry is broadly defined to include computer programming services, prepackaged software, software development, and end-user training, it may be the case that firms with larger IT scale purchase more services from the IT services industry. Taken together, it is not unreasonable to assume that firms with larger IT scale are more likely to purchase IT services.<sup>5</sup>

Our indices (as defined in Equations (2a) and (2b)) reflect the notion that the spillover benefits to a firm are higher when the level of interaction with trading partner industries is higher or when relative IT scale is larger, and when accumulated IT knowledge from investments in R&D in the IT services sector or from the IT investments of other industries is larger. As extreme cases, note that firm i in industry j will not receive any incremental benefits from the IT services industry when industry j purchases no intermediates from the IT services industry, or if the IT services industry does not engage in R&D activities.

Although our indices capture spillover benefits by accounting for transaction flows with trading partners, it is worth noting their limitations. First, they do not capture potential spillovers that may arise through labor turnover. In addition, our measures do not consider knowledge transmission that may occur in the context of formal agreements between trading partners for specific projects. However, the role of trade in shaping productivity gains due to spillover effects is well recognized (Coe and Helpman 1995). Accordingly, we conjecture that our measures capture a meaningful component of spillover effects by accounting for transaction flows with two distinct categories of trading partners.

## 3.2. The Duration of IT-Related Spillovers: Short-Run and Long-Run Implications

In this section, we examine the possibility of a sustained long-run effect of IT-related spillovers on firm productivity using our production function approach in addition to their recognized association with shortrun productivity gains. First, to analyze the shortterm impact of IT-related spillovers, we specify the production function in the first difference form as follows:

$$
\Delta y _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {1} \Delta k _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {2} \Delta l _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {3} \Delta i t _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {4} \Delta s p 1 _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {5} \Delta s p 2 _ {i j t},\tag{3}
$$

where ã indicates the first difference and lower case denotes the logarithm.

Equation (3) shows that the growth rate of output (ãy5 is a positive function of the growth rate of traditional inputs (k, l, and it) and IT-related spillover index terms (sp1 and sp2). Accordingly, we expect an increase in output when firms increase input levels. However, the impact of input growth on output will eventually decay because the inputs to a production process depreciate (Solow 1956, Barro and Sala-i-Martin 1995). As a result, current investments in inputs have transitory impacts on output growth and will not be sustained in the long run (Solow 1956, Barro and Sala-i-Martin 1995).

For illustration purposes, let’s say that a firm purchases an intermediate input and consumes it for production in a given period. The contribution to output is in the current period and is determined by its output elasticity. If the intermediate input is exhausted in the current period and there are no spillovers, the impact of the current investment on output is transitory and there is no future impact. In our context, when transactions between a firm and its suppliers are accompanied by knowledge transfer, these spillovers may have implications not just contemporaneously, but also for the long run. Spilledover knowledge may turn into organizational knowledge over time (Pilat et al. 2002) and can then be applied repeatedly at no additional cost (Gong and Keller 2003, Romer 1986).<sup>6</sup> For instance, a retailer can increase output in the short term by installing a new computer-based supply chain management system (reflecting an increase in a traditional input), which also facilitates the exchange of information with its trading partners. Due to changing business conditions, the new system will depreciate over time, similar to other inputs. Nevertheless, managers at the retailer may acquire knowledge related to improving its supply chain practices, which can be used repeatedly into the future. Given that knowledge is less likely to dissipate over time (Spender 1996), this can become the source of long-run impact (Shaw 1992). That is, even though the direct and contemporaneous impacts of the intermediate inputs dissipate (Solow 1956, Barro and Sala-i-Martin 1995), knowledge spillovers may have a longer impact.

In practice, it is not unusual for firms to acquire ITrelated knowledge when transacting with IT services firms or other trading partners (Caselli and Coleman 2001, Lee 2001, Lin et al. 2005). For instance, firms may benefit from improved business practices embedded in purchased software and IT services over a longer time period even after the value (price) of the software and services are completely amortized. Accordingly, IT-related spillovers can be continuously associated with output growth over a long-term horizon, which allows a firm to gain additional and sustained benefits from its trade either with the IT services industry, other industries, or both.

Given the notion that transactions with the IT services industry can engender the transfer of IT-related knowledge, it is worthwhile to examine the duration over which their impacts propagate. We could capture the long-run economic relationship by extending Equation (3) to the ECM form.<sup>7</sup> However, the ECM approach focuses on estimating stable long-run economic relationships while accounting for shortrun adjustments (Dolado et al. 1990, Greene 2000). It does not address the sustained long-run impacts of spillovers, which can matter if IT-related spillovers decay slowly, as may be the case. Therefore, we use the structural vector autoregressive (VAR) model, which we discuss later. This methodology allows us to explicitly model the interdependence between variables of interest over time and to examine whether this impact is realized and is economically significant (Dolado and Jimeno 1997).

## 4. Data

Because we model the production process of firms using the Cobb-Douglas function, our study requires data on the inputs and output of the production process. In our analysis, we utilize value added as output; ordinary capital, IT capital, and labor as inputs; and spillover indices from the IT services industry and other industries as primary independent variables. Our sample consists of large companies mainly from the manufacturing, transportation, trade, and services sectors. Our data are collected from two primary sources, Compustat and Computer Intelligence InfoCorp (CII). The key variable, IT capital, is obtained from the CII database,<sup>8</sup> which captures the computer assets and investments of Fortune 1000 firms. IT capital is an aggregate variable based on the total market value of installed systems. Financial information on firms including value added, labor, and non-IT capital is from the Compustat database. All variables are measured in constant 1990 dollars. The data set covers the sample period 1987 to 1994. Because CII redefined IT capital in 1995, we are unable to maintain data consistency for a sample period that extends beyond 1994. Specifically, in 1995 the definition of IT capital was changed from the value of all installed systems to the installed value of central processing units only.<sup>9</sup> We discard observations when any input or output variables are missing from our data set. We do have to sacrifice some data for the purpose of utilizing unit-root test techniques in our examination of the stationarity of the underlying process.<sup>10</sup> We ultimately have 3,088 observations in our balanced panel data set, consisting of 386 firms over eight years.

In our analysis, we divide our sample into two groups, high (above average) and low (below average) IT intensity firms, where intensity is measured as IT capital per unit of labor. We do so because a firm’s capability to assimilate spilled-over knowledge may depend on prior or current IT use, which may be reflected in IT intensity (Bharadwaj 2000, Mun and Nadiri 2002, Ko et al. 2005). In the IS literature, IT intensity is often regarded as an important factor in determining IT capability (Bharadwaj et al. 1999, Bharadwaj 2000). In this sense, it may be easier for high IT intensity firms to accumulate superior IT knowledge (Boynton et al. 1994).<sup>11</sup> Further, we are able to reduce potential heterogeneity between high and low IT intensity firms by splitting the sample based on the magnitude of IT intensity. ## 5. Empirical Estimation and Results

In this section, we first use the error correction model (ECM) to examine the magnitude of IT-related spillovers. The ECM is particularly useful when economic time series like investments and output levels follow a nonstationary stochastic process (Nelson and Plosser 1982) as an alternative to traditional regression techniques by separating short-run adjustments from the long-run economic relationship. Then, we further analyze the dynamic patterns of IT-related spillovers by using the vector autoregressive (VAR) model. This enables us to examine whether IT-related spillovers persist over time.

## 5.1. Estimating the Error Correction Model

As discussed in §3.1, we adopt the extended Cobb-Douglas production function with spillovers (Equation (1)) to describe the production process. After taking logs, Equation (1) reduces to

$$
\begin{array}{r} y _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {i} + \alpha_ {1} k _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {2} l _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {3} i t _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {4} s p 1 _ {i j t} \\ + \alpha_ {5} s p 2 _ {i j t} + c o n t r o l s, \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where lower-case variables denote logarithms. Control variables include industry and year dummies, and capacity utilization approximated by employment fluctuation to capture business cycle effects (Griliches 1998).

Prior to estimating the production function, we outline our estimation strategy. First, we examine the time-series properties of the input variables, spillover terms, and output variables. However, economic data are often not long enough to apply time-series techniques. Therefore, we examine the time-series properties by combining cross-sectional dimensions in a panel data context. Correspondingly, we check whether individual variables follow a stationary or nonstationary process in the panel data context (see Banerjee 1999 for an extensive review). Once we confirm the nonstationarity of data, we then see if a cointegrated relationship exists between dependent and independent variables. Engle and Granger (1987) define variables $y _ { i j t }$ and $x _ { i j t }$ as cointegrated if they are integrated of the same order and if a vector $\phi$ exists that makes $u _ { i j t } = y _ { i j t } - \phi x _ { i j t }$ stationary.<sup>14</sup> If $y _ { t }$ and $x _ { t }$ are cointegrated, we can construct the ECM because a cointegrated relationship guarantees the existence of the ECM (Engle and Granger 1987, Pesaran et al. 1999).

To examine the time-series properties of the variables in Equation (4), we exploit recent developments in testing the stationarity of panel data based on the residual-based augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) tests (Maddala and Wu 1999, Pedroni 1999, Im et al. 2003). Importantly, these panel-based estimation techniques work well in short time periods (Harris and Tzavalis 1999, Pesaran 2007). We are not able to reject the null hypothesis that the economic variables follow a nonstationary process when they are tested in levels.<sup>15</sup> Further, each individual variable becomes stationary in first differences. Therefore, we conclude that our sample is of integrated order (1). Because both dependent and independent variables are integrated of order (1), the differences between them may be stable around fixed values (stationary) if they are cointegrated. We conduct the cointegration test by examining the stationarity of the residual $u _ { i j t }$ because a cointegrated relationship would make the difference $( u _ { i j t } = y _ { i j t } - \phi x _ { i j t } )$ a stationary process. We are able to confirm the cointegrated relationship between output and inputs and find it to be significant at the 1% level.

Because Equation (4) is cointegrated, we can build the ECM in the panel data context (Ong and Maxam 1997, Pesaran et al. 1999, Breitung 2005). The intuition of the ECM is that economic agents tend to return to equilibrium over time by adjusting for previous equilibrium errors.<sup>16</sup> Note that whereas $u _ { i j t }$ follows a stationary process, what we observe in the real world is a disequilibrium relationship because the residual $u _ { i j t } \neq 0$ . Therefore, we formulate a dynamic relation between output and traditional inputs with spillovers from Equation (4) by adding lagged inputs and output as follows (Maddala 1992, Maddala and Kim 1999, Pesaran et al. 1999):

$$
\begin{array}{l} y _ {i j t} = \alpha_ {i} + \alpha_ {1} k _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {2} l _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {3} i t _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {4} s p 1 _ {i j t} + \alpha_ {5} s p 2 _ {i j t} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {1} k _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {2} l _ {i j t - 1} + \beta_ {3} i t _ {j i t - 1} + \beta_ {4} s p 1 _ {i j t - 1} \\ \qquad + \beta_ {5} s p 2 _ {i j t - 1} + \rho y _ {i j t - 1} + c o n t r o l s + u _ {i j t}. \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

After some simple algebraic manipulation, we obtain the ECM form as follows:

## 5.2. Estimating the Duration of IT-Related Spillovers Using the VAR Model

The previous discussion suggests that the IT-related spillovers from the IT services industry and from other industries are significant. However, these results are not informative about the dynamic pattern of IT-related spillovers. Given that knowledge can be transmitted through economic transactions with trading partners, these spillovers can have sustained impacts on productivity in the long run. Therefore, it is worthwhile to examine the fluctuations of IT-related spillovers over time using the structural vector autoregressive model (VAR). In this type of empirical setting, we are able to examine whether external shocks such as policy changes, technology, or spillovers have a transitory or sustained effect (Blanchard and Quah 1989, Gali 1999). As in the case of the ECM, the structural VAR model was originally applied to time-series data but has since been extended to the panel context by allowing for firm heterogeneity (Holtz-Eakin et al. 1988, Campbell and Lapham 2004). Accordingly, we are able to utilize a relatively short time period for the description of dynamics among variables.

To capture the impacts of IT-related spillovers on output over time, we specify a structural vector autoregressive (VAR) model based on Equation (1), and after taking logs and first differences:<sup>22</sup>

$$
B X _ {t} = C + \Gamma X _ {t - 1} + U _ {t}\tag{7}
$$

$$

$$

In Equation (7), the growth rate of output and spillover index variables are related to their own lagged and current values, reflecting the interplay between growth of output (ãy5 and spillover indices (ãsp1, and ãsp25. However, the structural VAR model (Equation (7)) does not provide consistent estimates using OLS because the independent variables are not orthogonal to the error terms. Therefore, we estimate the parameters in Equation (7) using OLS after converting it to a reduced form in which each endogenous variable is specified purely with exogenous variables (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1995, Gali 1999, Balmaseda et al. 2000).<sup>23</sup> However, it is important to note that structural parameters cannot be identified from the reduced form because it does not convey sufficient information for the estimation of the larger number of parameters (Hamilton 1994, Greene 2000). In other words, it is not possible to recover structural form parameters from the parameters obtained from a reduced form without additional restrictions. To this end, we impose the restrictions that productivity has no permanent effect on the spillover index variables because we have no prior reason to assume that ITrelated spillovers are determined by productivity as in Equation (7).<sup>24</sup> Once structural parameters are identified from the reduced form, the estimated parameters and covariance matrix are used to track the interactions and feedback among variables through the associated impulse response functions (IRFs)<sup>25</sup> (Hamilton 1994, Dolado and Jimeno 1997, Pauwels 2004). The underlying idea is that we can rewrite the VAR model into the mathematically equivalent moving average (MA) form that is the basis for IRFs. Then, we compute the association of the IT-related spillovers in period t with the fluctuations in output in future periods. We see that the impact of IT-related spillovers transmitted from the IT services industry persists for a long time and stabilizes at a nonzero constant in high IT intensity firms. Specifically, a 1% increase in the spillover index from the IT services industry (sp1) in the current period triggers output growth of 0.002% in year 10. This is consistent with the notion that IT-related spillovers decay slowly and are not completely depleted, as would be the case if spillovers have knowledgelike properties. The impact of ITrelated spillovers transmitted from the IT services industry does, however, disappear after four years in low IT intensity firms. This analysis points to the importance of a receiving firm’s IT intensity to realize long-term spillover benefits from the IT services industry.

In the case of the spillover index for other industries (sp2), the impact of IT-related spillovers converges to zero without regard to the level of IT intensity. We conjecture that economic transactions with other industries are less likely to involve the transfer of IT-related knowledge, which reduces the likelihood of persistent gains. Although the coefficients of the spillover terms for trades with other industries in both high and low IT intensity firms are also significant (see Table 3), their effects may be more likely to be rent associated (less knowledge), and therefore transitory. We note that lagged impacts of IT spillovers on output growth can occur due to the time it takes for firms to fully utilize IT-related knowledge. For example, building complementary assets is likely to take time and can defer the payoff. However, this alone does not guarantee the sustained and positive impact of IT-related spillovers, as we saw in Figure 1.

Our analysis provides evidence that the impacts of IT-related spillovers are sustained only when they accrue from transactions with IT services firms and when recipient firms are relatively IT intensive. In this case, it leads to larger returns over a long-term horizon. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that the impacts of IT-related spillovers will decay over time and converge to zero after the sample period. Accordingly, we must be cautious in generalizing these results. Further, one drawback of the VAR approach is that it assumes that all the error terms across equations are orthogonal. However, a common economic shock may have an influence on both the productivity and spillover equations simultaneously in the vector Equation (7). In such a case, the VAR model might give unreliable estimates (Faust and Leeper 1997). Despite these limitations, we argue that the VAR analysis sheds new insight on the persistence of spillover effects in the long run.

## 6. Concluding Remarks

This paper examines the effect of spillovers on the magnitude and duration of IT returns over a longterm horizon. Our analysis provides evidence of ITrelated spillovers from the IT services industry and from other industries with which a firm trades. Interestingly, a large portion of spillover benefits results from transactions with the IT services industry. The accrual of IT-related spillovers from the IT services industry is facilitated by own IT investment, which proxies the learning and accumulated knowledge acquired from prior use of IT. Accordingly, high IT intensity firms receive greater benefits from externalities than low IT intensity firms. This argument is consistent with the country-level study by Dewan and Kraemer (2000) that shows that the productivity impacts of IT are statistically significant in developed countries, but developing countries do not accrue significant returns from their IT investments due to the lack of complementary factors such as IT infrastructure and experienced human capital.

Using VAR analysis, we find that the impact of spillovers is more persistent when they accrue from the IT services industry and when a receiving firm’s IT intensity is relatively high, persisting in this case for over 10 years in high IT intensity firms. However, we do not find sustained spillover benefits from the IT services industry in low IT intensity firms. Finally, the spillover benefits from other industries converge to zero in all firms.

Our study makes several significant contributions. First, by examining the role and implications of externalities such as IT-related spillovers, we help develop a more comprehensive understanding of the means by which output growth results from IT investment. We incorporate spillovers into the production function and estimate the long-term effects of IT using advanced econometric techniques. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to include a consideration of externalities in an analysis of the longterm impacts of IT on firm-level productivity. Our findings also offer a new explanation of the source of unusually high IT returns in certain firms. Our analysis shows that the estimated magnitude of private IT returns is much larger when the impacts of IT-related spillovers are not taken into account. Accordingly, our research suggests that prior studies may overestimate the magnitude of private IT returns by not separating these two sources.

Our results have several important implications for managers. First, they must recognize that there are significant potential benefits to be had from the spillovers that accrue when interacting with IT services providers and trading partners. In particular, firms can reap larger and more persistent benefits from the knowledge accumulated in the IT services industry and represented in industry best practices when they have made their own substantial investments in IT. The results point to the critical importance of investing not just in IT, but in the ongoing capability to develop and assimilate knowledge associated with these IT investments, which will help a firm absorb spilled-over knowledge from its trading partners. Our findings demonstrate the value of participating in forums that facilitate the sharing of IT-related knowledge and industry best practices. Implicitly, the results also indicate that firms should consider the accumulated expertise of their trading partners and IT service providers in their sourcing decisions. Furthermore, the long-term nature of many types of IT investments such as ERP systems and the sustained duration of the payoff suggests that managers must consider the appropriate planning horizon in their financial analysis and also account for the benefits of spillover and learning by doing.

Although our analysis provides a clear explanation of IT-related spillovers, this study is not without limitations. First, due to the lack of available data, the time period for our analysis extends from 1987 to 1994. An eight-year period might be too short to fully examine the time-series properties of the data. For example, we cannot rule out the possibility that the impacts of IT may be sustained for several years and then return to equilibrium. In such a case, we might overestimate the impact of IT, especially for high IT intensity firms. However, we are able to improve the power of our test statistics by pooling cross-sectional information. Second, our data set does not cover the post-1994 period. Accordingly, we do not directly study the magnitude and pattern of IT-related spillovers in more recent years where IT investments have continued to increase and where purchased services appear to reflect a growing share of IT spending (Klein et al. 2003). At the industry level, Cheng and Nault (2007) find that the magnitudes of IT spillovers are not fundamentally different between the two sample periods of 1987–1993 and 1994–1999. Moreover, in his meta-analysis of IT productivity studies, Stiroh (2004) finds that there is no fundamental difference in IT returns between the two periods 1987–1995 and 1996–2000. He finds that the output elasticity of computer capital is not significantly different between the two sample periods, whereas telecommunication capital has a larger elasticity in the later period. Given the increased share of IT spending allocated to the IT services industry in the post-2000 period, spillover effects most likely will have increased. Taken together, we cautiously argue that the effects of IT-related spillovers are no less important after 1994, but it would still be informative to examine whether and how these effects are affected by the increase in trading volumes. Third, our data set consists of large firms, which could mean that the contributions of IT and spillovers are overestimated because firms that are successful in taking advantage of IT are more likely to be represented in the sample. Fourth, we use industry-level transactions to account for the trading volume of a firm with the IT services industry and with other industries. Although this is a standard approach, further research would benefit from a richer data set. Finally, we do not account for the role of labor mobility in explaining spillover effects. It would be an interesting future study to examine whether the movement of professionals fosters the flows of knowledge to other firms. Despite these limitations, we believe that our research provides valuable insights into whether ITrelated spillovers exist, and when and how spillovers play a critical role in creating extra value from IT investments.

## Appendix 3. Test of Nonstationarity

We provide a brief description of the panel unit-root test to examine stationarity of data and report the test results. Like the traditional time-series based unit-root test for testing nonstationarity of data, panel unit root tests have a similar structure as follows.

$$
\Delta u _ {i, t} = \theta_ {i} u _ {i, t - 1} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p _ {j}} \phi_ {i j} u _ {i, t - j} + \varepsilon_ {i, t},\tag{C1}
$$

where $i = 1 , 2 , \ldots , N , \ t = 1 , 2 , \ldots , T .$ 1 and p is lag order to reflect serial correlation.<sup>27</sup>

Equation (C1) assumes that an economic variable $( u _ { i , t } )$ depends on its own lag structure. We say that $u _ { i , t }$ follows a unit-root process if it is dominated by a randomwalk process. Therefore, the null hypothesis of unit roots is defined as:

$$
H _ {o} \colon \theta_ {i} = 0 \quad \text { for   all } i.\tag{C2}
$$

If  equals 0, original Equation (C2) reduces to

$$
u _ {i, t} = u _ {i, t - 1} + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {p _ {j}} \phi_ {i j} u _ {i, t - j} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}.\tag{C3}
$$

Therefore, $u _ { i , t }$ is dominated by the unit-root process if $\theta _ { i }$ equals 0, making the time-series process nonstationary. The basic idea of the panel unit-root test is to combine information from the time and cross-sectional dimensions to increase its power. It is especially useful when the timeseries dimension is not long enough because it also exploits other cross-sectional data (Banerjee 1999). We conduct several tests based on Im et al. (2003) and Maddala and Wu (1999), which test the nonstationarity of panel data. In Tables C.1 and C.2, we define MW1 as the Maddala and Wu test with an individual intercept, MW2 as the Maddala and Wu test with both individual intercept and trend, IPS1 as the Im et al. test with individual intercept, and IPS2 as the Im et al. test with individual intercept and trend. In the case of no intercept and no trend, we use the augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test (Maddala and Wu 1999), shown as MW3 in the tables. In general, we are not able to reject the null hypothesis that each series follows a unit-root process when they are tested in levels (see Table C.1). However, the variable becomes stationary after taking first differences (see Table C.2). Therefore, we conclude that our sample is of integrated order (1).
