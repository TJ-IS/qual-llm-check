# Mandates and technology acceptance: A tale of two enterprise technologies

Bongsug Chae<sup>a,</sup>\*, Marshall Scott Poole<sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Management, College of Business, Kansas State University, Manhattan, KS 66506, USA <sup>b</sup>Department of Information and Operations Management, Mays School of Business and Department of Communication, Texas A&M University, College Station, TX 77843-4217, USA

Available online 12 May 2005

## Abstract

Enterprise information systems are usually mandatory. However, surprisingly little research has been directed to the nature and operation of mandates in research on enterprise systems and on information technology implementation. This study takes a closer look at mandates and their role in the implementation and acceptance of enterprise technologies. Our analysis focuses on the implementation of two enterprise level systems in a major state-funded university system in the US: (1) a state-wide financial management information system that is mandated for use by a large university system; and (2) a system-wide financial and administrative information system that is designed at the university system level and mandated for the individual units of the university system. The analysis offers a deeper look at mandates and is intended to surface features not evident when mandates are regarded simply as another type of directive or order from top management. It also illuminates the politics of enterprise system implementation from the perspective of the negotiation of meaning and responsibilities. q 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

Enterprise information systems are usually mandatory: top management makes a decision to implement them and implementation and use are required. Top management support has been shown to be a critical factor in the success of ERPs and other enterprise systems (e.g. Brown and Vessey, 2003), and an important part of such support is normative pressure on employees to learn and utilize the enterprise system (Brown et al., 2002).

Surprisingly little research has been directed to the nature and operation of mandates in research on enterprise systems and on information technology implementation in general. Most previous research has treated the mandate as a relatively simple and straightforward directive, as an order from the top (e.g. Brown et al., 2002). As such, the mandate has been treated as a moderating variable in individual technology acceptance decisions (Venkatesh et al., 2003) and as a stimulus for resistance to IS implementation (e.g. Markus, 1983; Leonard-Barton, 1988).

This study focuses on mandates and their role in the implementation and acceptance of enterprise technologies. Our analysis focuses on the implementation of two enterprise level systems in a major state-funded university system in the US: (1) a state-wide financial management information system that is mandated for use by a large university system; and (2) a system-wide financial and administrative information system that is designed at the university system level and mandated for the individual units of the university system. Several features of the two cases enable us to explore how mandates are issued and socially constructed within the institutional environment and the resulting effects of social construction processes on the force and meaning of the mandates.

This analysis offers a deeper look at mandates and is intended to surface features not evident when mandates are regarded simply as a type of directive or order. It also illuminates the politics of enterprise system implementation from the perspective of the negotiation of meaning and responsibilities. In doing so, the study attempts to answer the call of Brown et al. (2002), p. 292 to ‘better understand the nature of voluntariness and mandatoriness’ in IS implementation.

## 1.1. The concept of mandate and its role in implementation of information technology

Most IS research stresses the ‘mandatoriness’ of mandates. Leonard-Barton (1988) wrote that, in the face of a mandate, the ‘user’s only freedom of choice, assuming that he or she does not want to leave the organization, is how wholeheartedly to accept the innovation’ (p. 604). This view of mandates as orders is reflected in studies which find different determinants of technology acceptance for voluntary and mandated information systems (Brown et al., 2002; Hodgson and AIken, 1998; Ram and Jung, 1991; Rawstorne et al., 2000; Venkatesh et al., 2003).

Brown et al. (2002) argue that mandates for enterprise systems derive their force not only from managerial imperative, but from the nature of the organizational system itself. Large scale IS require coordinated action among users, and as a result they do not allow individual employees the same discretion to reject the IS as the employees might enjoy with more limited and less expensive systems, such as word processing software. Brown et al. (2002) note that ‘[i]n these types of mandated situations, the system must be used to complete one’s own job tasks that are also tightly integrated with the tasks of multiple other job performers’ (p. 284). In such situations the exercise of individual discretion is bound up with consequences for others and so rejection is not a straightforward option for the individual (Gallivan, 2001; Lamb and Kling, 2003; Leonard-Barton, 1988).

However, other studies suggest that the impact of mandates is contingent on how they are interpreted. Karahanna et al. (1999) and Agarwal and Prasad (1997) report that perceptions of the voluntariness of an innovation vary considerably across individuals in an organization. Brown et al. (2002) suggest that there is a continuum of voluntariness ‘such that a given adoption decision may seem more or less voluntary to the individuals involved’ (p. 204). In line with the implications that mandates differ in their degree of mandatoriness is the finding by Robey et al. (2002) that a mandatory ERP was not universally accepted or used.

Studies of resistance in IS implementation have also been indirectly concerned with mandates. Markus’s (1983) classic study discussed how employees and departments resisted mandated information technology because it worked against their interests. Knights and Murray (1994) describe cases of resistance to technologies by managers when it threatens to change the balance of power. In studies in this tradition, the mandate is treated as a stimulus for resistance, but most attention is centered on resistance that occurs around the IT itself during processes or selection and implementation, and not on the role the mandate plays as contested object.

These studies exhibit several commonalities in their approach to mandates. First, they treat the mandate as a black box, a coherent object that individual adopters react to, positively or negatively. They do not consider the structure of the mandate or its meaning to users (the exception is the studies that consider user perceptions of the strength of the mandate). These studies also tend to treat the user’s reaction to the mandate as though it was a singular decision to either obey or reject the mandate (and, sometimes later on, a singular decision to discontinue use). Behavior subsequent to adoption—either acceptance or resistance—is then analyzed without further reference to the mandate. Finally, the studies treat mandates as originating in orders from management. While management always has a hand in transmitting and enforcing mandates, the mandate itself may stem from other sources than a management decision, such as a legal requirement, a regulatory body, or an agreement with another organizational partner. Mandates have more complicated origins than most previous research acknowledges and these other sources can contribute to the force of the mandate. Previous research has not given much consideration to the notion that the mandate and its force might be subject to negotiation or that the mandate might have a continuing role in the ongoing implementation and use of IT.

To better understand the role mandates play in the development and implementation of enterprise IS, it is useful to place them front and center in our analysis. A mandate is defined as an official order or commission to do or not to do something (cf. Jewell and Abate, 2001, p. 1037). In writing about mandates for public organizations Bryson (1988) notes, ‘The formal and informal mandates placed on an organization are ‘the musts’ it confronts’ (p. 49). Organizations and their members are mandated to do and not to do things by legislation, ordinances, charters, articles, contracts, and decisions taken by management. Bryson observes that organizations and their members may also have informal mandates, things they believe they must do or not do based on their mission, traditions, culture, and interpretation of what their customers, publics, or other stakeholders want. However, in our view, this is an overly broad interpretation that risks pulling almost everything into the concept of mandate, so we will restrict our discussion to formal mandates.

Mandates are typically not just simple directives or orders, but also have descriptions of what to do or not to do, accompanied by regulations and documents describing how the mandate must be put into effect. Even when a mandate is framed as a simple directive, attempts to follow it generally generate more detailed policies and procedures that are attached to and become part of the mandate. Orders and how to follow them must be explained as well, and this necessitates texts in addition to the order itself.

As we have noted, mandates are subject to interpretation, and this adds another layer of complexity. Those charged with following mandates must work out what the mandate means, what it requires them to do, the degree of leeway or ‘wiggle room’ there is in the mandate, and, if they are a manager, how to enforce the mandate with those further down the chain of command. The interpretation of mandates is fundamentally social in nature. If the organization and its members are to act on the mandate, shared interpretations must be developed. In some cases this interpretive process is formalized—as when a legal department develops interpretations of provisions of a law or set of regulations—and in others it is less formal—as when a group of employees meet to determine how they must change to accommodate the mandate. In most cases the issuers of a mandate are involved in the interpretive process as well, either in a consultative or constraining role. The interpretation of mandates cascades down through organizations, involving units and ultimately individuals the mandate applies to.

As Giddens (1984) argues, interpretation is inextricably bound up with norms, resources, and power structures. Hence, interpretation of mandates is a political activity that hinges in part on how the mandate fits with existing norms and how it might shift resource allocations in the organization. Clearly this entails the possibility of disagreements over interpretation of a mandate. In some instances these disagreements will spark a problem solving process from which a consensus on the mandate will emerge, but in other instances there may be a schism over interpretation of mandates, and different parts of an organization may read and act on the mandate differently. This extends down to the individuals who may go along with or resist the mandate.

The process by which mandates are interpreted and applied (or not) is a process of social construction of mandates by key actors in the organizational system. In referring to social construction, we draw on a long tradition that extends back to Berger and Luckmann (1967) argument that interpretations and enactments of social structures are reified and institutionalized so that they attain the force of taken-for-granted objects. More recently this tradition is embodied in Giddens (1984) theory of structuration. In particular, we conceive of the social construction of mandates as a process of structuration in which actors (including the issuers of the mandate and those affected by it) draw on organizational rules and resources to advance and maintain particular interpretations of the mandate. They then use these interpretations to legitimate their actions during the implementation of the IT. The mandate itself is both as a structural feature that can be drawn on during the development and implementation of the system and as an outcome of the process, exhibiting the duality of structure that Giddens advanced as a critical feature of structuration. The particular interpretation(s) of the mandate that has (have) been negotiated and stabilized at any point in time represent the premises that units and members assume when acting within (or resisting) the mandate.

The structuration of mandates occurs over time and across the space of the organizational system. Social construction occurs as the mandate is interpreted and enforced over the lengthy period of time involved in the development and implementation of an enterprise system. Articulation of interpretations over time can stabilize the mandate, while introduction of competing interpretations can lead to shifts or drift of the mandate. Social construction also may lead to local variations on the mandate in different parts of a large organization. Social construction also occurs as various parties engage in political actions that seek to undermine the mandate or to change it in ways advantageous to their interests. Our analysis highlights the fact that social construction is not something that occurs external to the mandate, but is part of the fabric of the mandate itself. Social construction is critical to the meaning of the mandate for units and individuals in the organization and, hence, to the impact the mandate has on system development and implementation. In this sense, this study is in the spirit of early work on the negotiation of mandates for planned change (e.g. Bryson, 1988; Kanter, 1983).

Social construction of the mandates for enterprise IS does not occur in a vacuum, but is influenced by both technical and institutional factors. In terms of technical factors, the role of embedded IT infrastructure and technological capabilities are likely to influence how mandates are interpreted and applied. The IT infrastructure provides a frame for understanding both what the mandate requires from a technical point of view and how the mandate might be implemented. Embedded IT infrastructure constrains what is possible and in some cases acquires an institutional force in its own right, as a standard to which implementation of the mandate must conform. Technical competencies of the organization are likely to evolve along with its IT infrastructure, and also place constraints on how the mandate can be carried out. Competencies also shape mandates when the technical experts are involved in interpreting the mandate.

Institutional factors that influence the social construction of mandates include the organization’s culture and history, other organizations or bodies that issue, interpret, and enforce mandates, and the general institutional environment in which the organization operates. Organizational culture and history are important because they present ‘precedents’ regarding how previous mandates have been dealt with. Organizations that issue and enforce mandates often offer suggestions as to how they can be interpreted and their enforcement regimens give some suggestions as to how tightly the organization must hew to the letter of the mandate. Consultants, user groups, and peer organizations are examples of other types of organizations that may also enter into the interpretive process. The general institutional environment of the organization also influences interpretation of mandates. For example, in a tightly regulated environment, such as the health care industry, mandates are likely to be taken much more seriously than they would be in an environment that is more loosely regulated.

To explore the social construction of mandates, we conducted a qualitative study of the development and implementation of an enterprise information system within a major university system in US and a state-wide financial management information system in a US state government. The two systems had many characteristics that made them suitable for this study. Both systems were introduced with a strong mandate in place. Both were also implemented in complex organizational systems comprised of very diverse units, which led us to expect the types of variation in initial fit to the enterprise system and in unit interests that would stimulate efforts to interpret and perhaps change the initial mandate.

In these two cases, the operative level of analysis was the unit level, rather than individual adoption and use decisions. Typically units—universities, colleges, state agencies—purchased and maintained their own hardware and software and the top managers and IT departments of these units made the relevant decisions. In some cases the relevant decision maker was an individual, but in others it was a small group in which an individual leader took responsibility for group decisions and represented the unit to the rest of the system. It is also important to note that units had histories of dealing with both the larger system and IT in particular ways and that these histories affected immediate decisions and stances.

## 2. Methods

The context of this research consists of two large-scale, organizational technologies, which we will call the University System Management Information System (USMIS) and Statewide Management Information System (SWMIS). The USMIS was expected to be adopted by the units of a state-wide university system [Land Grant University System (LGUS)] and SWMIS was expected to be adopted by the Land Grant University System as a whole, as one among a number of state agencies. In case of SWMIS, the IS originated outside the university system, while in the case of USMIS it was developed within the system in response to external pressures. Both cases exhibit strong institutional pressures, but differ in the locus and nature of the organizational response. In both cases, the adopters are not individuals, but units of the system, each a rather large organization in its own right. In each case the IS was introduced and championed by two powerful organizations, the State in the case of SWMIS and the LGUS in the case of USMIS.

Data collection initially focused on the development, implementation, and technology adoption of USMIS by members of LGUS from the earliest inception of the system in the late 1980s to the present. As we were conducting the case study of USMIS, we also gathered data relevant to the LGUS’s reaction to SWMIS. It became evident that SWMIS offered a further opportunity to understand technology acceptance, so it was included as a second case.

Multiple data sources were employed (Eisenhardt, 1989). Data included archival records, interviews, direct observation and physical artifacts. Document collection was accomplished through follow-ups on interviews, reading private collections of documents by selected key decision makers and adopters, site-visits, library search, archival research at the USMIS project site, contacting the Department of Information Resources in the State, and information provided by software vendors. We sought a diverse group of people in the interview in order to surface as many different accounts as possible. Twenty-five interviews were conducted between October 2001 and October 2002. Those interviewed included the initial sponsors of the USMIS system, the former and current USMIS project management, CFOs of LGUS’ units, members of IT steering committees at the system and unit level, unit-level and department-level users, directors of IT departments in a number of units, system analysts involved in implementing the USMIS system, and a university system-level IT auditor. The format of the interviews was semi-structured in that each participant was asked some general, open-ended questions, and then asked to expand on various issues that seemed important. The format combined elements of the interview guide approach, standardized open-ended interviews and dialogic interviews (Rossman and Rallis, 1998). The average duration of the interviews was over 70 min.

We identified a few broad themes (e.g. mandatoriness, politics, outside social institutions, leadership change) and framed them as questions as a stimulus for the participants to discuss their perspectives on the systems and the events surrounding their development and implementation. Standard themes and consistent ordering of themes insured that we gathered comparable data across the multiple sites. As subjects answered the questions we engaged them in dialogue, attempting to engage in ‘true conversations in which researcher and participant together develop a more complex and understandings of the topic’ (Rossman and Rallis, 1998, p. 125).

Rather than taping the interviews, we took handwritten notes to reduce the possibility that interviewees might feel uncomfortable if their confidences about politically charged issues were recorded. Within a few hours after each interview, the first author committed these notes and additional recollections to a digital file as soon as feasible after each interview. As a first step in the analysis, the first author wrote research memos detailing ideas and themes that seemed to stand out and questions that arose during the interview. The memos contained any cues (e.g. tone, mood) before, during, and after the interview, his interpretation of events, diagrams with events and other contextual information, a list of potential individuals identified for future interviews, and relevant studies from IS literature related to the themes and ideas from the interview. As more and more interviews were conducted, the researchers read the entire interview record along with the original memos and added further comments and notes by reflecting the tense and mood of each sentence or comment by interviewees. Later these memos were combined into interim case summaries that were then developed into the broader case studies presented here.

After repeated cycles of analysis, several themes emerged. These themes were used to return to the collected documents and interview transcripts and to investigate and reinterpret them. This also suggested questions to raise in future interviews and followups. In the course of the analysis, tables and matrix displays (Miles and Huberman, 1994) were also used to organize such data as organizational background, leadership changes, organizational response to the IS projects, types/modes and sources of institutional forces, and timeline of the development and implementation of both projects. Considerations of space prevent us from giving full details of the research methodology. A copy of a more detailed description of the methods is available on request from the first author.

To help us in finalizing our conclusions, we developed and shared a case report that included a case summary and analysis with a senior system analyst, the current USMIS project manager, and a top IT officer in LGUS who were involved with the USMIS project from its inception. They reviewed the analysis and conclusions, and confirmed and elaborated the identified issues and conclusions drawn.

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Basic chronology of the two cases

## 3.1.1. SWMIS

State legislators have long promoted information technology, particularly the use of automated information systems, for more than 200 state agencies and institutions of higher education. In 1987, legislation was enacted that required the State Comptroller’s Office to develop uniform procedures for the collection and reporting of state-wide payroll and personnel data. At the direction of the legislature, the SWMIS Committee was established to develop an automated financial and accounting information system. In 1988, the SWMIS Committee recommended a design for an automated information system for all agencies of the state government, including institutions of higher education. State signed a contract with Andersen Consulting in 1990 and following system design, development and testing from 1990 to 1992, the SWMIS commenced operation in 1993. The ideal scenario for its champions was that there would be ‘one financial information system’ that would replace all other State financial information systems. SWMIS incorporates both Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and cash-basis accounting and captures accounting activities supplied by state agencies and institutions of higher education in the state. Financial data in SWMIS is used by the comptroller’s office to generate payments, agency reports, legislative reports, and reports for appropriation management and state-wide budgets. SWMIS also performs specialized functions, such as budgetary and encumbrance accounting, cost allocation, payment processing and document tracking.

The implementation effort that began in 1993 has been only partially successful to date. Currently, less than half (over 130) of the agencies in State are using SWMIS as their primary financial system.

## 3.1.2. USMIS

USMIS is an enterprise information system that provides general financial and budgetary management for the units of LGUS. It was first introduced in 1990 for fiscal year 1991. The MIS project team has been responsible for the development and support of USMIS since the late 1980s.

The LGUS is one of the more complex systems of higher education in the US. Currently LGUS consists of nine universities and eight state research and extension organizations in the areas of agriculture, engineering, veterinary science, and forest service (Table 1).

A major factor in the development of USMIS was the growth of LGUS. During the 1970s and 1980s LGUS experienced tremendous growth in student enrolment, research expenditures, and overall budget. Between 1989 and 1995 eight universities and research agencies joined LGUS. This growth led top administrators and particularly the LGUS Board of Regents to recognize the need for comparable and consolidated information to properly manage the \$800 million annual operating budget.

In the mid-1980s, the President and managers in the fiscal office of Big Campus within LGUS initiated a project to develop a campus-level system that provided capabilities for decision support, executive reporting, online purchasing, budgeting, planning, investment management, streamlined integration across departments and colleges, and other functions. This idea, initially intended only for the single campus, was adopted by the LGUS system administration, and the scope of the project was expanded to the development of a large-scale financial and administrative information system (USMIS) for all units in LGUS. The initiators of USMIS included several top administrators from the HQ and Big campus. They later served on the USMIS Steering Committee.

## 3.2. Mandates and implementation: mission incomplete

## 3.2.1. SWMIS

As previously noted, the motivation for SWMIS was that uniform financial and accounting information would provide more reliable, cost-effective state-wide and agency-level financial information to the Comptroller’s Office. This would enable State to reduce its vulnerability to errors, irregularities, and fraud. Initially the use of SWMIS by state agencies (including universities) was mandated by the State, particularly the Comptroller’s Office. The following excerpts from State statutes describe the introduction of SWMIS and provide the basic substance of the mandate (parenthetical phrases added by authors).

The uniform statewide accounting project (SWMIS) is in the comptroller’s office. The project includes each component of the uniform statewide accounting system as designed in accordance with Acts of the 70th Legislature 1987<sup>.</sup>The comptroller by rule may require state agencies to modify, delay, or stop the implementation of individual accounting and payroll systems so that those systems are compatible with the uniform statewide accounting system. The comptroller may require a state agency to replace its internal accounting and payroll system with project components to provide uniformity in internal accounting.

It is interesting to note that the wording appears to leave some discretion to the Comptroller as to whether to make SWMIS mandatory for various agencies and also as to whether an equivalent system that met the same standards was an option for State agencies. This wording provided openings for agencies to advance their own interpretations of the mandate that offered resistance to the comptroller’s design to implement a single uniform system.

The SWMIS design project was completed in August 1988. According to the State Auditor’s report on SWMIS, the original report by the SWMIS project team to the SWMIS Committee in 1988 recommended a design for an automated accounting system which could be used by all State agencies, including institutions of higher education like LGUS. According to a top administrator at LGUS similar information systems had been designed and implemented in other states for use by all agencies, including universities.

From the perspective of the Comptroller’s Office, the Legislature, and the executive branch of State, it was desirable to maximize the number of users of SWMIS. The more users there were, the greater the benefits from uniformity in accounting procedures, a higher degree of control over State agencies, and, in addition, economies of scale. There was, however, another compelling reason to press for wide ranging adoption of SWMIS: the cost of the system would be too great to justify unless most state agencies adopted and use SWMIS.

The legislation authorizing SWMIS gave the Comptroller’s Office the authority to require agencies to adopt it. However, the Comptroller’s Office did not have direct power of enforcement, though it could audit agencies and indicate noncompliance with standards. These audits could then be used by the Governor, and later by the Legislature, as grounds for compelling implementation, if they judged this was in the interests of the state. This indirect circuit of power put the Comptroller’s Office in the difficult position of holding authority without power and encouraged a negotiative orientation toward potential users of SWMIS.

At first, there was enthusiasm in the SWMIS project team for a single, mandatory system. However, as the project unfolded, the team eventually recognized that ‘one system for the entire state’ was not a goal that could be realized because of variability among State agencies in terms of their size and the diversity and their unique requirements.

While the executive branch had control of the purse strings and the ability to issue executive orders, lower level agencies also had resources. One member of the USMIS Steering Committee observed:

Actually the state wanted every state agency to use SWMIS, but things in universities were very different so it was impossible to use SWMIS. Kevin [one of the authors], your boss [the State] cannot tell you [LGUS] directly what to do, maybe indirectly.

One top administrator of a research agency in LGUS observed that large state agencies had considerable power vis-a\`-vis the Comptroller’s office. He noted that LGUS made important contributions to the state, including resources brought in by tuition and grants and the important mission LGUS carried out. The administrator concluded, ‘We (LGUS) are a big customer for the State. They [the State] ask us ‘what can we do to help you?’’ The LGUS used its unique mission as leverage to advance an interpretation of the mandate that exempted it from adopting SWMIS. Instead it interfaced the financial system it was currently implementing, USMIS, with SWMIS.

LGUS argued for unique needs in part because it did not want its operations to be reviewed and controlled by the State. One respondent noted that ‘There was a kind of institutional logic involved<sup>.</sup>LGUS feared losing control due to SWMIS’. The former director of the USMIS project team at LGUS noted that

Around 1988 people [the State and specifically the State’s comptroller’s office] pushed to develop one common major IS for every state agency. This was a huge institutional force. The idea was ‘one system for everyone’ in the state. I met those people and convinced them that I could develop a big system [USMIS] which could support all parts of LGUS and provide an interface with SWMIS. They liked the idea.

The USMIS project was welcomed by the SWMIS project team, since it would provide the Comptroller’s Office with a single channel to communicate with all units in LGUS. Even though the path LGUS took undermined the goal of a single system, it seemed to be the path of least resistance to all parties involved. The project team expressed the hope that USMIS would be developed in a way consistent with SWMIS.

SWMIS is currently used as the internal financial management information system by over 130 agencies, somewhat less than half of the total number of agencies in the State. The other agencies and over 60 universities and colleges continue to maintain their own internal financial accounting systems. They generate separate reports that are incorporated into the central SWMIS database.

## 3.2.2. USMIS

As noted earlier, two primary goals of USMIS were (1) to meet the legal requirements and regulations imposed by the State and the Comptroller’s office and (2) to have ‘one information system’ for everyone in the LGUS. The second goal is very close to the original, underlying vision for SWMIS, which aimed to create ‘one IS for everyone in the State’. However, the adoption process turned out to be more challenging and complex than was envisioned at the outset. The original implementation plan projected a 4-year period for implementation of the fully designed USMIS in all units of LGUS. The initial projection was as follows:

1991—Adoption by Big campus

1992—Adoption by South campus and a major research agency

1993—Adoption by Southeast campus and second major research agency

1994—Adoption by all remaining units

But the actual infusion turned out to be a long (and ultimately incomplete) process, as shown below.

1990—Adopted by three units (Big campus, WMS, HQ) in LGUS

1991—Adopted by five units

1992—Adopted by three units

1993—Adopted by two units

1994—Adopted by one unit

1996—Adopted by three units

1999—Adopted by one unit

Currently—Three units not yet committed to USMIS

The nature of the mandate behind USMIS was one factor behind incomplete and halting implementation of USMIS. The original resolution from the Board of Regents contained the following directive to LGUS (excerpts from the minutes of the Board of Regents 1987):

Whereas [one of big five consulting firms] recently completed a management audit of State’s higher education for the Select Committee on higher education<sup>.</sup>Whereas to demonstrate the continued commitment of LGUS to effective resource management and cost-effectiveness, [LGUS] will initiate and/or accelerate a number of analyses and actions designed to insure strong and effective fiscal and resources management<sup>.</sup>Therefore be it resolved that the Board of Regents of the Land Grant University System authorizes the Chancellor to initiate and/or accelerate the following analyses and actions: Centralize cash and investment management functions and procedures<sup>.</sup>Require monthly reports from System parts to the Chancellor<sup>.</sup>Design and implement management information systems that will insure compatibility and consolidatability of accounting and fiscal information, analysis, and reports from all System parts.

A memorandum from one of the highest ranking officers in HQ to one of the research agencies in 1993 stated:

It is my understanding that<sup>.</sup>the Board directive is very clear. Their directive calls for The System to coordinate the joint development of an administrative system (USMIS). Additionally they have directed that efforts and costs should be expended for the development and operation of USMIS and not to develop maintain and operate independent administrative accounting systems.

This represents a bid to interpret the resolution as a firm mandate for uniform adoption and use of USMIS. The memo strikes an implicit bargain with units: USMIS must meet the needs of each unit and in turn the units will use USMIS. In cases in which units can argue that their needs are not met, this opens the door to arguments that they should be excepted from USMIS.

In the initial stages of USMIS implementation, the Advanced Certification Document, the official plan for the system, stated clearly that USMIS would serve as an integrated fiscal and administrative information system for all units of LGUS. The document recognized the importance of ‘full adoption’ to realize substantial savings and other benefits from USMIS. This understanding was shared by many units. The former director of the USMIS project team noted, ‘I went out and told the units of LGUS, you got to be on USMIS.

This is not an option. No alternative is allowed.’ The Chancellor and Board of Regents were very supportive of USMIS design and implementation as the system was developed and when it first went live. They strongly supported mandatory implementation of USMIS throughout LGUS.

However, several technical and institutional factors emerged as critical issues in the implementation process. A daunting challenge for the developers was the diversity of users and their needs. Every unit had its own chart of accounts, and accounting practices throughout LGUS were very diverse. Few units wanted to change their local accounting practices due to the overhead involved in learning a new system and rearranging accounts. A single system was not able to accommodate every unit’s needs. The former director of the project noted that ‘one system for everyone’ is ‘nothing for nobody.’ In addition, because of their diverse missions, each unit placed priority on different functionalities. Since not everything can be developed simultaneously, the order in which the USMIS project team tackled and delivered functions was symbolic. Those units whose preferred functions were placed later in the schedule interpreted this as indicating that USMIS would not be designed to meet their needs.

The two engineering research agencies (ERS and EXS) and Northwest Campus were strongly against USMIS. While they acknowledged the need for consolidated reports for system-level management, these units preferred to use their own financial systems and interface them with USMIS. Northwest Campus argued that it had already devoted considerable effort to developing a new student information system and a financial information system and did not want to put the time and resources into yet another implementation process. Particularly ERS and EXS advocated the need to maintain their own information systems based on two arguments. First, they pointed to functional deficiencies in USMIS for the support of contract and grant management and other research related activities. Second, they argued that, as research agencies, they differed from other units whose functions also emphasized teaching and outreach to the State.

In a memorandum in response to the previously cited memo, the head of one of the research agencies responded that:

It is our understanding that The LGUS Board of Regents directed that a management information system be implemented that satisfies the need for consolidated financial information. [Our agency] wishes to comply with the Board’s directive via the ‘compatibility option’ by remaining on its current accounting system (SCT Banner) and providing [our agency] data on a monthly basis to the headquarters in ‘USMIS format.

One research agency’s top administrators and IT manager argued that USMIS was inferior to their own system. Moreover, they noted that the USMIS project team had initially designated research support as a low priority item in the development schedule. A top administrator of the research agency insisted that

We will be asked to pay for a system we do not need nor want. We will be asked to pay for a system that, at the very best, will be mediocre. Our internal system can be interfaced with the USMIS system to provide required data on a monthly or more frequent basis.

Another respondent from a research agency argued: ‘We are not universities. We are research agencies that are very different from universities<sup>.</sup>.USMIS is for universities, not for us’. That USMIS had originally grown from a design for a financial system for Big Campus gave some backing to this assertion.

One factor that drove this resistance was concerns over power and control. A member of the USMIS Steering Committee noted that

In general, all units of LGUS—and particularly the engineering agencies—were scared that the LGUS would come to know too much about their operations. They thought that USMIS could expose their own operations to the LGUS. They didn’t like this. They wanted to be more independent in operations. There is a kind of institutional logic behind this kind of resistance.

While resisting units did not openly state that adopting USMIS would require them to expose their operations to outside scrutiny, this motivation is strongly suggested in several memoranda from one research agency to the HQ, the MIS project team and Chancellor:

The USMIS solutions for handling indirect costs currently fall short of providing flexibility that [our agency] needs to distribute indirect costs on inter-disciplinary research projects<sup>.</sup>Technically speaking, [our agency] does not have the database and software constraints inherent at the System level.

[Our agency’s information] system should be invisible to the System, Board and State.

The research agencies’ interpretations were not simply words, but were backed up by their considerable clout. Over the years they had brought in large grants and other resources to the LGUS and they were renowned as centers of excellence. Their publicity value was in many ways as important as the tangible resources they provided. In advancing their own claims regarding the force of the mandates, the research agencies were flexing their muscles and also reproducing the conditions that enabled them to remain powerful— their autonomy and control over their own resources.

The conflict between the research agencies and the USMIS project team and HQ was sharp enough to be clearly discerned in LGUS and parties to the USMIS implementation referred to it as ‘The Battle’ (see Chae and Poole, 2005, for a full account of this incident). A top administrator in LGUS observed that

From the beginning of USMIS implementation in 1990 until July 1995, when these agencies were officially waived from using USMIS, there was a six-year battle between the system and them.

In 1995 ERS, EXS, and Northwest campus were officially allowed to establish an interface with USMIS rather than adopting it as their main financial and accounting system.

A key dynamic in The Battle was changes in the Chancellor’s office. In 1991 a new Chancellor, formerly the Deputy Chancellor for Engineering at Big Campus, assumed office. Unlike the first Chancellor, who had been a strong advocate of uniform implementation of USMIS, the second Chancellor weakened pressure for acceptance.

During this Chancellor’s term implementation of USMIS was widely regarded as optional. However, the HQ and MIS project team continued to push for adoption. In 1991 six more units of LGUS became users of USMIS, and in 1992 three units implemented it.

The optional status for USMIS implementation changed dramatically when a third LGUS Chancellor, formerly President of Big Campus, took over. He had been on the steering committee of the original MIS project at Big Campus and thus was very supportive of USMIS. He made implementation mandatory again and announced that all units must be on USMIS. This exacerbated the conflict between HQ and the MIS project team and the units that wanted to avoid using USMIS. A top IT administrator at Big campus recalled that ‘it was not a happy time for everyone’.

The third Chancellor’s term lasted only 1 year. In 1994 the Board of Regents appointed the president of Northwest Campus as the fourth Chancellor to serve during the period of LGUS implementation. The new Chancellor stressed the importance of uniqueness and autonomy of each university and agency in LGUS. While he was not against USMIS implementation, he decided that units could choose not to use USMIS. Notwithstanding, acceptance of USMIS continued to spread. During this Chancellor’s term of office, all units except the two engineering research agencies and Northwest Campus implemented USMIS.

The swings in policy from mandatory to nonmandatory back to mandatory and finally back to nonmandatory created a ‘moving target’ that left considerable room for interpretation on both sides. That the mandate shifted gave resistors confidence that they could wait out the implementation and maintain their own financial systems.

Another factor that played a role in the interpretation of mandate was the organizational culture of the LGUS. Most interviewees noted that traditionally there had been a ‘decentralized culture’ within LGUS. Adoption by all units of the system was constrained by this ‘socially’ installed base. In USMIS, a primary driver of mandate reinterpretation was the organizational culture based on unit autonomy. USMIS promised to undermine financial autonomy and this led to unit resistance. Related to this, a top administrator of a research agency wrote to the headquarters of LGUS:

It is important to clarify the directives of the LGUS Board of Regents<sup>.</sup>Centralization seems to be effective in smaller State systems with less diversity of missions. Traditionally, the HQ had maintained a very workable interpretation of its role by providing overview and governance where a global perspective is necessary and where shared services reap benefits to the LGUS members. But the autonomy of the System members to exercise their authorities and means in order to do a good job is one that members have long cherished. In my opinion, the current USMIS philosophy threatens the traditional role of the HQ and threatens to share service even when such services are costly to some System members.

Not all units resisted the mandate. Some of the smaller units of LGUS were enthusiastic about USMIS because it gave them a powerful financial system that could meet system and State standards without requiring a large outlay that they could not afford. These units saw the mandate not as a threat, but as an opportunity. It eliminated the need to find an adequate financial system and provided it as a resource.

The meaning of the mandate clearly shifted over time. At the outset, when USMIS was a new system, the units seemed to interpret the mandate as a pre-emptory order from above. However, once implemented by a significant portion of units, USMIS became a ‘taken-for-granted’ system by units that later joined LGUS. Once it gained its momentum, the mere staying power of the system became a more powerful force for implementation than the verbally expressed mandate. A CFO of a university campus that joined in 1995 and implemented USMIS in 1996 noted

USMIS adoption was not mandatory for us. I believe we were allowed to choose something different but we did not. In fact there was no real selection process for a new information system. It was not needed. USMIS was our choice. It was natural. At the time many state’s higher institutions had implemented and been using USMIS, so we thought that USMIS would be the best for us.

The mandate for USMIS changed in meaning over time such that by 1995 it was accepted as an ‘institutional fact’ by most units. This taken-for-granted nature of the system is reinforced by the tendency of units who had adopted USMIS to stay with it due to the resources sunk into learning and infusing it into day-to-day operations, uncertainty about alternatives, and political concerns. Enterprise systems such as USMIS tend to lock in users for these reasons. Comments by several top administrators in LGUS reflected this:

There were proposals for replacing USMIS even in 1994, 1995, 1996<sup>.</sup>but these ideas had not materialized at that time. It was very difficult. This was a too big and a difficult task for anyone.

Replacement of USMIS will cost almost \$50 million. Given the magnitude of the project, there is no way we will replace USMIS.

We considered replacement of USMIS before. However people agree that ‘USMIS plays a large role of reporting to the State. People are saying ‘USMIS is functioning OK’ and ‘USMIS is serving OK’. USMIS will be staying around for a number of years.

There is also an internal lock-in. Once multiple users within a unit learn and start to depend on the system there is pressure to continue to use it because of the sunk costs of learning, because of task interdependencies (Brown et al., 2002), and because users eventually grow comfortable with the system (no matter how difficult or awkward it was at first). A top official of Big Campus expressed that ‘the most difficult task in changing to a new IS would be training people.’ As of 2004, there still has not yet been a decision on a new enterprise system to replace USMIS.

## 4. Discussion

These two cases show the complex and dynamic nature of mandates and the contested field on which implementation in response to mandates occurs. The cases have several important implications for our understanding of the implementation of enterprise level systems.

First, the cases show that mandates are more than simple orders to adopt and use IT. Potential users seemed to navigate the voluntary-mandatory continuum discussed by Brown et al. (2002) as the implementation unfolded. At the outset, emphasis was on the mandatory end of the continuum when the order to implement was issued as a fiat by the State Legislature or the LGUS HQ. However, as development and implementation of the systems proceeded, some adopters whose political or financial interests were threatened by the new systems began to search for ‘cracks’ in the mandate and to try out strategies for resisting or redefining them.

Both of the official mandates and their interpretations by top managers implied a ‘social contract’ between the central administration and the units. This contract asked for compliance with the mandate in exchange for assurance that the enterprise system would meet local needs as well or better than current systems did. Difficulties in developing and implementing the systems gave resistors ammunition to argue for exceptions, because such difficulties were generally related to the inability of the enterprise system to meet local needs effectively. This situation created a dialectic of incompatible goals: full adoption versus diversity in local practices and needs. The original goal for SWMIS was ‘one system for over 250 state agencies including universities,’ and for USMIS it was ‘one system for all 20 members of LGUS’. However, diversity and heterogeneity created a negotiative atmosphere that slowed acceptance by all units. The technology and its adoption meant different things locally. A rhetoric of distance developed such that units could argue that the enterprise systems, imposed from above, did not take local needs into account.

Development problems also gave potential resistors time to marshal their resources and arguments and to actively enter into the process of interpreting the mandate. The structuration of the mandate occurred via reinterpretations of the original text by powerful actors with means to make their interpretations stick. The research agencies could marshal their status as resource providers and their constituencies of powerful external actors who had benefited from their work or valued their contribution to the state. Northwest campus had the argument that changing from its newly acquired financial system would constitute a waste of resources and, at one point, a sympathetic Chancellor who had been there when Northwest’s system was implemented and agreed that independent systems were sometimes justified. In buttressing their interpretations with power moves, the resistors were also seeking to reproduce the foundation of their power, their independent status in LGUS.

As we noted at the outset, we would expect to find differing perceptions of mandates across potential users, just as previous research has found. In the LGUS case we can discern several dimensions along which the meaning of mandates differs. Units differed in the degree to which they believed the mandate allowed deviations. Units with stronger power bases in LGUS, such as the engineering research agencies, tended to expect that exceptions could be made, whereas those with less power tended to treat the mandate as an order. In the same way, units also differed in their beliefs about the degree to which the technology could redefine the relationship between unit and larger system. Units also differed in whether they regarded the mandated technology as adequate or just average and therefore inferior, and this seemed to color their reactions to the mandate as a whole.

Organizational factors influenced the social construction process. In LGUS changes in top management gave units the ability to weaken the mandate, and the tradition of decentralization gave them the warrant to do so. In the case of USMIS the Comptroller’s Office did not have the time or resources to enforce the mandate for 250 agencies, which gave units like LGUS the ability to suggest options that left them with greater autonomy.

The cases also highlight the political nature of enterprise system implementation. As LGUS feared losing control due to SWMIS, so members of LGUS also feared losing control due to USMIS. In this process units and their representatives were not equal in their influence on the social construction process. The dialectic of control in which units would make efforts to redefine the mandate and central management would attempt to counter them is illustrated in the memo exchanges related to USMIS. Whether the redefinition was accepted depended in part on the legitimacy of the arguments and in part on the will of top management of systems. In both cases will (or at least means to impose it) was lacking, which resulted in a weakening of the mandate.

It is commonplace to think of politics as a negative process that detracts from organizational functioning. However, it is not clear that this was entirely true in either case. While several informants’ comments portrayed the political process as counterproductive, the end result was still a connected system in which units shared uniform information. Though they may have been less efficient than a single system would have been, both USMIS and SWMIS represent cases which achieved a balance between unification and local adaptation. The negotiation around the mandate mobilized units to clarify their needs and enabled the system level to grasp the need for local tailoring and, in some cases, independent systems that were interfaced with the larger financial system.

Consistent with other research (Bloomfield and Vurdubakis, 1994; Pozzebon and Pinsonneault, 2003), the results of this study show that technical and institutional aspects of technology adoption are intermingled. The introduction of both SWMIS and USMIS was motivated by both technical and institutional rationales: both were integrated technical systems that increased control of the hierarchy over its units. Some units resisted the system for both technical and institutional reasons. For example, lack of a grant and sponsored project module was ostensibly a technical reason for the research agencies not to adopt USMIS. However, this rationale was tightly interlaced with political and institutional factors such as resistance to centralization and system-level control.

## 5. Limitations

It is important to note several limitations of this study. As with most case research, our study raises the issue of generalizability or transferability of results. This study was limited to one US state and members of one public university system. Thus, our discussion and implications may not extended without qualification to other types of organizations, particularly organizations in the private sector.

A second limitation of the study is that both enterprise systems had significant development on site. This is unlike some recent ERP implementations in which the organization simply acquires the ERP and reorganizes operations to fit it. There may be differences in the meaning and function of mandates for the two cases.

Third, the case studies relied on reconstruction of the history of development and implementation of the two systems. While historical case studies are certainly a valid approach to understanding lengthy and complex development processes, important insights could be gained through real time study of system development and implementation as it unfolded without knowledge of the ultimate outcomes.

Finally, both researchers were ‘insiders’ in that they had worked and studied in the LGUS. While insider status enabled the investigators to obtain access to key people and documents more easily than they would have if they were not insiders, it also may have blinded them to certain features of the institution that might be noticed by outsiders. In terms of the USMIS project itself, however, the researchers were ‘outsiders,’ because they had not participated in the development or implementation process.

## 6. Conclusion

The increasing deployment of enterprise systems by organizations demands new perspectives on the study of technology implementation and acceptance and on the management of a new IT (Grover et al., 1998). The adoption and implementation of enterprise level IT occurs in situations characterized by a heterogeneous group of social actors with widely divergent reactions to the IT, a longer period of IT adoption, multiple levels of adoption decisions, increasing likelihood of unintended consequences, resistance and failure, and increasing role of contextual and environmental factors. Mandates are an important part of this mix, and this analysis has attempted to shed some light on how mandates for enterprise level systems operate.

While on the surface they seem to be simple directives, deeper consideration shows mandates to be complex social constructions with diverse meanings for different parties. Mandates are always potential points of contention, and how mandates are advanced, interpreted, enforced, and used by various actors plays an important role in enterprise system implementation. Analysis of the processes by which mandates acquire their various meanings and how these meanings shape the system’s final configuration gives us a window on the interaction of rational and political processes in the development and implementation of enterprise systems.
