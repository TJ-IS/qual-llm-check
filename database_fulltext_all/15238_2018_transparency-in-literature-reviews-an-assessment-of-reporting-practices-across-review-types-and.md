# Transparency in literature reviews: an assessment of reporting practices across review types and genres in top IS journals

Mathieu Templier<sup>a</sup> and Guy Paré<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Département des systèmes d’information organisationnels, Université Laval, Québec, QC, Canada; <sup>b</sup>HEC Montréal, Chemin de la Cote-Ste-Catherine, Montréal, QC, Canada

## ABSTRACT

The central role of information systems review articles has been recognised in a recent explosion of interest in editorials, research articles, and opinion papers investigating methods and approaches for conducting standalone reviews. In continuity with recent developments in this area, this descriptive review seeks to determine the extent to which various types of review articles published in our field are transparent, i.e., they report important methodological elements about their design. To fulfil this objective, we identified, classified, and coded 142 review articles from the Association for Information Systems (AIS) senior scholars’ basket of journals published between 2000 and 2014. Overall, our findings indicate inadequate reporting of the methods, procedures, and techniques used in a majority of reviews. Our assessment also reveals that theory development and narrative reviews, which are the most frequently published types of reviews in our field, generally were the least explicit with regard to the methods they used. Based on our observations, we recommend that authors of all forms of reviews better document design decisions so to increase trustworthiness, get meaningful results, and develop a cumulative body of knowledge in our discipline. The list of reporting items developed in this study can serve as a framework to assist prospective authors of reviews both within and outside our field.

## 1. Introduction

The proliferation, accumulation, and rapid difusion of information systems (IS) research calls for reliable ways to organise and synthesise knowledge (Bandara, Miskon, & Fielt, 2011; Paré, Trudel, Jaana, & Kitsiou, 2015; Templier & Paré, 2015). Since individual studies contribute one step at a time to a larger understanding of a phenomenon of interest, the building of scientific knowledge requires cooperation, and interdependent research work (Cooper & Hedges, 2009). Yet, the development of new explicit knowledge exceeds the capacity of individual scholars to stay informed of new contributions, and organise and synthesise previous evidence (Card, 2012; Cooper & Hedges, 2009). By synthesising or integrating previous knowledge as well as ofering foundations for further research, literature reviews play a critical role in the development of science (Paré et al., 2015; Rowe, 2014).

A literature review might appear either as the background for an empirical study or as a standalone piece that provides a valuable contribution in its own right (Okoli & Schabram, 2010). The former is the most common form of literature review (Hart, 2001). The latter, also called standalone literature review or review article, is an independent paper whose purpose is to synthesise the extant literature in a field without collection of empirical data. Review articles are conducted for many reasons such as making sense of existing knowledge on a particular topic, facilitating theory development, synthesising the extant literature on widely studied and mature areas, or identifying research domains where further investigation is needed (Paré et al., 2015). High-quality reviews provide a valuable and trustworthy account of past research that scholars might seek out for inspiration and use to position their own works. Citation analyses have also shown that review articles frequently become “core” or “milestone” papers in a field (Paré et al., 2015).

Reviewing previous research on a given topic can be particularly challenging. Specifically, the IS body of knowledge is highly diversified in terms of its content, methods and research approaches (Palvia, Mao, Salam, & Soliman, 2003; Hirschheim & Klein, 2006), which makes it more dificult to make sense, integrate, and reuse. This central issue in conducting review articles has been evidenced by a recent explosion of interest in editorials, research articles, and opinion papers investigating review methods and approaches. As reported in Tate and colleagues (2015), the recent bulk of knowledge on this subject includes reflections on the nature, types, and genres of review articles (e.g., Oates, 2011; Okoli, 2012; Paré et al., 2015; Rowe, 2014), developments of methodological approaches and techniques (e.g., Houy, Fettke, & Loos, 2015; Sylvester, Tate, & Johnstone, 2013; Wolfswinkel, Furtmueller, & Wilderom, 2013) and reflections on quality considerations (e.g., Bandara, Furtmuller, Gorbacheva, Miskon, & Beekhuyzen, 2015; Templier & Paré, 2015). The diversity of recent contributions also emphasises confusion with respect to the genres of literature reviews and a lack of agreement about what constitutes a sound methodological approach for reviewing previous literature (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015; Paré et al., 2015; Rowe, 2014).

Misconceptions regarding standalone reviews and the methods for conducting particular review types raise further concerns towards the trustworthiness of the literature review genre. According to Lincoln and Guba (1985), trustworthiness refers to the truth value of a study, i.e., the extent to which the authors have established confidence in the truth of their findings. Recently, Paré and colleagues (2016) proposed a framework which highlights two basic, yet interrelated dimensions of trustworthiness that they consider relevant for positivist and interpretivist reviews. First, they posit that to ensure internal validity (positivist tradition) or credibility (interpretivist tradition), a review should be executed in a systematic fashion. Put simply, systematicity refers to the quality of conduct or the soundness of execution of a review. The second dimension, called transparency, refers to the extent to which the review process is made explicit, i.e., whether important methodological elements about its design are clearly reported (Shea et al., 2009). Inadequate reporting of the methods and procedures used in a review article represents a significant impediment to its reproducibility. We concur with Paré and colleagues (2016) that reproducibility of reviews must focus on the reproduction of the methods, and not an exact replica of findings, given it is primarily a human-based activity (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015).

While we recognise the importance of considering both dimensions of trustworthiness when conducting standalone reviews, our focus in this article concerns the particular aspect of transparency. As a preliminary assessment of trustworthiness in our field, our intent is not to assess how well threats to the internal validity of positivist reviews have been managed or how much the resulting analyses of interpretivist reviews are plausible and credible, i.e., grounded in the available evidence. Instead, our main goal is to assess the extent to which IS researchers have explicitly reported the important methodological aspects about the design of their reviews. As auditability of any review is only possible if the process is made explicit, transparency represents the principal opportunity to evaluate trustworthiness of a review article. It must be emphasised here that reproducibility of methods is critical for both positivist and interpretivist reviews because it contributes to their credibility and guidance (Paré et al., 2016). For one thing, credibility can be recognised through the use of explicit, detailed statements about the methods used, making it clear that the research design not only sounds reasonable when reported, but can, in fact, be replicated if required. Further, reproducibility of the review process also provides guidance with respect to future research. For positivist reviews, transparency mainly serves as a platform for an updated review on the same topic some years later. For interpretive reviews, transparency can help teach students and scholars how to go about similar research in other topic areas with no thought of replication of findings. As described in Campbell and colleagues (2011), a case in point is meta-ethnography which, as originally conceived by Noblit and Hare (1988), resisted making the review process explicit. In recent years, however, the growth in the application of this particular method has revealed that “potentially valuable lessons can be learnt from better understanding the practical actions and reasoning researchers employ in order to realise the methodology” (Lee, Hart, Watson, & Rapley, 2015, p. 337).

To the best of our knowledge, no prior research has attempted to conduct a formal assessment of review reporting practices in our field. The present study attempts to fill this important gap. Since a number of the reporting criteria we used to perform our assessment were proposed in recent years and/or derived from disciplines far removed from our field, we must stress that our intent is not to conduct a definitive assessment of any specific IS review article, but instead to reflect on our collective practices and to pinpoint areas where more work needs to be done. Indeed, our empirical examination of published review articles in top IS journals over the 2000–2014 period shall help identify trends and patterns in our reporting practices and, hence, serve as an instrument to reflect, as a research community, on our previous work.

The remaining of this paper is organised as follows. In the next section, we describe and illustrate the types of review articles that are most relevant to the IS community and we develop a list of reporting items from authoritative methodological sources. Next, we describe how review articles published in the “basket of eight” journals were searched, screened, and analysed. This is followed by the presentation of our findings. Last, we discuss the implications of our results for IS scholars and highlight the limitations of our study.

## 2. Background

As stressed by Tate et al. (2015), literature review approaches and methods are undergoing a period of rapid development in our field. Indeed, there exist various families of reviews that difer considerably in terms of their primary motivations, scope, epistemological positions, means by which they are conducted and targeted audiences. One way of classifying IS literature reviews was recently proposed by Rowe (2014). His taxonomy distinguishes four broad genres of reviews according to their primary goal with respect to theory, namely, describing, understanding, theory testing, and explaining. For their part, Paré and colleagues (2015) reviewed the extant literature on literature reviews to come up with a typology that identifies, defines, and contrasts nine distinct types of review articles. In the paragraphs below, we explain these two classification schemes and how they complement each other (see Table 1).

First, several reviews in our field aim at describing a phenomenon with little or no contribution to theory (Rowe, 2014). These reviews summarise the extant literature on a particular topic of interest to provide the readers with a broad and comprehensive background for understanding the current state of knowledge in that area. In their typology, Paré and colleagues (2015) distinguish two particular types of reviews under this first genre, namely narrative and descriptive reviews. While these types difer in terms of their coverage and data synthesis approaches, they address broad research questions and the researchers’ voice is usually neutral. Narrative reviews mainly assemble and summarise the extant literature and provide a comprehensive report on the current state of knowledge on the topic of interest (Byrne, 2016). These are often complemented by corresponding research gaps and/or a research agenda (Schryen et al., 2015). The article by Chan and Reich (2007) represents a good illustration of a narrative review in our field. The authors provide a review of the alignment literature in IT, addressing questions such as: What have we learned? What is disputed? Who are contributors to the debate? Although the methods used to search, screen and analyse the articles on IT alignment are not described, the review itself appears to be of great value to researchers, teachers, and practitioners. For the scientific community, they suggest several opportunities for future research in this area. For teachers and lecturers, the authors present alignment models to be used in IT strategy classes to explain and illustrate key concepts. For practitioners, they suggest ways to interpret research findings and implement research recommendations.

For their part, descriptive reviews, also called systematic quantitative reviews (Pickering & Byrne, 2014), investigate mature topics and aim at determining the extent to which a body of empirical studies reveals any interpretable patterns or trends over a given period of time (King & He, 2005). In comparison to narrative reviews, the search and screening processes as well as the data extraction and analysis methods of descriptive reviews are more structured and transparent (Paré & Kitsiou, 2017). In a descriptive review each article included in the sample represents a unit of analysis (Paré et al., 2015). As an example, Avison and colleagues (2008) reflected on the first 17 years of the Information Systems Journal (ISJ). Their reflections are considered under three categories, namely (1) origin of papers (authors, geographical location, gender, and departments), (2) research paradigms (positive/interpretive, qualitative/quantitative, non-empirical/empirical, espoused theories and research methods), and (3) topics. The numerous frequency tables presented throughout the Results section reveal some interesting trends that apply to research published in this particular journal.

The second genre of review articles aim at understanding a particular phenomenon or problem through related concepts that have been investigated in prior research (Rowe, 2014). Understanding primarily refers to examining consistencies and inconsistencies (Booth, Papaioannou, & Sutton, 2012), which may be accomplished with the help of scoping reviews and critical reviews. These two types of reviews usually provide a thorough account of prior research and generate creative propositions or ideas for future research. First, scoping reviews are primarily concerned with emergent topics and aim at assessing the size and scope of available literature and inform researchers about a new area for future research (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). These reviews usually address a broad spectrum of topics where many diferent study designs might be applicable (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). In line with their main objective, scoping reviews usually conclude with the presentation of a detailed research agenda along with potential implications for practice and research (Paré & Kitsiou, 2017). Unlike narrative and descriptive reviews, the whole point of scoping an emergent topic is to be as comprehensive as possible, including grey literature (Arksey & O’Malley, 2005). As an example of a scoping review in our field, Smith and colleagues (2011a) provide a clear description of how they identified and selected the 320 privacy articles and 128 books and book sections in their sample. They classified prior works in two ways: (1) using an ethics-based nomenclature of normative, purely descriptive, and empirically descriptive, and (2) based on their level of analysis: individual, group, organisational, and societal. The authors identified three major areas in which previous contributions reside: the conceptualisation of information privacy, the relationship between information privacy and other constructs, and the contextual nature of these relationships.

For their part, critical reviews primarily aim at analysing the extant literature on a particular phenomenon to reveal contradictions, controversies, or inconsistencies (Paré et al., 2015). Some critical reviews also seek to problematise a particular area of investigation by highlighting inconsistent theoretical assumptions (Rowe, 2014; Schultze, 2015). Problematisation goes beyond gap spotting by “challenging assumptions of underlying existing literature” (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 251–252). Unlike other review types, critical reviews attempt to take a reflective account of the research that has been done in a particular area of interest, and assess its credibility using appraisal instruments or other methods. An example of a critical review in our field is the article by Schryen (2013) on IS business value. In this review, the author provides a critical synthesis of the extant literature arguing that key limitations of prior research on this topic are based on the ambiguity and fuzziness of IS business value, the neglected disaggregation of IS investments, and the unexplained process of creating internal and competitive value. Addressing these limitations the author suggests numerous, yet very promising research paths.

The third genre of literature reviews aims at testing theory by aggregating prior empirical findings (Rowe, 2014). Aggregative reviews, which are rooted in the positivist ontological tradition, are particularly valued for providing evidence-based validations of pre-specified theoretical models or research propositions (Paré et al., 2015). The most prominent type of aggregative reviews, both within and outside IS, is called the meta-analysis (Rowe, 2014). A meta-analysis uses statistical techniques to summarise and synthesise prior empirical results on a given topic (King & He, 2005). Meta-analyses provide precise estimates of relations between variables because they adjust for sample size and reliability of measures (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001). It is important to note, however, that meta-analyses are not recommended if there is too much heterogeneity in prior studies (Paré & Kitsiou, 2017). As an illustration, Ortiz de Guinea and colleagues (2012) conducted a meta-analysis of virtualness on team processes and outcomes. They found that moderators like analysis at the group or individual level and the short or long time orientation of a team played a central role in some of the relations between virtualness and team processes and outcomes.

Other forms of aggregative reviews include qualitative systematic reviews and umbrella reviews (Paré et al., 2015). While meta-analytic reviews use specific data extraction techniques and statistical methods to aggregate quantitative data, qualitative systematic reviews use narrative methods to bring together the quantitative findings of a group of heterogeneous studies (Paré et al., 2015). An example of qualitative systematic review in our field is the article by Petter and colleagues (2008) which integrates 180 studies found in the academic literature for the period 1992–2007 dealing with some aspect of IS success. Using the six dimensions of the DeLone and McLean (1992) model, 90 empirical studies were analysed and the results summarised. A total of 15 pairwise associations between the success constructs were scrutinised. The authors considered many diferent types of IS under a variety of conditions and found reasonable support for the majority of relationships, suggesting the value of the DeLone and McLean (1992) model when evaluating utilitarian IS.

The third and last type of theory-testing review is called umbrella review (Thomson, Russell, Becker, Klassen, & Hartling, 2010). In comparison with meta-analyses and qualitative systematic reviews which extract and analyse research findings from primary studies, umbrella reviews aim at synthesising the findings of prior reviews (Thomson et al., 2010). This is why they are often referred to as tertiary types of evidence synthesis (Becker & Oxman, 2008). As an illustration, Kitsiou and colleagues (2015) conducted a review of prior meta-analyses and qualitative systematic reviews in order to test the magnitude of the efects of a particular type of IS in the healthcare domain. More precisely, they collected, appraised, and synthesised existing evidence from multiple reviews on the efectiveness of home telemonitoring systems for patients with chronic heart failure to inform policy-makers, practitioners, and researchers. Evidence from high-quality reviews with meta-analysis indicated that the use of home telemonitoring interventions reduce the relative risk of all-cause mortality and heart failure related hospitalisations compared with usual care.

The fourth genre of reviews is aimed at explaining (Rowe, 2014). These syntheses, which provide new conceptualisations, models, theories, or frameworks are called theory development reviews in the typology proposed by Paré and colleagues (2015). Their primary contribution lies in the novelty of the proposed ideas and, hence goes beyond simply gathering and synthesising prior studies. Most IS scholars are familiar with the guidelines proposed by Webster and Watson (2002) on how to motivate a theory development review, how to search and select the relevant literature, how to structure the results of the analysis, how to evaluate the resulting theory, and how to write the review article. An example of theory development review in our field is the article by von Krogh and colleagues (2012). These authors review prior research on what motivates open source systems (OSS) developers. The review methods adopted by the authors are clearly stated. They identified a large body of literature examining types of motivation that lead developers to contribute their time and efort to the development of OSS. Interestingly, they found that both the antecedents and consequences of motivation are more extensive and complex than what prior theorising and empirical research had suggested. As a key contribution of this review, the authors derived six research propositions and suggested a detailed research agenda on motivation in OSS.

Besides the generic, yet valuable approach proposed by Webster and Watson (2002), there exist specific meth ods which serve the purpose of theory development. Paré and colleagues (2015) identified one particular theory-driven approach which is highly relevant to the IS field, that is, the realist review (Pawson, Greenhalgh, Harvey, & Walshe, 2005). The main goal of a realist review is to unpack the mechanisms of how “complex interven tions” work in particular contexts. The basic research question – what works? – which is usually associated with qualitative systematic reviews changes to: what is it about this intervention that works, for whom, in what circumstances, in what respects and why? Realist reviews have no particular preference for either quantitative or qualitative evidence. As a theory-building approach, a realist review usually starts by articulating likely underlying mechanisms and then scrutinises available evidence to find out whether and where these mechanisms are applicable (Shepperd et al., 2009). Primary studies found in the extant literature are viewed as case studies which can test and modify the initial theories (Rousseau, Manning, & Denyer, 2008). An example of IS-related realist review is the article by Wong and colleagues (2010) whose primary goal is to produce theory driven criteria to guide the development and evaluation of Internet-based medical courses. The authors identified two main theories of the course-in-context that explained variation in learners’ satisfaction and outcomes. Results indicated that learners were more likely to accept an online course if it ofered a perceived advantage over available non-Internet alternatives, was easy to use technically, and compatible with their values and norms. Importantly, interactivity led to efective learning only if learners were able to enter into a dialogue with a tutor, fellow students or virtual tutorials and gain formative feedback.

Like any empirical method, the conduct of a standalone review involves a certain number of steps and activities. While not all design issues apply to all review types, methodologists agree on the existence of six generic steps, as shown in Table 2. Researchers must first properly justify the need for the review itself, identify the review’s main objectives, and articulate the research questions they propose to investigate. They also need to define the concepts or variables at the heart of their synthesis. The next step consists of searching the literature and making decisions about the suitability of material to be considered in the review. There exist four main coverage strategies, namely, exhaustive, representative, pivotal, and selective (Cooper, 1988). Screening for inclusion consists of evaluating the applicability of the material identified in the preceding step. Once a group of potential studies has been identified, researchers must screen them to determine their relevance. A set of predetermined rules or criteria provides a basis for including or excluding studies. In addition to screening material for inclusion, team members may need to assess the scientific quality of the selected studies, that is, appraise the rigour of the research design and methods. The next step involves extracting applicable information from each primary study included in the sample and deciding what is relevant to the problem of interest. Last, the research team must collate, summarise, aggregate, organise, and compare the evidence extracted from the included studies. The extracted data must be presented in a meaningful way that suggests a new contribution to the extant literature. Although the abovementioned steps are presented in sequential order, one must keep in mind that the review process can be iterative and that many activities can be initiated during the planning stage and later refined during subsequent phases.

To achieve our main objective, our next task consisted of developing a list of reporting items for each review type in Table 1. To identify relevant methodological sources, we followed a highly iterative search strategy. As a first step, we consulted the list of references cited in Templier and Paré (2015) who examined the challenging issue of evaluating rigour in literature reviews. As a second step, we reviewed the methodological sources cited in Rowe (2014), Schryen and colleagues (2015) and Paré and colleagues (2015), who developed taxonomies or typologies of literature review approaches. We included papers that are considered pivotal in their own field. In the end, 14 methodological sources were considered relevant to the present study. Because these sources did not cover the whole spectrum of review types in Table 1, we decided to perform a purposeful search on a few specific review methods, namely narrative, descriptive, and critical reviews. We therefore completed the search by consulting the ISI Web of Knowledge (Thomson Reuters) database, the ABI/Inform Global (ProQuest), and the Google Scholar search engine. We used keywords such as “review”, “research synthesis”, or “research syntheses” in conjunction with terms referring to specific review types, including “narrative”, “descriptive”, “critical”, and “problematisation”. As pertinent papers were identified, we updated the list of search terms to include other review designations (e.g., “mapping”, “integrative”, and “quantitative”). Further, backward and forward searches on pertinent papers allowed us to uncover additional relevant material. We looked for materials which provided detailed descriptions of best reporting practices. We added four methodological sources to our initial list.

## 3. Methodology

In order to assess the reporting practices of IS review articles, we surveyed the review articles published in the AIS senior scholars’ basket of journals. Like Paré and colleagues (2015), a pilot search using the ABI/Inform Complete and ISI Web of Science databases revealed itself to be inadequate, that is, several review articles in the selected journals could not be captured with the use of broad terms such as review, research synthesis, literature survey, and meta-analysis. Therefore, we performed a manual search of all volumes and issues of European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (JAIS), Journal of Information Technology (JIT), Journal of Management Information Systems (JMIS), Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS), and MIS Quarterly (MISQ) over a period of 15 years (2000–2014). A total of 3,297 articles were published in those journals during this period.

As a next step, we looked for standalone reviews by selecting those articles whose primary goal was to summarise the extant literature or integrate prior findings on a given topic, without any empirical data collected. Our procedure involved an initial screening of the titles and abstracts followed by a thorough examination of the full papers to ensure the appropriateness of inclusion (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Templier & Paré, 2015). Editorials, short research commentaries, and research notes were not considered. The initial search allowed us to identify 203 potential review articles.

The screening for exclusion process was performed by the two authors. All 203 papers were assessed in several meetings and decisions regarding the exclusion of review papers were taken jointly. To support this process, we relied on various sources including the editorials by Rowe (2014), Rivard (2014) and Jones and Gatrell (2014) which provide valuable insights to distinguish review articles from theoretical and conceptual papers. A total of 61 articles were excluded leaving us with a sample of 142 reviews. The complete list of review articles in our sample is available in Appendix B.

Next, we classified each of the 142 reviews along the nine review types listed in Table 1. First, the two authors independently classified 45 articles. This coding process resulted in a 91% agreement rate. While the level of agreement appeared highly satisfactory, we decided to classify together all remaining articles. Consensus was reached quickly for a vast majority of articles. It must be noted that while the classification by Paré and colleagues (2015) allowed us to cover the whole spectrum of literature reviews included in our sample, we found seven review articles which pursued multiple goals and, hence, combined multiple methods. These “hybrid” reviews (Paré et al., 2015) were classified according to what we perceived to be their dominant type (see Appendix B).

With regard to data extraction, we built a coding scheme (Poole & Folger, 1981) for each review type using the items listed in Table 4. The coding process, which relies on the directed content analysis technique (Hsieh & Shannon, 2005), was pretested by the second author with the help of a trained postdoctoral fellow. A total of 15 reviews were randomly selected from the sample and independently coded. The overall agreement rate between the two coders was 93%. The diferences were reconciled by consensus during a team meeting. Following this step, a few minor adjustments were made to the coding sheets. Coding of the remaining articles, with the exception of hybrid reviews, was performed by the postdoctoral fellow. When there was uncertainty regarding how to code a particular guideline of a review, the opinion of the first author was solicited. For their part, hybrid reviews were handled by the two researchers who first determined their respective dominant type and then coded them using the appropriate coding scheme. To further validate the coding process, a doctoral candidate whose thesis focuses on literature review methods coded a total of 30 randomly selected review articles (21% of our sample). From this process, we computed a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.82, which is highly satisfactory (Landis & Koch, 1977). The few disagreements were then discussed and reconciled. ## 4. Results

Our descriptive findings shown in Table 8 are structured around the six generic steps of the review process described earlier.

## 4.1. Step 1: Problem formulation

The first step, problem formulation, refers to the attributes associated with the identification of the purpose and the definition of the central topic of the review. There are two reporting items associated with this initial step: (1) authors must be explicit about the pursued objectives or questions and (2) define the key concepts or theories being explored. Our results indicate that virtually all reviews included in our sample met the established standards in terms of problem formulation, irrespective of their genre or type. For one thing, all of the reviews clearly articulated the research questions or primary objectives. This is an encouraging sign because clearly formulated objectives or questions help provide focus and militate against undisciplined data collection and analysis (Booth et al., 2012). Further, a vast majority of review articles in our sample defined the key concepts or theories under scrutiny. A solid conceptual basis presupposes a sound understanding of the corpus of literature to be reviewed.

## 4.2. Step 2: Literature search

A transparent search strategy that aligns with the initial research questions of the review is a fundamental requirement for all types of syntheses (Cooper, 1988; Templier & Paré, 2015). Searches can be complex and iterative, especially when researchers are investigating large and multifaceted bodies of evidence or emerging phenomena. Authors must be explicit about the search procedures, that is, “where to search, which terms to use, which sources are to be searched, time span, and language” (Levac, Colquhoun, & O’Brien, 2010:3). As shown in Table 8, theory testing reviews, descriptive reviews, and scoping reviews provided a description of the search process while critical, narrative and theory development reviews were less explicit.

The adoption of multiple search strategies (e.g., database search, manual search, backward and forward searches, contacting experts in the field) and the use of multiple data sources (e.g., journal articles, conference proceedings, theses, and grey literature) are highly recommended across several review genres. As shown in Table 4, search strategies and sources should then be reported explicitly. Our findings indicate that, with the exception of meta-analyses, these two elements were not reported in a consistent manner. It is also worth noting that theory development reviews, which represent the most frequent type in our sample, were the least transparent with regard to these two aspects.

When a review is concerned with the generalisability of its findings, it should ideally capture all studies of interest. No restrictions pertaining to the publication language, status or date should be applied unless the limitations correspond to the boundaries of the review itself (Higgins & Green, 2008). As shown in Table 4, such restrictions should be ideally avoided when conducting all types of reviews but narrative and realist ones. Our findings indicate that all review types but theory development reviews were relatively explicit with regard to this particular item.

It is also recommended for narrative, critical, and theory development reviews to consider the reputation of the sources when searching primary studies. While such strategy would be detrimental to theory testing reviews, as it could foster the risk of publication bias, other review types, which usually do not perform a formal quality assessment, must find ways to maximise the quality of the evidence on which they base their conclusions. As a result, the process places a strong emphasis on important and trustworthy contributions to the topic of interest. For instance, it is recommended to only consider peer-reviewed outlets and scholarly journals (Green, Johnson, & Adams, 2006; Machi & McEvoy, 2016). In the same vein, Webster and Watson (2002) suggest starting the search process with leading journals and conferences in the field, i.e., where the major contributions are most likely to be found. Our findings indicate that 63% of critical reviews, 28% of narrative reviews, and 13% of theory development reviews explicitly reported having taken this criterion into consideration.

Last, publication bias, which arises from the tendency to publish significant rather than non-significant results, is particularly harmful for the internal validity of meta-analyses (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Liberati et al., 2009). Table 8 shows that 75% of the meta-analyses in our sample did report the use of strategies to minimise publication bias. Wu and Lederer’s (2009) meta-analysis is an exemplar with regard to the application of this guideline. After searching several, yet complementary bibliographic databases they scanned a few databases referencing dissertations and theses. They also performed manual searches whenever back issues of journals were unavailable in electronic format. Last, they sent an enquiry for working papers and other unpublished materials to the IS community through the AISWorld mailing list.

## 4.3. Step 3: Screening for inclusion

Screening for inclusion refers to the selection of studies that are considered appropriate for further analysis. It first involves specifying how primary studies are screened or selected (e.g., specific method, inclusion and exclusion criteria, population, etc.). To claim internal validity of their findings, authors of describing, theory testing, and explaining reviews must select the relevant articles among those retrieved during the previous step. They should ask themselves if each article in their sample addresses the problem under enquiry and helps answer the research questions (Okoli & Schabram, 2010; Pawson et al., 2005). As shown in Table 8, a majority of descriptive, theory testing, and scoping reviews specified how the studies were selected while narrative and theory development reviews were much less explicit.

A parallel independent selection of studies is also recommended for descriptive, scoping, and theory testing reviews. This process usually helps minimise the risk of selection bias or variations due to researchers’ subjectivity (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Okoli & Schabram, 2010) which, in turn, might result in invalid findings (Oxman & Guyatt, 1988). Table 8 shows that authors of most scoping, descriptive, and theory testing reviews in our sample did not report such assessment and its findings.

Authors of theory testing reviews should search for studies using the same data-set, and if any make proper decisions regarding their inclusion in the sample and their treatment during data analysis (Kitchenham & Charters, 2007). Indeed, the inclusion of multiple publications using the same data-set can be very harmful for reviews which base their conclusions on repeated evidence. As shown in Table 8, 25% of meta-analyses and 17% of qualitative systematic reviews were explicit about searching for articles using the same data-set and how these studies were analysed.

Last, authors of scoping and theory testing reviews should also be explicit about how primary studies were screened and selected to ensure greater transparency and, hence, allow replication of the review process (Higgins & Green, 2008; Okoli & Schabram, 2010; Oxman, 1994). Liberati and colleagues (2006) also advise authors of theory testing reviews to provide a flow diagram to summarise the various activities involved during the search and screening processes. Our findings indicate that virtually all meta-analyses in our sample reported this item compared to 17% and 11% for qualitative systematic reviews and scoping reviews, respectively.

## 4.4. Step 4: Quality assessment

Whereas the previous step is concerned with the appropriate selection of primary studies, quality assessment focuses on the formal evaluation of the methodological quality of the selected studies (Higgins & Green, 2008; Kitchenham & Charters, 2007; Shea et al., 2007). This process represents a complex, yet an important practice in research synthesis. It has emerged over the years as a recommended guideline for theory testing reviews (Booth et al., 2012). When the primary goal is to test hypotheses or calculate efect sizes, it is critical to identify and report all potential flaws and risk of bias in the selected studies so to determine their impact on the findings of the review. It is also recommended to report results of a parallel independent assessment. As shown in Table 8, virtually none of the theory testing reviews in our sample reported how quality assessment was performed and, consequently, the results of the parallel independent assessment.

## 5. Discussion

Literature reviews have long been recognised as a major driver of scientific progress and growth (Cooper & Hedges, 2009; Watson, 2001). Indeed, review methods are well suited as means of unveiling research gaps, developing theories and frameworks, and identify new research directions (Rowe, 2014; Webster & Watson, 2002). The various genres and types of review articles listed in Table 1 represent an extraordinary enablement to facilitate knowledge development and scientific progress in our field. In a recent opinion paper, Paré et al. (2016) recommend that trustworthiness be considered a virtue that should govern all review types, irrespective of their goals, methods, and epistemological positions. The present study ofers an initial assessment of transparency in IS reviews. More specifically, our objective was to gauge the extent to which reporting practices in our field are aligned with the methodological guidelines found in the extant literature on literature reviews. Using an authoritative list of methodological sources from various disciplines, we identified 22 reporting guidelines which served as our framework to code 142 IS review articles from the “basket of eight” journals.

In a nutshell, our findings indicate that the two items associated with step 1 (problem formulation) are thoroughly reported across all review types. Reporting practices associated with step 2 (literature search) are not constantly applied across review types with meta-analyses following more closely the recommended practices and theory development reviews being less explicit. As for step 3 (screening for inclusion), results are on the low side except for one item (flow diagram) which is found in most theory testing reviews. Reporting practices associated with step 4 (quality assessment) are lacking in most theory testing reviews in our sample. On the positive side, the majority of reviews in our sample were transparent regarding the three reporting items associated with step 5 (data extraction), except for theory development reviews. Last, our assessment for step 6 (data analysis and interpretation) vary widely across reporting items and review types. Overall, our results indicate inadequate reporting of the design decisions in a large majority of IS reviews.

Importantly, our assessment indicates that the two most frequently published forms of reviews in our field, namely, theory development, and narrative reviews, were generally the least explicit with regard to the methods used. In the paragraphs below we discuss three reporting items that we consider to be most problematic with these forms of reviews and we explain, for each item, why a lack of transparency represents a problem. First, while reporting how the literature search is performed (which sources are searched, which keywords are used, whether restrictions are applied, etc.) is a requirement for all types of review, only a minority of theory development and narrative reviews in our sample complied with this guideline. Transparency in the context of the literature searching refers to a clearly articulated “search strategy” that describes the stages, activities, and overall anatomy (linear vs. iterative) of the search process. Narrative and theory development reviews generally ask open and contested questions in stark contrast with theory testing reviews which focus on narrow questions. Thus, literature search in narrative and theory development reviews are often highly iterative. Researchers generally engage in exploratory searches to learn more about the topic of interest, refine, and extend the initial search strategy to collect additional materials, and then repeat the process until saturation is reached. Providing a clearly articulated search strategy in narrative and theory development reviews is as important as in other forms of reviews because it allows readers to judge if the methods and decisions made were appropriate and aligned with the research questions. Scholars who proposed emergent, yet highly iterative approaches to searching the literature advocate for a systematic and transparent process (e.g., Sylvester et al., 2013).

Although reporting practices with regard to literature search are not commonly applied, we found a few narrative (e.g., Chiasson, Germonprez, & Mathiassen, 2009; de Vaujany, Walsh, & Mitev, 2011; Mignerat & Rivard, 2009; Robey, Im, & Wareham, 2008) and theory development (e.g., Elliot, 2011; Kappos & Rivard, 2008; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; Piccoli & Ives, 2005; von Krogh et al., 2012) reviews that we consider exemplars for their transparency. For instance, the theory development review by von Krogh and colleagues (2012), which aims at explaining the motivations of software developers to contribute to open source software projects, provides a clearly articulated search strategy. The authors clearly reported each decision they made with regard to the search terms, databases, and information sources to search and they explained the suitability of the methods and techniques they used to conduct their search. Specifically, they searched ISI Web of Knowledge to access published and peer-reviewed materials, browsed a paper repository for unpublished papers, complemented their search using Google Scholar and selected purposefully a few references that were known to them for additional theoretical contributions.

Second, it is also critical for narrative and theory development reviews to report whether (and how) the reputation of the sources was considered when searching and selecting articles to be analysed. In order to avoid the pitfall of garbage-in/garbage-out and produce an efective literature review, it has been recommended to start the search process with seminal articles on the topic of interest and to limit inclusion to trustworthy materials in leading peer-reviewed journals and conferences (Green et al., 2006; Levy & Ellis, 2006; Machi & McEvoy, 2016; Webster & Watson, 2002). There exist several resources, such as journals or conferences rankings, that prospective authors of narrative and theory development reviews could use to identify and select trustworthy sources. For instance, Levy and Ellis (2006) proposed lists of top-tier IS journals and conferences. Lowry and colleagues (2013) identified a broad set of 40 journals publishing IS research based on expert judgements and impact factors.

We found a few narrative and theory development reviews in our sample that provide good illustrations of the application of this reporting guideline. The exemplar we selected is provided by Elliot (2011) who investigated the topic of environmental sustainability and IT with the goal to develop a holistic, transdisciplinary, integrative framework for business transformation. Consistent with Webster and Watson (2002), the author adopted a highly structured and transparent approach to identifying and conceptualising relevant materials. Considering the huge volume of literature on this topic, “the initial or scoping phase sought to examine authoritative sources to determine a problem specification, propose a focus and scope, and suggest a structure for the review to meet the aims of this article” (p. 201). Seventeen authoritative sources, including seminal, intergovernmental, governmental, practitioner, and social activist reports, were identified by the author during several years of work in this domain. According to the author, purposive sampling was necessary as the authoritative sources were not necessarily identifiable through searches of academic literature.

Third, theory development reviews should also specify the tools or methods used to extract data. Whereas data extraction for these reviews usually includes some thematic or conceptual classification and is highly dependent on the researchers’ judgement, confidence in the results is essential and could be achieved through a reliable and valid coding procedure (Whittemore & Knafl, 2005). Authors should describe how the concepts were identified and how the literature was reviewed and categorised (Torraco, 2005). Further, when accepted models or frameworks do not exist, Torraco (2005) specifically recommends prospective authors to describe the “steps taken to verify the validity or authenticity of key ideas and themes that emerged from the analysis” (p. 361). Several techniques for extracting data from primary sources have been proposed. For instance, Webster and Watson (2002) encourage authors to adopt a concept-centric approach that involves the use of a concept matrix to extract key information from the selected studies. They argue that without the use of an organising framework for the extraction of relevant concepts from the included studies, there is a high risk of omitting, misclassifying, or misinterpreting key information in the synthesis stage (step 5).

As an illustration of these guidelines, Piccoli and Ives (2005) synthesised prior research that has examined the role of IT in sustaining competitive advantage. The review is organised around an integrative framework that explicitly models the characteristics of an IT-dependent strategic initiative and the dynamic processes through which that initiative evolves over time. The framework comprises four barriers to erosion that fully capture the determinants of sustainability in the context of IT-dependent strategic initiatives. The authors developed a concept matrix for each of these barriers which contribute to establish confidence in the truth of their findings.

Several considerations should be kept in mind when interpreting the results of the present study. First, we realise that our search strategy does not cover all literature reviews published in the IS field. Therefore, we should be cautious when discussing the generalisability of our findings and conclusions. In line with a descriptive review, we conducted a rigorous search of all volumes and issues of the journals included in the AIS senior scholars’ basket of eight. Our manual search allowed us to capture review articles not reported explicitly as such by their authors. Notwithstanding the internal reliability of our sample, it might not be representative of the whole population of literature reviews published in our field. Second, we must reiterate that our evaluation was performed in the light of the information that was reported in the review articles, not on the basis of what was actually done. There is an ever increasing tension in our field between the desire for detail and the need for brevity. For this reason, we also recommend that methodological details be placed in an appendix, especially in lengthy qualitative reviews. Third, although we meticulously classified and coded each of the 142 studies in our sample, we may have misclassified or miscoded some reviews. To make our own review process as transparent as possible, we provide the full list of reviews in Appendix B and the codes for each review article in Appendix C. Fourth, although the present study has considered a large number of reporting items, we do not pretend that our framework is exhaustive. Future studies could consider additional reporting items such as clearly identifying the type of review in the title or the abstract of the manuscript, adequately justifying the type of review chosen in the methods section, indicating whether a review protocol was developed or published, and, whenever applicable, providing a list of included as well as a list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion.

We acknowledge that adhering to all of the reporting items included in our framework does not necessarily make a review article good de facto. Our study has focused on transparency but, again, transparency is just one of many aspects of high-quality literature reviews. Other key aspects of quality must be considered in future assessments. For instance, systematicity, which refers to the soundness of the review, must also be considered. Paré et al. (2016) posit that when standalone reviews are not conducted rigorously using sound methodolog ical practices, they can contain biases, errors or other major flaws that can impair their trustworthiness and, hence, limit their usefulness or impactfulness. These authors identified several guidelines that aim to increase the internal validity of positivist reviews and the credibility of interpretivist reviews. As another example, future assessments of standalone reviews in our field could consider relevance which is defined as the primary utility or usefulness of the review and refers to its contribution to knowledge. Rowe (2012) argues that contributions of existing literature reviews in our field are often “trivial” and not “significant” (p. 470). He thus recommends that prospective authors of review arti cles should provide a substantial efort, “that is, done with some intelligence and applied to a suficient set of papers”, but most crucially, they should first “identify relevant phenomena that are amenable to such efort” (pp. 470–471). Relevance and contribution to theory and practice could be investigated through performing cita tion analysis, investigating award winning reviews, or surveying IS researchers about their perceptions of some published review articles. Seminal review articles could then be inventoried and the characteristics that make them relevant and significant could be identified. In this regard, several IS reviews aimed at describing (e.g., Chan & Reich, 2007), understanding (e.g., Schultze & Leidner, 2002), theory testing (e.g., Jeyaraj, Rottman, & Lacity, 2006) and explaining (e.g., Wade & Hulland, 2004) have had a considerable impact, both within and outside our field, if judged by citation counts and sources.

Importantly, IS researchers need to increase their awareness and knowledge of the various types of review articles and the specific items used to assess transparency of each type. Like Paré and colleagues (2015) we noticed several inconsistencies in the terminology used by IS authors to describe their reviews. According to these authors, “there may have been some confusion over the nature of [IS] reviews, the reasons why they are conducted, and how they are developed” (p. 26). Similarly, Rowe (2014) observed a diversity of literature reviews and stressed the importance of better understanding their similarities and diferences between review types. Interestingly, we found instances of seven review types in our sample. Yet, each review type in Table 1 has its own characteristics and pursues distinct overarching goals. We concur with Paré and colleagues (2015) that no review type is more valuable than another and, hence review articles of all genres have the potential to produce significant contribution to knowledge. Having said this, prospective authors of review articles in our field should make sure they select the type of review that best fits their primary objective. In this regard, Ortiz de Guinea and Paré (in press) developed a decision tree that can help IS scholars and graduate students choose the form of review that best suits their goal and can also assist reviewers and editors in the assessment of review articles.

Lastly, we concur with Schryen et al. (in press) that IS doctoral students must be better prepared for conducting and publishing high-quality review articles. We hope that our study will contribute to familiarise them with various review types, develop their understanding of diferent review methodologies and, most importantly, make them realise the importance of transparency. It is also our hope that students will develop their knowledge and skills by reading, analysing and replicating review articles that are considered exemplars in terms of reporting practices (Schryen et al., in press).

## 6. Concluding remarks

We concur with Rowe (2014) and Templier and Paré (2015) that the enhanced role of review articles in our field requires that this expository form be given careful scrutiny. Again, in the future, IS literature reviews need to better document design decisions as a way not only to increase trustworthiness, but also to help us learn, get meaningful results, develop a cumulative and coherent body of knowledge in our discipline, and attract greater interest from the IS practitioner community. The IS field as a whole can only greatly benefit from increased transparency in standalone literature reviews. Like Rowe (2014), we feel that review papers with illuminating findings but with little details about the process and how results were derived should be published as another genre such as theory development papers and issues and opinion. In short, we hope that the present study will contribute to the development of more transparent literature reviews of various forms by providing an initial benchmark along with a set of key recommendations for prospective authors. A promising avenue for future research is the investigation of transparency in quantitative research where sophisticated statistical analyses and structural equation modelling results are asked to be taken on trust without full inspection of the process of their derivation or enactment.
