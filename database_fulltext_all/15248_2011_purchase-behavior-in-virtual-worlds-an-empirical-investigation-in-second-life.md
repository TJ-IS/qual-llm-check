# Purchase behavior in virtual worlds: An empirical investigation in Second Life

Yue Guo, Stuart Barnes

Norwich Business School, University of East Anglia, Chancellor’s Drive, Norwich NR4 7TJ, United Kingdom

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 16 July 2009 Received in revised form 26 November 2010 Accepted 16 July 2011 Available online 30 July 2011

Keywords: Structural equation model PLS Virtual world Purchase behavior Second Life

## A B S T R A C T

Virtual worlds are an emerging online transaction context in which millions of players around the world participate and trade virtual items with one another. However, little research has been conducted into purchase behavior in this new context. To address this gap, we developed and tested a conceptual model of purchase behavior in virtual worlds using a combination of existing and new constructs. An online survey was conducted within Second Life (n = 250) and tested using structural equation modeling. We conclude with implications for practice and research limitations.

## 1. Motivation for the study

Virtual worlds are a relatively new online medium in which many people, represented by animated characters, may interact with each other and with virtual objects [1]. Similar to the real world, virtual world residents (players or users) also have consumer demand that leads to purchasing goods. However, such goods exist solely in digital form and within the virtual world environment, including virtual functional goods (used for increasing a character’s ability to compete, e.g. to be faster or stronger, or to assist in game-play, e.g. weapons and armour for fighting, magical items, or special in-world game modules) and decorative items (used for customizing a character’s appearance and for self-expression, e.g. character skins, body shapes, jewelry, clothing, pets, virtual homes and home items). Virtual worlds provide a significant change in economic activity: trading channels have been expanded from the traditional use of moneyto purchasing virtual items using real or virtual currency. DFC Intelligence estimated that virtual worlds will produce US\$13 billion in revenue by 2012, 40% of which will be attributed to trading virtual assets.

Buying behavior generally consists of five stages: need recognition, search for solutions, evaluation of alternatives, choice/purchase decision, and post-purchase behavior. Except for the last, the other four may be further simplified into three stages of Web-based shopping behavior illustrated in Fig. 1. A buyer’s choice between different shopping channels – such as offline stores, TV or Internet – is determined according to available marketing information and alternatives.

The first stage, problem/need recognition captures consumers perceptions of how products may help them to fill the difference between the desired and actual state. Traditional theories have provided insight into human motivations for purchasing real products. However, the emergence of the market for trading virtual assets has resulted in a need for studies to understand the value of virtual items: not all motivations that affect real products can explain actions in virtual worlds, such as the psychological and safety needs of Maslow. For example, customizing a character’s appearance and improving ability are two major reasons behind pursuing and buying virtual items. Players can obtain satisfaction through customizing characters’ appearance or showing a unique style using decorative virtual items [23]: in the virtual world There.com, players pay attention to their appearance, and one of the major activities is designing and selling clothes.

Thus, we were interested in establishing why individuals would spend considerable amounts of money on buying virtual items that exist solely in computer systems: what causes such seemingly irrational behavior?

Moreover, the second stage, the buying channel choice also suffers from gaps in research on virtual item shopping behavior. On the one hand, a customer needs to seek information about performance or other criteria. On the other hand, he or she needs to evaluate alternatives based on price, delivery time and so on. A variety of explanatory models have been developed aimed at predicting and explaining consumers’ choice of the Web-based purchase channel based on user acceptance theories such as TAM and the theory of planned behavior (TPB). Such studies have helped our understanding of why some individuals tend to purchase products/services online rather than through traditional channels [e.g. 14, 19].

Similarly, competition among multiple channels related to virtual item purchase behavior is also inevitable. However, the decision processes of consumer behavior in virtual worlds is quite different and more complex for two reasons: (1) the coexistence of non-purchase and purchase channels; and (2) virtual world trading platforms only assume channel functions and do not act as final vendors. Consequently, the decision processes of consumer behavior in the virtual world context involve five stages, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Thus, virtual item purchasing behavior requires new ideas generated by studies in the use of virtual worlds.

Understanding of the particular type of virtual world is also needed because specific mechanisms might alter the relative importance of factors on players’ purchase intention and actual purchase behavior. At present, popular 3-D virtual worlds are mainly divided into two categories: game-oriented (e.g. World of Warcraft, RuneScape and EverQuest) and socially oriented (e.g. Second Life, There and HiPiHi) [12]. The major difference between the two types is that a game-oriented virtual world provides a story-lined scene and fixed contents while a socially oriented one attempts to provide almost unlimited freedom in a simulated world in which participants use their imagination to create world content. In our research we focused on the latter.

Our two basic research questions therefore were: (1) what are the salient motivations underlying virtual world players’ intentions to pursue advanced, valuable virtual items in virtual worlds?, and (2) how do players obtain desired virtual items when facing multi-channel options, particularly including an in-world purchase channel?

## 2. The decision processes of shopping behavior in the B2C and virtual world contexts

## 2.1. Decision stages of shopping behavior in the virtual world

First, purchasing is sometimes the only method to obtain real products regardless of product type (physical or digital) although individuals may choose different purchase channels: online or offline. However, some authors have proposed that there are different channels to obtain virtual items in virtual worlds [11]; players may choose to obtain desired items through non-purchase channels or substitute non-monetary costs (e.g. time and energy). Players may get desired virtual items by fighting non-player characters (NPCs), that randomly disburse virtual items, or by finishing quests, or via self-design in socially oriented virtual worlds. The emergence of non-purchase channels therefore resulted in a new stage (stage 2).

Second, B2C Web-based shopping platforms (e.g. Amazon) not only assume almost all traditional retail channel functions but also act as vendors. However, virtual world trading platforms only offer an in-world virtual item transaction channel that allows players to trade with each other. Virtual world trading platforms do not supply any virtual items per se. Consequently, the choice of channels (vendors) in the B2C context is divided into two distinct stages (stages 3 and 4). From this point of view, the decision processes of purchase behavior in virtual worlds have some similarities to consumer-to-consumer (C2C) online shopping behavior, such as eBay.

![](/api/attachments/9JBPPE7W/fulltext/images/6c3fe72472ff1c373ec1ba46b8854b673fa8269712131e68632781c6da984b36.jpg)  
Fig. 2. Consumer behavior and the purchasing decision in the context of virtual worlds.

Due to the existence of stage 4, purchase behavior intention is categorized by one of the two kinds of purchase behavior intention: general and special purchase behavioral intention. We define general purchase behavioral intention as the subjective probability of one’s engagement in purchasing desired goods through the satisfied purchase channel. Special purchase behavioral intention is an immediate indication of an individual’s readiness to purchase an item from a specific vendor.

## 2.2. General and special purchase behavioral intention in the B2C context

In a B2C context, general purchase behavioral intention is the subjective probability of one’s engagement in purchasing desired physical or digital products via the website versus other available channels. Special purchase behavioral intention can thus be viewed as an immediate indication of an individual’s readiness to purchase.

Most studies of Web-based shopping behavior have not explicitly distinguished between general and special purchase behavioral intention. Websites are vendors so that the assessment of objects is related to specific sites. As a result, factors related to a user’s choice between the Web-based purchase channel and other channels are the equivalent of factors identifying a final specific purchase channel from available alternatives.

## 2.3. General and special purchase behavioral intention in the virtual world context

General purchase behavioral intention of a virtual item involves the subjective probability of obtaining a desired item through an in-world purchase channel. Special purchase behavioral intention is only an immediate indication of an individual’s readiness to purchase an item from a specific in-world vendor. First, general purchase behavioral intention is determined by an individual’s belief that the in-world shopping channel has an advantage and lower complexity than other in-world nonpurchase channels and any out-of-world purchase channels. Second, special purchase behavioral intention is mainly determined by a player’s belief that a specific vendor has a relative advantage over others (e.g. good reputation and reasonable price). Guo and Barnes’ exploratory research findings showed that factors related to a users’ choice between the in-world purchase channel and other channels were not equivalent to factors identifying a specific virtual vendor.

We therefore proposed that the main differences between general and special purchase behavioral intention should be clearly distinguished, because they related to two distinct decision stages (stages 2 and 4). The concepts are further illustrated in Fig. 3.

Thus, virtual item shopping behavior was also divided into two stages for addressing the issues. Here, we only focused on capturing key determinants influencing players’ general intention to purchase virtual items in virtual worlds rather than special purchase intention with a focus on specific purchase decisions. Therefore, the use of the term purchase behavioral intention in later sections of this paper pertain primarily to general purchase behavioral intention unless otherwise stated.

## 3. Theoretical background

We attempted to integrate factors related to general purchase behavioral intention into a unified conceptual model. These factors influence the first three decision stages of shopping behavior in the virtual world context.

3.1. Motivations behind pursuing virtual items when participating in virtual worlds (stage 1)

The majority of prior studies in this area have focused on legal issues of virtual item transactions or have explored the impact of virtual worlds on the real world from a purely economic point of view. Only a few studies have begun to explore factors that motivate individuals to pursue virtual items in virtual worlds. Nevertheless, these studies were limited as they were only based on a descriptive or theoretical analysis of virtual item transactions and lacked the rigor of empirical analysis. Their research findings needed to be further tested using large-scale samples before making generalizations. Guo and Barnes’ study offered an insight into the psychological processes of virtual item purchase behavior in virtual worlds from three perspectives: motivators for pursuing advanced virtual items, reasons for using virtual world trading platforms to obtain virtual items, and factors in making a specific purchase decision. Consequently, they sacrificed parsimony to illustrate the latent psychological processes that induce virtual item purchase behavior in virtual worlds. Although, the knowledge gained from their model provided essential elements for understanding the complex individual and contextual factors of virtual item purchase behavior, it was inappropriate for an empirical investigation. Some factors needed to be further improved and integrated into more abstract constructs.

Yee focused on investigating players’ motivations for participating in massively multiplayer online role-playing games (MMORPGs). From the motivation view, he proposed that the motivation for purchasing virtual items may be viewed as a subset of those for playing. Not all motivations are relevant to virtual items; some appear to be worthless in serving participation purposes of players, e.g. socialization, building relationships and teamwork. Lehdonvirta [15] further extended the functional decorative typology through functional, hedonic, and social attribute differentiation based on an empirical investigation of 14 virtual asset platforms. However, the platforms he examined were not limited exclusively to virtual worlds and referred also to virtual communities, such as Cyworld and Facebook. Accordingly, some extended attributes such as cultural references, provenance, and branding did not seem to be distinct attributes of virtual world items.

## 3.2. Transaction cost theory (TCT) (stage 2)

TCT was developed for solving economic problems in organizations; a transaction cost is the cost incurred in making an economic exchange. In the B2C context, it has been empirically examined and showed a negative influence on shopping behavior. When purchasing real products, consumers like to adopt a purchase channel associated with the lowest transaction cost; this is also true in a virtual world context by incorporating, for example, time and energy as cost elements. In addition, examination and postservice cost are eliminated because virtual items are free from performance and quality risk. We therefore proposed a more general category where all transaction costs were split into monetary- and non-monetary-cost.

## 3.3. UTAUT (stage 3)

In developing constructs that may explain players’ adoption of in-world trading platforms (stage 3) for purchasing virtual items, we needed to rely on theoretical models of IS adoption. TAM has been heavily applied in predicting online purchasing behavior. Similarly, virtual world’s trading platforms are complex IS. Hence, this analysis seemed to be applicable to the adoption of virtual world trading platforms.

We selected the UTAUT model developed by Venkatesh et al. [22] as the theoretical base for empirically developing constructs for explaining players’ adoption of the in-world purchase channel.

## 4. Formulation of a conceptual model of purchase behavior in virtual worlds

Our unified conceptual model (see Fig. 4.) was based on a threestep process for capturing factors influencing players’ decisions. We were interested in examining how much of the variance in general purchase behavioral intention could be explained by unifying these factors into a theoretical conceptual model.

![](/api/attachments/9JBPPE7W/fulltext/images/95d6221e5d1d1956edda708b3f31edbf7d610cabb2fb9406289caad1651bc6ff.jpg)  
Fig. 4. The research model.

At step one, we developed two new constructs (advancement and customization) that were empirically used in testing the influence of motivational factors on purchasing virtual items. Yee’s research findings had identified ten motivational factors for explaining participation in MMORPGs. Players may be satisfied and gain a sense of achievement through customizing character appearance and advancing the character’s overall competitive ability. Thus, we first added customization and advancement as basic constructs for capturing motivational factors; we did not recognize achievement as a higher construct in our research model.

Guo and Barnes’ study proposed three motivators for pursuing advanced virtual items: character competency (player advancement in characters’ overall fight or survival capability), perceived enjoyment, and the requirements of quest systems. In addition, prior studies [e.g. 21] suggested the importance of hedonic factors (perceived enjoyment) for explaining users’ IS adoption and online shopping behavior; however the meaning of perceived enjoyment was considered to depend on virtual world experience rather than IS usage behavior. Guo and Barnes suggested that an enjoyable participation experience should induce players to pursue advanced virtual items, resulting in a possible purchase of virtual items. The third motivational factor, the requirements of quest systems, was ignored in our research, because it has low consistency across various virtual worlds. Many players never finish any quest that they consider dull. Furthermore, most socially oriented virtual worlds do not integrate any quest systems.

Based on prior exploratory studies, three constructs (advancement, customization, and perceived enjoyment) were employed to capture important intrinsic motivators trigging player pursuit of advanced, valuable virtual items, leading to three hypotheses:

H1. Advancement will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

H2. Customization will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

H3. Perceived enjoyment will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

In the second stage, players needed to consider how to obtain desired virtual items: whether to pay or contribute non-monetary cost or effort. In prior studies, transaction cost and price have been found to have a negative impact on purchase behavioral intention, while users’ perceptions of potential benefits derived from actual purchasing behavior significantly motivate users. Moreover, perceived value has been found to be an antecedent of purchase behavioral intention in both offline channels [2] and Web-based platforms [6].

However, in virtual worlds, non-purchase channels having nonmonetary costs are available to players. Players first decide what kinds of costs they prefer, then they choose a specific channel which needed the evaluation of a new construct: perceived value of virtual items, involving a personal decision in which each player perceives different weights for monetary and non-monetary costs based on his or her present status, e.g. student or working professional. Apparently, the perceived benefits that a specific virtual item delivers have a fixed value for the player regardless of obtaining channels. Thus, a smaller perceived cost will result in a higher perceived value. We therefore hypothesized:

H4. Perceived value will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

Finally, at step 3, we formed a preliminary research framework for capturing key factors that are important in making decisions at stage 3 (i.e. affecting the adoption of in-world purchase channel). Effort expectancy, performance expectancy, and social influence were used as three initial key determinants. However, we did not include facilitating conditions, as these are not important in forming general purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds. Subsequently, we thoroughly examined key variables based on the work of Chang et al. [5] who reviewed the literature related to the adoption of online shopping published between 1990 and 2003. Their work considerably facilitates our literature search.

All key variables showing a significant influence on online shopping in at least one empirical study were assessed to see whether each was still likely to be significant for influencing players’ decisions at stage 3. When we perceived that one variable was more likely to contribute to players’ adoption of virtual world trading platforms, it was further conceptually compared with the three basic constructs of the preliminary framework to see whether it might fit into the domain of the three existing determinants or represented a new determinant. At the end of the comparison process, all determinants were again reviewed to ensure that there were no obvious overlaps among them. Variables indicating similar ideas or concepts were reviewed and combined into one new construct. Four constructs finally resulted; they were: effort expectancy, performance expectancy, social influence, and habit.

Effort and performance expectancies are two major beliefs with regard to system characteristics in the context of IS adoption (inworld trading platforms here).

Guo and Barnes proposed that two constructs, effort- and performance-expectancy, were utilitarian factors helping to explain the relative advantages of using in-world trading platforms, which influence players’ purchase channel choice when they consider buying desired virtual items from others. Social influence has been frequently used as an important construct for predicting behavioral intention. We therefore included three hypotheses:

H5. Effort expectancy will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

H6. Performance expectancy will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

H7. Social influence will positively affect players’ purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds.

Moreover, many studies have proposed that habit and purchase behavioral intention may be used as independent antecedents of actual behavior. The moderating effect of habit on the relationship between purchase behavioral intention and actual purchase behavior was also added to our conceptual model. Limayem et al.’s [16] research findings proposed that the relationship between behavioral intention and actual IS continuance behavior may be weakened when an individual habitually uses it. Thus, we placed research emphasis on one-off purchase behavior rather than continuance because the latter is difficult to measure in a virtual world.

Unlike other IS adoption behavior, it is impossible that a player never uses trading platforms in virtual worlds – especially in a new virtual world. Indeed, inexperienced players are relatively unaware of outside trading channels. During the early stage of virtual world participation, Second Life (SL) users may obtain many free items or buy basic virtual items through trading platforms. Moreover, an individual may sell or exchange virtual items by using trading platforms. Therefore, we believe that such daily use behavior has led SL players into the habit of using in-world trading platforms even though they first spend considerable money on purchasing valuable virtual items for meeting their intrinsic motivators. Thus, there is no conflict when adding habit into the conceptual model, even when we focus on initial purchase behavior towards valuable virtual items. Thus, two more hypotheses were postulated:

H8. Habit will positively affect players’ actual purchase behavior in virtual worlds.

H9. Habit will negatively affect the relationship between purchase behavioral intention and actual purchase behavior in virtual worlds.

In sum, determinants that appear to be significant for determining whether players may finally form general purchase behavioral intention in virtual worlds were empirically developed into eight theoretical constructs including: effort expectancy, performance expectancy, perceived value, perceived enjoyment, social influence, habit, advancement, and customization. Moreover, the first three were identified as extrinsic motivators, while perceived enjoyment, advancement, and customization were three intrinsic motivators based on Childers et al.’s [7] typology of motivations for purchase behavior. Extrinsic motives refer to the undertaking of an activity for the achievement of goals/benefits external to the system-user interaction while intrinsic motives refer to undertaking an activity solely for creating a pleasurable experience derived from the system-user interaction per se. Behavioral intention was modeled as the immediate antecedent to actual behavior although we focused on general purchase behavioral intention.

## 5. Study design and method

In our research, Second Life was selected as the target virtual world. Second Life is currently the most popular adult socially oriented virtual world. Since opening to the public in 2003, it has grown explosively and has more than 20 million accounts and approximately 800,000 unique residents who login more than once per month from around the globe. To obtain data for our research an online survey was carried out in SL. Its URL Web site was disseminated by means of virtual world internal communication facilities, official Web sites, and professional game forums. A total of 262 completed responses were received within one month and 12 invalid responses were deleted. Therefore, a total of 250 qualified responses were obtained for quantitative analysis. This sample is comparable to similar studies conducted in SL [3,24], which had samples ranging from n = 188 to n = 223. Prior to data collection, the required sample size was computed based on the power analysis technique of G\*Power 3.0 [9]. For our conceptual model and a medium effect size (1 - b = 0.95, a = 0.05) the sample size should therefore be at least 166. Thus, 250 responses were enough for detecting a medium effect size and use of the SEM-PLS technique.

## 5.1. Measurement

In our research, multi-item scale measurement was employed. Where possible, we adopted items used and validated in prior studies, however we were unable to use replications of many of the items because they were originally designed within the context of general IS adoption or Web-based platforms. Instead, we therefore developed 10 original scale items using the work of prior studies to guide us and modified 18 items from previous scales to relate specifically to our research. The items are provided in Appendix.

The final version of the questionnaire contained 43 questions (13 general ones and 30 scale items). Perceived enjoyment and perceived social status were measured using 7-point semantic differentials and advancement and customization were measured using a 7-point Likert scale with anchors ranging from 1 (not important at all) to 7 (extremely important). All other items were scored on a 7-point Likert scale with a score of 7 indicating ‘strongly agree’ and a score of 1 indicating ‘strongly disagree’. In addition, we used self-reported purchase frequency and money spent as a proxy for actual purchase. Prior to releasing the formal online survey, questionnaire pre-testing was carried out among 20 players who had high levels of participation experience in different virtual worlds; our intent was to further enhance content validity by assessing the difference between the understanding of participants on measurement items and the definitions of the variables that they are intended to measure.

Advancement consisted of four measurement items: becoming powerful (ADVANCE1), acquiring rare items (ADVANCE2), becoming richer (ADVANCE3) and well-known (ADVANCE4). All items were adapted from Yee, except for ADVANCE3 which was newly developed for this study, influenced by Manninen and Kujanpa¨a¨ [17]. The customization construct was operationalized with four items. Only CUS1 was a slight modification of one of the Yee’s items tailored to virtual worlds. No existing items were appropriate for measuring the construct for our study and thus, CUS2, CUS3 and CUS4 were developed based on the concept of customization.

For enjoyment, respondents were asked to choose where their position laid between two bipolar words about perceived enjoyment on four seven-point semantic differential scales (ENJOY1, ENJOY2, ENJOY3 and ENJOY4). They were directly adopted from van der Heijden; the question statement was tailored to the meaning of the construct in our study (i.e. enjoyment of the participation experience).

For measuring performance expectancy, PERFORM1 was adopted from Shang et al. [18] and modified for our context. PERFORM2 and PERFORM3 were developed based on previous studies [10].

Our effort expectancy scale consisted of EFFORT1, EFFORT2, and EFFORT3. Much of the literature that used effort expectancy was thoroughly reviewed and EFFORT1 and EFFORT3 were adapted from Shim et al. [20], Gefen et al., and Venkatesh et al. EFFORT2 was specifically developed for our study also based on these studies. Habit was operationalized with three items including automatic behavior (HABIT1), natural choice (HABIT2), and obvious choice (HABIT3), adapted from Limayem [16].

Social influence was operationalized with two items: SI1 was adapted from Hansen et al. [13] while SI2 was adopted from Venkatesh et al. and modified for this study. Based on transaction cost theory (TCT) and prior work, two items including PV1 and PV2 were newly developed by us to capture virtual item value perceptions of individuals when they attempt to pursue and obtain desired virtual items.

Purchase behavioral intention was measured with three items. PI1 was developed specifically for this study while PI2 and PI3 were adapted from previous literature [8].

## 5.2. Data analysis

The proposed model and hypothesized relationships among constructs were evaluated using SEM-PLS modeling in SmartPLS 2.0M. PLS has become popular in modern research, particularly because it has specific advantages, such as minimal demands on measurement scales, sample distribution, and sample size. It excels at causal-predictive analysis in which hypothesized relationships are complex and few bases have been established.

As illustrated in Table 1, all the values of AVE and CR for all constructs were satisfactory, with composite reliability at 0.746 or more and an AVE of 0.584 or above. Thus the measurement items we used converged on the same latent construct.

## 5.2.2. Discriminant validity

To assess discriminant validity, we developed a matrix of correlations between constructs with reflective measures; we replaced the diagonal with the square root of the AVE (see Table 2)

and found that the square-root of AVE for each construct was higher than the elements off the diagonal. We also assessed discriminant validity by making a comparison between the loadings of an item and its associated construct with its crossloading on other constructs. For our model, all items loaded on their corresponding constructs more strongly than their crossloadings on other constructs (see Table 3).

Therefore, there was strong empirical support for the reliability and validity of the constructs in our research model.

## 5.3. Non-response bias

In order to check for non-response bias, all collected responses were divided into two groups: early response (n = 225) and a late response (n = 25). Late responses, i.e. those received towards the end of the survey period are considered similar to non-responses. Late responses are typically used as a measure of non-response bias and numerous recent studies have employed the technique [e.g. 4]. There was no statistically significant difference between early and late respondents’ key demographic information (age, income, and education). Hence, we believe that non-response bias is unlikely to be an issue.

## 6. Results

We computed t-statistics and path significance levels for each of the hypothesized relationships using the bootstrapping method. Path coefficients and $R ^ { 2 }$ values were obtained by running the PLS algorithm to assess the predictive performance of the structural model. The construct for purchase intention had an $R ^ { 2 }$ value of 0.453 indicating that the model accounted for 45% of the variance in SL players’ general purchase intention. Thus nearly half of the intention to use the in-world purchasing channel in SL was explained by the respondents’ participation enjoyment, character advancement and customization, expectations about ease of use and performance, and perceived value of virtual items. Moreover, our model explained a moderate level of variance in SL players’ actual purchase behavior (R<sup>2</sup> = 0.258), which is surprising because of the focus on general purchase behavioral intention. Overall, the empirical results strongly confirmed the explanatory power of our research model over players’ virtual item purchasing behavior.

Overall, as shown in Fig. 5, there was strong support for most of the relationships in our model. The extrinsic motivators were all strong determinants of purchase behavioral intention at the 0.1 percent level of significance. The intrinsic motivators also showed significant relationships. The influence of customization on purchase behavioral intention was particularly strong – the path coefficient being the largest of all direct relationships on purchase behavioral intention in our model (p < 0.001). Perceived enjoyment was also significantly related to purchase intention, albeit at a lower level of significance (p < 0.05). However, our findings showed that the path between advancement and purchase behavioral intention in SL was negative and significant at the 5 percent level. Compared to game-oriented players, SL players may freely design and create virtual items – this is a major attraction of socially oriented virtual worlds. It seems that a stronger desire to pursue character advancement does not result in the higher subjective probability of using in-world purchase channels to obtain desired virtual items; this is an extremely interesting finding and contrary to our expectations.

The path between social influence and purchase intention was not supported and the path coefficient was close to zero. However, the lack of a significant relationship between social influence and general purchase behavioral intention was not surprising. Many previous studies found that the effect of subjective norms on behavioral intention only becomes significant in mandatory contexts. In our research, this means that only when a player perceives buying virtual items as a means to avoid punishments or gain rewards via others will the impact of social influence on behavioral intention be important. However, virtual world participation is voluntary and most virtual item purchases in the virtual world context are not compliant behavior.

Although habit exerted a strong direct and positive influence on actual purchase behavior (p < 0.001), it did not act as a moderating variable in the relationship between behavioral intention and actual purchase behavior in the context of SL; this was inconsistent with previous published research [16].

## 7. Discussion and conclusions

We attempted to understand the factors influencing virtual item purchase behavior in virtual worlds. We proposed that buyer decision processes become more complex in virtual worlds because non-purchase channels are available and virtual world trading platforms only assume channel functions. Vendor responsibilities are undertaken by individual players. Consequently, our findings indicated that the extant literature does not adequately explore factors that either discourage or encourage players to purchase desired virtual items when participating in virtual worlds.

We introduced a model for the measurement of virtual item purchase behavior in virtual worlds by using questions previously developed, and some created specifically for this study.

Overall, our research findings indicated that more than 45% of the variance in general purchase behavioral intention can be explained through our theoretical model. The statistical tests provided good support for our conceptual model.

Knowledge of factors influencing players’ virtual item purchase behavior, coupled with an effective measurement method should be valuable to virtual world developers. If a manufacturer were to add a function to in-world trading platform allowing a player to preview the change in a character after equipping desired virtual items, the player would have a better idea of the value of the addition. Players should also be afforded the freedom to customize their own avatars, providing an important driver for purchase behavior.

It is, however, necessary to recognize the limitations of our research. First, we cannot claim that our results hold equally well in other virtual worlds because characteristics unique to specific virtual worlds can potentially alter the relative importance of factors influencing players’ purchase intention and actual purchase behavior. Second, another potential limitation is that we adopted a self-reported measure of purchase frequency and money spent as a proxy for actual purchase. All seven independent variables were modeled as a direct determinant of behavioral intention (except for habit). Thus, it does not minimize the explanatory ability of the conceptual model even if we lack appropriate items for measuring actual purchase behavior in view of our placing emphasis on general purchase behavioral intention.

Finally, although our research developed new items or adapted old ones based on prior studies, this was the first time that these items were examined in the context of virtual worlds. The ability of these items to reflect the complexities of players’ perceptions and intentions in virtual worlds has not previously been fully explored; thus the measures for the conceptual model should be viewed as preliminary.
