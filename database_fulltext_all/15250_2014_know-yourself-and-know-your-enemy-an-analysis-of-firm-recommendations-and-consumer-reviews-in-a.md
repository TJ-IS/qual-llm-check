# KNOW YOURSELF AND KNOW YOUR ENEMY: AN ANALYSIS OF FIRM RECOMMENDATIONS AND CONSUMER REVIEWS IN A COMPETITIVE ENVIRONMENT<sup>1</sup>

Wael Jabr Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 U.S.A. {wjabr@gsu.edu}

Zhiqiang (Eric) Zheng Naveen Jindal School of Management, University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX 75080 U.S.A. {ericz@utdallas.edu}

Reviews and product recommendations at online stores enable customers to readily evaluate alternative products prior to purchase. In this context, firms generate recommendations referring customers to a wider variety of products. They also display customer-generated online reviews in order to facilitate evaluation of those recommended products. This study integrates these two IT artifacts to investigate consumer choice vis-à- vis competing products. We use a dataset we collected from Amazon.com consisting of books, sales ranks, recommendations, reviews, and reviewers. We derive the granular impact of reviews, product referrals, and reviewer opinions on product sale dynamics within a competitive market using comprehensive econometric analyses.

Keywords: Online review, eWOM, competition, recommendation system, instrument variable

## Introduction

Online retailers currently implement and leverage a variety of sales support tools. Two such prominent tools are recommendation systems (generated by the firm) and online review systems (generated by customers). Recommendation systems are widely used to entice customers into considering alternative products and evaluating competing offerings (Higginbotham 2010; Thompson 2008). They are effective in helping consumers reach a wider range of products (Anderson 2006), increasing firm profits by billions of dollars (Brynjolfsson et al. 2003). These systems also help firms convert browsers into buyers, cross-sell products, and increase customer loyalty via a customized searching experience (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). The importance of recommendation systems is manifested in firms’ willingness to pay large lump sums toward acquiring and improving these systems (a salient example is the much-publicized Netflix prize<sup>2</sup>).

The other tool, online review systems, helps consumers evaluate offerings through the lens of previous customers. This electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) benefits both consumers and sellers. It is believed that eWOM has become one of the key sources for consumers to obtain product information and reduce uncertainties regarding price, quality, shipment, etc. (Jiang and Srinivasan 2012). For example, the recent Nielsen Online Global Consumer Study (2009) found that consumergenerated WOM was the most credible form of advertising for 70 percent of customers. This has prompted online retailers to actively solicit user-generated content in addition to passively hosting it. For example, users are often encouraged to provide feedback as well as peer endorsement for other users’ reviews (e.g., via comments and reviews on reviews).

While recommendation systems and eWOM channels might appear as two separate tools that influence consumer choice, customers at online stores are often exposed to both simultaneously during a shopping session. For example, when searching for a book of interest (hereafter referred to as focal book) at a retailer site such as Amazon.com, it is very common for a shopper to examine related recommended books, reading reviews of both the focal and alternative books. However, despite the increasing prevalence of these books, there is as of yet no research examining the combined effects of multiple sales-supporting tools in online settings.<sup>3</sup> Our first research objective is accordingly to provide a unified view of the impact of recommendation systems and eWOM on product sales.

Furthermore, the literature on recommendation systems and eWOM has primarily focused on a single-product view (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Ghose et al. 2012), overlooking the entire network the product is embedded within. However, in practice, online retailers routinely recommend a wide variety of related products and effectively interlink them (Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan 2012). For instance, when evaluating a book at Amazon.com, a customer usually receives a list of recommendations related to the focal book. One type of recommendation is presented as “customers who bought this item also bought these other items.” This introduces referrals among products by linking them to each other, enabling potential customers to broaden their search space and makes it easy for them to explore related products. A given book can benefit from others referring it while at the same time suffering from other books being recommended along with it. This suggests that, in order to examine the overall effect of recommendations on a product, it is necessary to analyze the network of related product referrals.

Additionally, recommendations can have a subtle but significant impact on how online reviews influence purchase decisions. A customer routinely has access to not only online reviews for the product she is considering, but also all reviews of recommended products. This potentially confounds the influence that online reviews exert on consumer purchasing decisions and necessitates a more careful analysis that can tease out the differential effect of each component. For example, a product receiving high user ratings typically signifies high sales (Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). However, the effect of own reviews may not be so obvious when factoring in the effect of competing products (e.g., when competitors receive even more positive reviews). Our second research objective is, therefore, to study the impact of competition among a network of recommended products on consumer choice.

The textual contents and ratings of online reviews reflect the reviewers’ own experiences (Hu et al. 2008), the quality of which pertain to the reviewer’s identity (Forman et al 2008). A meaningful analysis of online reviews must, therefore, factor in reviewer characteristics. In online settings, various cues are available for constructing reviewer “profiles” based on reviewer history. Recent eWOM literature has proposed ways to identify the characteristics of an influential user or opinion leader’s profile (e.g., Iyengar et al. 2011). Opinion leaders’ agreements or disagreements with the reviewed product have been found to be a crucial influence on the word of mouth effect (Sun 2012; West and Broniarczyk 1998). Our final research objective is, therefore, to study the role of reviewers on customer choice.

Access to both recommendations and eWOM allows customers to readily identify related products and corresponding reviewer opinions, in turn influencing customers’ product choices. We accordingly seek to investigate the joint impact of three key factors on sales: (1) the referral position of a product, (2) the eWOM of competing products, and (3) the importance of reviewers and their level of agreement regarding a product. We conduct an empirical study addressing these questions with data collected from Amazon.com. Our data captures details on each book and its competition, along with complete information on all reviews and reviewers available at Amazon.com for the studied books.

Our empirical analysis yields three major findings. First, recommendation systems intensify competition among products vying for eWOM. Second, product referrals contribute significantly to sales, but these sales are highly subject to the prominence of a book within a network of referrals. Third, we find that reviewers’ prominence accrued from historical reviews plays a significant role in eWOM and, subsequently, sales. We use these findings to derive the implications for both content providers (authors and publishers) and intermediaries (retailers).

We begin by reviewing the related literature. We then develop our hypotheses, describe the data and variables used, and provide the econometric analysis. A discussion of the results follows. In the final section, we present our conclusions.

## Literature Review

## Recommendation Systems

Firms extensively use recommendation systems to promote sales and retain customers (Adomavicius and Tuzhilin 2005). These systems predict and anticipate what consumers may be interested in and accordingly display these predictions during the course of their shopping sessions in order to help consumers make better choices (Hennig-Thurau et al. 2012) or even influence consumer preferences (Häubl and Murray 2003). Senecal and Nantel’s (2004) experiments show that users who consult with product recommendations select recommended products twice as often as those who do not. Effective use of recommendations often leads to greater customer loyalty and higher sales via targeted cross-product promotions (Kumar and Benbasat 2006), as well as higher levels of consumer perceived usefulness and ease of use (Benlian et al. 2012). Brynjolfsson et al. (2003) show that recommendations by online bookstores enhance consumer welfare by approximately U.S. \$1 billion per year. These gains are typically attributable to the wide range of products customers consider in their basket set construction (Anderson 2006; Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). An interesting finding by Fleder et al. (2010) shows that users may become more similar to one another in their purchases because they respond positively to products suggested by recommender systems. This increased similarity occurs because consumers purchase more after receiving recommendations, and intriguingly tend to buy a more similar mix of products.

Yet the extant literature has so far focused on the beneficiaries of online recommendation systems (i.e., customers) but paid less attention to the impact of increased product availability on interproduct competition between sellers. This focus portrays an incomplete picture since the derived consumer welfare surplus must come at the expense of the marketer’s profit.

## Word-of-Mouth

Word-of-mouth has long been recognized as an effective communication channel for reducing uncertainties regarding product quality (e.g., Ellison and Fudenberg 1995). In the online setting, studies have focused on the influence that consumer reviews have on sales (e.g., Jiang and Srinivasan 2012). Researchers have proposed a variety of eWOM metrics such as volume (i.e., the total number of reviews), valence (i.e., the rating a user assigns to the product), and dispersion (i.e., the ratings variance). Liu (2006) finds that the volume of eWOM provides a good predictor of revenue. Chen et al. (2007) show that higher ratings are associated with higher book sales. Dellarocas et al. (2007) conclude that both the volume and average rating of reviews are statistically significant in predicting future revenues. Others show that variance in ratings mediates the consumer impact of product ratings (for an empirical analysis, see Clemons et al. 2006; for an analytical analysis, see Sun 2012).

Since reviews can become inconsistent and contradictory over time, a more granular analysis level (in contrast to the aforementioned aggregate-level metrics) is essential in order to elucidate the intricate impact of reviews on sales (Archak et al. 2011). For example, an average three-star product rating (on a scale of one to five) may not necessarily signal a neutral sentiment. It might be a result of two dichotomous groups, one giving a five-star and the other a one-star rating.

Furthermore, the online identity of the reviewers makes a difference (Van den Bulte and Joshi 2007). Consumers evaluate the helpfulness of reviews by relying on reviewer disclosure of identity-descriptive information (Forman et al. 2008). In an experimental setting, Connors et al. (2011) find that participants regarded reviews by “experts” more highly. A three-star rating assigned by a positive reviewer (one who always assigns high ratings) will be read differently than if a three-star rating is assigned by a negative reviewer (one who always assigns low ratings) (Archak et al. 2011). We address all of these issues by deriving more granular measures at the disaggregated individual review and reviewer levels.

## Competition Effect Embedded in Recommendations and eWOM

There have been some recent attempts to address the potential competition originating from either recommendations or online reviews. For example, Hinz and Eckert (2010) use an analytical model to show that recommendations yield a substitution effect. Jiang and Wang (2008) study the role of consumer reviews in intensifying online competition and find that a firm operating within a competitive market could even be hurt by an improvement in its product rating. However, only recently have studies begun examining the joint effect of recommendation and reviews, although with a different focus than our work. Kumar and Benbasat (2006) find an increase in perceived usefulness with the presence of recommendations and reviews, highlighting the implications for website design. Pathak et al.’s (2010) interest is closest to ours, showing that firm- and user-generated recommendations increase demand by providing additional information concerning the quality and match of products. However, they do not address the impact of competition generated by these services as we do.

The literature has primarily examined the effect of either recommendation or online review in isolation and from a single product’s perspective. These approaches overlook the combined effects of user reviews and firm recommendations as well as the resulting competition, substitution, and complementarity effects among products. We next develop our hypotheses, addressing these gaps.

## Hypotheses

Our hypotheses investigate the role that recommendations, online reviews, and reviewers play in influencing consumers’ purchasing decisions within a competitive setting.

## Impact of Product Referrals

Retailers routinely recommend related products. These recommended products are usually hyperlinked to the product a consumer is considering, enabling customers to navigate easily among the interlinked products. Oestreicher-Singer and Sundararajan (2012) liken this process to walking the aisles of a physical store; products displayed in proximity to each other compete more intensively for customer attention given their shelf position. The influence of position on demand within a network of associated products is well recognized in the brick-and-mortar space (Bultez and Naert 1988). In fact, demand for shelf space is so high that it has been dubbed “the most expensive real estate of the world,”<sup>4</sup> with consumer-goods manufacturers spending hundreds of billions of dollars annually to use this space (Forster 2002). Recommendations play the same role in the online world. This is evident in the sponsored search context where firms aggressively bid to secure the top paid positions (Ghose and Yang 2009). Prominent placement helps retailers earn significantly greater profit even in a virtually frictionless online world (Brynjolfsson and Smith 2000).

In the offline world, the relationship between products is often invisible to consumers and the product referrals must be explicitly solicited via instruments such as surveys (Bengtsson and Servais 2005). In contrast, online products are often associated through clearly identifiable referrals such as clickable hyperlinks (e.g., recommended books on Amazon.com) that enable objective and precise identification of product associations. This nexus of associated products effectively results in a network of product referrals. Within such a network, the flow of potential consumers from one product page to another is heavily influenced by the link structure between these pages, affecting the redistribution of demand among these interlinked nodes (Katona and Sarvary 2008).

Yet not all nodes benefit equally; the more visible and central nodes reap the greatest benefit (Stephen and Toubia 2010). When investigating strategic hyperlinking decisions among content providers, Dellarocas et al. (2012) show that this results in an increase of total traffic (and therefore profits) for the highest quality sites, but not necessarily for all sites. Understanding the structural importance of a node vying for attention within the network of referrals is, therefore, crucial. The literature typically uses centrality as a proxy for the importance of interlinked nodes within a network (Ahuja et al. 2003; Grewal et al. 2006).

Likewise, centrality of the competing product plays an essential (but opposite) role in the sales of the focal product. A higher level of centrality received by the competing product enhances its appeal and conversely weakens that of the focal product. Yet this important interplay between a product’s own influence and that of competitors’ has not been fully probed (Libai et al. 2009). We therefore examine the two sides of competition and hypothesize on the relationship between centrality and sales due to referrals as:

H1: A higher referral centrality of own (competing) product is associated with higher (lower) product sales.

## Impact of Competitor’s WOM

Marketing researchers have long called for extending the traditional marketing mix variables to include WOM among consumers (Weitz 1985). This is even more critical within a competitive environment. Jiang and Wang (2008) find that improvements in a firm’s review ratings result in further separation between the perceived quality of the product and that of its competitors. With the prevalence of consumer reviews, a vendor within a competitive market shows greater incentive than a monopolistic vendor to change its marketing mix in order to induce consumers into buying (Jiang and Chen 2007). Firms operating within a competitive environment are also compelled to spend additional resources on monitoring and even manipulating reviews because consumer perceptions can otherwise become biased against them (Dellarocas 2006). For example, Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) present a scenario where firms with inferior products must spend more resources on promotional chat activities than firms with superior products. More recently, Carmi et al. (2011) show that a product derives its value from intrinsic characteristics as well as extrinsic ones that originate from recommendations by other connected products. These findings prompt us to cast doubt on the common practice of examining the impact of a product’s reviews in isolation regardless of its competitor’s performance. We therefore extend the WOM literature by investigating the intricate effects of WOM within the context of online referrals (consisting of recommended products). We explicitly consider the impact of competing products’ reviews on a focal product. All else being equal, an improvement in the reviews of a competing product can lure customers away and lower the remaining customers’ evaluation of the focal product, in turn leading to a lower conversion rate for these potential customers.<sup>5</sup> We therefore postulate that:

H2: Product sales drop with improvements in the reviews of a competing product.

## Impact of Reviewers

While it is a well-known and well-addressed topic that all reviews are not created equal (Archak et al. 2011), less attention has been paid to the fact that all reviewers are also not created equal. Online reviews may not be verifiable, objective, or fully credible, and the impact of reviews must, therefore, be adjusted by the reviewers’ profiles (Chen et al. 2007). Studies in decision science have shown that people process information not only by seeking cues from the actual content of the information, but also consider a variety of extrinsic signals including those concerning the persuasiveness of the information communicator (Chaiken 1987). Specifically, the perceived quality of reviewers may shape consumers’ perceptions of reviews (Forman et al. 2008). Analysis of online reviews must, therefore, be complemented with a thorough understanding of who the reviewers are as they can differ drastically from each other in terms of expertise, devotion, and disposition in writing an online review. Reviewers exerting disproportionately high influence are typically referred to as opinion leaders (Iyengar et al. 2011; Van den Bulte and Joshi 2007). Various approaches have been proposed for identifying such opinion leaders. In an online marketplace setting such as Amazon.com, review contributors can be tracked accurately since each has a profile displaying detailed information on all previous posts. Each reviewer can be evaluated using the rating, valence, and content of the reviews she posts; an opinion leader can be readily identified via these measurements. The extent to which there is agreement (or disagreement) among opinion leaders vis-à-vis the attractiveness of a product becomes a key factor influencing consumer choice. Research has examined the influence of expert opinions on consumers’ product evaluations in terms of agreement or disagreement among critics (West and Broniarczyk 1998). A high level of agreement suggests that there is a consensus on product evaluation either favorably or unfavorably (as in Sun 2012). Such an agreement among reviewers can be traced to all products evaluated by the same reviewers (Hilary and Menzly 2006). This is analogous to the well-known phenomenon in financial analysts’ forecasts where disagreement among analysts over historical stock forecasts increases uncertainty concerning that stock’s future (e.g., Sadka and Scherbina 2007). Following this line of reasoning, we make the following conjecture:

H3a: Collective endorsement (rejection) by opinion leaders improves (reduces) product sales.

In line with our earlier discussion regarding competing products, it is also worthwhile to investigate the role reviewers’ agreement plays in the focal product compared to that of competing products. We therefore we hypothesize that:

H3b: The more the focal product receives endorsement relative to its competitor, the higher its sales.

## Data and Variables

We opt to study books because they are typical search products and have been found to be highly subject to eWOM influence. We restrict our analysis to four book categories: programming, business, health, and guidebooks. These are categories for which a customer is more likely to choose among competing books. Consider a customer who is interested in purchasing a PHP programming book. She does not have a predetermined book in mind to purchase. She is open to any good book on this topic and is willing to select the best among a list of related books recommended by the retailer or other users. This decision is accordingly a choice of which competing book to purchase.

We collected data from Amazon.com on 1,740 randomly selected books within the aforementioned four categories over a period from May 2007 to May 2009. We selected books that received at least 25 reviews in order to ensure a meaningful analysis. For each book in our dataset, we extracted detailed data on the book, its main competing book, reviews, and reviewers. We then constructed the variables of interest which we detail in subsequent sections and appendices.

## Dependent Variables

We obtained Amazon.com sales ranks for the books in our dataset from a third-party company. We calculated the mean sales rank on a weekly basis and then used a logarithmic transformation. The mean sales rank has been regularly used as a standard proxy for sales (e.g., Archak et al. 2011; Brynjolfsson et al. 2003). In the section on robustness checks, we used an alternative dependent variable calculated as the difference between the rank of the focal book versus the competing book,<sup>6</sup> showing that our findings persist.

## Independent Variables

We construct four sets of variables: (1) review-related, (2) reviewer-related, (3) competitor-related, and (4) referralrelated variables. We dynamically constructed and calculated all variables up to the current week under investigation.

## Review-Related Variables

The extant literature on the effects of online word of mouth has identified three main metrics: valence, variance, and volume (for a more complete list, see Moe and Trusov 2011). Valence is typically represented as a measure of the average rating (Dellarocas et al. 2007). At Amazon.com, reviewers can express their opinions regarding a product by rating it on a scale from one to five. However, a product might receive very heterogeneous opinions over time (see Figure 1), leading to a bimodal or J-shaped distribution of review ratings (Hu et al. 2009). When aggregated indiscriminately, valence values may provide a distorted view. Researchers have accordingly suggested measuring positive and negative reviews separately (Archak et al. 2011; Godes and Mayzlin 2004; Liu 2006). We follow this suggestion and label all reviews with a rating of three or lower as negative and those greater as positive. The variance in ratings has been measured in a variety of ways including statistical variance (Clemons et al. 2006; Sun 2012) and entropy (Godes and Mayzlin 2004); we adopt the former for its simplicity and informativeness.

Volume is most commonly measured as the number of reviews a product receives (Clemons et al. 2006; Forman et al. 2008). Some studies also weigh volume using the quality of reviews (Chen et al. 2007). Quality in this context is accounted for by Amazon.com’s feature that allows subsequent users to cast “helpfulness” votes on reviews, indicating the level of endorsement by users. These are posted above the original review as the number of helpful votes received out of the total number of users who voted. We used the number of helpful and total votes to compute the fraction of votes evaluating the review as helpful.

We include the following well-established control variables related to reviews:

Variables Pos Review Volume and Neg Review Volume: each is a count of the number of positive (negative) reviews received up to the period under study.

Variables Pos Helpful Vote and Neg Helpful Vote:<sup>7</sup> each is a measure of the agreement that reviews receive from the community, for positive and negative reviews respectively.

Variable Review Rating Avg: this is an average of the ratings received up to the period under study. It is a measure of the average support received.

Variable Review Rating StdDev: this is the standard deviation of the ratings received up to the period under study. It is a measure of the dispersion in opinions.

Variable Weighted Review Rating: this combines the ratings and votes received to produce a composite rating weighted by the helpful votes.

## Reviewer-Related Variables

One distinctive aspect of this research is that we distinguish reviewers based on their historical reviewing records. We use this information to characterize reviewers along two dimensions: agreement (i.e., the level of concordance among reviewers vis-à-vis the focal book), and visibility (i.e., based on how prolific and approved a reviewer is). We identified agreement among reviewers by examining the spread in ratings. Reviewers are in agreement when their ratings are one point or less apart; otherwise they are in disagreement. We identified visible reviewers, hereafter labeled as top reviewers, using the Amazon Reviewer Rank feature that assigns ranks to reviewers based on their contributions. Once we identified highly visible reviewers, we derived the count of high-visibility reviewers who endorsed each of the focal books (and are, therefore, in agreement) and then normalized it with the total count of top reviewers. We labeled this variable Top Reviewer Agreement. We derived the variable Top Reviewer Disagreement as the count of top reviewers who disagreed in rating the focal book normalized by the total count of top reviewers.

We then extended the measure of agreement beyond the focal book in order to investigate the agreement on all other products reviewers had evaluated. This information is readily available. In addition to displaying reviews pertaining to a product, Amazon.com maintains a homepage for each reviewer containing all reviews posted in the past. The information concerning what products (p<sub>j</sub>) any given reviewer (r ) has commented on is, therefore, readily identifiable. Reviewers who post reviews on a multitude of common products end up forming a network-like structure (which in this context has no social implications). We use this historical information concerning the reviewer to derive two variables related to reviewers through their commonly reviewed products. These are derived as follows: We first identified all products where a reviewer posted and all other reviewers (call them $r _ { - i } )$ who posted on the same set of products. We then built an agreement matrix between $r _ { i }$ and each of the other $r _ { - i } .$ We used the generated matrix for each of the books in our dataset to identify these common reviewers who posted a positive review and aggregated their corresponding agreement matrix by summing over the value of their ratings. In the framework of our panel dataset, these calculations were done up to each of the panel time intervals (weekly). We then derived the mean for common reviewers, Avg of Rating Agreement, and standard deviation, Std Dev of Rating Agreement, of the agreement matrix of all reviewers combined for a given book.

Finally, we derived a variable that measures the intensity of agreement for the focal book compared to that of the competing book, calculated as the ratio of the count of reviewer agreement for the focal book divided by the count of reviewer agreement for the competing book. We labeled this variable Ratio Reviewer Agreement.

## Referral-Related Variable

Whenever a customer browses a given product page, Amazon.com uses a recommendation feature labeled “customers who bought this item also bought” these other items. This feature lists the products purchased by a customer regardless of whether this was done simultaneously or during subsequent sessions. This list helps customers identify associated products.<sup>8</sup> Based on this feature, we identified and constructed a network of referrals as follows: For each of the books in our dataset we identified all books that were recommended alongside (up to 10 books). For each of these recommended books, we further identified all other recommended books using the same feature. We repeated these steps to build four levels of book recommendations for each book in our dataset. Each book becomes a source book that refers users to other related books (out-links). It also becomes a sink that attracts users from related books through referrals (in-links). This results in a directed network of books referring to each other. We constructed this directed network of in-links and out-links and calculated the eigenvector centrality for each of the books in our database, which we simply label Centrality. The eigenvector approach measures the importance of a node within a directed network based on its linkages with other high-scoring nodes and it factors in both directions (Appendix E further elaborates on our referral network construction and calculation of centrality). This allows us to find the most central actors within the global structure of the network.

## Competitor-Related Variables

For competitor-related variables, we used an Amazon.com feature that lists all competing books within a given category based on customer purchase behavior, namely “what do customers buy after viewing this item.” We excluded from this list all books that also appeared on the booklist of “customers who bought this item also bought” in order to prevent double counting. We can safely label this remaining list as competition since this feature displays crowd choices in the order of popularity (see Figure 2). This list of competing books shows the top four books that customers purchased after browsing the focal book. We identified the first best alternative ultimately purchased by customers as the Competing Book.<sup>9</sup> We then derived, for this competing book, variables comparable to those derived for the focal book, namely Competitor Pos Review Volume, Competitor Neg Review Volume, Competitor Pos Helpful Vote, and Competitor Neg Helpful Vote, Competitor Review Rating Avg, Competitor Weighted Review Rating, and Competitor Centrality.

## Empirical Analysis

We begin our empirical analysis by addressing several econometric issues that might arise in this study, namely model specification, serial correlation, heteroscedasticity, and multicollinearity. We then present three estimation models that we use to test our hypotheses and discuss how we use an instrument variable approach to deal with potential endogeneity issues.

## Econometric Analysis

## Model Specification: Fixed or Random Effects

Our panel data enables us to better disentangle the individualspecific effects via either fixed effects (FE) or random effects (RE) models. The random effects model assumes that the unobserved (time-invariant) individual effect is uncorrelated with the included regressors, while the fixed effects model does not rely on this restrictive assumption (Mundlak 1978). Hausman (1978) provides a specification test that is commonly used as a criterion to choose between these two models. In our case, the null hypothesis that both FE and RE produce consistent estimators is rejected (Hausman Specification Statistic = 6527.43, p-value <0.0001), and we therefore resort to the less restrictive fixed effects model.

## Serial Correlation, Heteroscedasticity, and Multicollinearity

In a panel data analysis, it is common that the error terms of individual units may be serially correlated (Bhargava et al. 1982). Serial correlation in the error term leads to biased standard errors, resulting in less efficient estimates. We therefore tested for the presence of a first-order autoregressive process AR(1) using the Wooldridge test (Wooldridge 2006). The test yielded a $\mathrm { F } _ { ( 1 , 1 1 9 4 ) }$ value of 1284.09 (at p-value < 0.0001), rejecting the null hypothesis of no serial correlation. The details of this test are presented in Appendix B. We accordingly adjusted our model by directly incorporating the AR(1) process.

Heteroscedasticity is another common challenge in a panel data analysis (Baltagi et al. 2006). We performed a Wald test for it using the residuals of a fixed effects model (Greene 2000). The test statistic $\chi _ { _ { ( 1 1 9 4 ) } } ^ { 2 } = 2 2 8 4 2 . 4 0$ with p-value < 0.0001 pointed to the existence of heteroscedasticity; we therefore controlled for it using robust standard errors (Baltagi et al. 2006).

## Econometric Models

We begin with a standard baseline model consisting of the variables commonly included in the WOM literature. We then extend it to two more comprehensive models that we propose.

## Baseline Model

Based on the earlier discussion, we adopt the following AR(1) error-component fixed effects model:

$$
\begin{array}{l} Y _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta \Gamma_ {i, t - 1} + \gamma \Lambda_ {i, t - 1} + \varsigma \Xi_ {i, t - 1} + \mu_ {i, t} \\ \text { with } \mu_ {i, t} = \alpha_ {i} + \eta_ {i, t} \text { and } \eta_ {i, t} = \rho \eta_ {i, t - 1} + \omega_ {i, t} \\ (\text { Baseline   Model }) \end{array}
$$

where $< i , t >$ indexes a book-week combination, Γ represents a vector of lagged variables related to the Review Effect, Λ represents a vector of lagged variables related to the Reviewer Effect, Ξ represents Control variables including WOM Volume, Valence, and Dispersion, and $\alpha , \beta , \gamma ,$ , and ς are the regression model coefficients.

Here $\mu _ { i , t }$ is the error term consisting of the unobserved bookspecific fixed effects component $a _ { i } ,$ the unobserved timevarying error term $\eta _ { i , t }$ which is serially correlated within cross sections with the correlation coefficien ${ \bf \nabla } \cdot \rho ,$ and $\omega _ { i , \ast }$ represents the error that is not autocorrelated. ## Extended Model Specification

While the specification presented in the Baseline Model (BM) has been widely adopted, it might be subject to endogeneity commonly inflicting WOM studies. Endogeneity can arise when the error term $\mu _ { i , t }$ is correlated with some of the regressors (Γ, Λ, or Ξ) leading to biased least square estimates (Greene 2000). In our context, we identify two main potential sources of endogeneity, omitted variables and simultaneity, which may bias estimation of the WOM effects.<sup>10</sup> We begin by identifying the likely sources of omitted variables.

The mainstream WOM literature has typically explained product sales via regressors solely related to the product itself. In this literature, WOM for individual products is implicitly assumed to be independent from that of competitors (including, among others, Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006). This convenient assumption may not hold well when books within the same category cross-influence each other. The potential impact of system recommendations and competing products has, therefore, been notably omitted. These (omitted) variables can have a direct impact on reviews (i.e., they are likely to be correlated with regressors). Whenever variables correlated with regressors are omitted, they enter into the error term, resulting in endogeneity. By explicitly constructing and incorporating the competition-related regressors, namely the set of variables related to competing books and those related to referrals, our proposed model mitigates the potential omitted variable bias.

We therefore extend the baseline model by incorporating the aforementioned omitted variables:

$$
Y _ {i, t} = \alpha + \beta \Gamma_ {i, t - 1} + \gamma \Lambda_ {i, t - 1} + \rho \Theta_ {i, t - 1} + \delta \Pi_ {i, t - 1} + \varsigma \Xi_ {i, t - 1} + \mu_ {i, t} (E x t e n d e d M o d e l)
$$

Once we have accounted for omitted variables we then identify two potential sources of simultaneity, one related to user reviews and the other to centrality. It is well known that user reviews can be determined simultaneously with book sales; current user review levels might reflect the previous sales levels and vice versa. Both of the two most commonly used WOM variables, volume and rating, are subject to this simultaneity problem. Researchers have argued that while volume may influence current and future sales, volume itself can be an outcome of past and current sales (Godes and Mayzlin 2004). While higher sales could be attributed to better ratings, higher sales also simultaneously enhance the appeal of the product to consumers and potentially lead to higher ratings (Chintagunta et al. 2010; Moe and Trusov 2011). This interdependency between sales and ratings is also related to the customer self-selection effect identified by Li and Hitt (2008) where early purchasers self-select toward reviewing certain products and tend to assign higher ratings than consumers in the general population. Simultaneity leads to biased coefficient estimates and standard errors (Woodridge 2006). One common remedy to this problem is the two-stage least square (2SLS) approach through identifying instrument variables (IV) (Greene 2000; Heckman 1997). Instrument variables must not be systematically determined by the outcome, but instead must be (adequately) correlated to the endogenous variables.

Simultaneity can also arise from the centrality measure. Higher centrality of own product leads to improved sales, while at the same time improved sales can attract more referrals and lead to higher centrality. This virtuous cycle has not been accounted for in similar contexts.

We construct novel IVs that we believe are more appropriate and generalizable to broader WOM studies.

Construction of Instrument Variables for Review: A few related studies have used an IV approach. Luan and Neslin (2009), and Archak et al. (2011) control for exogenous demand shocks using data on the search volume for products investigated based on “Google Trends.” However, Luan and Neslin acknowledge that this variable choice is appropriate only for new product adoption and diffusion, but may not be applicable to stable products where the search volume may not vary as greatly. Using lagged variables as instruments is generally a very common approach. However, this convenience choice is only preferred when no other, more meaningful exogenous variables are available.

We construct two sets of novel instrument variables for the two potentially endogenous variables: review volume and review rating. The IVs we derive are constructed based on the following intuition: Typically, a review is a reflection of both the user’s sentiment toward the focal product and her personal predisposition for posting and rating. Predisposition captures a reviewer’s idiosyncratic tendency to write a specific review and can be readily gauged by examining her historical reviews (before posting a review on the focal product). It is important to note that predisposition constructed in this manner ensures that it predates the time she posts a review (on the focal product) and is, therefore, logically not driven by the characteristics of that focal product (i.e., it is exogenous). We aggregated the IV for volume using the prior review volume posted to other books by the same set of reviewers who reviewed the focal book. We argue that this is a natural instrument because (1) the amount of reviews posted to other products is generated by the same set of reviewers and is, therefore, intrinsically related to the review volume the focal product receives, and (2) we can ascertain that reviews for other products are generally not caused by the focal product’s review.

The detailed process of constructing these IVs is as follows: Our dataset captures all reviews by any given reviewer, including both posts she creates for the focal book and those made for any of the other books available on Amazon.com. We construct the IV for WOM volume of the focal book i at time t by first identifying all reviewers for the focal book prior to time t, then identifying all other products where these (same) reviewers posted up to time t-1. We then derive the volume of these reviews (labeled as Volume Outside Reviews). In line with our earlier approach splitting the review volume into positive and negative volume, we separate the aggregate reviews posted elsewhere into positive and negative volume. This yields two instrument variables— Volume Outside Neg Reviews and Volume Outside Pos Reviews—for Neg Review Volume and Pos Review Volume respectively.

The same logic applies to the IV construction for review rating. We derive the instrument for the valence of user reviews by aggregating reviewers’ valences elsewhere in the review system (Average Outside Rating). We split these ratings into positive and negative ones to derive two IVs: Avg Outside Neg Rating and Avg Outside Pos Rating.

The IVs constructed in this way expunge the effect of the focal product sales, while at the same time they are closely related to the volume and rating of the focal book. We supplement these IVs with an additional lag of both review volume and rating as in Forman et al. (2008). Our choice of IVs is in accordance with the approach recently adopted by Chintagunta et al. (2010) in marketing studying the sequential rollout of movies in multiple markets. Chintagunta et al. instrumented user ratings in subsequent markets by using controls in earlier markets. These controls included the average critic scores for competing movies prior to the focal market, average star power of competing movies within these earlier markets, and average proportion of movies within the same genre as the focal movie playing in previous markets from which the user ratings were generated.

Construction of Instrument Variables for Referral Centrality: We construct a set of instrument variables for the other potentially endogenous variable, centrality. The IV we propose, pseudo-lift, is based on the notion of lift for association rule discovery in data mining (Linoff and Berry 2011). In our context, the pseudo-lift measure is computed as an aggregated metric over a list of relevant association rules from the recommendations “customers who bought this book also bought.” Denote RecommendedBook  FocalBook as the association rule for the focal book i and the other books j (recommending book i). The individual pseudo-lift for that rule is then specified as a ratio of the confidence of the association rule over the support (frequency) for the recommended focal book i. This is then averaged over all the other books j that recommend focal book i (we provide further details in Appendix E on the construction of this variable).

Pseudo-lift constructed this way is closely related to centrality first because the confidence component reflects the likelihood of the book being referred to. Second, lift is not systematically determined by sales (of the focal book) because it measures how much more likely a customer is to receive a recommendation for both the focal book and the other related book(s) normalized by the prior probability that a random customer would receive a recommendation for the purchased focal book. It is therefore the association between the focal book and its recommended books, rather than sales of the focal book, that systematically drives pseudo-lift. We augment this IV with one-period and two-period lagged first differences of the centrality measure known as the Arellano and Bond (1991) approach used by prior works (Aral et al. 2012).

Validation of the IV Approach: We validated our IV choices using the Hausman test. We performed this test by comparing the two sets of parameter estimates with and without IVs, $\beta _ { I V }$ and $\beta _ { P a n e l }$ and their asymptotic variance (Avar). The statistic is given by:

$$
H = \left\{\left(\hat {\beta} _ {I V} - \hat {\beta} _ {\text { Panel }}\right) ^ {\prime} \left[ A v a r \left(\hat {\beta} _ {I V}\right) - A v a r \left(\hat {\beta} _ {\text { Panel }}\right) \right] ^ {- 1} \left(\hat {\beta} _ {I V} - \hat {\beta} _ {\text { Panel }}\right) \right\} \sim \chi_ {E} ^ {2}
$$

where E is the number of endogenous regressors. In this study, $\chi _ { ( 4 ) } ^ { 2 } = 5 . 5 2$ with p-value < 0.001 indicates a preference forthe IV-based estimation (details are provided in Appendix C).

The IVs should further satisfy two important prerequisites: the relevance assumption and the exogeneity assumption (Greene 2000). The relevance assumption imposes that IVs should be correlated with the endogenous explanatory variables and that this correlation should not be weak. Weak IVs lead to unsatisfactorily large asymptotic covariance matrix and 2SLS estimators that are biased toward the panel estimator, known to be inconsistent in the presence of endogeneity (Bound et al. 1995). We discerned the strength of IVs using the standard approach based on the first stage least squares regression of the 2SLS using the Kleibergen–Paap (KP) (Kleibergen and Paap 2006) statistic (which is more convenient than the Cragg–Donald–Wald F-statistic when there is more than one IV in the presence of heteroskedasticity as in Baum et al. 2003). Overall, the KP statistic must be greater than the Stock-Yogo critical values (Stock and Yogo 2005). For the Review IVs, we have a KP Wald F-statistic = 285.233, which is greater than the critical value (15.72) for the Stock-Yogo weak IV test at the 5 percent maximal IV relative bias. For the Centrality IVs, this statistic is 21.655, which is greater than the critical value (18.37). Both cases confirm that the IVs are strong.

The other condition, instrument exogeneity, requires that instruments excluded from the structural equation must be uncorrelated with the structural errors; this rules out the possibility that IVs are influenced by some latent causes that also influence sales. This is typically done using a test of over-identifying restrictions via Hansen’s (1982) commonly employed J statistic. A test of over-identifying restrictions regresses the residuals from an IV or 2SLS regression on all instruments under the null hypothesis that all instruments are uncorrelated with the error term. The statistic is distributed as $\chi ^ { 2 }$ with degrees of freedom equal to the number of exclusion restrictions. In this case, for the Review IVs, $\chi _ { \scriptscriptstyle ( 4 ) } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 2$ with p-value = 0.77 fails to reject the null hypothesis and implies that the instruments are valid. For the Centrality IVs, $\chi _ { ( 4 ) } ^ { 2 } =$ 1.30 with p-value = 0.86, which also implies that the instruments are valid.

## Results

We ran three separate regression models: the baseline model (BM), extended model (EM), and extended model with IV estimation (EMIV). In model BM, we only included the variables related to a book’s own WOM (encompassing both reviews and reviewer-related variables), mimicking the conventional work in the extant literature. In model EM, we extended the BM model to include own as well as competing WOM variables. In model EMIV, we used instruments in the first stage least square estimation for the three endogenous variables we identified (review volume, review rating, and centrality). All three models use the log-transformed sales rank as the dependent variable and the coefficients are accordingly interpreted as the change in the log(sales rank) when a variable changes by one unit.<sup>11</sup> The exact percentage change in rank for a change in any of the regressors is defined as $\% \varDelta y = 1 0 0 ^ { \ast } [ \exp ( \beta _ { i } \varDelta x _ { i } ) - 1 ]$ , where y is the sales rank, $x _ { i }$ is a regressor, and $\beta _ { i }$ its coefficient (Wooldridge 2006). Since the dependent variable is sales rank, lower ranks indicate a better performance and a negative regression coefficient signals an improvement in the product’s sales. All three regression results are reported in Table 1.

## Results of the Baseline Model

The BM results largely confirm earlier findings in the WOM literature. Both review rating and positive review volume are significant and negative (therefore contributing to a better sales rank); the negative review volume is significant and positive, accordingly acting in the opposite direction. These findings are similar to those by Archak et al. (2011) and Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006). We also find that the dispersion in review ratings resulting from disagreement within the reviewer base (namely Review Rating Std Dev) is significant and negative,<sup>12</sup> similar to the finding in Clemons et al. (2006) where each additional unit increase in standard deviation contributes to improvements in ranking. The helpful votes received from the community on negative reviews (Neg Helpful Vote) worsen the sales rank, consistent with Chen et al. (2007); however, helpful votes for positive reviews (Pos Helpful Vote) show no significant impact. While customers resort to crowd opinions in order to alleviate any unreliability in reviews, they tend to be more responsive to negative reviews. This is in line with the prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 2000) demonstrating that people’s attitudes toward uncertainty concerning gains may be quite different from their attitudes toward risks concerning losses. This behavior underlines the asymmetry of human choices to what is perceived as positive or negative.

Regarding the variables related to the reviewer effect, only top reviewer disagreement is significant and positive (i.e., leading to lower ranks). The average rating agreement among reviewers is negative and marginally significant (at 0.1 level). This is consistent with earlier work reported in the marketing literature on the influence of opinion leaders (e.g., Van den Bulte and Joshi 2007), except that the other reviewer-related variables are not found to be significant in the context of this study.

The BM results indicate a more significant influence on the set of negative review metrics, namely negative review volume, negative helpful vote, and top reviewer disagreement. These findings are in line with the traditional views on WOM (particularly in offline settings) believing that potential customers are more likely to trust negative reviewers (Sen and Lerman 2005) and that negative eWOM looms larger (Cheung and Lee 2008). These beliefs can exert an adverse effect on how businesses manage customer relations. For example, some businesses have begun to include clauses in their product agreements that prohibit users from posting negative reviews (Segal 2011).

## Results of the Two Extended Models

We begin by highlighting the differences in the BM model and then proceed to discuss the support for our hypotheses. Both positive review volume and review rating average remain significant and negative, although we observe that the magnitude increases dramatically as we move from BM to either EM or EMIV. Positive review volume explains a mere 0.7 percent drop in rank for each 10 additional reviews in the BM model, while this drop reaches the levels of 1.4 percent and 3.5 percent for EM and EMIV, respectively. Similarly, the review rating average explains a 17.6 percent drop in rank for each unit increase in the average rating in BM, while this change jumps to 23.2 percent and 29.6 percent for EM and EMIV, respectively. We also observe that the coefficient for the review rating standard deviation (Review Rating Std Dev) switches sign from negative (BM) to positive (EMIV). The baseline model suffers from endogeneity issues, indicating that higher dispersion and uncertainty are beneficial. With endogeneity remedied, the positive sign now indicates that potential customers cannot overcome their uncertainty toward the product as the dispersion in opinion increases, which can turn them away from purchasing it. This more intuitive finding is in line with the overwhelming evidence in the finance literature on buyer behavior vis-à-vis uncertainty in the securities market, where the standard deviation of analysts’ forecasts is commonly used as a proxy for opinion differences regarding a stock. This literature stream finds that stocks with a higher standard deviation discourage investors, leading to underperforming stocks (e.g., Diether et al. 2002).

Another striking difference between BM and EMIV is that the variables related to negative attitudes (namely Neg Review Volume and Neg Helpful Vote) lose their significance while their counterparts related to positive attitudes (namely Pos Review Volume and Pos Helpful Vote) maintain their significance and contribute to better ranks. This seems paradoxical to traditional views on the effectiveness of negative WOM in discouraging potential customers; however, several recent professional studies lend support to this result and downplay the impact of negativity. For instance, in a recent Huffpost Media report (Lowe 2010), the author argues that customers do not seem to take negative reviews as seriously because customers think they may not share the same experience as the negative reviewers and these reviewers may have different tastes, rendering these reviews less relevant. By the same token, both eMarketer (2010) and McKinsey Quarterly (Bughin et al. 2010) indicate that positive buzz carries greater weight with consumers because positive WOM is able to generate a tailwind for a product or service. We argue that such findings are quite applicable to the book categories that we chose in that customers are making a selection choice among a set of competing books. Reviews, specifically positive ones, serve as a confirmation for a customer’s search and remain an influential factor; however, negative reviews may not carry as much weight because they may simply represent the opinions of a different customer type. We next analyze the findings related to our hypotheses.

## Evaluation of Hypotheses

The results support Hypothesis H1, investigating the role of centrality within the referral network on sales given the negative and significant coefficient. This finding shows that an increase in the centrality of a book due to directed referrals contributes to better ranks (3.86 percent and 5.27 percent improvement for each additional unit of centrality in the EM and EMIV models, respectively). The centrality measure we used represents “global” visibility and influence within the entire network. As more prominent books keep recommending the focal book, it gathers additional views and its prominence rises, improving its sales. Placing these nearly costless hyperlinks between products generates competitive dynamics that book authors must be wary of. While they can do little to alter the recommendation algorithms deployed by the retailer, they must still be cognizant of these underlying factors. In this regard, our finding indirectly endorses the “winner-take-all” view of recommender systems (Fleder and Hosanagar 2009). Central products that are frequently recommended eat up the share of less frequently recommended books. The impact of changes in the competitor’s centrality (last row of Table 1) is even more pronounced; improvements can lead to a nearly 6.35 percent deterioration in the sales rank of the focal book.

Hypothesis H2, investigating the impact of direct competition, is also supported. The coefficient for the volume of positive reviews received by the competing book (Competitor Pos Review Volume) is significant and positive. This indicates that positive WOM received by competitors deteriorates the rank of the focal book. For each 10 additional positive reviews that the competing book receives, the sales rank of the book of interest rises (i.e., sales drop) by an additional 2.1 percent and 2.0 percent for the EM and EMIV models, respectively. Book authors must, therefore, be extremely cognizant of their competitors’ performance. Furthermore, competitors’ helpful votes for positive reviews (Competitor Pos Helpful Volume) are significant and positive in the EMIV model, indicating that competitors receiving helpful votes for their positive reviews jeopardize the focal book’s standing. Finally, the coefficient of the variable for the competitor weighted review rating is significant and positive, indicating that improvements in this composite rating hurt the book of interest (sales rank rose by 2.2 percent and 2.2 percent for EM and EMIV models, respectively). When the other competitors are only a click away, diligent customers have a set of recommended alternatives at their fingertips. They can now adjust their decision-making process to factor in community opinions toward alternatives, and our results corroborate this potential impact.

Hypothesis H3a is partially supported in the EMIV model but not in EM; higher agreement between opinion leaders who endorse the focal book (namely Top Reviewer Agreement) sends a positive signal to potential customers and drives sales up with a relatively small magnitude (i.e., improves the sales rank by 0.08 percent). A closer examination of the reviewing system design of Amazon.com reveals that it does not allow users to sort reviews by reviewer status, negating this potentially valuable information. Users who want to use this information will have to sift through the voluminous review data manually. Furthermore, the reviewer’s status is not readily displayed next to the review at Amazon.com. The reviews of opinion leaders, therefore, seem to fall through the cracks. Customers will incur high search costs if they are interested in evaluating the credibility of these leading reviewers. The other part of H3a, that disagreement between opinion leaders hurts a book (namely Top Reviewer Disagreement), is not supported for either EM or EMIV.

We confirm H3b, stating that agreement among reviewers regarding the focal book in proportion to the competing book (namely Ratio Reviewer Agreement) matters substantially, with a 1 percent higher support of the focal book relative to its competitor translating into approximately a 2 percent improvement in sales rank. This is yet another indication that the relative perception of the focal book as opposed to its competition is significant.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted additional tests in order to rule out alternative explanations for the results presented earlier. We began by investigating the role that price might play in mediating our findings. We then addressed potential noise in the use of sales rank<sup>13</sup> by replicating our results using an alternative dependent variable.

First, we investigated the role of price that we had not included in our earlier analysis (mainly because historical price information was unavailable in the full dataset). Our data showed that this exclusion should not present any major concern.<sup>14</sup> Nevertheless, we conducted the same empirical analysis presented earlier with the price information on a subset of 167 books in our sample for which price was available. We obtained the prices from a third-party company<sup>15</sup> dedicated to tracking the listed price of books on Amazon.com daily since 2008. Conducting the analysis on a subset rather than the full sample may result in self-selection bias. We therefore began by ensuring that the data subset had the same characteristics as the full dataset. This was done using two procedures. First, we conducted two-sample t-tests for each of our independent variables comparing the data subset and the full set. The t-tests indicated that no significant differences exist. Second, we ran a formal Heckman bias correction procedure that yielded an insignificant inverse Mills ratio in the second stage. Both tests confirmed the absence of selfselection, indicating that the subset is representative of the full set along the dimensions of interest. The regression results following the Heckman correction are consistent with our earlier findings (see Appendix D).

Second, in our main analysis, we used the readily available sales rank as a proxy for sales since actual sales are not publicly disclosed, rank being obviously less informative than the actual sales volume. However, this widely accepted proxy has been found to behave well in measuring sales (e.g., Chevalier and Mayzlin 2006; Li and Hitt 2008). Recent findings (Brynjolfsson et al. 2010) conclude that sales rank remains a good proxy for Amazon.com sales but caution that different slope coefficients should be used to map the relationship between sales and rank. This is particularly significant when the books being studied span a wide spectrum of popular and niche books. The ranks of different products can be affected disproportionately by the sales of unrelated products.

We therefore supplemented our earlier analysis by using a novel dependent variable. In our setting, the gist of our analysis focuses on competition between a focal book and its recommended competing book. It is therefore reasonable to replace the sales rank with the difference in ranks between the focal book and that of its main competitor. This differencing of sales ranks largely expunges noise that simultaneously affects the focal as well as the competitor’s rankings. After constructing this new dependent variable we conducted the same regression analysis included earlier. However, this time the analysis is done on a subset of the original data where we also have sales rank for the competing product. As before, we ensured that the data subset had the same characteristics as the full dataset using t-tests and Heckman tests. The regression results presented in Appendix D arrive at the same conclusion presented earlier, confirming our findings.

## Conclusion and Discussion

We studied the joint impact of user reviews and recommendation systems on online sales. We adopted a systematic approach examining the linkages among products, reviews, and reviewers. We investigated the crucial role of competition among products. We also developed models that address several econometrics problems common to eWOM studies, particularly the endogeneity issue with novel IV approaches.

Overall, we found that recommendation systems introduce competitive pressure among products. We found that once products are hyperlinked through recommendations, those with higher centrality within the resulting network of referrals are associated with higher sales. We also found that these sales gains are hampered by improvements in the reviews of competing products. When investigating the impact of reviewers, we found that endorsement of opinion leaders and homogeneity in reviewer agreement improve product sales.

## Contributions and Implications

This paper contributes to the literature in four ways. First, we account for the compounding effects of two IT artifacts, namely online reviews and recommendation systems. Our modeling approach more closely emulates users’ online shopping behaviors. This answers the call from the commentary by Brynjolfsson et al. (2010) to study social media systematically rather than in isolation.

Second, from a methodological point of view, we propose a more natural treatment of the endogeneity issues long plaguing eWOM research. We delve into the onerous reviewers’ historical review data and related reviews to construct IVs for volume and valence. We also create an instrument measuring the extent to which a product is central in its referral network with its pseudo-lift in addition to the conventional Arellano and Bond (1991) instruments. Various econometric tests give credence to our IVs. Since such historical data is widely available for online retailing sites, our approach to constructing these IVs can be readily adopted as a standard methodology for future eWOM studies.

Third, we contribute to the eWOM literature by explicitly considering competition among the hyperlinked products vying for click-throughs and conversions. The referrals resulting from recommendations create a competitive environment where customers not only have easy access to an array of competing products, but also to the competing WOM of these alternative products. Our analysis reveals the existence of a reverse halo effect of eWOM among these competing products. The negative impact of competitors’ WOM shows that book authors should not only react to their own user reviews as suggested by the extant literature, but should monitor the WOM of their competitors as well.

Finally, we conduct a fine-grained review analysis. Instead of studying the overall review volume or average rating, we differentiate between positive versus negative reviews. We find that the effects of positive and negative reviews are asymmetric and customers appear to be aware that the average book rating is inflated (in a related work, Pavlou et al. (2007) reported that 97 percent of reviews on eBay are positive). Our finding that both the review and reviewers matter suggests that it is essential for shopping sites to improve their rating systems which currently factor in only review volume and community votes. We also differentiate reviewers based on the helpfulness vote they receive. We use this to identify highly acclaimed reviewers (opinion leaders) and find that reviewer visibility plays an important role (as also found in Iyengar et al. 2011). Currently, Amazon.com’s review system does not provide an easy way for users to navigate through reviewers. Our results suggest that retailers ought to fully make use of reviewers’ rich historical profiles and make this information easily accessible to users.

This study sheds light on several practical implications for the various content generators including authors, publishers, and firms, as well as the various intermediaries hosting them. Content generators require a better understanding of the dynamics of IT artifacts on their sales. For instance, we show that reviews for competing products are as effective at moving sales as own reviews are. Content generators must, therefore, monitor competitors’ eWOM as closely as they monitor their own. At times they might not necessarily worry about their reviews dipping as long as their competitors’ reviews dip even lower. We also find that positivity has a greater impact than negativity for our chosen book categories in line with new beliefs evolving in professional circles that weigh positive reviews more significantly than negative ones (e.g., Bughin et al. 2010). Content generators might need to promote through their own network of reviewers given the strong influence we show, occasionally providing them with incentives such as free samples to lure posts from prominent reviewers. One such program at their disposal is Amazon’s Vine Program, through which Amazon invites most-trusted reviewers to post opinions concerning new and prerelease items.

Regarding the role of intermediaries (or retailers), our study showcases how the current mechanisms adopted by retailers inefficiently use the wealth of resources at their disposal. We show that information revelation on competing products is highly influential, a finding in line with a long stream of research examining the optimality of information provision within a competitive environment (e.g., Kuksov and Lin 2010). Retailers must carefully design information revelation mechanisms in order to promote products that generate high profits. Our results suggest that one such effective approach is through integrating user reviews and recommendations. Another is to adjust information revelation based on customer profiles and “push” information in order to “pull” customers into high-value purchases. Finally, in their role as intermediaries, retailers face the additional challenge of aligning the incentives and interests of suppliers with consumers in order to develop viable business models (Granados et al. 2010). Our study highlights the value that hyperlinking products can play in balancing these various approaches.

## Limitations and Future Extensions

This study has a few limitations. First, our empirical analysis is performed on only four book categories, potentially limiting the scope of this study. However, as discussed earlier, these are categories for which a customer is more likely to make a selection choice among a set of competing books. We have no reason to believe that they are atypical, and we believe that our results can be generalized to similar settings.

Second, our data is at the aggregate level and not at the level of the individual customer. The data collected therefore does not track the actual consumption of reviews or recommendations. For instance, we do not observe who actually reads which reviews and what reactions individual reviews engender. This is a missing link for virtually all eWOM studies. We plan to address this shortcoming by collecting click-stream data that will help more closely connect behavior and action in future studies.

We plan to extend this work in two directions. First, we plan to include online search history as an additional component in the mix consisting of user reviews and system recommendations. Searches are one additional mechanism that influence customer choices online (Copeland 2009; Hinz and Eckert 2010). While in this research we assume that customers begin with a focal book and then make purchase decisions based on available information, a customer selection may stem from a search engine. Clickstream data on customers’ searching and purchasing can help us quantify the joint impacts of the various IT artifacts on individual behavior and purchasing decisions.

Second, we plan to investigate product adoption and diffusion within online competitive settings. Since products are now interconnected online, their life cycles are more intertwined than ever before. These cycles are impacted not only by the corresponding WOM, but also by the search and recommendation tools provided. Toward this end, we plan on studying the mutual progression of competing products during their life cycles under the influence of searches and recommendations.

## Testing Fixed Versus Random Effects

The Hausman specification test compares the fixed effects with random effects models under the null hypothesis that the individual effects are uncorrelated with the other regressors in the model (Hausman 1978). If correlated $\mathrm { ( H } _ { 0 }$ is rejected), a random effect model produces biased estimators, violating one of the Gauss-Markov assumptions; so a fixed-effects model is preferred. Hausman’s essential result is that the covariance of an efficient estimator with its difference from an inefficient estimator is zero (Greene 2007). We obtained the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value as

$$
\chi_ {(1 2)} ^ {2} = (b - B) ^ {\prime} \left[ \left(V _ {b} - V _ {B}\right) ^ {- 1} \right] (b - B) = 6 5 2 7. 4 3 \text {   and   } p \text {-value } <   0. 0 0 0 1
$$

This suggests that the null hypothesis that both FE and RE are consistent estimators is rejected and thus we choose the fixed effects model.

## Testing Heteroscedasticity

In our case, we use the modified Wald test for group-wise heteroskedasticity in the residuals of a fixed effects regression model (Greene 2007) where $\operatorname { H } _ { 0 } \colon \sigma _ { i } ^ { 2 } = \sigma ^ { 2 }$ for all i. The chi-square with 1194 degree of freedom i $; \chi ^ { 2 } _ { ( 1 1 9 4 ) } = 2 2 8 4 2 . 4 0$ with p-value < 0.0001. This indicates the presence of heteroscedasticity and thus we use the robust standard errors to address this issue.

## Testing Serial Correlation

We apply the Wooldridge test (2002) for autocorrelation in panel data where the null hypothesis is H : no first-order autocorrelation. The $\mathrm { F } _ { ( 1 , 1 1 9 4 ) }$ value is = 1284.09 with a p-value<0.0001 rejecting the null hypothesis. The test indicates the presence of serial correlation and hence we explicitly use AR(1) to account for this.

## Hausman Test of Endogeneity

Using IV estimation for the sake of consistency must be balanced against the inevitable loss of efficiency. Therefore, we perform a test of endogeneity where, under the null hypothesis, the specified endogenous regressors can actually be treated as exogenous. Durbin–Wu–Hausman (Durbin 1954; Hausman1978; Wu 1973) tests involve estimating the model via both panel and IV approaches and comparing the resulting coefficient vectors (sometimes referred to as the Hausman test). These tests implemented by Hansen–Sargan (Hansen et al. 1996; Sargan 1958) have been modified to accommodate the GMM estimation. The test statistic is distributed as chi-square with degrees of freedom equal to the number of regressors tested.

In this case, $\chi _ { _ { ( 4 ) } } ^ { 2 } = 5 . 5 1 9$ with p-value < 0.001 indicating the preference of the GMM based IV estimations.

## Exogeneity Assumption (IV Distributed Independently of the Error)

Because the exogeneity assumption cannot be directly tested, an overidentification test is used instead. In the context of GMM, the overidentifying restrictions may be tested via the commonly employed J-statistic of Hansen (1982), sometimes referred to as Sargan-Hansen J-statistic. This diagnostic statistic is the most commonly utilized in GMM estimation to evaluate the suitability of the model (Baum et al. 2003). The joint null hypothesis is that the instruments are valid ones (i.e., uncorrelated with the error term), and that the excluded instruments are correctly excluded from the estimated equation (Stata Manual 2011). The statistic is distributed as $\chi ^ { 2 }$ with degrees of freedom equal to the number of over-identifying restrictions. In this case, for the Review IVs, $\chi _ { \scriptscriptstyle ( 4 ) } ^ { 2 } = 0 . 5 2$ with p-value = 0.77. For the Centrality IVs, $\chi _ { ( 4 ) } ^ { 2 } = 1 . 3 0$ with p-value = 0.86. Thus both tests fail to reject the null hypotheses, implying that the instruments are valid.

If and only if an equation is overidentified, we may test whether the excluded instruments are appropriately independent of the error process (test referred to as refutability test). That test allows us to evaluate the validity of the instruments. The C-statistic (also known as a “GMM distance” or “difference-in-Sargan” statistic) allows a test of the exogeneity of one or more instruments (Stata Manual 2011). In this case, $\chi _ { \mathbf { \Lambda } ( 8 ) } ^ { 2 }$ = 68.78 with p-value < 0.0001.

## Relevance Assumption (IV correlated with Endogenous Variables)

To test the relevance assumption—that the excluded instruments are sufficiently correlated with the included endogenous regressors—we should consider the goodness-of-fit of the first stage regressions relating each endogenous regressor to the entire set of instruments. This is typically done through a Wald F-statistic that is based on the Kleibergen–Paap rank statistic (Kleibergen and Paap 2006) (which is more convenient than the Cragg–Donald–Wald F-statistic when there is more than one IV in the presence of heteroscedasticity). Overall, KP statistics have to be greater than the Stock-Yogo critical values (Stock and Yogo 2005). In this case, for the Review $\mathrm { I V s , }$ we have Kleibergen-Paap rk Wald Fstatistic = 285.233 which is greater than 15.72, the critical value of the Stock-Yogo weak ID test at 5 percent maximal IV relative bias. For the Centrality IVs, this statistic is 21.655 which is greater than the critical value (18.37).

## Generalized Method of Moments Approach

The different steps in the estimation procedure are detailed in Baum et al. (2003) and Chintagunta et al. (2010):

(1) Estimate the regression model using standard instrumental variables methods.

(2) Use the residuals from the above regression to obtain the optimal GMM weighting matrix.

(3) Allow for heteroscedasticity and correlation between error terms.

The GMM estimator and its asymptotic variance are

$$
\hat {\beta} _ {G M M} = \left[ (X ^ {\prime} Z) W (Z ^ {\prime} X) \right] ^ {- 1} (X ^ {\prime} Z) W (Z ^ {\prime} y)
$$

$$
V _ {\hat {\beta} _ {G M M}} = \left[ (X ^ {\prime} Z) W (Z ^ {\prime} X) \right] ^ {- 1}
$$

where X = Matrix of regressors in the model with endogenous variables

Z = Matrix of instruments

y = Dependent variable

W = Optimal weighting matrix

## Construction of Variables

We detail below our approach to constructing several of the main variables in this paper.

## Centrality

This measure is based on an Amazon recommendation feature labeled Customers Who Bought This Item Also Bought. We illustrate our construction of Centrality with graphs that represent the network of referrals.

In Figure E1, we start with book A1 at level 0 which represents the initial focal book. Then at level 1, we identify the books recommended when viewing book A1 (out-links); suppose these are books B1 through B10 (with loss of generality, assuming 10 books are recommended). At level 2, we do the same for each of the books B1 through B10 (each now becoming a focal book itself) by identifying books recommended along with each of these 10 books (i.e., out-links toward books C1–C10, D1–D10, …). We then repeat the same process at level 3. In this example, we end up with 1,110 books recommended one level away from book A1. Similarly, for all the 1,470 books in our dataset, this yields a theoretical total of 1,931,400 books recommended along with books A1 through A1740 (across all levels). These almost two million books constitute the universe we consider in this study.

![](/api/attachments/ZP74J894/fulltext/images/79485c353571ccf9026a8759552fb8c0fec1f63fdc23f70327994920239d50a2.jpg)

Note, however, that within these nearly two million books, a very large number of the links reverts back to books 1 to 1,740. Thus, the theoretical diagram displayed in Figure E1 reverts in practice to the more parsimonious diagram in Figure E2. Here, each book becomes a source book that refers users to other related books (out-links). It also becomes a sink that attracts users from related books through referrals (in-links). This results in a directed network of books referring to each other.

![](/api/attachments/ZP74J894/fulltext/images/893c8a856c87dba38d1f542eccc2136a506d90e18f16dfb0d1874ad1715b1121.jpg)  
Figure E2. Parsimonious Diagram

We then calculate the eigenvector centrality for each of the books in our database, and label it Centrality. The eigenvector approach measures the importance of a node within a directed network based on its linkages with other high-scoring nodes and it factors in both sides of directions (different variants of this approach have been recently used in social media contexts<sup>2</sup>). This allows us to find the most central actors within the global structure of the network.

## Pseudo-Lift as IV for Centrality

The pseudo-lift measure that we derive is used as IV for Centrality. It builds on the notion of lift for association rule discovery in data mining. In that context, an association rule links one or several antecedent items to one or several consequent items as being purchased together. In our context, the consequent is always the focal book and the antecedent(s) is(are) other books recommended along with the focal book. A representative association rule is of the sort RecommendedBoo $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { k } } _ { i j } \to$ FocalBook . Confidence is defined as the likelihood of observing both a recommended book and the focal book, given that the recommended book has been observed. We, therefore, operationalize Pseudo-Lift as

$$
\begin{array}{r l}\text {PseudoLift} _ {i j}&= \frac {\text {Confidence} (\text {Recommended} _ {i j} \rightarrow \text {Focal} _ {i})}{\text {Support} (\text {Focal} _ {i})}\\&= \frac {\text {Support} (\text {Recommended} _ {i j} \text {AND Focal} _ {i}) / \text {Support} (\text {Recommended} _ {i j})}{\text {Support} (\text {Focal} _ {i})}\\&= \frac {\text {Support} (\text {Recommended} _ {i j} \text {AND Focal} _ {i})}{\text {Support} (\text {Recommended} _ {i j}) * \text {Support} (\text {Focal} _ {i})}\end{array}
$$

At any given time, multiple books j would be recommending the focal book i. We use the same approach identified earlier with centrality to discover the set of recommended books j. We end up with several rules in which focal book i is the consequent. We aggregate these rule to derive the pseudo-lift at the focal book level as follows:

$$
P s e u d o L i f t _ {i} = \frac {\sum_ {j} P s e u d o L i f t _ {i j}}{| J |}
$$
