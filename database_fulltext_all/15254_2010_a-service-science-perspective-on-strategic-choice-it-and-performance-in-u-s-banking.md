# A Service Science Perspective on Strategic Choice, IT, and Performance in U.S. Banking

Paul P. Tallon

Paul Tallon is an Associate Professor and Director of the David D. Lattanze Center for Information Value at the Joseph A. Sellinger, S.J., School of Business, Loyola University, Baltimore, Maryland. He previously worked as a systems auditor and accountant with PricewaterhouseCoopers. His research interests include the economic impacts of IT, strategic alignment, IT portfolio analysis, and the economics of information management. He is currently researching the topic of information life cycle management in an attempt to better understand the complex interplay between information value, total cost of ownership, and value-at-risk. His research has been published in top-tier IS journals, including the Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Communications of the ACM. His research on IT business value received the JMIS paper of the year award for 2007.

Abst ract : With the move to an information-based economy, financial services has become a key contributor to the U.S. gross domestic product. Even as consolidation reduces the number of banks, small banks with under \$100 million in assets continue to report higher profit margins than large banks with over \$100 million in assets. Lacking scale, small banks employ a service-oriented business strategy (customer intimacy), whereas large banks focus on productivity and throughput (operational excellence). Information technology (IT) plays a key role in applying each strategy, but as banks move toward customer intimacy in general, the challenge is to grow without undermining service quality. Using a balanced panel data set from 43 U.S. banks, this paper finds that banking strategies are becoming more customer focused. Yet for large banks in particular, IT remains resolutely operations focused. This misalignment could restrict future banking performance. In this way, this paper contributes to the service science literature by using size to dissect banking strategies and performance.

Key words and p hrases: banking, business value, customer intimacy, financial services, relationship banking, service science, services, strategic alignment, strategic choice, value disciplines.

Wit h t he t ransit ion first t o a services economy and later to an information economy, the financial services sector has become the single-largest contributor to the U.S. gross domestic product (GDP).<sup>1</sup> Much of this contribution reflects the far-reaching technological, economic, and regulatory changes affecting the sector in recent years.

For example, the enactment of the Financial Services Modernization Act of 1999—repealing the Banking Act of 1935, which, as a way to protect depositors following the 1929 Wall Street crash, restricted banks to banking activities alone—allows banks to merge with other financial entities. The result has been the rise of global financial conglomerates that offer a range of products and services across banking, securities trading, and insurance. Removing regulatory obstacles helped competition but it also led to record consolidation. From 1990 to 2008, the number of FDIC-insured banks fell by 43 percent (12,343 to 7,085).<sup>2</sup> Small banks, defined by the FDIC as having less than \$100 million in assets, were hit the hardest. From 1994 to 2008, the number of small banks declined by 62 percent (7,411 to 2,784) while the number of large banks, defined by the FDIC as having more than \$100 million in assets, grew by 35 percent (3,183 to 4,301).<sup>3</sup> As a result, over this 15-year period, the assets of small banks grew by, on average, only 1.2 percent annually while the assets of large banks, driven in large part by progressively larger mergers and acquisitions, grew by, on average, 5.8 percent annually.

Despite this trend in which the five largest banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, and U.S. Bank) now control 50 percent of all U.S. bank assets, small banks continue to earn higher profit margins (see Figure 1). As a quintessential services industry whose sole product involves the distribution or manipulation of information, this trend informs our primary research question: Why are small banks more profitable and is information technology (IT) part of the reason, whether in the form of different IT spending levels or different IT uses?<sup>4</sup> To answer this question, we apply the logic and language of service science, an embryonic field of multidisciplinary research that offers an integrative view of service design, delivery, and innovation [5]. Banking is particularly amenable to a service science review in light of the many changes affecting the sector and the threats that banks face from innovative banking models such as peer-to-peer or retailer-based banking,<sup>5</sup> which in some cases have disintermediated traditional banks entirely. From the advent of e-banking in the 1990s, service innovation has grown to include bill payment and presentment, account aggregation, digital checking, paperless deposits, integrated cell phone payment systems, contact-less credit cards, radio frequency identification (RFID), and mobile banking. Today, banks use IT for customer outreach and back-office activities, signaling a shift toward high-speed transaction processing, greater risk analysis, and use of cross selling as a way to expand market share [5]. Using service science to explore banking strategies and how IT supports these strategies, whether through mainframes or new and more innovative Web-based applications—with size as a key contextual variable—contributes to the evolving literature on service science and to the already established literature on strategic alignment where alignment is defined as the level of fit, integration, cohesiveness, or harmony between IT and business strategy [9, 34, 48, 52].

While banks vary in several ways, the focus of this study is on size-related differences in business strategy and how, when aligned with IT, differences in the extent of fit between IT and business strategy lead to varied levels of banking performance. An extensive body of research confirms that alignment is a key driver of firm performance in the form of competitive advantage, profit, and market share [9, 10, 28]. Yet not all firms will adopt the same strategy, and so the form of alignment may vary even if the extent of alignment is similar [41]. Although alignment has not been formally evaluated under the rubric of service science, alignment is clearly relevant to service because misalignment—IT fails to support business strategy—can hurt service delivery. A resource perspective can also be added to this view of alignment and service to the extent that service systems comprise “value co-creation configurations of people, technology [and] value propositions connecting internal and external systems, and shared information” [36, p. 18]. Adding a resource dimension recognizes that banks can substitute IT for labor, depending on their specific choice of service strategy and whether that strategy is more IT (capital) intensive or labor intensive. Investigating service using the lens of size also exposes the possible rewards of staying small when the received view is that bigger is better. By focusing on size, this paper also helps to resolve issues with earlier studies where size has either been completely ignored or trivialized for estimation purposes as a control or dummy variable [23].

## Strategy, Size, and Service Delivery: An Integrated Service Science Perspective

Service science espouses a broad goal of applying “scientific understanding to advance our ability to design, improve, and scale service systems” [36, p. 18]. A servicedominant logic has grown up around service science to provide a framework and a vocabulary within which to assess service delivery [35, 36]. Part of this logic calls for a greater understanding of the organizational context within which services are designed and delivered, a context that speaks to the peculiarities of the service provider and the role of the consumer in service co-creation [57, 63]. The size of the provider organization is arguably a key aspect of service design and delivery to the extent that size can generate scale economies that help deliver services at lower cost. Yet size can also be an impediment if customers are depersonalized or if, in the push for ever higher profits, service becomes routinized. Anecdotally, there are many stories of customers being ignored or marginalized by large firms that respond to complaints using form letters or use their large size to intimidate customers; airlines are especially prone to this type of behavior. The perception, therefore, may be that superior service is a hallmark of small firms and that, beyond a certain size, service quality and the profit that flows from superior service delivery may be perhaps compromised by further increases in size. The fact that small banks continue to see higher profit margins—a trend that has stood for over a decade—suggests that there may be important differences in the service design and delivery models of small versus large banks and that these models may change as banks grow through mergers and acquisitions. But what then of the impact of IT? Would large banks not benefit disproportionately more from IT given their size? Remarkably, research reveals that while IT has greatly enhanced bank performance [16, 17], IT has had a greater impact on cost reduction and profit growth in small banks [8]. These studies do not say why small size matters or why profit effects persist, but with all banks facing the same interest rates, regulations, and capital requirements, certain size-based factors appear to be enabling small banks to survive and prosper.

Using the value disciplines typology devised by Treacy and Wiersema [56] and confirmed in later studies [41, 52, 60, 61], we posit that large banks use a strategy of operational excellence that emphasizes transaction throughput, accuracy, and productivity whereas small banks use a strategy of customer intimacy. In effect, size is a predictor of how banks provide service to customers. Because small banks tend to be more geographically focused—community or local banks, for example—they tend to know their customers and their banking needs personally and to be flexible in things such as voiding out-of-network automated teller machine (ATM) fees or using discretion to limit overdraft charges [56]. Small banks cannot differentiate themselves through economies of scale; other means must be found. In this way, we argue that small banks will place customer intimacy rather than efficiency, productivity, or throughput at the heart of all client interactions. These arguments are highlighted in the conceptual model in Figure 2 where bank size affects the choice of both business and IT strategy, the ensuing degree of alignment between these strategies, and their impact on firm performance.

This paper offers a theoretical discussion of how differences in service strategies impact the fit between IT and business strategy. The IT needs of two prominent strategies are assessed within the Treacy and Wiersema [56] typology and how these needs are met by banks of varied size. Data were drawn from a matched survey of 43 banks over two time periods (2003 and 2007).<sup>6</sup>

## Theoretical Overview

Business st rat egy, defined as “the determination of the basic long-term goals of an enterprise, and the adoption of courses of action and allocation of resources necessary for carrying out these goals” [11, p. 13], has been studied using typologies by Miles and Snow [39], Porter [42], and others. The aim of each typology is to separate out and label how firms compete and deliver value in the marketplace. Typologies are useful but they can oversimplify the choices firms make [28]. Firms rarely see themselves as having a single strategic focus but rather a mix of different strategies. For example, under the Miles and Snow [39] typology, a firm may consider itself as a defender in some markets and a prospector in others. Treacy and Wiersema [56] examine this issue of single versus multiple foci but also take a key step toward improving or extending Porter’s [42] typology of low-cost leadership, niche, and differentiation by offering more descriptive labels to reflect the range of activities within firms. The ensuing value disciplines typology applies to both manufacturing and service firms in various industries. It is academically rigorous, descriptively rich, and yet managerially pragmatic or relevant, as various strategy researchers have noted [41, 51, 52, 60, 61].

As described in Table 1, Treacy and Wiersema [56] identify three unique foci. Firms can focus on operational excellence, meaning low cost, reliability, accuracy, and availability; customer intimacy, with a focus on service quality; or product leadership, meaning innovation of leading-edge products and services. The authors call for firms to focus on just one of these three value disciplines, but they nonetheless urge firms to be competent in all other areas. As the examples in Table 1 show, in the context of retail banking, some banks readily fall into one of the three value disciplines, but others—notably, banks such as Wells Fargo and Bank of America that have been actively acquiring banks, brokerage houses, and insurance carriers—can have a presence in more than one value discipline at the same time. As research has repeatedly found, the problem with stuck-in-the-middle firms—a term coined by Porter [42] for firms with mixed strategies—is that they underperform single-strategy firms [51, 62]. From a service perspective, it is easy to see why banks might want to build out their range of product offerings as a way to increase wallet share, creating a one-stop shop for all financial needs. Yet we also know from past studies on vertical integration and from the experience of firms such as Ford or Dell that owning each link in the supply chain is not always best [31].

Unlike large banks, small banks do not have the financial means to expand their product portfolio by buying other firms, and yet a glance at any small bank’s Web site will reveal portal-like links that allow small banks to maintain the appearance of having a wide range of products and services. A portal solution is ideal if each customer wants something different. For example, retirees look to fixed or variable annuity products whereas business customers may be more interested in vehicle leasing or capital loans. Of the three value disciplines shown in Table 1, product leadership, is unusual in that banks tend not to be leading-edge innovators. For example, online bill pay was created by CheckFree and account aggregation was created by Yodlee, so few banks see themselves as pure innovators. Certainly, banks perform some innovation as Bank of America has shown with paperless ATM deposits, but most banking strategies tend to focus on operational excellence or customer intimacy or a combination of both. In Table 1, product leadership is linked to firms such as the Dutch bank ING, which uses high-yield online savings accounts to attract new customers, or Fidelity Investments, whose target date Freedom Funds are popular in retirement accounts because of their automatic rebalancing of risk and primary asset allocation based on the number of years left to retirement. Perhaps the best-known example of product innovation is Merrill Lynch’s cash management account, first introduced in 1977 as a way to compete with highly regulated state banks. Today, however, with the push toward banks becoming financial conglomerates, all banking entities offer some type of cash management account, and so, in general, banks have tended to be fast adopters of new ideas rather than pure innovators.

## The Role of Size in Service Strategy Determination

Small, owner-managed businesses are the norm in most industrialized nations, including the United States, where 99.7 percent of all businesses have fewer than 500 employees.<sup>7</sup> Because IT research tends to focus on large firms—in part because of access to data—size effects are often ignored [23]. Size is normally treated as a control variable, but in those rare instances where size is explicitly modeled, some interesting results have emerged. For example, Porter [42] first spoke about size in the context of sector profitability. Recognizing that small firms can, on occasion, be more profitable than their larger rivals, he asks, “under what industry circumstances can a firm select a specialist strategy [differentiation or niche] without being vulnerable to economies of scale . . . achieved by broader-line firms” [42, p. 146]? In other studies, Chen and Hambrick [12], Hofer [26], and Raju et al. [45] identify size as a key contingency variable in the link between strategy and firm performance, with small firms outperforming large firms. Smith et al. [49] report a similar result in using the Miles and Snow typology. In a study of the airline industry, Goll et al. [22] find that size also moderates the link between environment, strategy, and performance. In a result that holds implications for the present study of banks, Goll et al. find that large airlines tend to pursue low-cost leadership strategies whereas smaller airlines employ niche service strategies that offer a very different type of value proposition than low cost.

Separately, the marketing literature boasts an extensive body of work on perceived service quality with links to customer satisfaction and firm performance. For example, Anderson et al. [4] find a positive link between customer satisfaction and shareholder value; almost half of the variance in shareholder value is attributable to firm-level heterogeneity, which includes size-related factors. Anderson et al. [3] also find a positive link between perceived quality, customer satisfaction, and net profitability. However, consistent with prior research by Fornell [20] and Griffin and Hauser [25], Anderson et al. [4] also report that an increase in market share—indicative of the domination of the U.S. banking industry by a handful of banks—can lead to a fall in customer satisfaction. Their reasoning reveals a dilution in service quality with each increase in market share, particularly in instances where customer preferences vary or where one-on-one attention is needed.

Bigger is better when it comes to economies of scale, but these studies identify a possible downside risk of size if customers of large firms are less satisfied than those of small firms. If so, size can hurt profits even as economies of scale help drive efficiency. In a further indictment of size, Allon and Gurvich [1] employ analytical modeling to show that large firms are less likely to engage in service-level differentiation when market share is higher. Using wait times as a proxy for service levels, they reveal that large firms see little value in raising service levels beyond recognized industry norms as competitors are likely to retaliate with investments that restore competitive parity across the industry. Instead, large firms will use other forms of differentiation around price or other factors that are independent of service levels. What this means is that large firms will offer industry-standard service levels (for example, as measured by call center wait times) but will not necessarily regard service as a competitive differentiator. Instead, they will try to compete on ancillary service attributes such as price, location, convenience, transactional accuracy, and reliability [1].

Small firms may find it difficult to achieve service-level parity if it requires a significant amount of capital investment, but they may equally try to change the type of service relationship with the customer so that the focus is not on those factors that require significant investment but rather on factors that lead to a better all-around service experience. As Porter [42] argues, small firms may try to create a niche around service design or delivery that does not appeal to large firms because of its smaller scale. Large firms may not be able to replicate this service model if, as Allon and Gurvich [1] posit, it requires a level of resource commitment (often a labor commitment) that may not be viable at high transaction volumes as is typical of large banks. Thus, whereas large banks may see little value in ratcheting up service levels or by redefining service for a mass market—fearing retaliation from other large banks— small banks recognize the value in changing their customers’ service experiences, safe in the knowledge that large banks will not be tempted to duplicate the idiosyncrasies of their small-scale services model. What this will then lead small banks to is a high-touch, personalized service model, indicative of a customer-intimate strategy as noted by Treacy and Wiersema [56]. Personalization—or relationship banking in industry parlance—means that banks aim to identify and meet the unique needs of each individual customer. This level of intimacy assumes that all of the things that large banks do by default, such as efficient or reliable transaction processing, also apply here. Customer intimacy is not in lieu of operational excellence; it is in addition to operational excellence [42, 52]. It would be meaningless to offer personalized services if at the same time key banking activities such as check processing or deposit clearing were not handled in a complete, accurate, and timely fashion. Customers of small and large banks expect their transactions will be handled properly; they do not expect that any increase in service levels can only come at the expense of a precipitous decline in service quality.

At its core, banking products such as savings accounts are commodities; there is little to delineate the products of one bank from another. A small bank that sells a basic set of core products and little else can expect stiff competition from larger, established banks if there is no attempt to simultaneously provide a unique service approach. Large banks such as Bank of America use scale (6,139 branches; 18,685 ATMs)<sup>8</sup> to create a service model based on location and access. Small banks, in contrast, build a specific relationship with each customer based on trust, familiarity, flexibility, and commitment. Small banks such as Emigrant Bank in Long Island, New York (34 branches; 31 ATMs),<sup>9</sup> or Southbridge Savings Bank in Massachusetts (13 branches; 14 ATMs)<sup>10</sup> train their employees to be more engaging and to focus on developing lifelong customer relationships. Others, such as ING, which has no physical bank branches, use cafe concepts in major cities (similar to Apple stores) to identify, attract, and educate customers. Boston-based Wainwright Bank (13 branches; 13 ATMs)<sup>11</sup> also utilizes showroom concepts and in-branch cafes to offer a different service experience. Large banks claim to offer relationship banking, but, as noted by Porter [42] and White [62], a high-touch service is incompatible with low-cost strategies. It costs too much to offer high-touch services to a mass market; high-touch services can also limit productivity growth. Consistent with the trend toward higher profit margin, as found in small banks in recent years, these arguments lead to the first hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (The Operational Excellence Hypothesis): Large banks are more likely to adopt a strategy of operational excellence while small banks are more likely to focus on customer intimacy.

## The Evolution Toward Customer Intimacy in Next Generation Banking

The introduction of new technology into retail banking has produced a surge of interest in how IT can help banks find cross-selling opportunities. The Financial Modernization Act (1999), while protecting customer data, also formalized the process by which data may be shared with third parties and among the employees of a bank. Data mining has allowed banks to learn more about their customers, their customers’needs, and how the demands of each market segment (retirees, etc.) evolve over time. The Harvard Business School case study on Capital One [2], describing the use of client profiling as part of an information-based strategy, indicates how, in a banking context, IT can be used to learn how customers allocate their assets or how profitable they will be in the future. Profiling helps to reduce Type 1 (attracting loss-generating customers) and Type 2 (rejecting potentially profitable customers) errors. All banks want to deepen their relationship with their client base and to find ways of gaining new customers. In small banks, profiling is less likely to be IT driven, because of the type of IT investment required. Instead, profiling can be done, in part, through what personnel learn about their clients in personal interactions. For example, a passing reference to a pending foreign vacation can spark an opportunity to sell foreign currency or travel insurance. Large banks will rely on IT to make the case for how they can deepen their relationship with a client. Because personnel in large banks are not as acquainted with customers in the same way that a local bank may be (due, in part, to the sheer numbers of customers), IT is expected to play a greater role in the shift toward customer intimacy in large banks.<sup>12</sup>

This is consistent with arguments within the service science literature that see IT as a disruptive technology [5, 18, 47]. Lang and Colgate [32] further argue that banks that fail to use IT to enrich their relationships with customers will be at a competitive disadvantage, the implication being that large banks may be able to use IT to enhance transaction processing, accuracy, and throughput—typical operationally excellent activities—but that they must ultimately use IT to advance customer intimacy. This view is consistent with findings in Curry and Penman [15] where it was advocated that banks should avoid overusing IT to automate key customer interactions at the expense of pursuing a more personal or intimate approach to service delivery. At another level, IT investments may also be able to mimic some of the service-delivery features found in small banks. For example, customer relationship management (CRM) can make it easier for large banks to manage customer relations at a level that is more indicative of small banks [40]. These arguments lead to the second hypothesis concerning the likely shift in service strategies (chiefly in large banks) from operational excellence to customer intimacy:

Hypothesis 2 (The Customer Intimacy Hypothesis): Over time, banks will shift the focus of their service strategies more toward customer intimacy.

## A Service Science Perspective on Strategic Alignment

At the heart of service science is the concept that service is a co-creation process involving, in the case of banking, customers, service partners, business strategy, and a host of internal bank resources such as staff, managers, and IT. These co-creation processes are variously configured as value chains, as might apply in the case of structured check-clearing processes; value shops, where resources are pooled to resolve specific customer issues such as home refinancing; or value networks, where value is created from the diverse relationships formed between banks, their clients, and third-party service providers [50]. In the present-day banking environment, IT is often central to each of these value co-creation processes. Surveys reveal that financial service firms are the biggest IT spenders: 7 percent of revenues in 2007, twice that of firms in other parts of the economy [37]. This has important implications for value co-creation in terms of how IT is used to support key activities or processes underlying the business strategy. It also means that value co-creation—the sine qua non of service science—is irrevocably linked to the fit between IT and business strategy, a perennial topic of concern among practitioners [9, 14, 33]. Misalignment will almost certainly hurt service delivery if IT is lacking—due to insufficient or misdirected IT spending, for example—whereas alignment, a known enabler of competitive advantage, will likely spur even greater value co-creation [9].

Of course, IT is not the only resource underlying value co-creation. Service science explains that services are a product of complex configurations of IT, capital, and labor resources that may, on occasion, require the active involvement or acquiescence of the customer [57, 63]. Although small banks may not have the same volume of resources as large banks, they may nevertheless be able to configure critical processes to offer a truly unique service experience. The resource-based view is also useful in explaining why small banks have been able to achieve a sustained level of profit margin and why this level of performance may be unavailable to large banks. Theory posits that superior performance reflects the rarity or uniqueness of the resources that comprise a specific service model [6, 24, 59]. While individual resources may be easily duplicated or procured in factor markets, the integration of these resources is often a source of ambiguity, value, and immobility that protects a bank’s service model from competitors [6, 24, 44, 46, 59]. At a time when e-banking is seen as a commodity—small banks may now buy e-banking services from outsourcers rather than developing them in-house—IT is less of a leading source of enduring value than when IT was only available to large banks (this was the case with the first generation of ATMs, as the value of the ATM to customers was a function of network externalities and the size of the bank network).

Instead, it is likely that IT in combination with other resources—most notably, labor and the relationship information held by bank staff on their account holders—leads to higher performance. Bank staff, their knowledge of the local banking market, and their role in developing lifelong customer relationships are a vital resource for small banks, as noted previously [15]. What this means is that alignment can have a direct and indirect effect on overall performance. In the first instance, IT-led value co-creation—where IT is the driving force behind value co-creation or where IT is allowed to spearhead customer relations—will lead to higher IT business value, defined as the contribution of IT to firm performance [55]; higher IT business value, meanwhile, can lead to higher overall firm performance [54]. In the second instance, non-IT-led value co-creation— where IT is not the primary resource underlying customer relations—will still lead to tighter alignment of IT and business strategy, but the effects of greater alignment will flow directly through to firm performance rather than indirectly to performance via IT business value. Past alignment research has not tried to conceptually or empirically separate the effect of alignment on IT business value or firm performance into an ITbased component and a non-IT (namely, labor) component. This relationship can be shown as a mediation model with both direct and indirect effects [29]. Overall, these arguments culminate in the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3 (The Alignment and Non-IT-Led Value Hypothesis): Alignment is positively associated with non-IT-led value co-creation in the form of higher overall firm performance.

Hypothesis 4 (The IT Business Value Mediation Link Hypothesis): For large banks, in particular, IT business value is likely to mediate the link between alignment and overall firm performance.

## Value Co-Creation Size Effects

As part of the foregoing discussion, we alluded to differences between small and large banks, not just in terms of business strategy but their likely use of IT or non-IT resources as a way to drive customer value. Recognizing that each type of bank will make different strategic decisions and that this will almost certainly translate to different IT investment choices, the ensuing form of alignment will also be different. Previous research has considered the extent of alignment using a low–high scale, but this is not the same as the form of alignment [10]. Thus, Palmer and Markus [41] and Tallon [52] note that alignment between an innovation-focused business strategy and an innovation-focused IT strategy can generate different results from alignment between a client-focused business strategy and a client-focused IT strategy. The extent of alignment could be the same in each case and yet one firm may encounter innovation-related effects such as faster design times while the other sees client-focused benefits in the form of lower customer turnover. Therefore, lumping all firms together, regardless of differences in size or strategy, could produce spurious results [52]. Had IT impacts been averaged across all firms, innovation or client-related IT impacts might have seemed weak or nonexistent; taking size into account would have brought differences in IT to the fore [41]. Tallon [52] argues that moving from a firm-level to a process-level view of alignment could help to solve this limitation in the alignment literature while also ensuring a more realistic consideration of IT impacts.

Recognizing that size may be a key determinant of business strategy and that, in a service context, small and large banks may look to IT for different things, we argue that the approach to value co-creation may also be different across banks. If IT is more important to service delivery in large banks, the indirect effects of alignment on firm performance may be higher than in small banks where IT, although important, is not as critical to value co-creation. In contrast, non-IT resources may be more critical in small banks with the result that the direct effects of alignment on firm performance are higher than in large banks. Thus, the following hypotheses are offered as a way to isolate specific size effects on alignment and value co-creation:

Hypothesis 5 (The Direct Effect of Alignment Hypothesis): The direct effect of alignment on firm performance is likely to be higher for small banks than for large banks.

Hypothesis 6 (The Indirect Effect of Alignment Hypothesis): The indirect effect of alignment on firm performance with IT business value acting as a mediator is likely to be higher for large banks than for small banks.

## Data Analysis

To formally t est hyp ot heses 1 to 6, a survey of FDIC-insured banks was conducted using matched surveys of IT and business executives during two time periods—2003 and 2007. The purpose of the second data collection was to assess how banking strategies changed over time in the Customer Intimacy Hypothesis (H2). The sample was drawn from the Compustat Database of publicly traded companies.<sup>13</sup> From a population of 387 banks with revenues in 2002 of between \$100 million and \$3 billion—limits that excluded very small banks and those appearing in the Fortune 500 whose size might bias the analysis<sup>14</sup>—complete responses were obtained from 43 banks, an 11 percent response rate. Although this response rate may seem low, it is on a par with response rates in other studies [48]. A complete response includes two independently administered surveys per firm: a chief information officer (CIO) or IT executive survey detailing strategic IT goals, the extent of IT use at the process level, and measures of the Treacy and Wiersema [56] typology and a strategic planner survey that explores business strategy via process-level measures and that probes executives’ perceptions of IT business value at different points in the value chain. Respondents were identified using Hoovers (http://hoovers.com), a subscription database of corporate information, and the January 2003 Directory of Top Computer Executives compiled by Applied Computer Research.

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) found that the 43 banks in the sample were representative of the banks in the population based on sales, profit margin, and assets. Ten nonrespondents were contacted in order to assess nonrespondent bias. Reasons for failing to reply included policies precluding participation in surveys and time constraints. Table 2 presents a summary of the sample; using the FDIC definition of small (assets under \$100 million) and large (assets over \$100 million) banks, the sample can be broken into 22 small banks and 21 large banks.<sup>15</sup> Average assets across the entire sample were \$5.4 billion (standard deviation [SD] \$13.9 billion). Overall, the 43 banks in the sample control just over 3.17 percent of all U.S. bank assets. At first glance, this might appear to signal a small sample size problem and a broader problem of generalizability. Yet it must be remembered that the sample excluded by design 48 banks in the Fortune 500 as this group controls a disproportionately large share (71 percent) of U.S. bank assets. Overall, the present sample of 43 banks holds 11 percent of all non–Fortune 500 bank assets. To alleviate other concerns regarding small sample size, we later refer to a series of tests involving statistical power analysis as part of the detailed hypothesis testing [13].

## Measuring Alignment of IT and Business Strategy

Alignment has been interpreted, operationalized, and measured in a variety of ways [7, 58]. Chief among these has been moderation—interaction or product terms involving IT and business strategy—and profile deviation, signifying the difference between actual and ideal IT use. Although both approaches are operationally different, there is a significant degree of correlation between the measures produced by both methods [52]. Because moderation is the most straightforward of the two methods with values between zero (total misalignment) and one (perfect alignment), this approach was applied based on process-level measures of IT and business strategy as outlined in Tallon [52]. Using a set of five items that represent the processes in a generic value chain (all items appear in the Appendix), CIOs were asked to assess IT use on a seven-point scale anchored on “low IT use” and “high IT use” and strategic planners were asked to identify the extent of actual or implemented business activities on a seven-point scale anchored on “not at all” and “to a great extent.” Moderation scores representing alignment could then be found by multiplying each IT use item by its corresponding business strategy item. Next, each product score was divided by a scalar denoting the maximum product score (7 × 7) in order to map all scores into a unitary scale for ease of interpretation.

## Measuring Firm Performance

The effects of alignment were evaluated in two ways. First, objective measures of firm performance were considered using two widely recognized metrics: return on assets (ROA) (profit as a percentage of total assets) and profit margin (profit as a percentage of sales). Both metrics are accessible on a quarterly basis from the FDIC. Second, firm performance was evaluated in terms of IT business value, which signifies the contribution of IT to firm performance. IT impacts have historically been examined through productivity, profitability, and operating cost measures [19, 30, 38]. The problem with this approach has been that the first-order impacts of IT occur at the process level, whereas objective measures are typically only gathered at the firm level [54]. Recognizing this, Tallon [54] designed and validated a perception-based instrument to evaluate executives’ perceptions of IT business value at multiple points along the value chain; the items included in that instrument were found to correlate with objective measures of IT business value, with the implication being that perceptions can be used if objective measures do not exist. While we have financial measures of firm performance (ROA, profit margin), these measures can only offer a blunt assessment of the variety of IT impacts in firms, and so the entire set of 25 perception items was adapted from Tallon [54] for use in this study. These items were added to the strategic planner survey with the request that respondents focus on current, realized value rather than future value. Together, these items span the value chain, identifying IT impacts in each of five key business processes (five items per process): supplier relations,<sup>17</sup> production and operations, product and service enhancement, marketing and sales, and customer relations. All items were evaluated on a seven-point Likert scale anchored on “low IT impact” and “high IT impact.”

While these IT business value items have been rigorously tested in previous research [52, 55], we opted to perform an initial exploratory factor analysis. Using principal components analysis with varimax rotation, a five-factor structure covering 75.6 percent of the total variance was found in the model. No one factor was dominant—a concern raised by Harman’s single-factor test. Table 4 reports the factor loadings along with other item statistics showing that all items factored under their expected process headings. We also undertook a confirmatory factor analysis in order to establish the validity of the items. As observed in Table 5, discriminant validity is present as the square root of the average variance explained (AVE) for each factor exceeds the correlation for each factor-pair. Convergent validity is also noted by virtue of the fact that the AVE for each factor exceeds a minimum of 0.50 while reliability also exceeds a minimum of 0.80 [21].

## Hypothesis Testing

The first hypothesis suggested that large banks would pursue a strategy of operational excellence, in keeping with their focus on low-cost, productivity, and transaction throughput, while small banks would gravitate more toward customer intimacy based on their dedication to service. To test this hypothesis, chi-square test results were reviewed based on a simple cross-tabulation test. As seen in Table 6, 59 percent of small banks define themselves as customer intimate and 62 percent of large banks view themselves as operationally excellent, thereby confirming support for the Operational Excellence Hypothesis (H1). The Pearson chi-square (8.835) and likelihood ratio (9.218) were both significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ ; this shows how bank size and strategy are not independent, but are instead closely linked. Small banks clearly see themselves as providing a superior level of personal service whereas large banks see low-cost, throughput, convenience, and access as their dominant means of differentiation.

We also argued that all banks, regardless of size, would gravitate toward customer intimacy as a means to expand their profit margins. Theory argues that being the lowest-cost provider is good, up to a point, but that service differentiation is the ultimate driver of value [62]. To identify changes in strategic focus, in mid-2007 a short survey was mailed to the CIOs of each of the 43 participating banks. This was not a matched survey but rather a single-respondent survey. The January 2007 Directory of Top Computer Executives was used to update the respondent list. All 43 banks were still active—none had been bought out although some of the larger banks on the list had acquired several smaller banks. A 100 percent response rate was received due in large part to a shorter survey, author outreach to sample firms, and a growing interest among CIOs and other executives in relationship banking and how bank strategies are evolving over time.

Value co-creation, as noted earlier, can have both direct and indirect effects on firm performance. Indirect effects—mediated by IT business value—isolate the effects of IT-enabled value co-creation while direct effects isolate non-IT-enabled effects around other resources such as personal skills or learning. To determine these effects, a partial least squares (PLS) model was tested. PLS is more accommodating of small sample size, and its major focus is maximizing explained variance (R<sup>2</sup>) rather than model fit, as is the case with covariance-based modeling. Because the 25 items measuring IT business value factored under five headings, five first-order factors were constructed using PLS item weights from the initial confirmatory analysis; these composite first-order factors were then added to the PLS model as reflective indicators of a second-order IT business value latent construct. In the case of alignment, product scores representing each business process were used as reflective indicators of an all-encompassing firmwide alignment construct. Lastly, overall firm performance was modeled as a single latent construct with two reflective performance indicators—ROA and profit margin. Significance levels were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap samples. As the sample size may be considered small, statistical power was also investigated as a way to lend greater credence to the results. Using a computed medium effect size of 0.259, the model has statistical power of 0.83, which is above the recommended minimum level of 0.80 suggested by Cohen [13].

The results of the model are shown in Table 8. Three sets of results are reported: first, for the entire sample and then, independently, for both small and large banks. Note that for the overall sample, the direct effect of alignment on firm performance is insignificant and the indirect effect as mediated by IT business value is significant $( p < 0 . 0 5 )$ Hence, the Alignment and Non-IT-Led Value Hypothesis (H3) is not supported but the IT Business Value Mediation Link Hypothesis (H4) is supported. What this also confirms is that IT business value completely mediates the link between alignment and firm performance [29]. On that basis, one can conjecture that IT-enabled value co-creation is of paramount importance to all banks and that banks should look more to IT rather than non-IT resources to lead the charge toward value creation for banks and their customers. Alternatively, the push toward next-generation banking—evident in areas such as mobile banking—will be propelled more by IT rather than personal service as favored by small banks. At first glance, this result may seem to favor large banks as they are more likely to have resources to invest in IT and may be more willing to accept the extreme risk of working with untested IT.

However, the results look very different when the model is independently estimated for small and large banks. In the case of small banks, we find a significant direct and indirect effect; for large banks, we find a significant indirect effect only. A comparison of the direct effects between small and large banks is significant (0.276; p < 0.05), thus supporting the Direct Effect of Alignment Hypothesis (H5).This result shows that value co-creation is heavily biased toward IT in the case of large banks but that both

IT and non-IT resources play a key role in directing the design and delivery of services in small banks. This result is examined in greater detail in the next section.

Differences in the indirect effect between small and large banks was also examined. While the size of the effect appears much larger in small banks (0.555 versus 0.398), a t-test finds no significant differences between both groups. Accordingly, the Indirect Effect of Alignment Hypothesis (H6) is not supported. The implication is that IT is equally important to firm performance in small and large banks. This result seems surprising given the amount of IT resources typically found in large banks. What this result does is call attention to, not the extent of IT resources, but how banks use whatever IT resources are available to them. To help in this comparison, we decided to decompose the process-level alignment scores to gain insight into how value cocreation is accomplished in both small and large banks; the result of this process-level alignment decomposition appears in Table 9.

Decomposing alignment is a useful step as it allows us to consider potential reasons for why there are such glaring differences in the direct effects of alignment on firm performance. Theoretically, it was suggested that small banks’ focus on personalized service, unlike the heavily standardized and automated service models used by large banks, would have a far greater direct effect on firm performance. If service models are very different in each bank—as banking strategies may suggest—it is likely that each type of bank will rely to a greater or lesser extent on different processes. As Treacy and Wiersema [56] described, operationally excellent strategies emphasize supplier relations (to avoid errors, for example) or production and operations (to enhance productivity, throughput, and order flow). In contrast, customer-intimate firms emphasize sales, marketing, and customer relations. Tallon [52] found a relationship between the locus of alignment—that is, processes where alignment is highest—and business strategy, and so in comparing banks, we can expect to find differences in alignment from one process to the next.

As shown in Table 9, several key differences can be observed, but there are also processes where no material differences arise, which is contrary to expectation. For example, there are no significant differences in alignment within supplier relations and production and operations—two parts of the value chain where we can expect large banks to excel. Although small banks do not have the same emphasis on automation or product standardization as their large rivals, they appear not to be at a disadvantage, because IT business value, the value created by IT in these processes as indicated in the lower panel of Table 9, is statistically equal to that of large banks. In the areas of sales, marketing, and customer relations—key processes for customer intimacy—small banks have an alignment advantage, with IT business value being statistically higher in each case.<sup>18</sup> These results have implications for large banks that, while espousing a dominant strategy of operational excellence, are trying to become more customer focused. Quite simply, the data in this paper point to a gap between their aspiration and current reality.

The challenge facing large banks in their quest to develop relationship banking is compounded by intransigence in their goals for IT. As part of the CIO survey, data were collected on goals for IT using four measures devised by Tallon et al. [55]. These items evaluate strategic goals for IT via efficiency (using IT to deliver greater throughput), effectiveness (using IT to improve overall firm performance), market reach (using IT to find new customers or to boost sales to current customers), and structure (using IT to radically alter banking practices as seen in the move to relationship banking). Data were collected during 2003 and 2007, the purpose being to find if the evolution in goals for IT kept pace with changes in business strategy (see the Appendix for items). Respondents noted their agreement with each item on a seven-point Likert scale anchored on “do not agree” and “agree completely.” As shown in Table 10, large banks continue to view efficiency as their primary goal for IT, and even though they increasingly see IT as a way to extend market reach and to redefine banking practices, they still lag small banks. An analysis of variance further confirms that there are lingering differences between small and large banks in their consideration of how IT might alter banking practices (7.63, p < 0.01). What this means is that large banks aspire to relationship banking, but their overall goals for IT remain entrenched in an era where IT was used to drive efficiency. As can be seen in Table 9, the fact that alignment is weak in the areas of sales and marketing and customer relations further shows that larger banks have a tough struggle ahead to reorient their IT to support customer intimacy. The irony of this result is that small banks—despite limited resources (lower IT spending in absolute terms)—have been able to outperform their larger rivals with a superior service model and more astute use of IT.<sup>19</sup>

Another way to approach this question of whether goals for IT are able to keep pace with changes in business strategy is to consider a regression model of first differences that uses changes in goals for IT from 2003 to 2007 as a way to predict changes in business strategy over the same period. A bank that has designed a customer- or service-oriented architecture may be better able to make the leap from operational excellence to customer intimacy. Thus, for larger banks especially, we would expect that changes in goals for IT would predict changes in customer intimacy. As noted in Table 11, some interesting results show that even though large banks have made some progress toward revising their goals for IT, there is still some room for improvement. Specifically, regression analysis shows a positive association in large banks between changes in IT goals for extending market reach and changes in customer intimacy. This indicates that large banks are using IT to expand market reach, but, anecdotally, this is primarily around using IT to integrate customers from newly acquired banks and to attract more customers to their scale-driven model.

As various researchers have noted, customer intimacy is not the same as simply adding more customers if it does not also entail treating each of those customers as if they were their own market segment [25, 56]. Larger banks may simply be using scale economics to justify efforts to boost market share without having to fundamentally alter their service model. Note that for large banks, there is no link between goals for IT around changing banking practices (as one would expect with a move toward relationship banking). In the case of small banks, we find some scaling back in IT goals for effectiveness as a way to become more customer intimate. This result suggests that small banks are less inclined to use IT to push for higher firm performance through cost-cutting measures or by removing the human element from their service model. Ironically, small banks may be more willing to sacrifice performance effectiveness— adding costs to their service delivery or expanding wait times—in order to ensure a truly unique customer service experience.<sup>20</sup>

## Discussion

Size and t he long-honored maxim t hat bigger is better represent a double-edged sword for banks seeking to become more service oriented. As banks grow larger through further consolidation—subject to a federal restriction that no bank can hold more than 10 percent of all U.S. deposits—IT is seen as providing an essential cushion against rising labor costs. Automation, whether through ATMs, telephone call centers, or banking Web sites, can help to limit labor costs, allowing a marked increase in the number of customers per employee. Productivity gains are certainly welcomed, but an often overlooked question is how size shapes the service models that banks are trying to implement. As outlined by Curry and Penman, in a market “where quality of service can be a differentiator . . . the balance between personal interaction and technologically delivered services must be right if customers are to be retained over time” [15, p. 331]. Besides seeing differences in profit margins (noted in Figure 1), research finds that small banks enjoy a higher return on IT investment [8].

By opening up the notion of value co-creation to closer scrutiny—principally, by looking at the link between banking strategies and IT use—we can begin to offer some explanation for why small banks are so successful over time. We learn, for example, that small banks enjoy significantly higher alignment in two processes in the value chain—sales and marketing support and customer relations—processes that are central to a customer-intimate strategy. Small banks may not have the same resource base as large banks and they may not be as open to the idea of placing bets on emerging technologies (e.g., mobile banking), but what IT they are using is proving to be highly effective. The difference may lie in how small banks use IT in their service model. In high-touch service models, IT is arguably less of a substitute for labor, as in the case of extreme automation, and more of a complement to non-IT resources, as some empirical and case-based research has concluded [8, 27].

Research also notes that different demographic segments look for different things in their banking relationships, something that might play to the advantage of small banks. Retirees, for example, want service over convenience [27]. Because small banks do not have a scale advantage, it makes sense to seek differentiation through service. Large banks recognize the inherent value of providing a higher level of service but with a focus on operational excellence, this translates into a push for higher productivity, standard products (less variability), and reduced costs. What the current results reveal is that a focus on supplier relations and production and operations, as a way to secure this type of service model, will not create an alignment advantage. This result is consistent with earlier alignment research by Tallon [52], who found that customer-intimate firms were just as capable of aligning IT and business strategy in noncore parts of the value chain as operationally excellent firms. What this simply means is that any bank that seeks to differentiate itself on the basis of personal service, as small banks are now doing, must be capable of processing transactions, clearing checks, and managing transfers in a manner that is no worse than large, operationally excellent banks. As an example, boutique banks such as U.S. Trust (now a division of Bank of America) that service a tiny cadre of high–net worth individuals or trusts could not survive if their customers’ basic transactional needs were not being processed correctly, just as any large bank would do. The implication for larger banks that continue to focus on operational excellence is clear: apart from a more extensive branch network, there is little that makes their service model superior to that of small banks [1]. It was also observed in the present results that large banks have an expressed desire to move toward customer intimacy but there is a question mark over whether this is really possible given that personal service is costly to scale and their IT continues to focus on transaction efficiency and cost rather than trying to offer a unique service experience to each customer.

A key result in this research is the marked difference in value co-creation between small and large banks. In the case of large banks, as noted earlier, value co-creation is predominantly driven by IT; hence, the impact of alignment on firm performance is primarily indirect, through IT business value. The lack of a direct effect, coupled with no clear alignment advantage in those processes that are especially critical to large banks—supplier relations and production and operations—is troubling. A move toward a customer intimacy strategy will call for a different approach in how IT is used and in how non-IT resources such as banking personnel interact with IT. Small banks, meanwhile, enjoy a more balanced approach to value co-creation. IT and non-IT resources are joint contributors to value co-creation and firm performance. Although small banks may not be as vertically integrated as their larger rivals, their use of portals as a way to offer a broad range of banking products (through online referrals to third-party providers) and their use of IT to better understand the customer and their banking needs is providing a significant and enduring advantage.

## Contribution and Limitations

This research contributes to the expanding literature on alignment and service science in three key respects. First, we assess the service implications of size—an often trivialized variable in information systems research [23]—in a sector that is increasingly dominated by large banking entities but whose profit margins have failed to exceed that of smaller banks. Despite the oft-held belief that bigger is better, it is shown that small size is highly enabling of a personalized form of relationship banking. Second, as part of the embryonic study of service science, it is shown that value co-creation— in this case, proxied by alignment or fit between IT and business strategy—varies by size and strategy type. Contrary to prior research in the specialty retail sector where alignment was unrelated to retail-oriented performance metrics [41], it is found that alignment can be a source of value. However, discovering that source of value means deconstructing the strategies adopted by banks and the IT resources they use to support key processes. Consistent with prior alignment research by Tallon [52] and comments in the strategy literature [62], we see that operational excellence—such as low-cost leadership in the Porter [42] typology—presents a difficult set of challenges for firms trying to create a sustainable advantage using this one strategy. Third, it is found that banking aspirations toward a strategy of customer intimacy need to be better matched with a focus on using IT to define customer relationships. In the case of large banks, IT is still focused on goals of enhancing efficiency and productivity. These are worthy goals and necessary for IT to create value, but for customer intimacy to succeed, the primary locus of alignment needs to move to other parts of the value chain. For this to happen, goals for IT will certainly need to be revised. However, as noted by Porter [43], shifting goals for IT from being transaction focused to something that is more market focused can be difficult when, in the case of large banks, consolidation, downsizing, employee rationalization, IT integration, and a return to profitability are the order of the day.

Despite these contributions, the study has certain limitations. The sample is small and, by design, omits Fortune 500 banks in order to give as balanced a view as possible of the contrast between small and large banks. Also, self-reported measures of IT business value were used. Although research declares perceptions to be accurate, perceptions are still susceptible to bias and error [54]. In order to maximize responses from the later round of data collection, we did not use a matched survey design but rather a shorter CIO survey. A matched survey might have yielded richer insights into alignment, albeit at the risk of a smaller sample size. Many of these limitations can be addressed in future studies that call for a larger sample size and that purposefully include the largest banking entities, such as Citigroup, JPMorgan, and Bank of America.<sup>21</sup> A further limitation of the current research, as noted in other studies involving IT business value, involves the lack of objective process–level data. The richness of objective process–level data that speak to rates of account openings in response to marketing programs or that attest to the performance of call centers could provide deeper and more meaningful insights into IT business value in both large and small banks. Unfortunately, it is still the case that for strategic reasons banks remain highly protective of objective process–level data. In addition to accessing FDIC data, future research would benefit greatly from enhanced data collection efforts.

## Implications for Research and Practice

As outlined in Figure 1, financial services, and the banking industry in particular, will continue to evolve through consolidation, legislation, and new innovation. As a quintessential services industry, retail banking is a tool of national economic prosperity and a driver of shareholder value. If size is so critical to bank performance, future studies, whether looking at alignment or other aspects of value co-creation, may need to take more notice of size rather than marginalizing it as a control variable. It is also worth asking if the manner in which firms have aligned IT with their business strategy may somehow hurt efforts to align IT to a future (but different) business strategy, as is happening with large banks. Managers also need to be wary of the risks of using operational excellence as a long-term services strategy and understand how the use of consolidation as a key growth strategy for banks might impede efforts to become more customer intimate.

As noted by Goode and Gregor [23], size is multidimensional and important to our understanding of firms in practice and yet research has often been quick to dismiss the role of size in shaping theories of how firms use IT resources and how IT affects firm performance. This research confirms that size matters, not just in terms of profitability and the belief that bigger is better, but also in how firms use IT to transform their strategies and service models. In the case of the U.S. banking industry, IT has undoubtedly allowed some banks to scale to unprecedented size, but this does not mean that their cost-focused service delivery is superior to that of small banks. Ironically, the service-centric models of small banks continue to yield higher margins.

## Conclusion

Service firms have delivered a host of IT innovat ions in recent decades, from package tracking at FedEx to product design at Zara. The banking sector has similarly seen a surge in IT innovation as part of a transaction-oriented strategy geared to automation, standardization, and efficiency. Yet data continue to identify small banks as having an advantage in service delivery, something that has produced higher profit margins for the past decade, even as consolidation reduces the number of small banks. As next-generation banking sets an expectation that IT will be a hallmark of how banks interact with their customers, there is not only a hint of nostalgia as older local banks succumb to consolidation but also the question of whether personal service will decline. Research shows that it is difficult to be a low-cost leader and a differentiator at the same time, yet that is precisely what large banks seek. Consumers may be willing to compromise on service quality in return for lower costs and convenience (to a point), but the question facing large banks is how to scale personal service without raising costs. IT might help, but the results in this paper sound a word of caution that value co-creation—the true objective of service—may need to look beyond IT for continued success.

With the U.S. banking industry facing tremendous uncertainty amid the economic turmoil of the real estate bubble, with its subsequent bank failures, bailouts, and rushed government-led buyouts, there is also some question as to whether large banks service models will become even more cost conscious. For many small banks, their reluctance to engage in risky lending practices could allow their profit margins to continue. Small banks may see opportunities to capture market share through greater customer intimacy if large banks begin to impose higher account fees. Bigger may be better on one level, but small is beautiful.
