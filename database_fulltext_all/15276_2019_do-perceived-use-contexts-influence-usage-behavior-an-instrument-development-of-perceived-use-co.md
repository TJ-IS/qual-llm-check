## Highlights

 The effects of perceived use contexts were extended to TAM.

## Abstract

In this study, we advance current knowledge on IS usage behavior by introducing the concepts of time, location, social, and technological use in perceived use context. We develop that contextual factors of perceived use context (i.e., perceived time use, perceived location use, perceived social use, and perceived technological use) influence users’ behavioral beliefs (i.e., perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use), which leads to their subsequent attitude and behavioral intention toward using game services. Grounded in our findings, key implications for theory and practice are discussed.

Keywords: use context, digital service, game, use behavior, activity theory, activitycentric view

## 1. Introduction

The recent prevalence in the usage of smartphones and other mobile devices has helped to accelerate the digitalization of existing offline services; thus, providing services through mobile devices has become a vital strategy for the traditional offline service provider (Matt et al., 2015). With the transformation and integration of existing offline and online services, a new digital service type called offline-to-online (O2O) has been introduced in the market. The mobile- and smartphone-oriented ecosystems, along with advancements in

mobile communications (i.e., 4G-LTE, 5G) and smart device technologies (i.e., Artificial Intelligence, Internet of Things), have brought about a ubiquitous environment in which people can be connected to the internet anytime and anywhere (Liberati, 2016). All these advancements and evolutionary leaps have led to changes in user online behavior.

According to recent statistics published by the Korea Information Society (KISDI, 2018). As per KISDI statistics, 56% of respondents used smartphones for most of their media consumption and communication activity during the average day (KISDI, 2018). They mostly used digital services, with messaging apps leading in terms of usage, followed by search portals, games, news, and music (KISDI, 2018). Mobile e-commerce accounts for nearly 50% of all e-commerce transactions in Korea (KISDI, 2018). Because of the rapidly changing market and user behavior, competition among digital services has become intense and fast-paced (Matt et al., 2015). Therefore, it is now essential to provide customer-oriented service designs and customized services that incorporate users’ needs and specific use contexts. Since its introduction, the theoretical concept of “use context” has become among the most important research topic for understanding related situational and conditional factors (Hong et al., 2014; Mallat et al., 2009). In use contexts, we can identify the particular time and place of user consumption and determine which social conditions and technical environments support satisfying digital services (Kim et al., 2017). However, existing studies on technology acceptance models (TAMs) and currently studies on use contexts are limited to the goal of understanding relationships between specific user behaviors and use contexts. A recent study by Hong et al. (2014) identified context-specific factors as being very critical in influencing a user’s technology acceptance decision, and, as such, this study aims to empirically verify the relationship between context-specific factors and traditional TAM

constructs such as perceived usefulness and ease of use with three context-specific factors related to individual characteristics, performance, and efforts for digital library users. However, Hong et al. (2014) did not consider the relationship between consumer behavior and specific use context factors for mobile users who consume services freely anytime and anywhere. Another study on mobile application usability was conducted by Hoele and Venkatesh (2015), and they focused on the viewpoint of design in mobile apps and user interfaces, while neglecting the examination on use contexts, which is an essential variable in mobile digital services. In this regard, previous studies on use context and user experience seem insufficient to study user acceptance of digital services affected by such contexts in a ubiquitous environment employing various media and situational factors.

In the human–computer interaction (HCI) and information systems (IS) fields, a user’s environment and situation are referred to by different terms, such as “context,” “context-awareness,” “context-aware computing,” or “context-specific factors.” However, in the mobile environment, where interaction between humans and computers evolved through various technologies that were sensitive to temporal, spatial, social, and technological boundaries—such as smart mobiles, wearables, and Internet of Things devices; locationbased services; and various social technologies—this new emerging concept is called “use context.” Use context refers to “the very concrete environment in which technology is going to be used” (Van de Wijngaert & Bouwman, 2009, p. 86).

As the importance of use contexts in the smart information technology (IT) environment is strongly emphasized, when users are using a device, system, or service, all contextual data and factors that may exercise influence over users, directly or indirectly, are considered very influential to users’ technology usage behavior (Lee et al., 2005). For instance, when a user wants to play games using mobile phones, especially on the way to work or school by train or bus, this user needs to confirm the time, location, social, and

technological situation and context where he or she is at that time (Mallet et al. 2009). When a user is in a fixed location like at home, the use context will have lesser influence on the user’s behavior toward playing a game.

Since technology usage behavior and contexts have an important relationship from a theoretical perspective, such contextual information is crucial for a more precise Hence, recent empirical studies have proposed approaches to measure use contexts in mobile computing environments (Lee et al., 2005; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). For example, Liu and Li (2011) adopted a situational factor and explored the effects of this factor on behavior in the increasingly popular field of mobile gaming. However, most studies that have tried to measure use contexts assume certain situations experienced by users when using a technology (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). Furthermore, prior use context categories and instruments developed to measure such contexts have limited ability to explain user behavior and lack theoretical background. Therefore, use contexts and their categories need to be verified against changes in user behavior (Mallat et al., 2009).

Hence, this study aims to verify a model of use context of digital services empirically. The main goal of this study is to introduce the concept of use context in IS behavior research. Recently, there have been many virtual reality content schematics and services, which illustrate the growing importance of contextual information. For an IT service to reflect reality, it is very important to have accurate measurements and a sophisticated understanding of the usage context. The development of virtual reality services in the gaming field is especially prominent. With the advent of new IT technologies, the game service environment is becoming very diverse, thereby highlighting the necessity of exploring the use context of gaming.

This paper has several important contributions. First, this study suggests a new general categorization of use contexts and develops a research framework based on the findings of a literary and theoretical background review. Second, there is not much empirical evidence on use context with regards to digital services, aside from a few studies by scholars such as Liu & Li (2011) and Mallat et al. (2009) on how use context was explored as it analysis on the use context of various digital services, social networking services (SNSs), games, information content, video/movies, music, and e-commerce, which has not been attempted in prior studies or literature. The second empirical phase was aimed at instrument development and validation for measuring the perceived use contexts of games services by using factor analyses. Instruments were developed based on use context components from the research framework and previous studies. The final empirical phase aimed at confirmation of causal effects of four higher order constructs that represent four core use context categories: time, location, social, and technological categories that have an effect on the belief, attitude, and intention framework. The four types of use contexts that represent user perception include the temporal (i.e., time), spatial (i.e., location), social, and technological factors that may influence him or her while using a device, system, or service. The user recognition function records details of use during game play, such as the current time and place, concurrent users, and/or technical factors, and these are measured in each corresponding use context type. Each of the perceived use contexts has related variables: time-of-day, day of the week, physical distraction, location characteristics, social places, occurrence of social relations, connected device or devices, and network connectivity.

The users’ overall perception toward the four-dimensional contextual factors was investigated to overcome the measuring limitations of factors in certain situations that users meet when they use technology. Furthermore, the research model was investigated for causal relationships that reflect possible impacts for finding causalities.

To summarize, the most important contribution of this study is that it empirically examines the mutual relationships between physical situations/circumstantial factors and human activity in the digital service context. This study is expected to contribute to theoretical backgrounds and empirical data analyses, as suggested in this study, could be applied to related further studies.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1. Categories of Context and Use Context

Despite its importance in IS research, the concept of contexts is not well defined (de Reuver et al., 2013; Maguire, 2001). Therefore, many researchers have aimed to define the categorization and measurement of contexts (Dey, 2001; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009;

In computer science research, a large number of papers have proposed context under different terms such as “context,” “context-awareness,” “context-aware computing.” All these terms focus on the contextual issues of using computer systems or applications. In this study, we use the term “context” to represent context-awareness and context-aware computing. Dey (2001) defines context as “any information that can be used to characterize the situation of an entity” (Zimmermann et al., 2007, p. 559). In this definition, an entity can be a person (e.g., user), place, object (e.g., application), or computational thing (Dey, 2001; Lee et al., 2005). This concept is being extended to the current computing environment. The importance of the “user” in mobile computing and the smart IT environment is strongly emphasized so that

when users are using a device, system, or service, all contextual data and factors that may exercise influence over the user of a device, system, or service—directly or indirectly—are considered important (Lee et al., 2005). This contextual information surrounding one’s technology usage, which can help characterize a user’s behavior in terms of the situation, is defined as “use context” (Lee et al., 2005). However, use of this definition has been confusing researchers for a long time, and this study seeks to suggest a classification system based on organizing related research systematically.

To categorize contexts, Schilit et al. (1994) proposed three important aspects of context: (1) where you are, (2) who you are with, and (3) what resources are nearby. Furthermore, they stated that the lighting, noise level, network connectivity, communication costs, communication bandwidth, and social situations are included in the context. Several other studies have proposed that context consists of location, identity, time, temperature, noise, beliefs, desires, commitments, and intentions (see Zimmermann et al., 2007). According to Zimmermann et al. (2007), any context falls into one of five fundamental categories: time, location, activity, relationships, and individuality. Time and location as context information refer to “the spatio-temporal coordinates of the respective entity.” Activity is “all tasks which an entity may be involved in.” Relationships denote “any possible relationship the entity may establish with another entity.” Finally, individuality refers to the “properties and attributes describing the entity itself” (Zimmermann et al., 2007, p. 560). Similarly, Abowd et al. (1999) proposed primary context types of location, identity, time, and activity (see Table 1 for more details).

In several studies, the contexts of specific devices or systems were considered as the use context, and the researchers tried to classify the components of use contexts. Maguire (2001) considered the components of tasks, technical environments, physical environments, and organizational environments. Lee et al. (2005) proposed that the use context of the

mobile Internet comprises emotions, time, movement, physical contexts, and social contexts. De Reuver et al. (2013) stated that the use context includes time, task, social situation, and work (see Table 2 for more details).

From a theoretical point of view, previous studies have thoroughly defined the concept of “context” and presented its precise components. However, there is still a need to to create a classification of related components. Few studies have examined the concept of “use context” in an empirical way, and prior studies have suggested measurements for similar concepts, e.g., situational factors (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). Liu and Li (2011) proposed and examined the role of situational factor on use behavior in mobile gaming. Liu and Li (2011) measured use context as a single construct with the conditions that certain situations are met when users play a mobile game and showed that this construct has a significant impact on mobile game adoption mechanisms, including the perception of usefulness, ease of use, enjoyment, and cognitive concentration, etc., whether directly or indirectly.

These previous studies have clearly highlighted that use contexts need to be measured in a multifaceted way in order to take into considerations of the various components within a broad concept. However, most studies measuring use contexts assume certain situations such as time and locations are met by users of technology (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). In this regard, the categorization of prior use contexts and instruments developed to measure such contexts are too limited in scope to explain important behavior predictors of users. Given the complexity in use contexts and user behavior, the study addressed takes a broader approach by considering and measuring as many important factors as possible that are based upon existing literature and empirical evidence. ## 3. Conceptualization of Use Context Constructs

## 3.1. Activity Theory and Digital Services

In HCI research, activity theory provides an important theoretical background for explaining the use of technologies human behavior (Greenberg, 2001; Kaenampornpan & O’Neill, 2004; Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006; Kuutti, 1995; Nardi, 1995, 1996). Activity theory can diversify research by providing a conceptual framework for the evaluation of human activities (i.e., typing, operating, and watching the computer keyboard, system, and monitor) and a theoretical lens for empirical analyses (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2012).

According to activity theory, the use of technologies is understood as part of human activities (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006). Furthermore, an activity can be explained based on the context (Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006). Activities are always situated in a specific context, without which they would be impossible to understand (Kuutti, 1995). Indeed, previous research has claimed that activities and contexts have an important relationship (Greenberg, 2001; Nardi, 1996). Van der Heijden et al. (2005) suggest that it is essential to have fit between context and user activity to understand more about user behavior on mobile services. If user activity using mobile technology does not fit with user context, the user may evaluate the technology negatively (Van der Heijden et al., 2005). This means that a user can do more activity using a technology when they are in a right use context. Therefore, user activity and context have a very close relationship.

Expanding the notions of activity theory, its applications led to the ideas of activitycentric computing (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006, 2012). According to activity theory, a human activity consists of a subject, an object, and tools. The subject manipulates the object to achieve the desired outcomes by using the tools (Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006). Also, the activity-centric view (computing), which is derived from activity theory, emphasizes the meaningful context of interaction between the real world and subjects (Kaptelinin & Nardi,

2006). This meaningful context is crucial in order to understand concretely what the activities are (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006).

Digital services are those that are acquired and/or subscribed through a digital network over the Internet (Williams et al., 2008). Digital services provide a more interactive and ubiquitous environment than normal services (Williams et al., 2008). Because of the advancement in smartphones and mobile technologies, more offline services are transforming their services into digital services. Examples of such offline services are the taxi and hotel apps. Williams et al. (2008) defined digital service as “an activity or benefit that one party can give to another that is provided through a digital transaction.” Therefore, the use of digital services can be considered to be a human activity. Moreover, this activity is always situated in a specific context and this contextual information is essential for understanding and explaining the activity (Kuutti, 1995; Van der Heijden et al., 2005; Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006). Therefore, the concept of use context includes contextual information that is closely related to a user’s specific activity. Hence, this research takes upon the activity theory and the activity-centric view, which focuses on the relationship between activity and context as well as on the structure of those relationships from a broader perspective. Several works related to computing and computing behaviors employ this theoretical lens (Park et al., 2011, Prekop & Burnett, 2003). Based on the above theoretical background, this study proposes the influencing relationships between the intention to use (i.e., human activity) and the perceived use contexts (i.e., contextual information of activity).

## 3.2. Development of an Analytical Framework

Previous categorizations of context and use context can be summarized as having five major components: location, time, activity, social, and technological (see Tables 1 and 2). However, it is important to carefully examine and understand these five major components based on the definitions of context, user contexts and activity theory rather than just adopting them into our study. Previous literature has inconsistency when an activity is considered to be a context component. There are two different viewpoints for exploring the contexts: agentcentric and activity-centric views (Raghu et al., 2004; Zacarias et al., 2010). Most of the previous literature in Tables 1 and 2 appears to take an agent-centric view. Agent refers to a system, or intelligent/semi-intelligent machine) (Prekop & Burnett, 2003; Zacarias et al., 2010). Activity refers to something being done by the user (Prekop & Burnett, 2003; Zacarias et al., 2010). These two viewpoints can make a big difference in the categorization of context components. As mentioned before, the use of technology is understood as a single activity based on activity theory (Kaptelinin & Nardi, 2006). In the agent-centric view, the context should be understood concerning the entity. Consequently, the activity being done by the agent (i.e., entity) can be considered as core context information. In the context of specific devices or systems, it is logical that what users do (i.e., the activity) should be considered to be one of the major components of context. However, in the activity-centric view, the context should be understood in relation to a single activity. Therefore, in the case of the context of use of digital services (i.e., the context of a single activity), it is logical that the activity interacts with the other use context components.

Furthermore, the activity-centric view is more strictly in accordance with the using a device, system, or service (Lee et al., 2005). On the other hand, the agent-centric view is more in accordance with the definition of context: “any information that can be used to characterize the situation of an entity” (Zimmermann et al., 2007, p. 559).

For this reason, this study proposes four use context core categories, namely time, services. Furthermore, the instruments used to measure the perceived use contexts are developed based on the operational definition of these four use contexts and their components. The four categories of the use context of digital services and use context components used in this research are presented in Figure 2.

The time use contexts include information on the time-of-day during usage, the day of the week, and whether usage is during weekdays or weekends (Dey et al., 1998; Kim, 2012; Lee et al., 2005; Zimmermann et al., 2007). The location use context includes information on the actual use location, indoor or outdoor, public place, movement, congestion, and noise (Kim, 2012; Lee et al., 2005; Schilit et al., 1994). According to Lee et al. (2005) and Kim (2012), actual use location means the physical information that can indicate the current location of user, such as places that can be represented by coordinates, partitioned spaces, etc. While the actual use location is physical spatial information, other components—indoor or outdoor, public place, and congestion—capture information related to spatial composition (Lee et al., 2005; Kim, 2012). Movement and moving lines are also important location use context components, and other elements also can be included such as lighting, temperature, noise, and so on (Kim, 2012; Lee et al., 2005; Schilit et al., 1994). The social use context includes the relationships between users and other social entities such as people, things, information, and social circumstances (Schilit et al., 1994; Zimmermann et al., 2007). In this research, the location of being at home or in the workplace is considered to be a social use context because these places have different social meanings and relationships between colleagues, family, and so on (Kim, 2012; Schilit et al., 1994; Zimmermann et al., 2007). Another social use context is whether people are physically next to the user. Furthermore, special events that occur are considered to be social circumstance information that can be used to express the extraordinary relationships between users and other entities (Dey et al., 1998; Lee et al., 2005; Zimmermann et al., 2007). Finally, network connectivity and connected devices for assisting a user’s activity are considered to be a component of the technological use context (Schilit et al., 1994; Schmidt et al., 1999; Zimmermann et al., 2007).

## 3.3. Belief-Attitude-Intention Framework

In accordance with many psychological theories, human behaviors and activities have certain structures (Kuutti, 1995). Explaining these structures of human behaviors and activities, especially in IT contexts, is often considered as one of the most well-implemented research areas in the information systems (IS) literature (Venkatesh et al., 2003). However, researchers in this area are confronted with several challenges: to select one theoretical model from many for explaining the targeted IT user behavior, and the need to select constructs across the models while ignoring the potential contributions from alternative models (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Hence, there is a need for exploration and development of new constructs and frameworks based on existing theories in order to expand the scope of research and to consider more possibilities. The unpreceded rapid growth in smartphone and mobile technology boom has diversified available devices and services, and these devices and services are influenced by contextual elements such as time, location, social conditions, and technological issues (Kim et al., 2017). However, it is not easy to generalize a specific theory to cover all possible devices and services. Therefore, we need to choose the model that is easiest to generalize among different technologies.

One of the most popular theoretical models for explaining user behavior is the Belief-Attitude-Intention framework that was introduced by Ajzen and Fishbein (1977). Ajzen and Fishbein (1977) found that external factors influence behavioral beliefs about the outcomes linked with a behavior, which in turn forms behavioral attitudes toward a behavior. Behavioral attitude influences behavioral intention and the actual behavior (Ajzen, 2007). This theoretical framework is the foundation for several important theories in the behavioral research field such as the theory of reasoned action, the theory of planned behavior, and the TAM. Among all the theories developed in the IS field based on the concept of the Belief-

Attitude-Intention Framework, TAM is the most widely used research model for understanding and predicting IT user adoption and behavior.

The reason TAM is a famous and widely used model is that it has demonstrated consistency and generalizability through years of research replication in different contexts and with varying technologies. Moreover, in the last few years, well-established IS journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of Association for Information Systems (JAIS), and Information & Management are still publishing papers that use TAM as a general model and overarching theory.

Hong et al. (2014) theorized the context-specific antecedents of TAM. Gefen & Larsen (2017) used TAM to test lexical closeness in survey research. Sun & Fang (2016), in studying mindfulness in technology adoption, used TAM as a theoretical background for the adoption stage of their research model. Hess et al. (2014) and Steelman et al. (2014) used TAM to test reliability generation and the effect of MTurk, which is a crowdsourcing method of data collection. Venkatesh (2000) and Venkatesh & Davis (1996) acknowledged that we need to continue exploring the antecedents and different ways of explaining more thoroughly user acceptance and usage behavior. With the suggestion of Venkatesh (2000), a large amount of behavioral empirical research on digital products and services has been conducted based on TAM (Chung et al., 2014; Ingham et al., 2015). In addition, many studies extend the theoretical concept of TAM in various different contexts such as in the mobile context (Chung et al., 2014; Cyr & Head 2006; Kim & Garrison 2009; Lopez-Nicolas & Molina-Castillo 2008; Mallat et al., 2009), E-Commerce acceptance (Ingham et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2011; Gefen et al., 2003), cloud computing (Shiau & Chau 2016), website dimensionality (Visinescu et al., 2015), and software testing (Wallace & Sheetz 2014). Besides that, Featherman & Pavlou (2003) extended TAM to test the effect of negative utility on e-service adoption while Steelman et al. (2014) used TAM to compare different sampling methods,

focusing specifically on a crowdsourcing method such as MTurk. Based on comprehensive review of literature, we concluded that TAM is the most widely adopted Belief-Attitude-Intention framework in different IT research contexts. We believe it will remain applicable in various research topics in the future. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the impacts of user contexts on TAM.

Because the concept of use context is closely related to perception, cognition, and reasoning of humans (Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006), there are several previous studies that have explored the relationship between contextual factors and user behaviors based on TAM (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; van der Heijden, 2005; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009, Thong et al., 2002). Through this research, we can also contribute by extending the Belief-Attitude-Intention framework in contextual computing context.

## 4.3. Analysis 2: Instrument Development

This section presents the instruments developed and refined for measuring the perceived use contexts. In the game context, 63 measurement items (reflective indices) were developed to measure the perceived use contexts based on several related studies that suggested measurements for situational variables and similar concepts (Mallat et al., 2009; Liu & Li, 2011). Mallat et al. (2009) and Liu & Li (2011) were referenced to develop the format of the questions on situational elements in specific contexts. Indicator indices for measuring the perceptions of contextual factors in a given usage behavior were referenced from de Reuver et al. (2013). Furthermore, the measurement items were developed based on the four core use context categories (time, location, social, and technological) and 14 use context components that are presented in the development of the analytical framework section (see also Figure 4).

In the first phase, the measurement items were re-designed based on their content (content validity analysis based on Polit & Beck (2006)) and extracted values from preanalysis (a pilot test with limited samples), resulting in 34 items that were finally used for the factor analyses. In the second phase, EFA with principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted by using Varimax rotation with an eigenvalue of 1 (Kaiser Criterion) and a visual scree plot to extract the factors. In the PCA, eight variables with statistically sufficient loading values—the .40-.30-.20 rule suggested by Howard (2016)—were extracted (time-ofday, day of the week, physical distraction, location characteristics, social place, occurrence of social relations, connected device, and network connectivity). The results of KMO and Bartlett’s test showed sufficient values (measure of sampling adequacy: 0.793, approx. Chi square: 4945.8, degrees of freedom: 561, significance level: 0.000).

In the third phase, CFA was conducted based on these eight extracted variables. For the sake of simplicity, the model fitness results of eight extracted variables are named as an eight-factor model. The results of the CFA showed sufficient values (x^2/df: 1.83, CFI: 0.92, TLI: 0.90, RMSEA: 0.08). These factors were adopted as the perceived use context variables and verified by using structural equation modeling.

## 5. Research Model and Hypotheses

## 5.1. Belief-Attitude-Intention Framework - TAM

We choose TAM as the overarching research framework to test four different perceived use contexts in this study. TAM is widely acknowledged as one of the most influential and robust models in IS behavioral research. TAM is also important because it can consistently explain a substantial proportion of the variance in user acceptance behavior (Gefen & Larson, 2017). Moreover, it is still popular and the most widely used model in IS because of its simplicity and understandability of the theoretical concepts and applications (King & He, 2006; Gefen & Larson). TAM suggests that behavioral beliefs influence attitude, which affect behavioral intention, while behavioral intention links to actual behavior (Davis, 1989). TAM postulates that IT acceptance is the result of two cognitive behavioral beliefs, which are perceived usefulness and ease of use (Davis, 1989). Perceived ease of use is defined as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free from effort” (Davis, 1989, p.320). Perceived usefulness refers to “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” (Davis, 1989, p.320).

Perceived usefulness is influenced by perceived ease of use and external variables (Davis, 1989), whereas behavioral intention is influenced by both perceived ease of use and usefulness (Davis, 1989). Many studies have empirically validated, applied, and replicated TAM in different research contexts and with varying technologies (Hess et al., 2014; King & He, 2006, Venkatesh, 2000). Based on the evidence from previous studies, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1-1: Attitude toward games will positively influence intention to use games

H1-2: Perceived usefulness will positively influence attitude toward games

H1-3: Perceived ease of use will positively influence attitude toward games

H1-4: Perceived ease of use will positively influence perceived usefulness

## 5.2. Use Contexts

The eight factors for measuring perceived use contexts (see Figure 2) should be viewed as factors that formed the constructs of perceived use contexts, rather than being caused by those constructs. Specifically, these factors are developed based on subcomponents of categories in the theoretical perspective and extracted using factor analysis (dimension reduction technique). Hence, based on the findings of previous studies (Diamantopoulos et al., 2008; Jarvis et al., 2003), they were redefined as four formative second-order constructs: perceived time use, perceived location use, perceived social use, and perceived technological use contexts.

Most studies that have measured use contexts considered certain benchmarks that users met when they use digital services and thus the overall measurement was limited to concepts such as waiting time, crowding, vacancy, and urgency (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). This study examines users’ overall perception of given four-dimensional contextual factors and reflects these definitions in the instruments developed to measure use contexts.

Several studies have suggested that situational factors influence user behavior (van der Heijden, 2005; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009; Thong et al., 2002; Visinescu et al., 2015). Mallat et al. (2009), for example, proposed that use context within a mobile ticket system involves situational factors, such as the lack of alternative payment methods and urgency, affecting the adoption of mobile ticket payments. Users tend to involve more active cognitive processing when they are confronted with new IT artifacts such as mobile ticket services (Rodr´ıguez-Ardura & Meseguer-Artola, 2015).

## 5.2.1 Time and location use contexts

The consumption of internet and mobile services are increasingly dependent on time and location (Balasubramanian et al. 2002; Hong et al., 2014). The location, in other words, spatial condition, greatly influences human behavior (Germain and Bloom, 1999; Balasubramanian et al. 2002). An individual’s emotions and behavior are determined by where they live, work, and commute, which geographical location they are located at any moment, and the type of social space they are involved in (Germain and Bloom, 1999; Balasubramanian et al. 2002). For instance, people who live in cities have better education systems and infrastructure, as well as opportunities for social engagement, than those who live in rural areas. In addition, people selectively use IT based on their location. When an individual works in a company, they need to use a workstation technology like a desktop computer or device that is specially designed for an individual task. While location and space are fixed and can be appropriated, time is a resource that is in one sense unlimited, yet in the context of an individual’s lifespan, it is absolutely a limited resource. Sharp (1981) underscored the importance of time, commenting that “since the scarce resource of time must be spent, a basic problem of human existence is to spend it well, to use it to bring in the greatest return of happiness that can be achieved” (p. 2). Therefore, as an individual living in society, it seems almost commonsense to assume that location preference and time allocation decisions for everyday life are a critical part of one’s happiness and well-being.

Perceived time use contexts are defined in this study as the user’s perceptions system, or service (e.g., time of day and day of the week). Perceived location use contexts refer to the user’s perceptions of location-related contextual factors that may influence him or her when using a device, system, or service (e.g., physical distractions and location characteristics). When users are playing a game, the time and location are critical factors that determine their behavior. For instance, when an employee finishes work and takes a bus home, they may be more relaxed and have higher motivation to play computer games. They may perceive a high usefulness for playing games because doing so helps them to relieve stress from the workday. In other words, users actively seek temporal and spatial changes and conditions when they are using certain IT services in a particular situation. Temporal and spatial changes are core concepts in our use contexts. Rodriguez-Ardura & Meseguer-Artola (2015) suggested a spatial factor influenced behavioral belief in an e-learning context and Kroenung & Eckhardt (2015) suggested that temporal factors influence behavioral beliefs and attitudes in various information systems. Visnescu et al. (2015) suggested temporal factors of user absorption influence perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and the intention to use 2D and 3D website designs. In addition, Liu and Li (2011) also found that time-elapsed feelings influence perceived usefulness and ease of use in the mobile gaming context.

Based on the foregoing discussions, we propose the following hypotheses: H1-5: Perceived time use context will positively influence perceived usefulness H1-6: Perceived time use context will positively influence perceived ease of use

H1-7: Perceived location use context will negatively influence perceived usefulness H1-8: Perceived location use context will negatively influence perceived ease of use

## 5.2.2 Social use contexts

Junglas et al. (2013, p.587) defined the term social as “a characteristic that describes western philosopher Aristotle’s view of man: “Man is by nature a social animal; an individual who is unsocial naturally and not accidentally is either beneath our notice or more than human.” Humans are social animals who cannot live without engagement and interaction with others and as members of one society. Moreover, civilizations and societies have evolved and changed because of social movements and activities. The information systems field was founded on a belief that information systems are a socio-technical system that refers to interrelatedness of humans and systems. These systems can perform well dependent on the operator or user and the organization or environment. Therefore, the social context of users is of great importance when using technology. In this study, we define perceived social use context as the user’s perception of socially related contextual factors, such as type of location and likelihood of social interaction. In other words, social contexts represent the certain situation or conditions of a social space in which an individual engages with others (Junglas et al., 2013). A single context can create various emotions and feelings in different users, as well as encourage differing behaviors toward the same object. For instance, when an individual plays a game at their home with their family, they may feel that the game is a useful tool providing a better option for passing the time. On the contrary, when an individual plays a game in a work place, this behavior will be considered a work ethic issue and perhaps an attitudinal issue with their boss. Therefore, an individual’s attention to their social context can influence perceived usefulness. In addition, people may feel that playing games is easier when they are around skilled gamers and in a comfortable place where they can focus on the game. In a study by Thong et al. (2002), they proposed a relationship between a server’s organizational contextual factors and TAM in the context of digital libraries. For social, and technological contexts, Xu and Yuan (2009) found that mobile technology and social urgency influence consumers’ behavioral beliefs. Junglas et al (2013) emphasized that social conditions and sociability are key factors that influence an individual’s behavioral beliefs, as well as their actual behavior.

Based on the discussions, the following hypotheses are proposed: H1-9: Perceived social use context will positively influence perceived usefulnes H1-10: Perceived social use context will positively influence perceived ease of use

## 5.2.2 Technological use contexts

Not everyone with reliable access to computers or mobile phones will use the Internet or digital services every day and anywhere. Technology access or availability is the key foundation for people’s daily media consumption and goals toward digital services. The current “network society” is possible because of technology development and the technological paradigm of informatization (Castells & Cardoso, 2006). The availability of and access to technology in today’s society have been widely studied in digital divide research (Chang et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2016). In the information systems field, technology access and quality are significant factors influencing an individual user’s beliefs and behavior (Chang et al. 2015; Chang et al., 2016; Wei et al., 2011; Wixom and Todd, 2005). Technological use contexts are also linked to the technological conditions and situations (i.e., LTE/3G, Wifi connection, available digital devices) that an individual faces when they try to use digital services. Perceived technological use contexts refer to user’s perceptions of influential technology-related contextual factors such as connected devices and network

connectivity. When an individual wants to play a game, especially on the internet or a social game, they need network connection to access the game server and interact with other players. The game user also needs the proper device and accessories that will enable them to play their game of choice. Chang et al., (2016) found that technological access strongly influences perceived usefulness and an individual’s skill access influence perceived ease of influenced the user’s perception of both usefulness and ease of use.

Based on the foregoing discussions, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1-11: Perceived technological use context will positively influence perceived usefulness H1-12: Perceived technological use context will positively influence perceived ease of use

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

The objective of this research is to elucidate the use context of digital services and investigate its causal relationships through the instrument developments. In spite of the importance of context in IT environments, there is little empirical evidence and no well defined concepts on the use context of technologies. In particular, there are two major problems based on current academic debates that must be overcome.

First, a serious theoretical gap in the relationship between the use context and user behavior has been revealed. Therefore, this study provided a theoretical basis for this relationship based on activity theory and the activity-centric view (Greenberg, 2001; Kofod-Petersen & Cassens, 2006; Kuutti, 1995; Nardi, 1996).

The second problem is the absence of instruments for measuring usage contexts. Several prior studies have suggested measurements for similar concepts, e.g., situational factors (Dabholkar & Bagozzi, 2002; Liu & Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009). However, in these studies, only the influences of variables that were limited to the specific situation were confirmed, and the user’s overall perception toward use context in the broader scope was not investigated. Therefore, this study suggested and developed instruments for measuring use contexts. One of the major findings of this research is that the perceived use context can be measured by time-of-day, day of the week, physical distraction, location characteristics, social place and occurrence of social relations, connected device, and network connectivity. These variables were grouped into four higher order constructs that represent four core use context categories: time, location, social, and technological.

To find stronger and more robust explanatory causal relationships, we evaluated the research model that reflected possible impacts on users’ behavior. The results showed that users’ intention to use games is affected by the perceived time use, perceived location use, and perceived technological use contexts.

First, we found that the perceived time use contexts have positive effects on both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. This finding suggests that if users recognize time-related factors, they feel that games are useful and convenient to play. For example, many people use game services for leisure purposes in the evening and at nighttime. Users may feel usefulness and ease of use toward games when they perceive a vacancy to play games in their spare time. These positive perceptions might also be related to a positive

attitude and willingness to play games. However, the perceived location use contexts can also have a direct negative effect on perceived ease of use, as users recognize the location environment as a restriction when playing games. What we found interesting here is that despite the expansion of the mobile game market, users are still constrained by the concept of location because users’ game-playing can be restricted in outdoor places and on transportation because of physical distraction issues such as congestion or noise.

Hence, according to the results above, the positive and negative effects of perceived use contexts can coexist. These results suggest that contextual perceptions are not only of motivational factors but also hygiene factors. In terms of the motivational (i.e., excitement) factors (Matzler et al., 2004), users are tempted to play games in their spare time because of the usefulness and ease of playing. In terms of the hygiene (i.e., basic) factors (Matzler et al., 2004), if users perceive that their current location has restrictions, the ease of playing games can diminish.

Perceived social use contexts may not affect perceived usefulness or perceived ease of use because environmental and physical social factors, such as social place and the occurrence of social relations, are affecting other hedonic factors such as perceived enjoyment and flow (Jungals et al. 2013). Junglas et al. (2013) suggested that humans have a tendency to socialize with other people for pleasurable, rather than utilitarian, reasons. Simmel & Hughes (1949, p.254) described the reality of social life as the process of association with value and satisfaction. They further insisted (1949, p.255-8) that the specific social conditions and sociability are the “sociological play-form” and “contingent upon the joy of others.” Games have a hedonic purpose and can affect various emotional elements. Therefore, generally, utilitarian-related factors are not influenced by them. Future research needs to investigate the effect of use contexts on pleasure-related constructs. In addition, these factors did not have a negative effect on the cognitive concepts directly affecting the

intention or attitude toward the game. This result shows that perceived social use context is not hygiene (i.e., basic) factor that must be satisfied like perceived location use context and is not important enough to influence the perception of the game if users sometimes play games at school or with friends. Furthermore, many studies have already shown that game use is influenced by social influences and social norms (Hsu & Lu, 2004). It seems that the negative sociability.

However, the perceived technological use contexts have direct positive effects on perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. Most games are possible to play only with devices that support the game, rather than on multiple devices. There are a relatively large number of games supported on both PCs and consoles. However, in most cases, users will have to pay twice to play on both devices. Furthermore, because of the differences in operating systems and device specifications, mobile games and PC games are clearly distinguished. This means that when we want to play a specific game, the devices that support the game are also very important. Furthermore, most games need an Internet connection for sending and receiving game data from servers because users often play games with desktops and smartphones. Therefore, if users value technology-related contextual factors, they feel that games are useful and convenient to play.

The most important contribution of this study is that it empirically examines the effect of the physical situation/circumstantial factors and human activity in a digital game context. Furthermore, it suggests a multidimensional use context perspective based on strict theoretical backgrounds and empirical data analyses. In previous empirical research, use context was treated as a one-dimensional construct to measure a certain situation or condition that might be related to the target technology (Liu and Li, 2011; Mallat et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2015). However, most previous context research emphasized use contexts as having a

multidimensional nature, and it has multiple dimension in which context is characterized (Zimmerman et al., 2007). Therefore, an important contribution of this study is to introduce the four multidimensional constructs that embrace eight sub-dimensions of the use context concept. This study provides four types of new use contexts (i.e., time, location, social, and technological) based on the systematic classification of prior literature and empirical evidence. Current forms of constructs can be adapted by any information systems research to measure the effect of use context on various technology adoption and user behaviors. For business practitioners, this study underscores the importance of time, location, and technological use contexts on service adoption. For instance, service providers need to carefully monitor the user’s time and location context when they are operating the service. Based on these observations, they can provide customized functions, promotions, and alerts to maximize usage of service and user satisfaction. At the same time, service providers need to carefully monitor the technological challenges that users face with their service. If there is a limited network connection, providers should expand their coverage to ensure no difficulties in using the service will occur.

Furthermore, we found that games are most related to use context components based on previous analyses (i.e., CA) and data (Figure 3, Table 3, and Table A1-2), and so we conducted this study in the game context. This research is the first study to measure the perception of use context in game usage behavior based on systematic frames and various factors, and it could be expanded to apply to other adoption studies for services such as information content and video/movies. The usage context of each digital service varies in the exploratory analysis, but whether the user perception of these usage contexts has a positive or negative effect on service use can be investigated with reference to the model of this study. Furthermore, the instruments for measuring perceived use contexts in this study can be transferred to diverse research targets beyond simple situational variables, which is suitable

for certain situations when users use a digital service. Hence, this research not only sheds light on previously ill-defined academic concepts of use contexts (de Reuver et al., 2013; Maguire, 2001) in the behavioral MIS research field to explain IS user behavior but also develops new instruments and research models in the broader perspective to overcome the limitations of existing measurement tools and models.

Another significant aim of these research results is to provide managerial and practical implications for companies and industries. Understanding use contexts is considered to be fundamental for improving IT products from a business perspective. Investigating the use context of IT products is thus practically important for two reasons. First, it may enable developers and designers of IT products to produce innovative products based on the special nature of the use context. Second, it can provide a clearer understanding of use contexts for developers and designers, which could lead to the selection and determination of important use contexts that should be considered in the development of their products. This kind of approach can provide significant opportunities to improve performance and increase users positive experience and satisfaction, offering clear market advantages to IT companies.
