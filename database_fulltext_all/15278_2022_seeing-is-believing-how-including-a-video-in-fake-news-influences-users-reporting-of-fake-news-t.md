# SEEING IS BELIEVING? HOW INCLUDING A VIDEO IN FAKE NEWS INFLUENCES USERS’ REPORTING OF FAKE NEWS TO SOCIAL MEDIA PLATFORMS<sup>1</sup>

Shuting (Ada) Wang Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York New York, NY, U.S.A. {shuting.wang@baruch.cuny.edu}

Min-Seok Pang Fox School of Business, Temple University Philadelphia, PA, U.S.A. {minspang@temple.edu}

Paul A. Pavlou Bauer College of Business, University of Houston Houston, TX, U.S.A. {pavlou@bauer.uh.edu}

Social media platforms, such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter, are combating the spread of fake news by developing systems that allow their users to report fake news. However, it remains unclear whether these reporting systems that harness the “wisdom of the crowd” are effective. Notably, concerns have been raised that the popularity of videos may hamper users’ reporting of fake news. The persuasive power of videos may render fake news more deceptive and less likely to be reported in practice. However, this is neither theoretically nor empirically straightforward, as videos not only affect users’ ability to detect fake news, but also impact their willingness to report and their engagement (i.e., likes, shares, and comments) which would further spread fake news. Using a unique dataset from a leading social media platform, we empirically examine how including a video in a fake news post affects the number of users reporting the post to the platform. Our results indicate that including a video significantly increases the number of users reporting the fake news post to the social media platform. Additionally, we find that the sentiment intensity of the fake news text content, especially when the sentiment is positive, attenuates the effect of including a video. Randomized experiments and a set of mediation analyses are included to uncover the underlying mechanisms. We contribute to the information systems literature by examining how social media platforms can leverage their users to report fake news, and how different formats (e.g., videos and text) of fake news interact to influence users’ reporting behavior. Social media platforms that seek to leverage the “wisdom of the crowd” to combat the proliferation of fake news should consider both the popularity of videos and the role of text sentiment in fake news to adjust their strategies.

Keywords: Fake news, users’ reporting of fake news, video, text sentiment, social media

## Introduction

In the new era of social media, internet users have gained the ability and the means to publish an ever-increasing number of news stories to a broad public. This deluge of news, unfiltered by professional media outlets, places the burden on the public to evaluate what news to believe. Unfortunately, this has made social media a fruitful soil for the proliferation of fake news (Kalsnes, 2018; Lazer et al., 2018). In response, social media platforms, such as Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter, have introduced systems for users to report fake news. For example, if a Facebook user thinks a news post on the platform is fake, the user can report the news post to the platform by clicking the report button beside the post and then selecting the reason as “it is a false news story.” This allows Facebook to leverage the “wisdom of the crowd” by making the public active rather than passive users in detecting and reporting fake news. However, to our knowledge, whether this reporting system is effective remains unknown. Critically, the popularity of videos on social media may hamper the systems’ effectiveness, since the use of auditory and visual cues in videos may make fake news more convincing to ordinary users (Berkowitz & Schwartz, 2016).

As videos have become the preferred medium for online content publishers, fake news on social media has shifted from simple text or image posts to videos (Bleed, 2005). While many users believe that videos are not manipulative, fake news producers are increasingly employing videos to mislead users. Nevertheless, it is ambiguous whether and how videos influence users’ reporting of fake news, which is an artifact of users’ ability to detect fake news, willingness to report fake news, and engaging in activities (i.e., likes/comments/shares) that may further spread fake news. First, as mentioned earlier, including a video may make it more difficult for users to discern whether the news is real or fake (Nash et al., 2009), thus reducing the frequency with which fake news is being reported. Second, if a user does detect fake news, seeing a video may also increase the user’s reporting behavior by raising the user’s willingness to report—as the power of visual evidence may increase the perception of the risk that fake news presents to the social media community (Berkowitz & Schwartz, 2016). Third, videos may increase the number of users reporting fake news by leading to more shares, comments, and likes on a fake news post, thus exposing the fake news story to a larger audience that can also detect and report the fake news (Kim et al., 2018; Tandoc et al., 2019; Tandoc et al., 2018). Given the mixed effect of videos, we empirically examine the following research question: What is the impact of including a video in a fake news post on the number of users reporting the post to social media platforms?

Further, motivated by the role that sentiment (i.e., attitudes toward a situation or event) can play in individual judgment (Rout et al., 2018) and the fact that fake news producers frequently use affective words to evoke a certain sentiment (Bakir & McStay, 2018), we ask a second research question: How does the sentiment intensity of the text of a fake news post moderate the impact of the inclusion of a video in the post? While sentiment can be elicited through different content (e.g., text, images, videos), we follow the literature on fake news detection (Ajao et al., 2019; Thelwall et al., 2010; Wang, 2017; Zhou et al., 2020) by focusing on the sentiment in the text of news posts, which can be directly measured by existing text mining techniques (e.g., nltk.sentiment packages in Python). Specifically, we measure sentiment intensity using the strength (i.e., the absolute value) of the positive or negative emotion expressed in the text content of each fake news post (Bravo-Marquez et al., 2013).

To examine these two research questions, we leverage a longitudinal dataset of around 100,000 fake news posts circulated on Weibo, a social media platform in China that is similar to Twitter. We focus on nonpolitical fake news posts, as they are generally less controversial and more likely to be correctly reported by social media users. Weibo is an ideal setting for our empirical study as it allows users to report fake news in a manner similar to that of other major social media platforms (e.g., Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter). But, unlike other platforms, Weibo publishes all report-related information (e.g., detailed information about the reported posts, the users who report fake news to the platform, the reasons that users report the posts as fake news, and other details). The uniqueness of Weibo allows us to collect rich data on users’ reporting behavior and the posting history of each instance of reported fake news, including the format in which each fake news story was posted (e.g., with or without a video), the different user accounts that were used to post each fake news item, and the different times at which each fake news was posted. These data enable us to execute pooled regressions and a multitude of robustness checks to answer our research questions. To uncover the underlying mechanisms, we conduct mediation analysis and randomized experiments with English fake news on Twitter and Chinese fake news on Weibo.

Our study yields several interesting findings. First, including a video in a fake news post has a positive effect on the number of users reporting the post to the platform. Second, the sentiment intensity of the text of a fake news post attenuates the positive effect of including a video. Third, the sentiment intensity of the text attenuates the effect of including a video to a greater extent if the sentiment of the text is very positive and to a lesser extent if it is very negative. In an attempt to tease out the underlying mechanisms, we conducted additional experiments and incorporated several mediation analyses. Results suggest that including a video (1) decreases the number of users reporting a fake news post by undermining users’ ability to detect fake news, (2) increases the number of users reporting by raising their willingness to report, and (3) increases the number of users reporting by facilitating their engagement with the post.

Our study contributes to the information systems (IS) literature in three main ways. First, although IS scholars have examined many policies taken by social media platforms to combat the proliferation of fake news (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Kim et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2018), our study is among the first to examine a policy that relies on the “wisdom of the crowd” to report fake news, with notable exceptions (Kim & Walker, 2020). Second, although videos are increasingly used to deliver news on social media (Hjorth & Hinton, 2019), to the best of our knowledge, very few if any studies in IS have examined how videos influence users’ reporting of fake news.

Our study fills this gap. Third, this work explores how the sentiment intensity of the text of fake news interacts with videos to influence users’ reporting behavior. While the literature suggests that negative sentiment is generally stronger and more salient (Pham, 2007) than a positive one, our study finds that positive sentiment is more likely to enhance the role of videos in clouding users’ judgment and accordingly their reporting of fake news. These findings suggest that social media platforms need to better understand the interaction between videos and text sentiment in users’ reporting behavior and better leverage users to detect fake news correspondingly.

## Theoretical Development

Before theoretically discussing the impact of including videos in fake news, we outline the scope of our study as follows. First, we focus on videos rather than static images or sounds, as videos have become the most popular media format for online posts and constitute a more sophisticated format that combines the effects of images and sounds (Artz & Society, 2000; Vallet et al., 2015). Second, our study concentrates on the inclusion of videos (or not), and we do not differentiate videos by their quality or sentiment. Our empirical models, along with a set of robustness checks, make us believe this is not a major limitation (more discussion below). Third, our study focuses on users’ reporting of fake news to social media platforms. While we believe that most users do not randomly report regular posts as fake news,<sup>2</sup> users may inadvertently report authentic news as fake. This issue is beyond the scope of our work. Our study aims to understand users’ reporting behavior as a means for social media platforms to find potential fake news for further investigation.

## The Impact of Including Videos in Fake News Posts

Based on the literature, we propose three channels through which including a video in a fake news post may influence the number of users reporting the post (Figure 1): (1) users’ (i.e., post audience) ability to detect fake news (Xiao et al., 2019), (2) users’ willingness to report fake news (Tolsma et al., 2012), and (3) users’ engagement with the fake news post and thus the size of the audience (Schreiner et al., 2019). We discuss each of the three proposed channels below.

## Users’ Ability to Detect Fake News

Including a video in a fake news post could decrease the number of users reporting the post by inhibiting their ability to detect fake news. Videos are often considered concrete and immutable representations of reality, and people often accept videos as evidence to support the credibility of news stories (Akehurst et al., 2001). The persuasive power of videos has led fake news producers to exploit videos in their fake news posts by, for example, manipulating important messages (e.g., time, location, conversation, environment, and involved subjects) to mislead users into believing the fake news.

In general, videos in fake news can be created in three ways. First, fake news producers can borrow videos from similar events. For example, a fake news post in our sample claims that a flood occurred in China but includes video footage of flood damage in Vietnam. Second, the producers can cut or reorganize clips to present incomplete information and bias viewers. For example, in March 2020, a manipulated video of then-US presidential candidate Joe Biden went viral on Twitter. The video clip stated: “We can only re-elect Donald Trump.” It turned out to be fake news, as it did not include the rest of the sentence from the speech: “We can only reelect Donald Trump if, in fact, we get engaged in this circular firing squad here.”<sup>3</sup> Third, producers with advanced technical skills can alter the content in existing videos, including conversations and involved persons (Plaufcan, 2010). For example, a video of Nancy Pelosi, the Speaker of the U.S. House of Representatives, in which she looked convincingly drunk, spread rapidly across social media in 2019. The video was actually created by slowing another video down to about 75% of its normal speed and raising her voice pitch to keep it sounding natural. As many people still believe that videos are recorded by cameras and that cameras do not lie (Walther et al., 2001), such videos are often considered “evidence” to support the events reported in the fake news post (Hsieh & Chen, 2011).

One might argue that although fake news producers are unlikely to use low-quality videos in posts, videos in fake news are rarely perfect. If users can spot manipulation in the videos, they may detect the presence of fake news. However, social media users are easily distracted: many of them only watch the titles of online news stories or the first few seconds of a video before making a judgment (Statistic Brain, 2015), as our experiments empirically demonstrate. Users generally believe that videos accurately capture what happens in the real world, which makes them less vigilant.

Users who casually watch a video are more likely to trust the video and believe the fake news. Even those who have prior knowledge of the events or common sense that can disprove the fake news could be lulled into a false sense of security because of the existence of videos in news posts. Worse, some may even start questioning the validity of their prior knowledge because of the norm of “seeing is believing.” From this perspective, the existence of “video evidence” may strongly support the authenticity of other parts of the fake news post (e.g., text) and undermine users’ ability to detect the post as fake news (Hsieh & Chen, 2011).

## Users’ Willingness to Report Fake News

We next theorize that including a video may increase the number of users reporting a fake news post by raising the willingness of users who have detected the fake news to report it. Reporting fake news is not directly remunerated, as most social media platforms do not materially reward or recognize those who report fake news. Yet many users volunteer to do so, as people often feel intrinsically motivated to share knowledge with their community (Burtch et al., 2018; Hossain, 2012; Zhang & Zhu, 2006). For example, many users contribute information to Wikipedia without any material rewards because they desire to contribute meaningfully to society (Zhang & Zhu, 2006). Likewise, many others voluntarily write online reviews to share their experience with a product or service because they want to help others (Lafky & Behavior, 2014). From this perspective, if users believe a fake news post could deceive people they care about (e.g., themselves and their peers on the platform), they may report the post (Rafaeli & Ariel,

2008; Zhang & Zhu, 2006) with the expectation that the fake news will be flagged or publicly corrected by social media platforms.

As mentioned earlier, including a video in a fake news post could make fake news appear more believable. Combined with the fact that videos can easily go viral on social media (Scott, 2009), the inclusion of a video may indicate a higher risk of the fake news misleading and deceiving a larger audience, raising the value of correcting the misinformation and increasing users’ willingness to report the fake news (Hsieh & Chen, 2011).

## Users’ Engagement with Fake News

Including a video in a fake news post could also increase the number of users reporting the post by facilitating user engagement (i.e., likes, shares, and comments), which spreads the post to a larger audience. This new wider audience may comprise more users who can detect fake news and report it to the social media platform.

Social media users are more likely to engage with posts that have videos than posts with only text or images (Pittman & Reich, 2016). In general, videos present information more vividly (Rainie et al., 2012). With a combination of text, visual effects, and sound effects, videos attract users’ attention and stimulate their imagination, influencing their feelings and emotions (Hsieh et al., 2012). Thus, on social media, users are more likely to engage with video content (Berger & Milkman, 2012). For example, the number of shares generated by posts with videos is 12 times larger than other posts.<sup>4</sup> When fake news stories with videos receive more shares, likes, and comments, they are further spread across users’ social networks, leading to a larger audience that could detect and report the fake news to the social media platform.

Summarizing these theoretical arguments, the impact of including a video in a fake news post on the number of users reporting the post is theoretically ambiguous. On the one hand, including a video can reduce the number of users reporting the fake news, as it may lower users’ ability to detect fake news. On the other hand, including a video may increase the number of users reporting the fake news because videos may raise users’ willingness to report and encourage their engagement in exposing the fake news post to more users who can also detect and report the (fake news) post. These channels are not mutually exclusive, and the goal of this work is to empirically identify which effect prevails in practice.

## Moderating Role of Sentiment Intensity

We next examine how sentiment intensity (i.e., the absolute value of the strength of a positive or negative sentiment) of the text of fake news posts moderates the impact of the inclusion of a video. Following the fake news detection literature (Ajao et al., 2019; Wang, 2017; Zhou et al., 2020), we focus on the role of sentiment in the content of fake news text, instead of the content of the videos per se.<sup>5</sup> Text sentiment can be directly measured by widely used text mining techniques, whereas, to our knowledge, there are no accepted standardized data mining tools to measure sentiment in videos. Because of these challenges and the inability to accurately measure sentiment in videos, which is the combined effect of the text, narration, sounds, and music within the videos, our focus on the text can provide more practical implications for social media platforms.

## Moderating Role of Sentiment Intensity

Given the three channels through which videos can influence users’ reporting of fake news, we discuss how the sentiment intensity of the text can moderate the effect of videos via each of these channels.

First, sentiment intensity could deepen the negative impact of videos on users’ ability to detect fake news (Brosius et al., 1996; Walma van der Molen & Media, 2001; Zhu et al., 2007). Theories on emotions and cognitive reasoning suggest that users are less likely to properly exercise reasoning under the influence of emotions (Blanchette & Leese, 2010). Reasoning is a process that allows people to draw conclusions from inferences (Wilson, 1991). However, this process can be interrupted by a strong sentiment that reduces people’s ability to accurately make inferences or logically link one or more inferences to a certain rational conclusion (Lance & Tanesini, 2004). Specifically, a high level of autonomic arousal associated with a strong sentiment impairs working memory capacity (Darke, 1988). A reduction in such mental work capacity hinders cognitive reasoning, which includes people’s ability to recall and organize information. This effect increases the time needed for users to make logical inferences and renders users unable to follow a general logic or structure to solve analogical problems (Pham, 2007).

In the context of fake news posts on social media, text content can often express the author’s sentiment, which may be contagious to readers (Calvo & Mac Kim, 2013). The sentiment interferes with readers’ rationality, as readers often unconsciously regard their emotional reactions as cues to assess a particular situation (Elster, 1996). Therefore, when fake news producers use affective words (e.g., “shocking secrets,” “surprising discovery,” and “very dangerous”) (Bakir & McStay, 2018), social media users’ immediate emotional responses to these words may work as a shortcut for judgment or be cognitively absorbed into their evaluation process of the news content (Shivhare & Khethawat, 2012). In this situation, users may be less able to spot video manipulation or contradiction, increasing the chance that they will believe the fake news (Zentner et al., 2008).

Second, sentiment intensity may undermine the positive impact of videos on users’ willingness to report fake news by shifting the population of users who can detect fake news. Willingness to report fake news only matters to those who have detected it in the first place. Compared to those who can detect fake news with a weak sentiment, those who can detect fake news with a strong sentiment have a different degree of willingness to report the fake news. As mentioned earlier, a strong text sentiment heavily interrupts users’ rational thinking abilities (Blanchette & Leese, 2010). Yet some users may still be able to detect fake news with a strong text sentiment in two ways. One way is if a flaw in the fake news post is so obvious to these users that they would not need sound reasoning to detect it. For example, if a user previously saw a fake news item that was publicly corrected in the media, the user should be able to detect the news item as fake without conscious reasoning, no matter how deceiving it may be. The other way is when such users are highly rational and unlikely to be influenced by text sentiment to the degree of misjudgment (Parfit, 2001). Given that people often see their judgments as relatively common (Krueger et al., 1994; Solan et al., 2008), users who detect fake news in both ways would tend to assume that other users have similar abilities to detect fake news or rationally analyze all the facts to arrive at the same conclusion, leading them to believe that videos in fake news are less likely to deceive other users. In other words, when fake news has a stronger text sentiment, the population of users who can detect fake news may be different. For these users, including a video would be less effective in increasing their willingness to report the fake news to the social media platform.

Third, sentiment intensity could attenuate the positive impact of videos on users’ engagement with fake news. As mentioned earlier, videos in fake news are likely to increase user engagement by eliciting users’ feelings and emotions (Hsieh et al., 2012). This effect overlaps with that of the text sentiment. When the text sentiment is of strong intensity and evokes high-arousal emotions, such as anger and happiness, there is less room for the video to further enhance users emotional arousal (e.g., an image or music in the video is unlikely to make an already extremely sad user feel even sadder). In other words, strong text sentiment and videos serve as substitutes for each other. As such, a strong text sentiment may cannibalize the effect of the video on increasing user engagement. Consequently, when fake news posts exhibit a stronger text sentiment, we would expect to see the inclusion of a video to be less effective in increasing user engagement with the fake news on social media.

In sum, the above discussions indicate that the sentiment intensity of the text could negatively moderate the effect of including a video on the number of users reporting a fake news post by: (1) intensifying the negative effect of including a video on users’ ability to detect the post as fake news, (2) weakening the positive effect of including a video on users’ willingness to report the post, and (3) cannibalizing the proposed positive effect of videos on users’ engagement with the post.

## Moderating Role of Positive versus Negative Sentiment Intensity

Next, we discuss how the moderating effect of the sentiment intensity of the text differs between positive versus negative sentiment. First, the sentiment intensity of the text would be expected to moderate the effect of videos on users’ ability to detect fake news to a greater extent when the sentiment is positive and to a lesser extent when the sentiment is negative. The preceding discussions suggest that a strong sentiment can bias users’ judgment of videos in two ways: (1) weakening their ability to engage in cognitive reasoning (e.g., impairing their working memory capacity) (Shivhare & Khethawat, 2012), and (2) promoting their deployment of immediate emotional responses as a shortcut for judgment (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007). While both positive and negative sentiments lower people’s ability to engage in cognitive reasoning, positive sentiment promotes whereas negative sentiment inhibits users’ adoption of immediate responses in judgment (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007). Specifically, positive sentiment (e.g., happiness and joy) usually indicates an easy environment where users are less likely to be vigilant and motivated to engage in effortful processing (Lambie, 2009; Schwarz, 2002). In contrast, a negative sentiment (e.g., fear and worry) signals that a problem exists, triggering defensive mechanisms (e.g., attempts to deny or avoid the problem) and resulting in more thorough scrutiny by users. In other words, positive sentiment prompts less thorough and more intuitive processing, whereas negative sentiment prompts more logical reasoning (Browne et al., 2010; Koenig-Lewis et al., 2014).

In the context of social media, when people see news posts that elicit a negative sentiment such as anger, fear, and worry, they are likely to perceive possible danger and uncertainty. When the negative sentiment is strong, users often seek to mitigate the emotional distress by invalidating the danger and uncertainty in the reported event. Their emotional distress will be reduced if they can deny the authenticity of the news that disturbs their emotional calm, or if they believe they can avoid these problems in the future by familiarizing themselves with the situations described in the news posts (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007). For example, when users see negative news about food safety on social media, their desire to protect themselves or others from consuming unsafe food motivates them to inspect the news post to identify which food or brand is involved. If a video is included in the post, it invites more scrutiny from users, increasing the chance that users may identify a potential manipulation or contradiction (Kolodzy, 2006; van der Kaa & Krahmer, 2014). In contrast, when people see a fake news post that elicits a positive sentiment such as joy or gratitude, they would feel more relaxed, leading them to exert less effort to evaluate the video or seek to invalidate the news post (Lee et al., 2005). For example, when users see positive news about a food harvest, the lack of danger and uncertainty is likely to relax users, making them less willing to conduct a cognitively costly analysis of the authenticity of the news post, and making them more likely to pursue their immediate emotional response after reading the (fake) news post. Hence, a strongly positive sentiment of a text enhances the effect of videos on reducing users’ ability to detect a post as fake news to a larger extent than a strongly negative sentiment.

Second, given the different abilities of users to detect fake news with positive versus negative sentiments, we expect to see different users who can detect fake news and different levels of willingness to report the fake news. As previously discussed, a strong sentiment interrupts users’ sound reasoning (Blanchette & Leese, 2010), which shifts the population who can detect fake news to those who see obvious flaws in the post or to those who are strongly rational and unmoved by the sentiment. Naturally, when positive sentiment interrupts users’ sound reasoning more strongly than negative sentiment (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007), this shift would also be larger, meaning that the users who detect fake news are more likely to be those who can see obvious flaws or are strongly rational. As people often think that other users have similar judgment and behaviors (Krueger et al., 1994; Solan et al., 2008), these users would tend to consider the additional video in the fake news riskless. Thus, including videos in a fake news post may be less likely to increase the willingness to report among users who can detect fake news with a strongly positive sentiment than among users who can detect fake news with a strongly negative sentiment.

In sum, while the prior literature suggests that both positive and negative sentiments cannibalize the effect of videos on facilitating user engagement (Calvo & Mac Kim, 2013),<sup>6</sup> a negative sentiment of the text triggers defense mechanisms and evokes motivations to carefully investigate the reported event (Dahlin et al., 2018; Rausch et al., 2017), leading to a smaller decrease in users’ ability to detect a post as fake news, and a larger increase in the willingness of users who have detected the fake news to report. Given these reasons, we posit that the moderating role of sentiment intensity in attenuating the effect of videos is stronger for positive sentiment and weaker for negative sentiment.

## Context, Data, and Variable Descriptions

## Context

To empirically examine our research questions, we leveraged data from Weibo, a leading microblogging platform in China. Users can browse, post, share, like, or comment on posts or send messages to other users on this social media platform. In addition, Weibo allows users to report fake news using a system similar to that of other major social media platforms, such as Facebook and Twitter, and it publishes rules for judging fake news on its website.<sup>7</sup> If a user considers a news post to be fake, the user can report it to the platform by clicking a button beside the post and selecting the reason for reporting it as “fake news.” The resemblance of this reporting system to other platforms makes our findings generalizable to other settings.

Weibo is an ideal setting for our study given its transparency regarding its reporting system. In particular, the platform publishes all the details of the posts reported, including the user who reported the post, the user that was reported, the number of occurrences of the same post being reported, the time of the reports, time of the posting, the content of the post, and evidence that supports or disproves the authenticity of the post on both sides. This feature not only enabled us to collect data on fake news posts that were reported to Weibo by users but also allowed us to collect data on fake news posts circulated on the social media platform that were not reported. More explanations can be found below.

## Data

We collected our data in January 2020. We first collected all fake news posts that were reported to Weibo before January 2020. Since one fake news post can be reported by multiple users, we collected a total of about 36,000 fake news posts that were reported about 470,000 times as of January 2020; around 28,000 of these posts had unique text content. We next collected posts of these fake news items that were not reported. To do so, we retrieved unreported posts by searching for posts that had the same text content as any of the fake news items collected in the first step. Considering that a minor difference in the text content can often lead to a different meaning and sentiment, we only included posts if their text content exactly matched any post that was reported as fake news.<sup>8</sup> Still, there were many duplicate fake news posts with the same text, with or without videos, posted by different accounts at different times. For example, as shown in Figure A1 in the Appendix, one fake news post stated: “According to a CCTV report, Funing County in Yancheng City of Jiangsu Province was hit by a severe rainstorm and a 12-level tornado. This is the most severe rainstorm in the history of the nation.” Four posts with the same text were published on June 24, 2016; among these, one included two photos, one included a video, and two had text only. One potential explanation for different fake news posts using the same text content is that writing fake news requires significant time and work but copying existing fake news can draw attention and clicks with minimal effort. In light of the concern that some posts might be created by computer algorithm-controlled bots (Shao et al., 2017), we randomly checked 100 fake news posters in our sample. As most of these accounts were found to have real followers who organically interacted with these accounts’ posts, presumably because of the type of fake news we include in the sample,<sup>9</sup> we believe that the concern of bots is immaterial for our study.

To ensure robustness, we added additional restrictions to the sample selection. First, when collecting fake news posts that were not reported, we only include posts in which the text content exactly matched a reported fake news post. For instance, to find the unreported posts associated with a reported post that stated: “A news presenter of Handan TV station suddenly encountered a heart attack at work, only 26 years old,” we searched the text content with the quotation mark operator (exact match) and excluded posts from the search results if they had a different length than the reported post. The spaces within the text and the source of videos (if any) listed after the camera symbol at the end of posts were excluded from the length calculation. Second, shared posts were excluded from our sample, as in most cases only original posts are reported. Third, we excluded fake news posts that were posted after November 2019 to allow a reasonable period (60 days) for users to report them. We believe that a 60-day window is a reasonable threshold, as our data show that 95% of fake news posts are reported within 60 days of being published. Fourth, given that social media users are generally more familiar with events relevant to their daily lives and are more likely to accurately and responsibly disprove fake news related to such daily events, we focused only on fake news related to societal issues and excluded posts related to politics and the government from our sample. With the above restrictions, our final sample included 100,824 posts of 10,696 unique fake news items in total.

We recognize some limitations in our sampling. First, unreported fake news posts that did not exactly match the text content of the reported posts were not included in our sample. We do so to ensure that the effect of videos could be tested when the other content of the post remained the same. As we see no clear indication of the incidence of this issue being related to our explanatory variable (i.e., whether a fake news post includes a video), this limitation did not likely bias our estimation of the effect of videos. Second, some fake news may never be reported to Weibo. As Weibo’s reporting system has been publicly available for more than eight years, we believe that our sample likely included most influential fake news items, which is what the platform seeks to target. This is supported by the fact that each reported fake news post in our sample was shared 1,559 times on average. Third, some fake news may have been deleted before our data collection began. As we do not believe that a user is more or less likely to delete a fake news post because of the inclusion of videos in the news item (supported by our robustness check in the Including a Video and the Deletion of Fake News section below), we do not believe that this limitation biased our estimation of the effect of videos. Fourth, legitimate news might be mistakenly determined as fake. While we believe that transparency and public scrutiny largely ensure accuracy in the reporting system, the determination of fake news or the accuracy of the reporting is beyond the scope of our study. We focus on users’ reporting behavior and our findings inform how social media platforms can leverage their users to target potential fake news stories for their own investigations. Regardless, to alleviate concerns about potential bias in our estimations due to the above sampling decisions, we also conducted a randomized experiment to uncover the underlying mechanisms (as described in the Underlying Mechanisms section below).

## Key Variables

## Dependent and Explanatory Variables

Our unit of analysis is each fake news post. The dependent variable in our study, Ln(ReportedTimes+1)<sub>ij</sub>, is the logtransformed number of users reporting the post i posted by user j to Weibo as fake news. We used the number of users reporting a fake news post rather than a dummy for the dependent variable, as posts can be reported multiple times by different users. Among the fake news posts that were reported in our sample, each post was reported by 13 users, on average. In addition, since the actual number of reports can be viewed as a more informative measure of the influence and deception of a fake news post, our analysis on the number of users reporting a fake news post provides more practical insights.

The explanatory variable is $V i d e o _ { i j } ,$ a dummy with a value of 1 if a video is included in post i posted by user j. Before analyzing the data, we note that we do not differentiate videos according to their quality or sentiment, as it is virtually impossible to measure the quality or sentiment of videos directly and objectively. That is to say, lifelike videos are not necessarily high quality, in terms of supporting fake news, and blurry ones are not necessarily low quality in this respect. It is also impractical to measure the sentiment in videos because of the lack of reliable standards and widely used sentiment analysis tools for videos in the literature and practice. Yet this should not be of concern, as the quality or sentiment of videos is irrelevant to our explanatory variable (Video) after controlling for fake news fixed effects, and omitting them in the model is unlikely to bias the estimation of Video. We reason that fake news authors are unlikely to include obviously low-quality videos in their posts. In addition, we did not observe any two fake news posts that had the same text content but different videos (if any), which is potentially due to the cost of manipulating videos for those who simply duplicate other users’ fake news posts.

To capture the moderating role of sentiment intensity, we leveraged a commonly used measure: SentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub>, a score from 0 (a neutral/objective sentiment) to 1 (a very strong sentiment) for the sentiment intensity of post i posted by user j (Thelwall et al., 2010). We calculated SentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub> using nltk.sentiment packages in Python. We note that our first attempt at conducting sentiment analysis for the original Chinese version of the posts yielded less accurate results. This is to be expected, as the literature suggests that sentiment analysis for Chinese is still under development (Peng et al., 2017). To obtain a more accurate measure of sentiment, we used several approaches: (1) following the literature (Balahur & Turchi, 2012; Pak & Paroubek, 2010), we translated all posts in our sample into English using Google Translate and asked two graduate students to conduct manual validation and corrections; (2) we then evaluated the sentiment scores of each post using the VADER module,<sup>10</sup> as suggested by the literature (Hutto & Gilbert, 2014; Ramteke et al., 2016; Ribeiro et al., 2016); and (3) we validated the sentiment scores using user engagement with each post in the robustness check (see the Validation of the Measure of Sentiment Intensity section below). As VADER calculates sentiment scores ranging from -1 (very negative) to 1 (very positive), we used the absolute value of VADER scores for SentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub>. That is, a sentiment intensity of 1 means the text has a strong sentiment (either positive or negative), and a sentiment intensity of 0 means the text shows no sentiment.

To compare the moderating role of sentiment intensity across positive versus negative sentiments, we further split SentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub> into two variables: PositiveSentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub> (the absolute value of the sentiment score if it is positive and zero otherwise) and NegativeSentimentIntensity<sub>ij</sub> (the absolute value of the sentiment score if it is negative and zero otherwise).

## Control Variables

We incorporated a set of control variables in our estimations (Table 1). First, we controlled for whether a post included any image with Image<sub>ij</sub>, as images can influence the chance of a post being reported (Gupta et al., 2013). Second, we controlled for the length of each post with Ln(Length)<sub>ij</sub>. Recall that posts of the same fake news can have different lengths if they include spaces or the source of a video after a camera symbol at the end. Third, we controlled for whether a post disclosed the source of the news or videos with Source<sub>ij</sub>, as the disclosure of sources can influence users’ evaluation of the credibility and their likelihood of reporting the post (Park et al., 2016). Fourth, we controlled for the sequence in which each post appeared in the history of the fake news with $L n ( P o s t S e q u e n c e ) _ { i j } ,$ since the chances of a post being reported can change as users see more instances of the fake news item in their news feed (Lazer et al., 2018). Fifth, we controlled for the number of times that the same fake news item was reported prior to the post by user j with Ln(PreviReportedTimes)<sub>ij</sub>. If a fake news item was reported prior to the focal post, users’ ability and willingness to report may differ. Sixth, we controlled for heterogeneity in the chance of a post being reported based on the time-varying number of followers. To do so, we controlled for Ln(TimeRegistration)<sub>jt</sub>, the number of days that user j has been registered as of day t; Ln(Follower)<sub>j</sub>, the log of the number of followers of user j at the time of data collection; and the interaction term of Ln(TimeRegistration)<sub>jt</sub> and Ln(Follower)<sub>j</sub>.<sup>11</sup> Lastly, we controlled for day and hour fixed effects, which measure the overall traffic patterns of the platform. We also used fake news fixed effects and fake news poster fixed effects to account for fake news characteristics and heterogeneity across users who post fake news. Table 1 summarizes the definitions of our variables, and Table 2 shows the summary statistics.

We discuss the endogeneity concerns in the models. First, the study may suffer from omitted variable bias. As mentioned earlier, $V i d e o _ { i j }$ could be endogenous as users may self-select to include a video based on the characteristics of the fake news or their engagement with other users. Although fake news fixed effects, time fixed effects, and fake news poster fixed effects help account for potential confounders, we further alleviated the concern of endogeneity by using an instrumental variable approach with 2SLS models (see An Instrumental Variable Approach with 2SLS Models section below). Second, the model may have measurement errors, particularly in our measurement of SentimentIntensity. While prior studies have shown that the results of sentiment analysis using Google Translate are consistent with analyzing the original language (Balahur & Turchi, 2012; Pak & Paroubek, 2010), we validated the results of the sentiment analysis by examining the correlation between sentiment and user engagement (see Validation of the Measure of Sentiment Intensity section below). To further ensure robustness, we included both English fake news in the United States and Chinese fake news in China in our randomized experiments; the results are consistent. Third, reverse causality was not likely a concern in our study, as fake news posters are unlikely to include videos in their posts for the purpose of improving their chances of being reported by other users.

## Robustness Checks

Our findings rely on several identifying assumptions, the violation of which would call into question the robustness of our results. We therefore subjected our work to a battery of robustness and falsification tests to rule out alternate possibilities. Specifically, we (1) alleviated the endogeneity concern of our independent variable using an instrumental variable (IV) approach, (2) validated our measurement for text sentiment, (3) replicated the analyses using fake news posts that included images, (4) replicated the analyses using users who reported fake news in post comments, and (5) examined whether sentiment intensity or including a video influenced the deletion of fake news posts. All analyses show consistent results that support our main findings.

## An Instrumental Variable Approach with 2SLS Models

To alleviate potential endogeneity concerns due to selfselection, we used an instrumental variable (IV) approach with 2SLS models. Specifically, we introduced two IVs for Video as follows.

Our first instrument is an interaction term: PostOriginallyWithVideo<sub>i</sub>× Ln(PostSequence)<sub>ij</sub>. The intuition behind this is that not all users like to include videos in their posts, as adding videos requires time and consumes data when they post via cell phones. As a result, fake news items originally posted with videos tend to gradually evolve to new versions using pure text or images. When users see more posts of one fake news item with pure texts and images, they are also more likely to copy and repost the fake news with no videos, leading to a decreasing trend in the probability of reposted fake news to include videos over time (Figure A2 in the Appendix). In contrast, fake news items originally produced with no videos obviously do not follow this trend. Here, we introduce PostOriginallyWithVideo<sub>i</sub> , a dummy that is one if fake news post i has a video in its original version (i.e., the first post of the fake news item appearing in our sample), and Ln(PostSequence)<sub>ij</sub> as the log value of the appearing sequence of post i in the history of the fake news item. We expect that PostOriginallyWithVideo<sub>i</sub>× Ln(PostSequence)<sub>ij</sub> is negatively associated with Video. One might raise a concern that fake news items that were originally produced with a video may be fundamentally different from other fake news items. However, we expect such fundamental differences to be controlled by the fake news fixed effects. Thus, after controlling for fake news fixed effects, this IV is unlikely to correlate with the residuals.

Our second instrument was constructed by leveraging a design change at Weibo. Weibo was initially designed as a text-andimage-friendly platform. In 2016, however, it changed its settings, allowing users to share videos directly from several other video-sharing platforms (instead of downloading and uploading them from their own equipment).

## Validation of the Measure of Sentiment Intensity

We measured SentimentIntensity using the nltk.sentiment package (VADER) in Python. This method has been widely used for sentiment analysis, especially in microblogging-type contexts (Balahur & Turchi, 2012; Li et al., 2018; Ribeiro et al., 2016). Nevertheless, we verified the accuracy of the method in our context by estimating the correlation between SentimentIntensity and the number of shares/likes/comments each post received. The results are shown in Table 5.

Columns 1-3 of Table 5 show that SentimentIntensity is positively correlated with the number of shares and likes, and Columns 4-6 demonstrate that both PositiveSentimentIntensity and NegativeSentimentIntensity are positively correlated with the number of shares/likes/comments. These results are consistent with the findings in the prior literature that the strength of sentiment positively correlates with users’ engagement with posts on social media (Berger & Milkman, 2013), thus supporting the validity of our measure of sentiment intensity.

## Replication with Fake News Posts that Include Images

In this paper, we focus on the impact of including a video in a fake news post. As a supplementary analysis, we replicate our main models with a different explanatory variable— Image<sub>ij</sub>, a dummy that is 1 if the fake news post i posted by user j has any images. Given the similar effect of images on user engagement (Li & Xie, 2020), the ability to detect fake news (Mnookin & Human., 1998), and the willingness to report fake news (Hew et al., 2007), one might expect to see a similar effect of images on the number of users reporting fake news to platforms. Results are provided in Table 6.

From Table 6, while results suggest a similar moderating role of sentiment intensity (Columns 2 and 3) in the effect of

Image, the overall effect of Image (Column 1) is different from the impact of Video. Specifically, the effect of Image on the number of users reporting is significantly negative (Column 1 of Table 6) while the impact of Video is significantly positive (Column 1 of Table 3). One possible explanation is that while both videos and images can increase the number of users reporting fake news by facilitating their engagement with the fake news item, such a positive effect is likely to be smaller for images than for videos, as images are generally less effective in facilitating user engagement (Hsieh et al., 2012). The results provided in Table 5 support this argument: Columns 1-6 of Table 5 all show a significantly smaller coefficient of Image than Video, indicating that compared to videos, images are less strongly associated with an increase in the number of likes/shares/comments of fake news posts.

## Replication with Users Reporting Fake News in Post Comments

When users detect fake news, they may “report” it in the comment section of the fake news post rather than reporting it officially to the social media platform. Since reporting fake news in comments is merely another reporting method, we used this to supplement our analysis. Specifically, we replicated our analysis with Ln(ReportedInComments+1), the number of users reporting a fake news post in the comments of a post. We expected to see consistent effects of videos and sentiment intensity on both behaviors. The results are shown in Table 7. By comparing Columns 1-3 of Table 7, we can see that the effects of sentiment intensity and including a video are generally consistent with our main analyses (Table 3).

It could be a concern that users may shift between reporting in comments and reporting to social media platforms. We replicated our analyses with Ln(TotalReported+1), the total number of users reporting to platforms and in comments for each news post (Columns 4-6 of Table 7). The results are consistent and support our theoretical arguments about the impact of videos and sentiment intensity.

Our data could be incomplete if some fake news posts were deleted prior to this point. Our estimation would be biased if the probability of a post being deleted is related to a certain sentiment intensity or whether a video was included.

## Underlying Mechanisms

To further ensure the robustness of our empirical analyses and uncover the underlying mechanisms, we conducted randomized experiments and additional mediation analyses.

## Mechanism of the Effect of Videos via Users’ Ability to Detect

In the theoretical development, we propose that including a video in a fake news post inhibits users’ ability to detect fake news. To test this mechanism and ensure the generalizability of our findings, we conducted a randomized experiment on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), with English fake news and users in the United States. We also replicated our experiment with 20 Chinese fake news posts and users in China. The results are consistent.<sup>13</sup>

## Experiment Design

We first conducted the experiment with 20 English fake news posts that spread in the United States. We selected fake news items from a list of the top 50 fake news items that were most widely spread on Facebook in 2016-2017,<sup>14</sup> using three criteria: (1) the topic is non-controversial (i.e., we excluded all fake news about politics and the government), (2) the posts were confirmed as fake news by us using external information, and (3) the posts included a video, allowing us to randomize the treatment of whether a video was included in our experiments. We initially identified 16 fake news posts that met these criteria. We searched for additional fake news items with the same criteria from FactCheck.org, a nonprofit website that verifies and publishes fake news (Brandtzaeg & Følstad, 2017). After searching by topic, we included the first four fake news that met our requirements—three were about climate change and one was about COVID-19. A full list of the 20 fake news items and their sentiment scores are included in Table A1 in the Appendix.

Our experimental design is described below. First, by removing or retaining highly emotional statements and the video in each fake news item, we created four posts for each of the 20 fake news items: (1) a weak-sentiment version without a video, (2) a weak-sentiment version with a video, (3) a strong-sentiment version without a video, and (4) a strong-sentiment version with a video. By examining users’ responses to each fake news item, comparing (1) and (2) as well as (3) and (4), we tested the effect of including a video in a news post. In addition, by comparing the effect of videos in the weak-sentiment version to that in the strong-sentiment version, we tested the moderating effect of sentiment intensity. We created a survey in which we randomly assigned one of the four posts to every participant. To mimic the real social media environment, we posted each fake news on a real Twitter account and used the screenshot of the post to display the fake news item in the survey (Figure 2).

Second, we added three questions following each post to test how participants responded to each fake news post (Figure 3). Specifically, in response to the first question—“What will you do if you see the above post on Twitter?”—we asked each participant to choose among the following options: like, share, comment, ignore, hide the post, unfollow the user, or report it as fake news to Twitter. In response to the second question— “Please evaluate the credibility of the above post.”—we asked each participant to choose among the following options: true, possibly true, not sure, possibly fake, and fake. Finally, in response to the third question—“How confident are you about your above judgment (a score of 0-9)?—we asked each participant to provide a score between 0 and 9. We also recorded how much time each participant spent reading each post before they started to answer questions for that post.

Third, given the large number of questions (i.e., three questions for each post and 60 questions for the 20 pieces of fake news), we conducted the experiment in two rounds. In the first round, we recruited participants to answer questions about the first 10 pieces of fake news in Table A1, and in the second round, we recruited another group of participants to answer questions about the last 10 pieces of fake news in Table A1.

Fourth, we recruited participants for our surveys from Amazon MTurk. We created our survey using Qualtrics and leveraged functions such as randomization and time recording for the survey questions. We posted our survey to MTurk in January 2021 and paid each participant 0.3 US dollars. We collected 500 surveys for each of the two rounds.

Fifth, we excluded invalid surveys. To determine whether a survey was invalid, we added two additional questions (e.g., what is your age, and how many years have you been in school) at the beginning of the survey. A survey was tagged as invalid if its answers to the two questions were unreasonable (e.g., age is 999). A survey was also tagged as invalid if any post in it was judged as fake news but still “liked,” or if any post was judged as “true” but still “reported as fake news.” In total, 226 out of the 1,000 surveys were tagged as invalid and removed, leaving a final sample of 774 surveys.

Due to the nature of our research questions, we could not use a t-test to examine the effect of videos and sentiment intensity. Instead, we used OLS to estimate the effect. Specifically, we created a panel for each post in the 774 surveys. Since each survey included 10 posts, we had a total of 7,740 observations of the participants’ reactions to different versions of the 20 pieces of English fake news, including (1) the time each participant spent reading each post, (2) the type of engagement with each post, (3) participants’ evaluation of each post’s authenticity, and (4) participants’ confidence in their evaluation. We regressed users’ reactions to each fake news post on Video (whether a post includes a video) and SentimentIntensity (sentiment intensity of the text content of a post). To alleviate potential heterogeneity concerns across fake news and participants, we included fake news fixed effects and participant fixed effects in the model.

## Mechanism of the Effect of Videos via Users’ Willingness to Report

We further leveraged the experiment results to examine how the inclusion of a video influences users’ willingness to report fake news. In Column 4 of Table 9, we tested whether including a video increases the willingness of the users who have detected the fake news to report the fake news. We thus aimed to measure the willingness of only those users who definitely detected the fake news. The coefficient of Video is positive but not statistically significant because of the moderating effect of sentiment intensity, which we discuss below.

## Mechanism of the Effect of Videos via Users’ Engagement

To understand how the inclusion of a video influences the number of users reporting fake news via increased user engagement (i.e., likes, shares, and comments), we leveraged our sample data to conduct several mediation analyses.

We carried out a classical mediation analysis proposed by Baron and Kenny (1986) and tested whether user engagement with fake news posts mediates the impact of videos (Table 10). Columns 2-4 of Table 10 show that including videos increases the number of likes, shares, and comments on the fake news post. Comparing the coefficients of Video in Columns 1 and 5, we observe that while the coefficient of

Video is significantly positive in Column 1, its magnitude drops substantially after controlling for the mediators (i.e., likes, shares, and comments) in Column 5. These results support our theoretical argument that including a video in a fake news post increases the number of users reporting by facilitating users’ engagement with the fake news posts.

## Mechanism of the Moderating Role of Sentiment Intensity

In this section, we empirically examine the moderating role of sentiment intensity on the effect of videos via each of the three channels, as discussed above. The channels’ impact on users’ ability to detect and willingness to report are examined using the results of the MTurk experiment (as explained in the Experiment Design section above), and we investigate the channel impact on users’ engagement through our sample data.

We first examine the effect of sentiment intensity on users ability to detect fake news using the results of the experiment (Columns 1-3 of Table 11). In Column 1, the coefficient of Video×PositiveSentimentIntensity is not significant, but Video×NegativeSentimentIntensity is significant and positive. This suggests that while including a video in fake news posts slightly increases users’ time spent consuming the content, this increase is significantly larger when the text content elicits a strongly negative (versus positive) sentiment. This finding supports our earlier argument that compared to positive sentiment, negative sentiment triggers defensive mechanisms (e.g., attempts to deny or avoid the problem) and results in more effort from users in processing the news. In Columns 2 and 3, the coefficient of Video× PositiveSentimentIntensity is significantly more positive than that of Video×NegativeSentimentIntensity, suggesting that including a video in a fake news post makes users more likely to trust the post as “true” news if the text sentiment is strongly positive versus strongly negative. All results from Columns 1- 3 support the mechanism of the moderating role of sentiment intensity and indicate that the effect of videos on undermining users’ ability to detect fake news is enhanced to a larger extent when the text sentiment is strongly positive and to a lesser extent when the text sentiment is strongly negative.

We next examine the effect of sentiment intensity on users’ willingness to report fake news using the experimental results (Column 4 of Table 11). In Column 4, we only include the observations when participants considered a post to be “fake” in Question 5 of Figure 3 (the same observations of Column 4 of Table 9). While the coefficient of Video is positive and significant, the coefficient of Video×PositiveSentimentIntensity is significant and negative. This result suggests that while the inclusion of videos in fake news posts increases users’ willingness to report fake news, such an effect is more strongly attenuated if the text has a strongly positive sentiment than if the text has a strongly negative sentiment. This result is consistent with our theoretical arguments about the moderating effect of sentiment intensity on users’ willingness to report fake news.

We also examined the effect of sentiment intensity on users’ engagement with our sample data. To understand the moderating role of sentiment intensity in the effect of videos through user engagement, we adopted a mediated moderation test (Edwards & Lambert, 2007; Muller et al., 2005). Recall that in our theoretical discussion, we proposed that for both positive and negative sentiment, sentiment intensity weakens the impact of videos on the number of users reporting by cannibalizing the effect of videos on facilitating user engagement. Columns 1-5 of Table 12 show the mediation effect of sentiment intensity. We see that both Video×PositiveSentimentIntensity and Video×NegativeSentimentIntensity are negatively correlated with the mediators (Columns 2-4). Also, the effects of Video× PositiveSentimentIntensity and Video×NegativeSentimentIntensity become substantially weaker in Column 5 (where we control for the mediators—i.e., likes, shares, and comments) than in Column 1 (where we do not control for the mediators). These results are in accordance with our theoretical argument that both positive and negative text sentiment can cannibalize the effect of videos in facilitating user engagement with fake news.

## Discussion

## Key Findings

In this study, we empirically examine how including a video in fake news posts influences users’ reporting of the posts to social media platforms. Our results demonstrate that including a video in a fake news post significantly increases the number of users reporting the post to the social media platform. Furthermore, the sentiment intensity of the text of the fake news, especially when the sentiment is positive, significantly attenuates the effect of the inclusion of videos. A series of randomized experiments and mediation analyses uncover the underlying mechanisms, and our robustness tests yield similar results.

## Generalizability

We take several precautions to ensure the generalizability of our findings. Accordingly, we discuss generalizability from three perspectives: (1) the Weibo setting, (2) the selection of fake news, and (3) the proposed mechanisms across platforms and countries.

We believe that the observed effects of videos and text sentiment can be generalized beyond the Weibo setting. After comparing the interface and functions of the platforms, the definitions of fake news, and the reporting processes for fake news on Weibo and Twitter (the two leading microblogging platforms in the world), we found no observable differences.

Specifically, both platforms initially had a 140-character length limit for posts (a requirement that was loosened after 2017) and allowed users to follow/unfollow other users and share/comment/like others’ posts. In addition, both Weibo and Twitter define fake news as news posts that are completely false or misleading because of exaggerated, outdated, or incomplete information. The process of reporting fake news is also similar on both platforms, where post readers can click a button next to a post if they want to report it (see Context, Data, and Variable Descriptions section above).

It is also worth considering whether the fake news posts selected in the sample are representative. While there are no clear reasons to believe that fake news content is fundamentally different across countries, our sample excludes all fake news relevant to government or politics because of the sensitivity of these topics. This helps alleviate concerns that our findings are not generalizable across countries with different political and cultural environments.

To ensure that our theoretical mechanisms on videos and sentiment intensity can be generalized outside of Weibo and China, we conducted experiments using fake news in both Chinese and English with users in both the United States and China (see Experiment Design section above). All results support the key assumptions and arguments critical to our theorization. This assures us that the proposed effects are not unique to fake news in Chinese or users in China.

## Contributions to Theory and Research

We contribute to the information systems (IS) literature in three key ways. First, although IS scholars have investigated the effectiveness of many policies taken by social media platforms to thwart the proliferation of fake news, we extend this work by examining one such strategy that encourages users to directly report fake news. Given the challenge of policing all content posted on social media, the potential of this policy, which leverages the large number of users to combat fake news, has received much attention from social media platforms. Yet, to our knowledge, no research has systematically examined this approach. We fill this major gap in the literature by examining users’ reporting behavior, providing insights into how social media platforms can leverage the wisdom of the crowd to combat the proliferation of fake news on social media.

Second, while the format of online content is becoming increasingly richer (Hjorth & Hinton, 2019), to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to examine how the format of fake news posts (specifically videos) influences users’ reporting of fake news to social media platforms. Although the literature has examined how users detect fake news based on the content of social media posts (Kim & Dennis, 2019; Rubin et al., 2016), our study differentiates and separately discusses users’ detecting and reporting behaviors when videos are embedded in fake news. Our findings indicate how the growing adoption of videos can affect the role of ordinary users in fighting fake news.

Third, we contribute to the literature on information diffusion over social media (Bakir & McStay, 2018; Susarla et al., 2012; Vosoughi et al., 2018) by exploring how the sentiment intensity of fake news text content interacts with videos to influence users’ reporting of fake news. The prior literature has found that sentiments interrupt users’ rational thinking, and certain sentiments have specific effects (Clore & Huntsinger, 2007; Ellis, 1962). However, to our knowledge, our work is the first to empirically examine the role of sentiment intensity in the context of users reporting fake news. More importantly, we investigate the heterogeneous role of sentiment intensity across different sentiment valences. While the literature, in general, suggests that negative emotions are stronger and more salient than positive ones (Pham, 2007), our findings show a stronger moderating effect of positive sentiment intensity than negative sentiment intensity. The findings suggest that the text sentiment (e.g., intensity and valance) of fake news can significantly affect user reactions to videos in fake news posts, and that videos and text should be jointly considered in platforms’ strategies for detecting fake news.

## Implications for Practice

To the extent that videos drive more engagement on social media (Lee et al., 2018) and that fake news producers utilize affective text to evoke various sentiments to make videos more misleading (Bakir & McStay, 2018), social media platforms should be cognizant of the ways in which the growing adoption of videos can affect the role of ordinary users in combating fake news. Our findings provide the managers of social media platforms with practical knowledge on both the potential and limits of leveraging the wisdom of the crowd to prevent the proliferation of fake news on their platforms.

First, our findings inform social media platform managers that fake news posts with accompanying videos are more frequently reported by the crowd. In other words, social media platforms should devote more effort to actively detecting fake news made up of pure text and images, which may not spread as rapidly as those posts with videos but are likely to stay unreported for a longer time.

Second, our study finds that fake news posts with videos are less frequently reported if the text content conveys a strong sentiment, suggesting that social media platform managers should incorporate the interplay between videos and text sentiment in their strategies for targeting fake news. For example, social media platforms could rely more on users to spot fake news that has a video and elicits a weak sentiment, as videos can provide more materials for users to disprove made-up stories. However, to target fake news with a video that can elicit a strong sentiment, the user reporting system may become ineffective as strong sentiment may further enhance the deceptiveness of videos. In this case, the use of machine learning algorithms to actively detect fake news would be more important.

Third, our study finds that the moderating role of sentiment intensity on users’ reporting of fake news is stronger if the sentiment is positive versus negative. This result informs social media platform managers that while fake news with a negative sentiment may perpetuate more anxiety among users, fake news with a positive sentiment, such as joy, gratitude, or inspiration, may be more deceptive to users and remain undetected and influential on the social media platform for a longer time. Managers might consider incorporating sentiment strength and valence into their algorithms to combat fake news and devote more effort into actively detecting fake news posts with positive sentiment.

## Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

This paper is subject to certain limitations, which create opportunities for future research. First, our study only investigates the effect of including videos and the moderating role of sentiment intensity, and only includes nonpolitical fake news. Future studies could investigate how other factors, such as the characteristics of fake news posters (e.g., influence, age, gender, and reputation), the quality of videos, the content of posts (e.g., popularity, length, and topic), and the demographics of post readers, affect users’ reporting decisions. Second, while our study focuses on what factors drive users to report fake news, the accuracy of users’ reporting is also an important topic to study, as a large number of misreported posts would lead to a waste of resources that social media platforms need to spend on manually reviewing the veracity of all reported posts. Future studies could investigate what factors (e.g., characteristics of news posters and demographics of the post readers) may make the audience more likely to mistakenly report legitimate news as fake and provide social media platforms with insights into when and how to motivate the crowd to report fake news. Third, while our study uses experiments and mediation analysis to uncover underlying mechanisms by capturing users’ ability to detect, willingness to report, and their engagement with the post, data limitations do not allow us to compare the effect sizes across these three mechanisms. Future studies could examine how much each mechanism contributes to the effect of videos and the possibility that mechanisms other than videos and text sentiment may influence users’ reporting behavior. Fourth, some social media platforms have started to use rewards to encourage users to report fake news.<sup>15</sup> One question for future studies is: “How do material rewards influence users reporting behavior, and should platforms include monetary incentives in their user reporting systems?”

## Concluding Remarks

Social media has become a major source of news stories and a conduit for the wide dissemination of fake news (Guess et al., 2019). Yet, the current social media news ecosystem rewards misinformation over reality, raising serious concerns regarding the influence of misinformation in political campaigns, financial activities, cyberstalking, and public health (Lazer et al., 2018). Given the large user population on social media, it is tempting for social media platforms to leverage the “wisdom of the crowd” by allowing users to report fake news. Yet there has been a lack of understanding of users’ reporting behavior and how effective this kind of policy could be in practice. Our analysis of more than 100,000 fake news posts on a leading social media platform explores how users report fake news to social media platforms, especially when a video is included to deceive users. We find that including a video in a fake news post significantly increases the number of users reporting the post. Additionally, we find that the sentiment intensity of the text content, especially when the sentiment is positive, undermines the effect that videos have on increasing the number of users reporting a fake news post. Integrating these results, we conclude that practitioners must seek to better understand how the popularity of videos among online content impacts the proliferation of fake news and suggest that they adjust their strategies to combat the proliferation of fake news by using more diversified techniques (e.g., user reporting system, sentiment analysis, and machine learning algorithms).
