# The impact of personal dispositions on information sensitivity, privacy concern and trust in disclosing health information online

Keywords: Information privacy concern Utility Theory Trust Health status Personality Personal dispositions Information sensitivity Intrinsic factors Intrinsic and extrinsic perspectives of trust

## a b s t r a c t

Reluctance to provide personal health information could impede the success of web-based healthcare services. This paper focuses on the role of personal dispositions in disclosing health information online. The conceptual model argues that individuals' intention to disclose such information depends on their trust, privacy concern, and information sensitivity, which are determined by personal dispositions—personality traits, information sensitivity, health status, prior privacy invasions, risk beliefs, and experience—acting as intrinsic antecedents of trust. The data (collected via a lab experiment) and the analysis shed light on the role of personal dispositions. This could assist in enhancing healthcare websites and increase the success of online delivery of health services.

## 1. Introduction

Online users have a serious privacy concern about how their personal health information is used, disclosed, and protected, and the degree of control they have over the dissemination of this information [113]. They are also concerned about possible undesirable economic and social consequences resulting from the misuse of such information [68]. Indeed, 88.2% of Internet users express concern about the privacy of their personal information [105], and research suggests that privacy of health information is of focal concern to individuals [13,15,49,70,98,101,112]. Health information privacy concern may even cause individuals to avoid healthcare in sensitive areas [93]. The same presumably applies online where individuals must reveal private health information to receive appropriate care. The advent of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) has further increased awareness regarding privacy infringements, bringing privacy concerns to the forefront [65].

This study examines such health information privacy concern and its relationship to trust, and how personal disposition, composed of personality traits and various measures of information sensitivity and experience, affects these concerns. Information privacy is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the “claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others” ([111], p.7). Privacy concern, on the other hand, is the concern over the loss of privacy and the need for protection against unwarranted communication and use of personal information [17,100,61]. Thus information privacy concern is about being in control of information, security of information exchange, and whether the collector of this information will behave appropriately [66,114]. In this paper, we propose that personal dispositions play an important role in privacy concern and trust. This proposed role of personal dispositions is based on Utility Theory and its application to choice theory in which consumer preferences depend on personal characteristics. The research question addressed is: how do personal dispositions (such as personality traits, health status, information sensitivity, and other personal circumstances) impact privacy concern and trust levels in the decision to provide personal health information online?

Numerous studies on trust have investigated the extrinsic factors (website features and elements) as antecedents of online trust. The major contribution of this study is in revealing the intrinsic factors (individuals' dispositions) that impact online trust. In doing so, the study extends trust theory into the dual perspective—intrinsic and extrinsic—of online trust in the health context. The second contribution of this study to is in identifying the process by which personal dispositions impact individuals' trust and behavior intention. Rather than doing so directly, personal dispositions impact trust and behavior intention through information sensitivity and privacy concern. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings should enhance managers' understandings of the information needed to personalize and customize health sites to address private health information.

## 2. Theoretical background and research model

Disclosing health information online is a decision that individuals have to make in their encounter with online healthcare and healthinformation providers. Web-users disclose personal information online in the hope of gaining bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that such a disclosure may entail, such as receiving health advice, access to health providers, and personalized health and <sup>fi</sup>tness evaluation. At the same time, once personal health information is disclosed online, it may be abused or accessed without authorization. For example, disclosure of personal health information may result in discrimination by employers and insurance agencies if they get access to such information [93]. Additionally, website personnel may release health information for personal gain or to settle personal scores. Hackers could access health information if the provider lacks adequate data security. Moreover, online providers may themselves make such information available to others without requesting permission. This may be done legally if the terms of use allow it or it may occur through negligence and mistakes. As industry reports indicate, compromising information in this way is not uncommon [107]. Additionally, once information leaves the hands of direct care providers, its use and control are subject to the ethical practices of the “secondary” users [93].

These potential undesirable outcomes are negative incentives, or disutility, in disclosing personal health information that increase individuals' privacy concern. Hui et al. [51] have noted that such disutility is due to people's desire to avoid unwanted disclosure. Given the usefulness of online health services, individuals need to balance this potential disutility against the potential desirable outcomes, or utility, of disclosing their personal health information online. To investigate this decision, we rely on Utility Theory to inform our examination of constructs that play a role in individuals' decisions to disclose their personal health information.

Contemporary Utility Theory posits that people make choices by maximizing their utility function over multiple choices (or alternatives) [10,67,72,73]. These choices could have compensatory attributes in that the utility of some attributes may compensate the disutility of other attributes. In other words,

$$
U _ {D} = f (B, C),\tag{1}
$$

where $U _ { D }$ is the combined overall utility level of decision D. In this analysis, D is the choice between disclosing and not disclosing personal health information online, and f is a functional form. B is the vector of established bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (desirable attributes) individuals receive from disclosing their personal information to the website, such as receiving needed health services and useful health-related information and evaluations, including the perceived high quality, cost effectiveness, and comfort of these services. B increases the utility of decision D. C is the vector of concerns (undesirable attributes) that have negative impact on $U _ { D } ,$ hence increasing the disutility of D.

The utility of decisions and preferences for B and C are not uniform across individuals. Utility Theory posits that individuals differ in their preferences. Individuals' personal dispositions and circumstances (such as their personality traits, current health status, salient experiences, and risk beliefs) in<sup>fl</sup>uence their preferences and the extent of their judgment about the utility of choices available to them. McFadden ([73], p. 356) observes that “the expressed preferences of the consumers are functions of their taste template, experience, and personal characteristics, including both observed and unobserved components.” The differences in individuals' utility level stem from their personal dispositions:

$$
B = g (P),\tag{2}
$$

$$
C = h (P),\tag{3}
$$

where g and h are functional forms and P represents the vector of personal dispositions.

Based on Utility Theory, we argue that the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to visitors of a health services website, or B, are the health services that the website provides. In the absence of negative consequences, individuals should readily disclose their personal information in order to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from health services. Bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using a website to receive health information are not the focus of our work. Our focus is on C. We attempt to identify factors that reduce disutility (decreasing C) and those that increase disutility (increasing C). In what follows we call these two categories of factors as disutility reducers and disutility enhancers, respectively.

Since people in general regard their health information as private, privacy concern related to providing personal health information is a disutility enhancer—it increases C in Eq. (1). The level of this disutility should depend on the particular health circumstances of people involved and on their personality traits. Such an increase in disutility may, however, be countered by trust in the website (disutility reducer) because trust leads people to put aside their risk perceptions [86]—trust reduces C in Eq. (1). This reasoning leads to the research model in Fig. 1. In the conceptual model, we argue that personal dispositions (including personality traits and health status, mediated by information sensitivity) in<sup>fl</sup>uence privacy concern, which in turn is countered by trust, both affecting intentions to disclose personal health information. Salient prior experiences, both positive and negative, and salient risk beliefs are included to complete the picture, as they are an integral part of how trust operates.

## 2.1. The personality part of personal dispositions

It is widely accepted that information sensitivity varies with individual differences [81,89,97]. Stone and Stone [103] argued for links between a number of individual personality traits, value, and information privacy issues. Pedersen [88] demonstrated that privacy choices are associated with personality characteristics. Gefen et al. [42] also highlighted the need to include personality in the study of trust in online environments. Personality is an intransient dimension; it is consistent over time [64].

Based on Utility Theory, Stone and Stone [103] argued that individuals tend to maximize their positively valued outcomes and to minimize their negatively valued outcomes. Using previous privacy studies, they demonstrated that by protecting their privacy, individuals want to maximize their physical and psychological well-being and to minimize their physical and psychological harm. Berscheid [11], Cozby [21], Stone [102], and Stone and Stone [103] are among several researchers who have argued for the relationship between the personality trait of introversion–extroversion and privacy orientation.

Basing their organizational privacy model on theories of motivation, advanced by Porter and Lawler [91] and others, Stone and Stone [103] observed that personality impacts cognition, which, in turn, impacts motivational forces that, in turn, lead to behavior. In the same vein, it could be argued that personality traits impact how people assess their health information sensitivity—a cognition process in which a person evaluates health information from the perspective of its possible positive and negative outcomes—and its related disutility of privacy concern. Karahanna et al. [59] also argued that personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence cognitive beliefs. If people assess the sensitivity of their information as a cognitive belief, then it too should be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by personality traits.

There are good reasons to argue that personality differences affect health information sensitivity because personality traits are related to how people seek information and to their behavior [32].<sup>1</sup> Such links have been well-documented in the extensive research relating personality and job performance (see [8] for a review and meta analysis), personality and job satisfaction (see [56] for a review and meta analysis), personality and sustained attention and workload [94], and personality and career success across life span [55]. Ajzen and Fishbein [3] have observed the importance of personality in forming individuals' beliefs and motivating their behaviors. For this research, we use Goldberg's Big-Five factors since they have been used in published research. Furthermore, a series of studies have “demonstrated the ubiquity of the big <sup>fi</sup>ve” ([26], p. 425).

Extroversion or surgency is a trait related to an individual's orientation toward others. The terms for describing this factor include being talkative, bold, assertive, sociable, and demonstrative [46]. In their interactions with others, extroverts are talkative and seek attention. The opposite trait of extroversion is introversion, which is found to be correlated with the preference for isolation [88]. Extroverts enjoy social interactions, which normally involve sharing personal information. This indicates that they are less sensitive towards disclosing their health information, which is in line with Ajzen and Fishbein's observation [3] that extroversion could in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals' social norms. Extroverts gain energy from interacting with others and need to have social interactions more than introverts do. As a result, extroverts should have lower information sensitivity in general, and health information sensitivity in particular, so as to accommodate their higher need to interact [18,108]. Hence,

H1a. Extroversion is negatively associated with perceived health information sensitivity.

Agreeableness is a trait related to the likeability and social conformity of individuals. “Agreeableness or sociability refers to friendly, considerate and modest behavior.” ([35], p. 100). Agreeable individuals have been described as being warm, kind, cooperative, trusting, generous, <sup>fl</sup>exible, considerate, and agreeable [46]. It is argued that agreeable individuals have a higher extent of intimacy [20] and get along better with others [56]. Although warm and trusting in their social interactions, agreeable individuals are apprehensive of deviant behaviors [18]. Since requesting excessive health information online (as sometimes happens because it is hard to program an online system to ask only relevant information) violates many people's notion of respect for their privacy, they may consider it as deviant behavior. Furthermore, agreeable individuals may feel that disclosing private health information, particularly when they have serious health issues, may lower others' opinions of them, thus, reducing the level of their social intimacy and harmony. Therefore individuals with this trait should, more than others, be sensitive about their health information.

H1b. Agreeableness is positively associated with perceived health information sensitivity.

Emotional instability or neuroticism is a trait commonly associated with being anxious, depressed, stressed, suggestible, volatile, and fearful [46]. This trait is considered negative in nature, and those who have this trait experience more negative emotions. For example, it is reported that neurotics experience more job dissatisfaction [56], whereas those with emotional stability are more fearless in hazardous situations [18]. In line with these results, we argue that people who are fearful and anxious are more disposed to being more nervous about their health information than others are and should, therefore, have higher health information sensitivity.

H1c. Emotional instability is positively associated with perceived health information sensitivity.

Conscientiousness is a trait that re<sup>fl</sup>ects individuals' extent of determination, will, and volition. Some of the terms used to describe this trait include being organized, dependable, thorough, and exacting [46]. Conscientious individuals have more precaution and foresight [18]. Such people are detail-oriented and action-oriented and undertake initiatives needed for successful completion of tasks. They are also less risk-oriented and less willing to get involved in unsafe situations [84,109]. They see deviant behaviors as more risky and hazardous [18]. Since they are detailed-oriented and investigate various consequences of a decision, they are able to identify, more than others, the potential hazards involved in disclosing private health information. Moreover, such individuals may view health information privacy invasion (as may happen when one is asked more information than may seem necessary) as a deviant and risky behavior that should be avoided. Thus, individuals with high conscientiousness should be more sensitive about their health information than others.

H1d. Conscientiousness is positively associated with perceived health information sensitivity.

Intellect is a trait described by terms such as imagination, curiosity, broad-mindedness, open-mindedness, and artistic sensitivity [8]. Individuals with high intellect are more rational and open to new ideas and possibilities. In our work we focus on intellect in terms of having new ideas [46]. These individuals use their intellect, logic and rationality to analyze and understand new situations. One could argue that people high on this trait also understand better how these risks are mitigated (such as the awareness about the chance of identity theft or an understanding of website's security measures) and do not worry as much about information sensitivity. Furthermore, such people may better realize the health bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of disclosing and be more cognitively driven than emotionally driven (e.g., driven by fear of embarrassment). Hence, they should be more aware of possible risks. However, they can analyze these risks more rationally and take actions to mitigate them as needed. Therefore, they should have lower levels of health information sensitivity than others.

H1e. Intellect is negatively associated with perceived health information sensitivity.

## 2.2. The personal health status part of personal dispositions

Apart from personality traits, personal health should also impact how sensitive people are about their health information. Tisnado et al. [104] examined the impact of demographics and health status on the level of concordance (agreement) between actual health records and self-reported health information. They found that the concordance varied signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly only by patients' health status. Those who perceived their health to be “poor” were more sensitive about their health information than others were. Patients are concerned that once their information is released, it cannot be made secret again [93] Extrapolating from those studies to online environment, perceived health status should also impact perceived health information sensitivity.

H2. Perceived poor health status will be positively associated with health information sensitivity.

## 2.3. The health information sensitivity part of personal dispositions

Information sensitivity contextualizes the impacts of personal traits and health status. Previous privacy studies found that consumer willingness to disclose personal information depends on the sensitivity of this information [69,76,81,89,97]. Wang and Petrison [110] noted that consumers' reaction to privacy threats depended on the type of information requested by marketers. Echoing similar thoughts, Milne [76] observed that when obtaining information from people, compliance rates vary according to the relative sensitivity of the information being requested. If this applies also to online health information, then the more sensitive this information is perceived to be, the greater the concern the person will have about revealing it.

H3. Perceived health information sensitivity is positively associated with privacy concern about disclosing health information.

## 2.4. Privacy concern: a disutility enhancer

Viewed from the Utility Theory perspective, privacy concern increases disutility. Privacy violations are undesirable. Having sensitive health information made public is such a case. There are many reasons why consumers may prefer to keep their private information private [66,76]. Among these are intrusion (invasion of privacy), disclosure (publicly disclosing embarrassing facts), false light (false public portrayal), and appropriation (use of a person's name without permission) [80,92]. Privacy concern negatively impacts the willingness to provide personal information on the Internet [27]. Hence,

H4. Higher privacy concern is negatively associated with intention to disclose health information.

Privacy concern is also negatively associated with trusting beliefs [31,58,69,106]. Using the privacy calculus model and social exchange theory lens, Dinev and Hart [27] demonstrated that willingness to provide personal information depends upon a risk– bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis in such a way that perception of higher risk lowers the trust and, hence, the willingness to disclose the personal information. Extrapolating from their study to the context of this study, it could be argued that heightened privacy concern should lower trust as well. Moreover, since trust is determined by feedback from experience [71], it follows that individuals who are wary of privacy concerns, at least in part on account of negative experience, should be less trusting. Hence,

H5. Higher privacy concern is negatively associated with trust in a website.

## 2.5. Trust: a disutility reducer

Being in a trustworthy environment is desirable for people. Trust is a valuable attribute in its own right in both personal relationships [12] and business ones [63]. It is a disutility reducer to know that the online vendor can be trusted and so one can put aside concerns about it would act inappropriately. This favorable assessment leads to increased intentions to interact with the vendor [6,38] and even to paying premiums [7]. While in some research trust increases behavioral intentions by reducing perceived risk [86], trust, as conceptualized here in accordance with marketing research, is an asset in its own right because of the nature of the relationship it creates [63]. Indeed, related to this study, the perceptions that the vendor is honest encourages consumers to provide personal information [27,75] because trust “can positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence willingness to disclose personal information” ([27], p. 66). Extrapolating to the context of this study, if a user trusts a vendor, then trust adds to the relationship because the user “knows” it can rely on the vendor to care about the user and its well-being, a positive aspect of the relationship. Hence,

H6. Trust in a website increases intentions to disclose health information on the website.

## 2.6. Previous online privacy invasion

One of the aspects contributing to disutility, and indirectly reducing utility, is previous online privacy invasion. Pavlou and Gefen [87] have shown that negative experiences create a negative sense of the market value, including about others who are unrelated to the negative incidents. As Culnan [23] and Stone and Stone have argued [103], previous privacy invasion could increase individuals' concerns for privacy in other scenarios as well. As the saying goes, once bitten, twice shy. Indeed, previous privacy invasions have been shown to be negatively associated with willingness to be pro<sup>fi</sup>led online [5].

This association can also be explained based on integrative social contract theory (ISCT) [28–30]. According to ISCT, members of a given community or industry behave fairly if their practices are governed by social contracts. Whenever customers provide their information to the online vendors, a social contract is implied. “A social contract is initiated, therefore, when there are expectations of social norms (i.e., generally understood obligations), that governs the behavior of those involved” ([16], p. 14). This social contract ensures that the collector will undertake the responsibility of managing consumers' personal information properly [16,24,77,89]. This acts as a disutility reducer.

In our context, privacy invasion can be linked to an implied psychological contract violation [87]. A psychological contract is considered breached, acting as a disutility enhancer, if consumers perceives that their privacy has been invaded by the “trusted” other party. Using the context of online marketplaces, Pavlou and Gefen [87] showed that a psychological contract violation by a seller will be perceived as a psychological contract violation by the community of sellers. This heightens the individual's perception of risks involved in dealing with the community of sellers. Thus, it could be argued that an online privacy invasion is viewed as a breach of psychological social contract, which would generate a perception of betrayal and injustice [83,95]. Such a perception could lead to negative assessments about unrelated other sellers in the same marketplace [87]. In the same vein, it could be argued that previous online privacy invasions could lead to increased risk beliefs associated with providing health information on other online sites, and this heightens the privacy concern of individuals who have experienced such invasions.

H7. Perception of previous online privacy invasion increases privacy concern.

H8. Perception of previous online privacy invasion increases risk beliefs associated with providing health information online.

## 2.7. Risk beliefs associated with providing health information online

Risk has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the possibility of loss” ([115], p. 4). Perception of risk can be related to the uncertainty caused by the possibility of the vendor's opportunistic behavior that can result in consumers' loss [36]. Pavlou [85] argues that “the distant and impersonal nature of the online environment and the implicit uncertainty of using a global open infrastructure for transactions have rendered risk an inevitable element of e-commerce” (p. 109). Using the privacy calculus model and social exchange theory lens, Dinev and Hart [27] demonstrated that willingness to provide personal information depends on a risk-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, in such a way that perception of higher risk lowers trust, and, hence, the decreased willingness to disclose personal information. Although researchers of trust generally argue that trusting beliefs enhance trusting intentions by lowering risk beliefs [69,75,87], the privacy calculus model demonstrates that risk beliefs about the overall situation can lower trust. There is no contradiction here. Mayer et al. [71] also explain that experience and the overall assessment of risk that it entails determine trust. Trust research has shown that trust then determines speci<sup>fi</sup>c risk assessments. Since the measure of the trust construct in this study relates to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c website, whereas the risk beliefs are associated with providing health information online in general, we argue that risk beliefs impact trust in the website. Hence,

H9. Risk beliefs associated with providing health information online are negatively associated with trust in the website.

## 2.8. Prior experience with the website

Previous research shows that prior positive experience leads to both trust in a website and to increased use [37–41,53,75]. In the context of health infomediaries, Song and Zahedi [99] demonstrate that past positive experience leads to favorable trusting beliefs. Replicating these <sup>fi</sup>ndings to this context,

H10. Prior positive experience with a website is positively associated with trust in the website.

H11. Prior positive experience with a website is positively associated with increased intentions to disclose health information on the website.

## 3. Methodology

We tested the research hypotheses using the student population at a Midwestern university. Students were recruited through an email announcement. The research study was conducted in a lab using online access. Participants were randomly assigned to view one out of three health websites. After viewing the website, the participants answered a few questions about the contents of the website and completed the instrument. The total number of collected observations was 367. Basic statistics of the data set appear in Table 1.

## 3.1. Instrument development

To ensure construct validity, we used items from existing scales wherever possible. We converted the items to semantic differential (0–10) so as to minimize common method bias. Table 2 shows the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of constructs and the sources of items. Tables A-1 and A-2 in Appendix report the instrument.

## 3.2. Analysis and results

We conducted exploratory factor analysis (Table A-4 in Appendix) to examine the discriminant validity. No cross loading above 0.40 [48] was observed. All items measuring each construct were loaded into the intended factor, supporting the discriminant validity of constructs. The Cronbach Alpha values are well above the cut-off point of .70 [82], and the composite factor reliability values are greater than the recommended threshold of 0.70 [54] (A-3 in Appendix).

The AVE values for the constructs exceeded the threshold of .50 [37], indicating that the constructs have captured a relatively high level of variance [4,33]. We also estimated the measurement model. In the con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (Table A-4, Appendix), factor loadings for all items were high, with t-values above the 2.54 threshold (ranging from 10.01 to 47.97) supporting the statistical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of factor loadings [78]. This result supports the convergent validity of the constructs. The square root of AVE (Table A-5, Appendix) for each construct was greater than the correlation values of the construct with other constructs, supporting the discriminant validity of constructs [33] Common method variance—shared variance among the items because they were all collected with the same instrument [90]—was examined using Harman's single factor test. This test involves exploratory factor analyses of all items to “determine whether the majority of the variance can be accounted for by one general factor” ([90], p. 890). The <sup>fi</sup>rst factor in this study extracted 14% of the variance, which is not large enough to indicate common method bias [52]. As Table 3 shows, the <sup>fi</sup>t indices of the measurement model were within the threshold, supporting model <sup>fi</sup>t [50].

Previous online privacy invasion has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on both privacy concern and risk beliefs, as hypothesized. Prior experience with the website also has a positive in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust, as well as the intention to disclose health information online. Risk beliefs have a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of privacy concern on intention to disclose information is direct. The mediated in<sup>fl</sup>uence of privacy concern through trust was not supported. The results show that privacy concern and trust both determine intention to disclose health information.

The relatively high values of $R ^ { 2 }$ for intention to disclose health information online (0.47) suggest the high explanatory power of the model. $R ^ { 2 }$ values of the intermediary dependent variables are also satisfactory, except for the $R ^ { 2 }$ values for risk belief and trust, which are 0.04 and 0.06, respectively. This could be because the antecedents of trust and risk belief are limited to the mediated in<sup>fl</sup>uences of personality dispositions, and not a catalog list of their antecedents.

## 4. Discussions

## 4.1. Summary of the results

The study shows that perceived health information sensitivity in<sup>fl</sup>uences privacy concern, which in turn is impacted by personality traits and health status. Personality traits in<sup>fl</sup>uence perception of health information sensitivity in different ways. Emotional instability increases this sensitivity, whereas intellect marginally decreases, and agreeableness marginally increases, information sensitivity. Emotional instability heightens the fears of possible negative outcomes, which leads to increased sensitivity. The hypothesis related to emotional instability is strongly supported, suggesting that those who are emotionally unstable are anxious and fearful about their health information and have higher sensitivity about such information.

Agreeable individuals, as hypothesized, also have higher health information sensitivity, but the hypothesis is only mildly supported at p <.10 level. Agreeable individuals might be more concerned about possible undesirable social consequences of disclosing their health information and, hence, have more health information sensitivity. Individuals with high scores on the intellect personality trait in our data have, as hypothesized, less health information sensitivity (with p < .10). This suggests that the intellect trait may counter unfounded fears, giving individuals a higher perception of control over risks and mitigations of risks, which, in turn may enable them to have a rational and sympathetic assessment of potential outcomes.

Surprisingly, we did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any relationship between extroversion and perceived health information sensitivity. Extroverts generally are forthcoming and are perceived as open in sharing their personal information in social interactions. However, it appears that private health information may carry a potential social stigma that makes extroverts less open to sharing such information, possibly because they may fear reduced social acceptability. Interestingly, while extroverts are reported to be relatively more open about revealing their private information in face-to-face interactions, in leaner online settings they apparently are less forthcoming. This is an important <sup>fi</sup>nding because it may indicate the need to distinguish the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality traits in the context of different communication settings. This requires further investigation.

We did not <sup>fi</sup>nd any signi<sup>fi</sup>cant relationship between conscientiousness and perceived health information sensitivity. DeNeve and Cooper [25] argued that conscientiousness people are more likely to feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their lives. These people set higher goals for themselves and tend to achieve more in work settings [8,9]. Instead of getting concerned by higher goals, conscientious people believe in their efforts. Perhaps it is their tendency to feel satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with their lives. These people experience “<sup>fl</sup>ow” more often [22]. They enjoy their work. They set goals high but consistently improve their skills as well and, hence, experience more satisfaction and happiness in their work and life in general [25]. Research has shown that those who are satis<sup>fi</sup>ed experience less stress and have low anxiety levels as well [22]. Perhaps their low anxiety levels keep them from having serious health issues and becoming too sensitive about their health information. The negative correlation between health status and conscientiousness (Table A-5 in Appendix) shows that, indeed, conscientious individuals in our data set have fewer health issues, which may counteract their reasons to avoid disclosing such information due to potential risks in such an action.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed that poor health status heightens information sensitivity, which, in turn, in<sup>fl</sup>uences privacy concern.

Understandably, less healthy individuals may be more concerned that disclosing their health information could damage their status, employment opportunities, or social standing. Hence, realizing the importance of personal dispositions is the second main contribution of this study. In this regard, this study is one of the <sup>fi</sup>rst to provide a list of constructs related to personal dispositions in the context of disclosing personal health information and is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to tie these to disutility.

Interestingly, the lack of support for H5 indicates that the disutility of privacy concern is unrelated statistically to the utility of trust. Disutility enhancers and disutility reducers seem to operate separately. This supports the overall perspective of Utility Theory in which various disutility reducers and disutility enhancers combine independently to create overall utility. This is in line with the additive form of utility function that is commonly used in utility analysis [60].

The results also show that having previously experienced privacy invasions is an antecedent of both disutility enhancers and disutility reducers—increasing privacy concern and decreasing trust through its impact on risk beliefs. Our results related to personal experiences and risk beliefs not only replicate and con<sup>fi</sup>rm previous <sup>fi</sup>ndings but also shed some interesting light on the ways different personal experiences increase or decrease the disutility of disclosing information online. The negative experiences related to previous invasions of privacy increase privacy concern, being a disutility enhancer, whereas previous positive experiences with the website increase trust, being a disutility reducer. This is consistent with other research on trust (e.g., [44]). A previous privacy invasion increases the awareness and perception of risk, which, in turn, negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uences trust, thus lowering the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the disutility reducer. Thus, this negative experience increases the disutility in two ways: by increasing privacy concern (increasing the disutility enhancer) and by decreasing trust (decreasing the disutility reducer). Similarly, a positive previous experience with the website has a double in<sup>fl</sup>uence. It increases the disutility reducer (trust), and acts as a disutility reducer itself by positively in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the decision to disclose information.

We also investigated the direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality and poor health status on risk belief, trust, and privacy concern as related to health websites in three post hoc analyses. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst post hoc analysis, we added personality dimensions and poor health status as antecedents of risk beliefs. Four dimensions (extrovert, agreeable, emotionally unstable, and conscientious) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and the $\mathtt { R } ^ { 2 }$ value for risk beliefs increased from 0.04 to 0.11, indicating that personality explains 7% of the variations in risk beliefs. Agreeableness had highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive effect on risk beliefs $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , supporting the assertion that agreeable individuals avoid risky conditions. The other three dimensions were also signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p<0.05), with extroversion having a negative in<sup>fl</sup>uence on risk beliefs.

In the second post hoc analysis, we added personality dimensions and poor health status as the direct antecedents of trust in the conceptual model. The $R ^ { 2 }$ value of trust increased from 0.06 to 0.16, indicating that personality dimensions could explain 10% of the variation in trust as related to health websites. Agreeableness (with positive sign, $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ and poor health status (with negative sign, $p { < } 0 . 0 5 )$ were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. It is interesting to note the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of agreeableness in increasing the risk beliefs of individuals as well as in increasing individuals' trust. It shows that while agreeable individuals could be more trusting, they also are more aware of risky situations.

In the third post hoc analysis, we added the personality dimensions and poor health status as direct antecedents of privacy concern in the conceptual model. The $R ^ { 2 }$ value for privacy concern increased from 0.19 to 0.21, indicating only a 2% increase in the explanatory power for privacy resulting from the direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality dimensions. Emotional instability $\left( p < 0 . 0 1 \right)$ , and agreeableness and conscientiousness $\left( p < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ ) were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant. In a survey research, Korzaan and Boswell [62] have also found that of all personality dimensions, only agreeableness in<sup>fl</sup>uences information privacy concern. The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of some personality traits on privacy concern has also been demonstrated by Junglas et al. [57].

The study provides insight on how personality affects privacy concern through increased information sensitivity, which, in turn, heightens privacy concern, although additional veri<sup>fi</sup>cation in other settings is necessary. The primary analysis combined with the post hoc analyses support the hypothesized in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personality and health status on the decision to disclose personal health information online.

## 4.2. Limitations

As is typical with this type of data, the study has limited generalizability since the participants were college students living in the Midwest region. However, it should be noted that the younger generation is more dependent on the Internet and is the primary population using online health services. Harbour and Chowdhury [47] reported that students use the Internet for health information more than the general public does. Their study also compared students with the general population and found that the number one reason students seeking health information is to <sup>fi</sup>nd out about a diagnosed condition, as opposed to general population whose number one reason of seeking health information online is to <sup>fi</sup>nd out about healthy living. This suggests that the use of a student population is appropriate for the study. Furthermore, the Digital Future Report [105] reported that the highest levels of Internet use are by age group 24 or younger, which is the population our sample represents. Even though the younger population is generally believed to be healthy, they still have many reasons to be concerned about their health such as mental stress, alcohol abuse, smoking, poor levels of nutrition and physical activity, accidents, and sexually transmitted diseases.<sup>2</sup> Moreover, that the model is supported even with these data, where presumably the population because it is younger is less sensitive to medical-information breaches, testi<sup>fi</sup>es to the strength of the model. Even young, and therefore mostly healthy, online healthcare consumers are sensitive about their health information, especially so when they believe to have poor health.

## 4.3. Contribution to theory

The relationship between trust and behavioral intentions (in our case revealing personal health information) is well established. But that this trust is dependent on information sensitivity and its related privacy concern is new, as is that trust is indirectly related to personality through the effect of personality on information sensitivity and its related privacy concern. Both these contributions, while clearly important in the healthcare context, also have implication for trust theory.

The vast majority of online-trust studies have focused on what websites and online vendors (trustees) should offer and how they should behave to enhance visitors' trust. These are the factors that are extrinsic to website visitors (trustors). However, there is inadequate research on the factors intrinsic to trustors that could in<sup>fl</sup>uence the extent of trust in any given situation. We know from past research that trust propensity increases trust [37,74], but this study is the <sup>fi</sup>rst to expand the research to a broader context of personality traits beyond those directly related to trust (i.e., trust propensity). That different people will be more or less sensitive to information breaches because of their personality and therefore will be less or more trusting is also an important addition to trust theory that has hitherto not really differentiated among personality traits. Hence, the major contribution of this study is identifying trustors' dispositions as the intrinsic factors impacting online trust. This is new and provides trust theory with a dual perspective for trust antecedents—intrinsic and extrinsic. Trust is not only about the assessment of trustees and their behaviors, as previous theory and research have shown, but depends also on trustors' intrinsic characters and situations in terms of their personality, salient status, situational positive and negative experiences, and salient risk beliefs.

Zucker [116] has argued that process-based trust is an important component of trust formation. Process-based trust refers to the nature of the interaction and living up to expectation. The trustor's intrinsic factors contribute to the expectation formation in the trust process. This work's second contribution is identifying the process by which trustors' intrinsic factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence trust through information sensitivity and privacy concern.

Utility analysis is the foundation of decision making and is crucial in determining behavioral intentions. This study shows that privacy concern could counter the health service bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of disclosing personal health information online. This work casts trust and privacy concern as two opposing constructs—a disutility reducer and a disutility enhancer. We found that these two operate separately and in tandem without affecting each other. This result is consistent with the additive form of utility functions [60]—a commonly used form in the utility analysis. In other words, in $U { = } f ( B , C )$ in Eq. (1). The vector C has elements $C _ { 1 }$ (trust) and $C _ { 2 }$ (privacy concern). $C _ { 1 }$ and $C _ { 2 }$ are independent of each other. This is an important and new <sup>fi</sup>nding in that attempts to reduce individuals' privacy concerns will not directly lead to an increase in trust in the context of disclosing personal health information.

Considering the dimensions of personality, while the reason why some personality traits affect this disutility and others do not is still an open question, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do support the need to include personality in the utility analysis of disclosing health information online. The same applies to poor health status and information sensitivity, which, as other aspects of personal dispositions, play important and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant roles in increasing the disutility of disclosing personal health information online. This indicates the need to include treat health information as a special category of personal private information and to incorporate situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c experiences in the study of utility and disutility enhancers.

On the issue of perceived risk, previous research has shown that both perceived risk and trust in<sup>fl</sup>uence behavior intention, and propensity to trust reduces these perceived risk beliefs [99]. One can view perceived risk beliefs as another disutility enhancer, in addition to privacy concern. However, the effect of this disutility enhancer is mediated by trust.

The notable roles that positive and negative experiences play in directly and indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uencing disutility enhancers and reducers sheds light on another aspect of personal disposition: the contextspeci<sup>fi</sup>c personal disposition. This adds to previous research that showed how previous experience changes positive decision making [43]. Prior experiences shape individuals' attitudes and form their dispositions with respect to a given context or circumstance. For example, painful memories from an incident of privacy invasion (such as online disclosure of social security information that had led to identity theft) could shape individuals' beliefs about their vulnerability in the online environment. Such an experience could lead to a negative disposition regarding the disclosure of sensitive information online to other sites. This disposition is situation speci<sup>fi</sup>c, since the same individuals may not be as apprehensive in disclosing their information in an of<sup>fl</sup>ine environment, say, to their employers. Context-related positive and negative experiences (experiences related to using the website and to privacy invasion) indicate situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c dispositions of individuals. This is in line with Utility Theory in that context-free dispositions (such as personality) as well as context-dependent dispositions (such as positive and negative experiences and risk beliefs) in<sup>fl</sup>uence individuals preferences and choices—disclosing or not disclosing personal health information, in this case. In other words, there may be reasons why the vector P in Eqs. (2) and (3) should be divided into two sub-vectors: (i) elements relating to situation-independent dispositions (information sensitivity, personality, and health status) and (ii) elements relating to situation-dependent dispositions (negative and positive experiences and risk beliefs).

## 4.4. Implications for practice

This study suggests that taking an approach of “one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all” personalities as implicitly taken by previous research may not be the optimal approach. Rather, the study supports the understanding that website presentation and content should be adjusted to what is known of the personality and situation of the user. Tailoring content and presentation in this way may add to usability and increased motivation to disclose information online when needed.

The identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the intrinsic factors and the mediating role of information sensitivity and related privacy concern provide a number of potential courses of actions for online health providers to boost their visitors' trust through customization and personalization of their websites. First, visitors with poor health may be more sensitive about their private information, have more privacy concern, and, therefore, need greater assurances that their health information will be treated with con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality and security. Health websites could be personalized to offer such reassurances as soon as online health providers evaluate individuals' health status. This could be achieved, for example, by indirectly asking users about their perceived health status. Second, if the personality of users could be evaluated either by administering a simple survey or by deducing from visitors' online behaviors through data mining, this information could help in evaluating visitors' information sensitivity and personalizing the website to provide the assurances needed to counter the information sensitivity and related privacy concerns. Another way for dealing with high levels of information sensitivity is through relying on sociallybased traits, a critical aspect related to trust in our data. Opportunities for social interactions on context sensitive websites could bolster trust in some customers, depending on their personality, while strategies to reduce information sensitivity (such as legal and emotionalsupportive assurances) could counter personality traits that re<sup>fl</sup>ect other customers' heightened personality-created anxieties. The signi<sup>fi</sup>cant double role of positive and negative situation-speci<sup>fi</sup>c experiences shows that in pro<sup>fi</sup>ling customers in order to serve them better, managers need to collect data about customers' experiences since the nature of experiences is one of the major distinguishing factors in customers' decisions to disclose their personal health information online.

## 5. Conclusion

The results extend privacy and trust literature by showing that personal dispositions should be taken into consideration when examining privacy concern and behavioral intentions to disclose health information online. This extension is supported by Utility Theory and the understanding that behavior choices should depend not only on the circumstances but also on individuals' dispositions and experiences. Adding personality traits, a central theme in social psychology, to MIS research opens up new and interesting avenues that future research could explore. As the reliance on web-based systems for delivery of services increases, privacy concern related to disclosing various types of personal information online gains prominence. This research could be extended to examining personality and disutility within different contexts and different cultures.

Dr. Gaurav Bansal is an Asst Prof in Business Administration Department at University of Wisconsin-Green Bay. He earned his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, M.B.A. from Kent State University, Ohio and B.E. in Mechanical Engineering from MMM Engineering College, Gorakhpur, India. Dr. Bansal has published in Journal of Management Information Systems. His research was nominated for best paper award at the International Conference on Information Systems, 2008. Dr. Bansal has also served as minitrack chair for Americas Conference on Information Systems 2009. His current research interests are in the areas of internet privacy concerns e-commerce and trust, He is a member of AIS.
