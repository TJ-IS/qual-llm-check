# Information technology adoption and continuance: A longitudinal study of individuals’ behavioral intentions

Yuan Sun <sup>a,</sup>\*, Anand Jeyaraj <sup>b,1</sup>

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 30 January 2013 Received in revised form 15 June 2013 Accepted 11 July 2013 Available online 20 July 2013

Keywords: Information technology Innovation Adoption Continuance Diffusion

## A B S T R A C T

Extant research on information technology (IT) adoption and continuance has not adequately modeled the times of adoption by individuals. This study argues that individuals adopt an innovation at different times and are likely to be influenced by different factors over time. The theoretical models are empirically validated using data gathered at three points in time through surveys of 132 users of a new innovation. The results indicate that the innovation attributes and individual characteristics influence individuals’ intentions to adopt the innovation during the early stage, and the innovation attributes and contextual factors impact the individuals’ intentions during the later stage.

## 1. Introduction

The adoption and continuance of information technology (IT) innovations by individuals continues to be an important consideration for organizations. Whereas adoption generally refers to an individual’s decision to use the innovation for the first time, continuance refers to the individual’s decision to persist with the innovation well beyond its first use [7,25]. Prior literature has examined these phenomena quite extensively and there is a general consensus that individuals’ adoption and continuance intentions are impacted by the attributes of the innovation, the characteristics of the individual, and other contextual factors [e.g., 26, 29].

However, prior empirical research on technology adoption and continuance has not adequately accounted for the diffusion of an IT innovation. More specifically, individuals within a population may choose to adopt the innovation at different points in time and may be subject to different influences at the respective times of their adoption decisions [25]. Moreover, individuals may choose not to continue an innovation subsequent to adoption for various reasons [25]. Similar to adoption, the decision to not continue (and by extension, to continue) an IT innovation may be attributed to different influences as the innovation diffuses through the population. Thus, it may be inadequate to model similar effects for all individuals within a population without allowing for the different times of adoption [6].

This study seeks to answer the following research question: What factors influence individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue an IT innovation, subject to its diffusion over time? Using data from surveys administered at multiple time points to individuals in the same population, we apply regression analyses to identify factors that are salient in shaping individuals’ decisions over time. Our research provides preliminary evidence on the importance of explicitly modeling differences in factors that influence the adoption and continuance of IT innovations over time.

## 2. Theoretical development

When an IT innovation becomes available or is deployed, individuals within a social system adopt it at different points in time. Individuals could be categorized as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, and laggards depending on the time they adopt the innovation [25]. One way to conceptualize such differences in the times of adoption is to consider the diffusion cycle of the innovation, generally known to resemble the S-curve based on the cumulative rate of adoption by individuals. There is some understanding that the rate of adoption is accelerated when a ‘‘tipping point’’ is reached [5,25]. Prior to the tipping point, the rate of adoption is typically slow and adoption decisions are primarily made by a relatively smaller number of individuals known as innovators and early adopters, who constitute approximately 2.5% and 13.5% of the population, respectively. Subsequently, the rate of adoption is accelerated considerably and is driven by a larger number of individuals, known as the early majority and late majority, comprising approximately 34% and 34% of the population, respectively. Laggards represent the remaining 16% of the population, and they provide the final impetus for the diffusion of the IT innovation. The time period before the tipping point may be viewed as the ‘‘early stage,’’ and the time period following the tipping point may be treated as the ‘‘later stage’’ of diffusion.

Diffusion represents the cumulative decision to adopt an innovation by individuals within a population. Innovations are known to diffuse through a population due to two types of influences – mass media communications and interpersonal relationships. Mass media influences interpreted by innovative individuals generally drive diffusion during the early stage of an innovation’s life whereas interpersonal influences drive diffusion during the later stage [25]. Based on this, it can be surmised that the innovators and early adopters adopt the innovation during the early stage whereas the early majority, late majority, and laggards adopt the same innovation during the later stage [1,8].

The innovators and early adopters are generally innovative and technology-savvy, and they possess the ability and expertise to evaluate the innovation on their own and adopt new technologies without assistance from others. During the times when innovators and early adopters decide to adopt an innovation, the innovation is new and there is not much information about the usefulness or effectiveness of the innovation that they can gather from others – in other words, innovators and early adopters base their adoption decisions on their own expertise or abilities and personal evaluations of the innovations [25]. Moreover, there may not be opportunities for the adopters to gain expertise or assistance prior to adoption due to the newness of the innovation.

The early majority, late majority, and laggards may not possess expertise or savvy in dealing with an innovation compared to innovators or early adopters. These individuals may rely on evaluations provided by prior adopters regarding the innovation and are likely to attach importance to word-of-mouth communications pertaining to the innovation. Thus, when the early majority, late majority, and laggards decide to adopt the innovation, it may be possible for them to obtain information about the innovation through word-of-mouth from early adopters or innovators [25]. Further, they may need assistance and interventions from others to counter their knowledge barriers prior to adoption.

Thus, individuals making the decision to adopt an innovation during the early stage are likely to be influenced by different factors than individuals who adopt the innovation during the later stage [e.g., 11]. During the early stage, adoption decisions are likely to be driven by the attributes of the innovation and the individuals’ own characteristics. This is because innovators and early adopters consider themselves innovative or possess sufficient expertise, driving diffusion during the early stage. The decisions of innovators and early adopters are based on their own evaluations of the capabilities and advantages of the innovation. During the later stage, adoption decisions are likely to be driven by the attributes of the innovation and other influences in the social context because the early majority and late majority of individuals, who influence diffusion during the later stage, are likely to benefit from the wordof-mouth generated by prior adopters as well as other facilitating conditions within the social context.

Individuals confronting the spread of an innovation within a social system may exhibit two different responses – intention to adopt and intention to continue. Intention to adopt is a response from those individuals who have not yet adopted the innovation (i.e., non-adopters). This response may be observed during the early stage (due to the innovators and early adopters) as well as the later stage (due to the early majority, late majority, and laggards). Intention to continue is a response from those individuals who have already adopted the innovation (i.e., adopters). This response may be largely observed during the later stage as adopters decide to continue or discontinue the innovation.

Based on these arguments, we put forward the following propositions (P1 through P3) that form the basis for the theoretical models shown in Fig. 1.

P1: Innovation attributes are likely to influence an individual’s decision during both early and later stages

P2: Individual characteristics are likely to influence an individ ual’s decision during the early stage only

P3: Contextual factors are likely to influence an individual’s decision during the later stage only

## 2.1. Innovation attributes

Rogers (2003) proposed that adoption is determined by several innovation attributes [25], of which three – relative advantage, complexity, and compatibility – are salient [28]. Relative advantage refers to the perceived benefits of adopting an innovation, relative to other alternatives, and is similar to the notion of perceived usefulness in technology adoption research [13]. Complexity represents the difficulty in adopting an innovation, which may be considered as the inverse of perceived ease of use in technology adoption research [13]. Compatibility describes the fit between the technology and the adopter’s work, needs, and preferences and is related to the notions of work compatibility in predicting technology adoption [22,27]. Consequently, we include perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and work compatibility as the innovation attributes in this study.

All three innovation attributes are expected to exert a positive influence on the individual’s intention to adopt or continue the innovation. During the early stage, non-adopters (i.e., innovators and early adopters) are likely to consider the innovation attributes in their decisions to adopt the innovation. During the later stage, non-adopters (i.e., early majority, late majority, and laggards) may attach some importance to the innovation attributes whereas adopters are likely to evaluate the innovation in deciding to continue to use it.

## 2.2. Individual characteristics

The characteristics or dispositions of the individuals adopting or considering the adoption of IT innovations also play an important role in their intentions to adopt innovations. As summarized by [20], individual characteristics such as personal innovativeness, self-efficacy, and user experience or expertise are among the salient individual characteristics examined in technology adoption research. Personal innovativeness is defined as the degree to which an individual is willing to try any new information technology on his or her own [2]. Self-efficacy refers to the judgment of one’s capability to use a new information system [12]. Expertise refers to an individual’s skills and abilities that enable him or her to adopt innovations with relative ease [18]. Thus, we include personal innovativeness, self-efficacy, and expertise as the individual characteristics in this study.

All three individual characteristics are expected to positively impact the individual’s intention to adopt the innovation. During the early stage, non-adopters (i.e., innovators and early adopters) are likely to attach importance to their own characteristics in their decisions to adopt the innovation [10]. During the later stage, individual characteristics are not likely to be influential because non-adopters (i.e., early majority, late majority, and laggards) may not possess similar levels of expertise or have the confidence about their capability to use a new information system or be as innovative as the innovators and early adopters.

## 2.3. Contextual factors

Venkatesh et al. [29] proposed the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) by synthesizing prior empirical findings and eight theories. The UTAUT extends the original technology acceptance model (TAM) [13,14] in terms of including two contextual factors, i.e., social influence and facilitating conditions. Facilitating conditions are defined as the degree to which an individual believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support the use of the innovation [29]. Social influence is defined as the degree to which an individual perceives that others important to him or her believe that he or she should use the innovation [29]. The impact of facilitating conditions and social influence on users’ behavioral intention to use IT or usage behavior have been extensively tested and validated across a wide range of IT and organizational contexts both in the original UTAUT study [i.e., 29] and other empirical studies [e.g., 4]. Hence, we include facilitating conditions and social influence as the contextual factors in this study.

Both of the aforementioned factors are expected to have a positive influence on the individual’s intention to adopt or continue the innovation. During the early stage, contextual factors are not likely to be influential because social influence or word-ofmouth has not gained momentum, and facilitating conditions, such as training sessions or help desks, may not have been routinized yet. During the later stage, non-adopters (i.e., early majority, late majority, and laggards) may be influenced considerably by social influence and facilitating conditions, whereas adopters may reconsider their continuance of the innovation based on word-of-mouth generated by peers and facilitating conditions that may have been established to ease the use of the innovation.

We propose that innovation attributes and individual characteristics drive adoption in the early stage, whereas innovation attributes and contextual factors drive adoption and continuance in the later stage. Moreover, we propose that individual characteristics will not be influential in the later stage due to the low levels of expertise and innovativeness possessed by individuals comprising the early majority, late majority and laggards. Further, contextual factors are likely to exert a stronger influence on individuals during the later stage. Fig. 2 shows the detailed theoretical models of our research.

## 3. Research methods

The research model requires the context to be supportive of the voluntary adoption and use of information technologies. An excellent opportunity presented itself at a Chinese university, which recently deployed the Blackboard course management system for the first time on its campus for students and faculty. The Blackboard system offers many features for managing content (e.g., course modules), management (e.g., assignments), communications (e.g., discussion boards, mail), and personnel (e.g., students). The Blackboard system enables faculty to post course-related documents at a common repository that may then be accessed by students, and enables students to submit their deliverables to the instructor or communicate with others taking the course. The university did not mandate use of the Blackboard system – some faculty members adopted it and introduced it to students as an alternative to existing systems, and other faculty members did not adopt the system at all.

One of the co-authors, in his role as an instructor, made the decision to introduce the Blackboard system as an alternative to existing systems for undergraduate students taking his course. (The existing system was essentially an email system, i.e., students could choose to log in using one email address to obtain course-related documents and send their course-related deliverables to another email address, both of which were managed by the instructor. If students had to communicate or collaborate with others but were unable to do so in person or by telephone, they used the email system.) To facilitate the process, the co-author announced the Blackboard system to students as just another alternative for completing course-related activities, including obtaining course documents, submitting deliverables, and communicating or collaborating with others. Throughout the course (and the time of this research), students could access the old email system and the new Blackboard system for keeping up with the course proceedings. The instructor kept both systems current, such that students could use either of the systems, depending on their personal preferences.

Data were collected from students through a questionnaire at three time points. The first round (i.e., Time T0) of data was collected immediately after the instructor introduced the students to the Blackboard system. The second round (i.e., Time T1) of data was gathered following the deadline for submission of the first assignment, which was approximately 4 weeks after the introduction of the system (i.e., T1 = T0 + 4 weeks). The final round of data was completed after the deadline for submission of the second assignment, which was approximately two months after the previous round (i.e., T2 = T0 + 12 weeks). All data were gathered during the latter part of the 2011 calendar year.

The surveys were administered to all 200 students who registered for the instructor’s course during a single semester. Participation in the research was voluntary, and there were no incentives for students to complete the survey instrument. The number of responses varied in each round of data collection – 184 responses at T0, 163 responses at T1, and 179 responses at T2. A total of 141 students completed the surveys at all three-time points. We excluded nine surveys due to missing data. Data analysis was based on the surveys completed by 132 students at all three time points. Thus, the effective response rate was 66%, based on the number of respondents included in the data analysis.

All constructs were measured using three items on a 7-point Likert scale. The items were extracted from previously validated instruments found in prior literature and modified to fit the research context for the Blackboard system [e.g., 2, 12, 13, 27, 29]. Appendix A lists the items used at time T0 and Appendix B shows the items used at times T1 and T2.

## 4. Results

Table 1 shows the descriptive statistics and reliabilities of the items for variables as well as the correlations between the variables. Panels A, B, and C of Table 1 show the relevant statistics for T0, T1, and T2, respectively. The reliabilities of all constructs measured using multiple items are above the recommended threshold of 0.70 [23]. The mean values are computed as the average of the respective three items for all multi-item constructs.

All 132 respondents were non-adopters at T0. Of the 132 respondents, 19 individuals had become adopters by T1 and 43 individuals had become adopters by T2 (of which 27 individuals were new adopters between T1 and T2); 86 individuals had not yet adopted the Blackboard system and three individuals had discontinued using the system at the time the data collection campaigns ended. Fig. 3 shows a visual depiction of diffusion within the population. The 19 individuals who had become adopters by T1 represent 14.4% of the population, which is comparable to the innovators (2.5%) and early adopters (13.5%) suggested by the literature on diffusion. The 27 individuals who had become new adopters by T2 represent approximately 20.5% of the population and may be considered the early majority. Our research was terminated when approximately 65% of the population (i.e., 86 of 132 individuals) continued to be non-adopters.

We examined the differences between adopters and nonadopters based on their adoption behaviors reported at T1 and T2 (Table 2). Specifically, we used a t-test to determine differences in means of the study variables at the previous time period depending on the adoption status at any given time period. We classified the individuals as adopters and non-adopters based on their status at T1 and examined their reports for innovation attributes, individual characteristics, and behavioral intentions at T0. Panel A of Table 2 shows that adopters and non-adopters at T1 were significantly different in their assessments at T0 – adopters possessed greater levels of personal innovativeness, expertise, and self-efficacy and also perceived the innovation to be easier to use and more compatible with their needs than non-adopters. Perceived usefulness was the only measure that did not show significant differences between adopters and non-adopters, which is an indication that both groups of individuals perceived the innovation to be similarly useful. We also classified the individuals as new adopters and non-adopters based on their status at T2. Panel B of Table 2 shows that new adopters and non-adopters at T2 only had significant differences in their assessments at T1 for two contextual factors: facilitating conditions and social influence, and their behavioral intentions. The innovation attributes and individual characteristics did not show significant differences between adopters and non-adopters, except for expertise (which was marginally significant). These results show that non-adopters may be influenced by innovation attributes and individual characteristics in the early stage and by contextual factors in the later stage when faced with the adoption of a new innovation.

Thus, the results offer considerable support for our research propositions that different factors influence adoption behaviors during the early and later stages of the diffusion cycle. Specifically, we found that innovation attributes and individual characteristics influence adoption in the early stage, whereas innovation attributes and contextual factors – but not individual characteristics – drive adoption in the later stage.

## 5. Discussion

This study was initiated with the aim of identifying the types of factors that impact individuals’ intentions regarding the adoption and continuance of IT innovations. The results indicate that the innovation attributes (i.e., perceived usefulness and work compatibility) influence individuals’ intentions to adopt an innovation during the early stage and their intentions to adopt or continue the innovation during the later stage. The results also show that individual characteristics (i.e., personal innovativeness) affect individuals’ intentions to adopt an innovation during the early stage, and contextual factors (i.e., social influence) impact the individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue the innovation during the later stage. Together, these findings provide excellent support for the three basic propositions that guided this research.

Among the three innovation attributes examined in the study, perceived usefulness and work compatibility were found to have significant effects on individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations. This is consistent with similar findings in prior literature. That is, the usefulness and compatibility of the innovation are relevant in both the early and later stages – in other words, individuals evaluate the innovation regardless of whether it is for first time use or continued use. Perceived ease of use was found to be non-significant in both the early and later stages. This may be consistent with some prior research that found perceived ease of use to affect individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue through perceived usefulness [e.g., 17]. In other words, perceived ease of use enhances the usefulness of the innovation but does not by itself serve as an incentive for adoption or continuance. This finding offers initial evidence that individuals may not be averse to complex ITs as long as they are useful and compatible with individuals’ needs and expectations.

Of the three individual characteristics in the study, personal innovativeness had a significant effect on individuals’ intentions to adopt an innovation, consistent with prior findings [e.g., 2]. However, self-efficacy and expertise were found to be nonsignificant. While somewhat surprising, it is possible that selfefficacy and expertise may be more closely related to an individual’s usage of an IT innovation rather than to the individual’s intention to adopt. Taken together with the significant effect of personal innovativeness, it is conceivable that intention to adopt (but not continue – there is no expectation that personal innovativeness is salient during the later stage) is primarily determined by the extent to which an individual is innovative enough to evaluate, try, and adopt new IT innovations. This finding offers initial evidence that expertise and self-efficacy may not be sufficient for adoption of IT innovations.

In regard to the two contextual factors examined in this study, facilitating conditions was found to be non-significant, while social influence was significant on individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue in the later stage. This finding is consistent with the UTAUT model proposed by Venkatesh et al. [29] and with other studies [e.g., 4] and is possibly due to the direct effect and relevance of facilitating conditions for IT usage, rather than the intention to adopt or continue. The impact of social influence is consistent with expectations and shows that individuals attach greater importance to social influence in their decisions during the later stage. Taken together, the results show that social influence, rather than facilitating conditions, is relevant during the later stage in shaping individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations. This finding offers initial evidence that facilitating conditions may not determine individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations in the later stage; social influence becomes a major consideration for individuals.

In summary, innovation attributes serve as enduring considerations for individuals in their adoption and continuance decisions over the early and later stages. Beyond innovation attributes, personal innovativeness (reflecting willingness to evaluate and experiment with IT innovations) determines individuals’ intentions to adopt IT innovations in the early stage, whereas social influence (e.g., word-of-mouth on the IT innovation through interpersonal relationships) determines individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations in the later stage. These findings show that diffusion-related effects should be explicitly modeled in studies that examine the adoption and continuance behaviors of individuals.

## 5.1. Limitations

The findings of this study must be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, our research was conducted in a single context, i.e., the Blackboard system adoption by students at a Chinese university. Although a meta-analysis by King and He [19] confirmed the value of employing students as surrogates for professionals, future studies could validate our results in other contexts, such as adoption of enterprise systems by professionals. Second, our study collected longitudinal data over three points in time; future studies could collect data at additional time points to validate our findings. Third, the social influence construct in this study is based on social psychology theories, such as the theory of reasoned action (TRA) [15] and the theory of planned behavior (TPB) [3]; hence, it cannot capture how social influence is transmitted through different types of influence mechanisms (e.g., direct persuasion and social comparison) [9]. Future studies could apply social network analysis to model and examine social influence mechanisms [30]. Finally, because all of our items were measured using a self-reported questionnaire, our findings may be susceptible to common-method bias [24]. Future studies may combine some measures of objective data with the survey data to minimize common method bias.

## 5.2. Implications for research

This study has several important implications for future IT usage research. First, it validates the importance of incorporating innovation attributes, individual characteristics, and contextual factors when investigating the individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations over time, consistent with prior research [e.g., 26, 29]. Second, our results indicated that individuals are subject to different influences at the respective times of their adoption or continuance decisions. Individuals are subject to innovation attributes and individual characteristics during the early stage, and innovation attributes and contextual factors during the later stage. These findings imply that contextual factors play an important role during the later stage and that early adopters may not be susceptible to social influence. Finally, IT usage studies have often been criticized for employing crosssectional designs [21]. Because the individual’s perception and intention can change over time, it is important to measure behaviors over time, as measured in this study.

## 5.3. Implications for practice

For practitioners, our study suggests that organizational managers can focus on innovation attributes because they exert a sustained impact on the individuals’ intentions to adopt or continue IT innovations during both the early and later stages. Our study shows that perceived usefulness and work compatibility are critical innovation attributes. Hence, IT should be customized to the specific needs of users and be designed to have utility for users, and organizational managers should gainfully use the innovation attributes in planning IT deployment. Our study finds that personal innovativeness influences intention to adopt during the early stage and social influence affects intention to adopt or continue during the later stage. Therefore, when deploying a technology, mangers should target innovative individuals to adopt the technology and provide positive influence to others for rapid diffusion. Mangers should try to establish organizationwide support for the innovation and promote positive word-ofmouth reviews among users to influence individuals to adopt or continue to use the innovation.

## 6. Conclusion

In closing, this study proposed a model of information technology adoption and continuance, which incorporated varying influences of different factors over time. Our model was empirically validated using data gathered at three points in time through a field survey of 132 individuals on their adoption and continuance of a newly introduced IT innovation at a Chinese university. The results demonstrate the importance of modeling diffusion-related impacts in studies of adoption and continuance of IT innovations.
