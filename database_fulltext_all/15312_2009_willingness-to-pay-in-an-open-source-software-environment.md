## 1. Introduction

Increasing adoption of open source software and free software (OSS/FS) present a challenging business environment for established proprietary software producers. The overarching objective of the OSS/FS community is to promote a business model for the private provision of software as a public information good (Bitzer et al. 2004) and a source of significant cost savings (Galli 2001, Wheeler 2005).<sup>1</sup> The OSS/FS philosophy runs counter to current business practices of the proprietary software industry where software is regarded as a private information good and is subject to protection under copyright law.<sup>2</sup> Proprietary software producers who until recently grappled with widespread piracy problems now have to contend with competition from OSS/FS communities. This complex market scenario is forcing proprietary software producers to rethink strategies to jointly counter piracy as well as compete against substantially lower priced or zero-price substitute products (Wheeler 2005). Early efforts to prevent piracy included the elimination of a physical software distribution network (Burke 1995, Christensen 1997). More recently, however, the Internet has facilitated piracy by significantly reducing the costs and risks of physical transportation (Burke 1995). According to the 2006 Global Software Piracy report issued by the Business Software Alliance (BSA), a trade group that tracks software piracy rates around the world, 35% of the software installed on computers worldwide was pirated in 2005, representing a loss of nearly \$34 billion (Business Software Alliance 2006).

Piracy controls placed on software applications have often limited the flexibility of usage for a legal user, for example, installing software on multiple computers or sharing it with other potential users (Gopal and Sanders 1997, 1998; Haruvy et al. 2004; Chellappa and Shivendu 2005). As a result, efforts to enforce licensing agreements and limit the use of software applications have not been well received. An example is the negative response from consumers to Intuit’s decision to include copy protection features in their 2002 version of TurboTax software.<sup>3</sup> With the tightening of controls in the software industry, more users are considering alternatives to proprietary software.<sup>4</sup>

The extant literature has investigated the piracy of proprietary software and related information goods and the impact on seller profits (Besen and Kirby 1989, Bakos et al. 1999, Varian 2000, Sundararajan 2004, Chellappa and Shivendu 2005). Interestingly enough, continued research has presented a contrasting view that sharing also conveys a corresponding benefit: consumers are likely to be willing to pay more for information goods that can be shared or traded with others (Bakos et al. 1999). Liebowitz (1985) defines this increase in consumer willingness to pay (WTP) as “indirect appropriability” in that a firm is able to indirectly appropriate downstream revenue reproduction. Extensions to this stream of research have further confirmed the impact and importance of user base (Takeyama 1994, Conner and Rumelt 1991).

Although past research has focused almost entirely on the producers’ perspective, the consumers’ response to such strategies is not well understood. This study provides a first look at the impact of producer strategies to prevent piracy in the software market on consumers’ willingness to pay for a software product. Our primary objective is to better understand consumers’ reactions to changes in the software market introduced through the availability of OSS/FS alternatives and the concurrent use of protection strategies. In this study, we focus on the impact of an open source alternative and piracy controls on a commercial product, namely, Microsoft (MS) Office.

The willingness to pay for this study is evaluated in a controlled experimental setting. The main variables of interest are presence of an open source alternative to the commercial product and piracy control measures. The results indicate that open source alternatives have no impact on the willingness to pay for MS Office once we control for the value that consumers derive from MS Office. Interestingly, the findings support the use of piracy controls in commercial products even in the presence of open source alternatives. Preventive controls on piracy appear to be more effective than the social deterrence approach to piracy control. Finally, consumer attitude toward piracy and software firm profitability seem to influence willingness to pay in a significant manner.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: §2 presents the research background and hypotheses for this study; §3 develops the empirical model; §4 presents the results, analysis and limitations considered; §5 provides a discussion of our findings and suggests opportunities for future research. We conclude with the implications of this research in §6.

## 2. Research Background

Conventional wisdom suggests that piracy reduces profit for software developers and studies conducted by software industry associations (BSA, Software Information Industry Association (SIIA)) corroborate this view. However, other studies have indicated that a reasonable level of piracy can promote software sharing and adoption, thereby increasing firm profits (Takeyama 1994, Osorio 2002). In supporting this position, Slive and Bernhardt (1998) identify two distinct classes of users: business (institutional) and home (individual). Their work suggests that software developers may maximize profit by tolerating piracy from home consumers, most of whom have a relatively low willingness to pay. In this study, the focus is on scenarios from the consumer side through an analysis of preferences for information goods (Varian 2000).

The notion of network externalities plays an auxiliary role when evaluating protection strategies (Conner and Rumelt 1991). When a positive network externality is present, software piracy can actually increase firm profits. Subsequent research suggests that for certain types of software, where the word-ofmouth interaction among users and potential users is important to the growth of the user base over time, pirates play an important role in converting potential users into users of the software (Givon et al. 1995). As such, when strategically managed through the use of protection measures, piracy establishes the initial adopters of software, who in turn influence others to buy (Haruvy et al. 2004). Although we do not explicitly study impact of network externality, the context utilized in our study involves a product with known network externality properties.

A contingent valuation (CV) approach (Carson 2000) is utilized in this study to measure the willingness to pay. As is traditionally the case, the mean WTP is a measure of the potential value of the alternative under consideration, i.e., the amount of money forfeited for consuming the alternative. Thus, a change in the evoked set of alternatives can increase or decrease the mean WTP for the population. In this research, prevention and deterrence controls and presence of OSS/FS is assumed to alter customer choice based on the relative utility values under each condition. The WTP estimates may be interpreted in that a consumer is maximizing utility subject to budget constraints or that consumer is minimizing expenditure subject to a given level of utility.

## 2.1. OSS/FS Impact on WTP

There are two factors that influence the impact of OSS/FS on the consumer’s utility function: (a) the cost of acquiring OSS/FS, and (b) functional similarities between OSS/FS and proprietary software. Switching to an open source alternative is not always costless due to costs associated with media choice, upgrades, support, and other maintenance expenses. Despite the low cost associated with OSS/FS programs, market shares for OSS/FS programs are still substantially lower than one would expect (Deckmyn 2000, Mustonen 2003). The decisive factor for many purchasers has had little to do with price, features, or even file compatibility (Slive and Bernhardt 1998). From the perspective of business consumers, the cost of transition (product knowledge, training, rewriting applications) may far outweigh other factors. Intuitively, a free software alternative should reduce the average WTP of the consumer market, especially if OSS/FS is perceived as a close substitute. In the practitioner literature, comparative field studies on the relative ease of use of commercial and open source software reveal that less advanced users were more likely to be receptive to a move to open source software (Brooks 2004). This becomes particularly salient as software consumers typically use only a small set of features in any application. However, when switching costs (including learning, document format changes, or uncertainties) are considered, it is not easy to conclude that open office should reduce average WTP. As a result, we intend to test whether presence of OSS/FS lowers average WTP for proprietary software.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). The presence of OSS/FS significantly lowers WTP for proprietary software.

## 2.2. Piracy Control Strategies

Typically, piracy can be classified into three categories: soft lifting, which involves the illegal copy of software by individuals; commercial piracy, which involves the illegal sale and distribution of software copies; and, corporate piracy, which addresses copyright violations by business users (Mishra et al. 2005). Due to the incremental cost of piracy control, software producers have attempted to control piracy at the corporate level by enforcing licensing contracts and seeking legal ramifications against violations. However, legal deterrence is not practical at the individual consumer level due to the high cost of containment. For the purposes of this research, we make a distinction between preventive and deterrence controls. Preventive control is defined in terms of technology-based (hardware/software) control, whereas deterrence control can be classified into legal and social categories. In this study, we focus on software-based preventive controls and take a social orientation to deterrence control. In constructing the arguments for Hypotheses 2, 3, and 4, we focus on the potential impact on the “pirating consumers.” In essence, the mean WTP value will vary based on the relative utilities of the pirates with respect to the available alternatives (pirate, buy, or use Open Office).<sup>5</sup>

2.2.1. Preventive Controls. Software producers can adopt protection technologies that make piracy much more difficult (Conner and Rumelt 1991, Cronin 2004). Improvements in technology such as encryption-based software protection solutions have been increasingly successful in restricting the use of illegal software (Christensen 1997). Although hardware-based protection schemes (e.g., nonstandard disks) may be used, most protection strategies utilize software-based protections (e.g., online registrations to activate a purchased application). Additionally, to prevent unauthorized installations, software applications may use unique software license key to limit use to one computer. Gopal and Sanders (1998) argue that preventive controls force consumers to legitimately acquire software.

Research that investigates economic factors proposes that the goal of technology-based control strategies is to increase the difficulty and cost (e.g., search, acquisition) of engaging in acts of piracy (Gopal and Sanders 1997, Cronin 2004). Research findings indicate that, although preventive controls can be effective in preventing piracy, increasing the degree of copyright protection can be costly for the producer (Landes and Posner 1989, Sundararajan 2004). As preventive control measures increase the costs to a pirate, the true pirates may be forced to either buy the software product or look for other alternatives. WTP for legitimate users would either remain the same or it may reduce due to the additional restrictions imposed by the producer. The theoretical rationale for preventive controls can also be understood using moral and ethical frameworks. In a review of approaches to piracy control, Siponen and Vartiainen (2004) use Kohlberg’s (1981) theory of moral development as a framework to explain various approaches to piracy control. Kohlberg’s (1981) theory proposes six different stages of moral development.<sup>6</sup> Each stage is associated with specific behavior patterns of individuals, Siponen and Vartiainen (2004) also associate possible piracy control measures associated with each stage. Preventive controls such as the ones studied in Gopal and Sanders (1997), and Taylor and Shim (1993) are termed Stage 1 measures (punishment and obedience orientation). Preventive controls implicitly incorporate monitoring technologies and serve to highlight intellectual property rights of the producer. Thus, preventive controls can be effective at increasing the costs of pirating, while also making salient moral and ethical issues, thereby increasing the pressure on “pirates” to make legitimate software purchases. Prevention controls can therefore impact the behavior of the pirates and provide the basis for our second hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2 (H2). The presence of preventive controls increases WTP for proprietary software.

2.2.2. Deterrence Controls. As with preventive controls, the impact of deterrence controls is predicated on legal and moral considerations. Software publishers have also utilized deterrence controls to protect intellectual property rights (Gopal and Sanders 1997, 1998; Altinkemer and Guan 2003). Legal deterrence controls communicate the legal ramifications of piracy to discourage the criminal use of software and communicate ethical norms to make piracy morally unappealing (Cronin 2004). Additionally, using the Siponen and Vartianen (2004) framework, we can apply a social deterrence approach as a Stage 3 measure where it appeals to a moral development (good boy/nice girl) orientation. The underlying rationale with the social deterrence approach is that it can influence an individual’s actions in the context of the social environment within which the individual operates. Practical limitations of enforcing legal deterrence have limited the use of this approach at the consumer level. At the corporate level, however, legal deterrence is still considered a useful strategy against piracy (Mishra et al. 2005). Given the willingness-to pay-focus of this study, we only address the impact of social deterrence.

A number of studies have explored the ethical issues behind piracy (Harrington 1989, Glass and Wood 1996). Past research has supported a positive relationship between ethical behavior intentions, individual beliefs, and the corporate ethical environment (Wagner and Sanders 2001, Banerjee 2003). These findings suggest a higher likelihood of success for preventing software piracy through social awareness, which has led to the common recommendations for preventing software piracy by focusing on ethical education (Cohen and Cornwell 1989, Harrington 1989, Glass and Wood 1996, Harrington 1996). However, ethical training may not be the most appropriate remedy to address software piracy if the problem is not perceived as an ethical issue (Glass and Wood 1996). This is particularly salient when the accompanying software measures do not draw attention to violations of ethical norms. Therefore, software producers can build mechanisms that highlight possible unethical use of their products. It would make sense to extend and apply the prescriptions of the ethicsoriented software piracy studies to include social deterrence also as an approach to limiting piracy. Our knowledge of how well social deterrence approaches work in this context is very limited. Many behavioral studies on software piracy have proposed that piracy control solutions incorporate the social setting as well. This research attempts to investigate how consumers may react to social deterrence approach to piracy control. In their refinement of deterrence theory constructs, Grasmick and Bursik (1990) find “threat of guilt feelings or shame” and “embarrassment or socially imposed punishment” as important factors in crime deterrence. This view of deterrence clearly suggests that social deterrence is not solely contingent upon the severity or certainty of legal sanctions. Subsequent research has confirmed the importance of these two factors from a deterrence perspective in a number of scenarios (Tibbetts 1997, Cochran et al. 1999). This view is also resonated in Kohlberg’s (1981)

theory of moral development. The “threat of guilt feelings or shame” maps to Stage 3 of moral development (“good boy/nice girl” orientation) where a subject’s moral judgment is based on a need to avoid rejection and disapproval from the immediate social circle. According to Siponen and Vartianen (2004), Stage 3 solutions should aim to influence people’s actions through their social environment.

In this context, the role of social norms is especially relevant. Social norms literature has attempted to explain how norms and legal enforcement interact to drive legal obedience or disobedience. Social norms can often substitute for legal norms and legal sanctions, in turn, can leverage social norms to encourage society to obey the law (Tushnet 1998). Currently, intellectual property laws are generally not perceived to be fair and there is a lack of trust with legislative efforts (Jensen 2003). Empirical research on investigations of norm effects on copyright law is limited and there is a general lack of understanding of how norms impact consumer behavior. In a recent study, Depoorter et al. (2005) provided empirical support for increased backlash by subjects to severe copyright enforcement attempts on illegal music downloads. In this paper we address how social deterrence mechanisms can impact consumer behavior by utilizing the WTP framework.

Interestingly, social deterrence mechanisms are beginning to be adopted in the dissemination of digital documents and business software. For instance, Harvard Business School Publishing uses watermarks to protect its case studies and articles. Several software vendors such as NetPlay, HDRSoft, iRise, and others allow unlimited trial version downloads but insert watermarks to identify the use of the trial version. We test the impact of a similar social deterrence strategy in this research and hypothesize its impact on willingness to pay as follows.

Hypothesis 3 (H3). The presence of social deterrence controls increases WTP for proprietary software.

2.2.3. Combined Effect of Prevention and Deterrence Controls. Although prior research reveals that both preventive and deterrent measures control piracy (Gopal and Sanders 1997), some recent empirical evidence and anecdotal information points in the direction of a “backlash” against stricter piracy control measures. Prior research indicates that piracy protection raises the cost of pirating, causing some potential pirates to buy, whereas others would do without the product or switch to a cheaper alternative (Altinkemer and Guan 2003, Liebowitz 1985). When preference factors such as ease of use and shareability are lost, software with known network externalities can lose value previously gained through network effects (Nascimento and VanHonacker 1988).

There appears to be a generally negative sentiment among legitimate buyers on copyright protection measures. A non-profit organization, Digitalconsumer.org (http://digitalconsumer.org/) states in its website that

Recent changes to copyright laws have given increased power to the content industries at the expense of ordinary citizens. For most of the past 200 years, Congress and the courts maintained a careful balance between the rights of creators and the rights of citizens. Creators were given the sole right to profit from their works, but in exchange, citizens were given some degree of flexibility to use content that they owned. But over the past few years, that balance has shifted dramatically. The content industry now has unprecedented power in their ability to control the use of digital content, and consumers are left with almost no rights at all.

Therefore, even legitimate users may view excessive piracy controls as an invasion of their rights to use the digital product. In a review of the legal ramifications of copyright protection strategies, Katyal (2004, p. 227) asserts that

All of these different strategies share one thing in common: they rely on, invariably, private mechanisms of surveillance for their execution and control. And these techniques of surveillance—whether instituted by private entities, or public law enforcement—demonstrate copyright’s increasingly tenuous relationship with information privacy.

As described in related literature on the social impacts of legal sanctions (Fagan and Meares 2000), reaction to application of multiple control policies can produce punishment paradoxes when the control is perceived as unjust, harsh, or excessive. To this extent, punishment can be justified only if the harm that it prevents is greater than the harm inflicted on the offender (Walker 1991, Rotman 1994). The implications of excessive controls are only recently being studied in the context of intellectual property protection. Depoorter and Vanneste (2005) borrow the notion of social norms (Kahan 2000, Schultz 2006) to study the impact of excessive piracy control strategies with respect to peer-to-peer music file sharing. They find that excessive use of piracy control resulted in a backlash due to negative effect of existing social norms. The main conclusion from this literature is that control strategies should pursue “gentle nudges” as opposed to “hard shoves” (Kahan 2000). In discussing the implications of the application of moral development stages theory to software piracy, Siponen and Vartianen (2004) specifically address the dangers of overstepping an individual’s autonomy in applying piracy control solutions. Siponen and Vartianen (2004) view Stage 1 and 3 piracy control solutions as mainly psychological approaches that can impinge on individual autonomy and lead to “indoctrination.” As such, we expect that applying both preventive and deterrence control strategies simultaneously will have an adverse effect on consumers’ willingness to pay.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). The combined use of piracy prevention and social deterrence controls reduces WTP for proprietary software (in comparison to the settings where only a single control is used).

2.2.4. Joint Impact of OSS/FS, Prevention, and Deterrence Controls. The availability of OSS/FS challenges the proprietary software market because strictly “free” software can be obtained at no cost and OSS/FS is either free or available at a cost substantially lower than comparable proprietary software applications. In the presence of an OSS/FS option, the perceived use of intrusive and burdensome protection strategies such as recent registration schemes that proprietary software producers have imposed on legal, license-paying users has created resentment toward leading software producers and caused some consumers to consider viable software alternatives (Galli 2001). As such, consumers’ willingness to pay for proprietary software could be significantly reduced in an OSS/FS environment (O’Reilly 1999, Bhattacherjee et al. 2000). With users objecting to the “draconian new licensing conditions imposed by proprietary software producers,”<sup>7</sup> the availability of a viable alternative could position OSS/FS as valuable to users and reduce willingness to pay for proprietary software. This argument establishes the basis for the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 5 (H5). The presence of OSS/FS and piracy prevention controls decreases the WTP for proprietary software.

Hypothesis 6 (H6). The presence of OSS/FS and social deterrence controls decreases the WTP for proprietary software.

## 3. Assessing Willingness to Pay

Consumer preferences and reservation prices are typically analyzed as they are revealed in market settings. A variety of techniques such as conjoint analysis and logit models have been proposed to measure the reservation price or willingness to pay for products. These models require respondents to evaluate a product relative to other products that exist in the market. However, market data are not available for public goods or for new products that have not been launched or for products that are offered for free, as in the case of OSS/FS. In such situations, it is necessary to use a procedure that does not rely on market data. The CV method has been proposed in the environmental literature for such situations (see Hanemann 1985, Haab and McConnell 2002) and is one of the most popular methods for measuring and analyzing the value of public or reproducible goods (Wertenbroch and Skiera 2002).

## 3.1. WTP Model

To develop a framework for analyzing the willingness to pay, we define WTP as the amount of income forsaken by an individual to make him indifferent between not purchasing and purchasing. ## 3.2. Experiment Design

The experiment used a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ between-subjects design with MS Office as the representative proprietary software. The first factor, piracy prevention, was varied using two levels (high and low).<sup>8</sup> The second factor, social deterrence, was also varied using two levels (presence or absence). The third factor, availability of OpenOffice as an OSS/FS alternative, was also varied using two levels (available and not available).

3.2.1. Task Description. The basic experimental task for the subjects was to make a series of decisions on the amount the subject was willing to pay for MS Office. The survey instructions had to be slightly altered for each experimental cell (reflecting the experimental factor levels), whereas the WTP choice questions and their sequence remained identical. Participants were given appropriate visual (through PowerPoint slides and in the questionnaire) and verbal instructions for each scenario concerning open source availability, piracy prevention controls, and deterrence controls.

Because it is possible that students already would possess a copy of MS Office on their personal computers, the WTP question asked the subjects to consider a context where they were purchasing a new computer and were asked to state their willingness to pay for a new version of an Office product (in essence, the option to reuse any older versions of Office was excluded).

The introduction for the experimental task lasted 10–20 minutes. This was followed by brief questions intended to assess whether the subjects understood the task, for example, in cells where subjects were made aware of OpenOffice, a debriefing question before the WTP survey was “Do you understand that OpenOffice can be downloaded free of charge?” Once all subjects indicated their understanding of the experimental task, the WTP survey was administered.

3.2.2. WTP Elicitation. The WTP responses were elicited using a closed-ended contingent valuation

framework (i.e., closed-ended, where respondents make repeated choices of whether they would buy a good at a given price versus open-ended, where respondents are required to state their WTP for entire goods or for attribute level changes directly (Wertenbroch and Skiera 2002)). The bids required respondents to evaluate their willingness to pay for MS Office given repeated choices of whether the participant would buy the application at a given price (e.g., Would you be willing to pay \$70?). Subjects for the study were drawn from a population of undergraduate and graduate business school students at all levels and across the cells. All students were familiar with MS Office and understood its basic functionalities. At least one of the coauthors and a graduate student were present when the WTP survey was administered to groups of students in each experimental session (respondents were given instructions for a single cell in the experiment). Prices were presented as a dichotomous choice, incrementing above and below the typical selling price and beginning with an opening bid in each survey. Four distinct price ranges within the typical selling price of MS Office were utilized. Each subject randomly received bids corresponding to one of the price ranges. An important issue in contingent valuation surveys is one of optimal bid design. Clearly, the distribution of the chosen bids impacts the efficiency of the estimators (the bids enter as regressors and determine the variance–covariance matrix), and should therefore be chosen after careful deliberation. A number of studies have derived optimal bidding mechanisms (Hanemann et al. 1991, Alberini 1995, Kanninen 1995), but they all require some prior knowledge of the WTP distribution. This leads to a paradox because the entire purpose of these studies is to estimate the distribution in the first place. The consensus in recent studies is to utilize as much information as may be available or inferred about the distribution and to then set the bids around this inferred distribution; for example, these bids may be designed based on focus groups and pretests (Cameron and Quiggin 1994) or, in the case of new products, they may be based on the price of the existing product. We set the bid ranges for our study based on the student price for MS Office. The four price ranges are: \$45–\$70–\$90; \$70–\$90–\$120; \$90–\$120–\$150; and \$120–\$150–\$180. The bid starts at the middle (see Figure 2 for WTP survey questions). Based on the response, the next higher bid is presented if the response is “yes,” or the next lower bid is presented if the response is “no.” For example, in the price range \$45–\$70–\$90, if the opening bid is \$70, the next bid is \$90 for a “yes” response to the opening bid, or \$45 for a “no” response. If the response to both bids is “yes,” the follow-up question is “What is the maximum you would pay for MS Office?” If the response to both bids is “No” the follow-up question is “Would you pay anything at all for MS Office?”

Following the WTP bids, the subjects filled in responses to additional questions. Specific controls utilized to manipulate the factors are explained in the following sections.

3.2.3. Piracy Prevention Factor. For subjects in the cells involving the piracy prevention factor, the following verbiage appeared on the WTP survey:

You are ready to purchase a new computer. The computer is equipped with the latest version of the Windows operating system. However, you must acquire any additional application software such as MS Office, which includes Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and Access among others. In this scenario, MS Office has extensive piracy prevention controls in place. Multiple installations cannot be made from the disk. Online registration is required before using the software. A unique license key is required to operate the system. Please answer all questions under these conditions.

Additionally, during the briefing session, subjects were reminded about the piracy prevention controls in MS Office and were specifically asked if they understood the following conditions:

In the scenario we have explained just now, do you think it is difficult to make copies of MS Office software for your own personal use?

In the scenario we have explained just now, do you think you can borrow a copy of MS Office from anyone to install on your computer?

Once all subjects acknowledged their understanding of the experiment task they were allowed to fill in the WTP responses.

3.2.4. Social Deterrence Factor. The social deterrence factor was operationalized by informing the subjects of a watermark that would be inserted in any document created using a pirated version of MS Office. The subjects were informed that

If you are able to pirate, install, and use a copy of MS Office on your computer, any document you create or edit will be embedded with a large watermark that says, ‘PIRATED MS OFFICE USED TO CREATE THIS DOCUMENT,’ as shown along the left margin of the survey. All printouts and screen images will contain this watermark. Any person you share the document with will see this message (see Figure 2).

Additionally, we confirmed that the subjects understood the scenario by asking them if they agreed with the following two conditions: (a) If I use a pirated copy, all my documents will contain the watermark “PIRATED MS OFFICE USED TO CREATE THIS DOCUMENT,” and (b) If I use a pirated version, any person I share documents with will see the watermark.

3.2.5. Open Source Factor. Participants in cells evaluating OSS/FS were given two choices: purchase a computer with preinstalled open source software at one price, or purchase the same computer preinstalled with MS Office at a higher price. Subjects watched a 10 minute video presentation developed for this study that compared MS Office with OpenOffice. The video focused specifically on common usage scenarios and features of word processing in MS Word and OpenOffice Writer. A sample screen shot comparing the two applications is provided in Figure 3. The video included basic and advanced tasks that one would perform in a word processing application. The video closely followed step-by-step instructions from an introductory text book on MS Office (O’Leary and O’Leary 2003). A table showing keyboard shortcut commands comparison between MS Word and OpenOffice Writer was provided to the subjects as well. We posed the following two questions before subjects filled the WTP survey to ensure that the students understood the free availability of OpenOffice: (a) Do you understand that OpenOffice is a substitute for MS Office? (b) Do you understand that OpenOffice can be downloaded free of charge?

3.2.6. Covariates in WTP Survey. WTP is often impacted by individual attitudes and demographic characteristics. The WTP survey collected several covariates for later incorporation into the model. We collected demographic information including age, gender, employment status, years of experience using Office application, and number of hours of computer use per week. Subjects were asked if they were presently using an open source software (Freealt). Perception of OpenOffice was checked by a five-point scale item, “Using an Open Source alternative for MS Office will negatively impact my ability to complete tasks” (OSSNeg).<sup>9</sup> We also used to other measures of perceptions of MS Office—perceived usefulness of MS Office and years of experience using MS Office. Perceived usefulness scale items were adapted from the technology acceptance model literature (Davis 1989).

Attitude toward software piracy and software price has been found in previous research to be a significant variable in influencing intentions to pirate (Glass and Wood 1996). Collected data items included attitude toward sharing software with others and sensitivity of sharing to software price. Sharecont asked respondents to provide an estimate of their willingness to pay (on a five-point scale) for a shareware product (shareware is copyright protected and available for free; a new user is typically asked to send a small contribution (usually from \$10 to \$50) to the owner in return for its use). 3.2.7. Data Collection. A pilot study was conducted initially using responses from 75 undergraduate and graduate business school students. Pilot study subjects also provided feedback on the usefulness of the video, which resulted in some changes to the video on comparison between OpenOffice Writer and MS Word. The pilot study phase was used to test the bid ranges and questionnaire design as well. We initially tested a wider bid range (\$45–\$90–\$180) with the first group of subjects in the pilot study. We refined this range with the subsequent groups and tested the multiple bid ranges used in the final study. Additionally, minor modifications to phrasing of WTP questions were necessitated to clarify the sequence of the DBDC bids.

Data for the complete study were collected from a population of 1,076 undergraduate and graduate business school students. Sixty-three graduate students were evening MBA students, representing a small segment of the business community. Although most data were collected from multiple campuses of a large, public university system, a small comparison sample was collected as a test case from a smaller private university. There were 803 usable responses in the sample. In each session, we administered manipulations relating to one cell of the experiment. In the final sample, we tested whether the unequal samples impact the results by randomly eliminating responses in cells with larger samples. We found no difference in the statistical results. Therefore, we have retained the full sample for presenting the final results.

The final survey responses were first checked to see that respondents gave reasonable responses to the questions in the survey. In particular, we checked whether respondents followed the sequence of questions given in the dichotomous choice format. Responses that did not follow the sequence correctly were removed from the final sample.

Specific manipulation check items in the survey included checks on whether subjects understood the conditions of piracy control and deterrence control. Results from manipulation checks revealed a high level of transparency and confirmed that respondents understood the level of control captured by the scenarios.<sup>10</sup> There was a novelty element with respect to OSS/FS because a majority of the subjects were not currently using an open source application. The subjects who viewed the video comparison of OSS/FS and MS Office believed that open source applications were generally more difficult to use than proprietary applications. However, subjects typically believed open source applications were as reliable as proprietary applications. A large majority of subjects expected open source software to be comparable to proprietary software.

## 4.2. Study Limitations

As with any study of this nature, it is important to recognize the limitations of this research. CV methods, although often used to gather data to estimate WTP distributions, have received critical reviews from researchers. It is not known whether CV methods are truly incentive compatible (i.e., the dominant response is to bid one’s true WTP). Response incentive effects can occur when surveys prompt respondents to make inferences about the value of a good. Therefore, the external validity of the contingent valuation approach may be limited due to hypothetical responses, thus providing little incentive for consumers to truthfully reveal their WTP. Clearly, open-ended CV questions (such as how much are you willing to pay for MS Office?) have been shown to result in biased responses. Closed-ended DBDC questions such as the one used in this study have been the preferred method in CV literature (Hanemann et al. 1991, Carson 2000). DBDC methods yield substantial improvements in point estimates of WTP values by reducing the width of the confidence interval estimates (Carson et al. 2000). Additionally, given the experimental approach, the relative changes in WTP are of importance here.

It is possible that there is always a population of users who absolutely refuse to buy legal software, i.e., their WTP is zero (a subject with a zero WTP would answer “no-no-no” to the sequence of three WTP bids). To address any concerns of bias due to this possibility, we asked a series of questions on a subject’s intent when they responded to the WTP bids with “no-no-no.” Excluding these subjects from model estimations did not alter the nature of the results.

Respondents in this study are all undergraduate and graduate business students (laboratory setting). Although our data does contain a representative sample of full-time working respondents (including evening MBA students), results should be replicated using respondents from other population groups. However, despite being a student-based subject pool, the experimental task and context is reflective of a typical market situation for evaluating WTP (because students also are consumers of these products). Results from this study can be extended by collecting data from institutional consumers responsible for the purchase of software applications.

The social deterrence approach using the watermark is a hypothetical scenario that some subjects may not find credible because MS Office does not use watermarks. To prevent possible confounds, we carefully described the social deterrence scenario to the subjects with an example document. Additionally, we used manipulation check items to verify if the control worked. Manipulation check results indicate no major difference in the subjects’ perceptions of preventive and deterrence controls. We evaluate the impact of OSS/FS by comparing office productivity applications. This approach was necessary to evaluate software with known network externalities and one that consumers were familiar with. Given the limited exposure to OSS/FS in our experimental setup, it is possible that they did not fully comprehend the capabilities and usefulness of OpenOffice. However, our results reflect the lack of penetration of OSS/FS in the marketplace today.

## 5. Discussion

Although the cell means indicate that OSS/FS availability may reduce WTP, model estimations with covariates suggest that perceptions of usefulness have a greater impact on WTP. This is an important clarification on Hypothesis 1. Intuitively, it would be easy to conclude that a free alternative should reduce the average WTP of the consumer market, especially if the OSS/FS is perceived as a perfect substitute. However, when switching costs (including learning, document format changes or uncertainties) are considered, the impact of OSS/FS may be lesser. This may partly explain why OSS/FS products have still not penetrated the mainstream market segments.

The most effective piracy control strategy in an OSS/FS environment is the use of preventive controls without social deterrence. In the context of the moral development framework applied to piracy behavior (Siponen and Vartianen 2004), this implies that the Stage 1 solution (punishment and obedience) is more effective than the Stage 3 solution (good boy/nice girl orientation). Effect of punishment paradox is evident when preventive control and social deterrence are applied simultaneously. Our models strongly support the significant importance of social norms in copyright enforcement. The strong negative interaction effect of PC and DC is indicative of the consumer backlash to increased piracy controls suggested by social norms literature (Tushnet 1998, Depoorter et al. 2005). Interestingly, this finding resonates with the Siponen and Vartianen (2004) proposition that excessive application of Stage 1 and Stage 3 solutions could lead to curtailment of autonomy of individuals. Whereas from a producer’s perspective Gopal and Sanders (1997) find that deterrence is a more effective control strategy, in the context of the present consumer-focused study, we find that piracy prevention is more effective. It should be noted that we used a social deterrence mechanism, whereas Gopal and Sanders (1997) approach deterrence from a legal perspective. We find no significant joint impacts of OSS/FS alternative and piracy controls (i.e., no support for Hypotheses 5 and 6). This finding suggests that piracy controls can continue to be effective even in the presence of OSS/FS alternatives.

Although in this experiment the maximum retail price of MS Office indicated was \$180, on an average, subjects were willing to pay a price of \$98.95 for the application.<sup>13</sup> The fact that this price is less than the retail educational price for MS Office supports a conjecture that consumers may believe that software is overpriced compared to the utility derived from its use. Reasons for this finding could include underutilization of software application functionality, monopolistic pricing approach, and a belief that software is a public good.

Consumer responses indicate that WTP is strongly impacted by perceptions of profitability of the software producer. Monopolistic behavior has been studied extensively in microeconomics literature, with a particular focus on the pricing of information goods that can be shared and copied (Maskin and Riley 1984, Cabral et al. 1999, Varian 2000). The results of this study empirically confirm prior theoretically derived conclusions that, despite monopoly power, consumers would exercise some level of control over the software market by simply leaving the market (Conner and Rumelt 1991). This outcome would imply that control strategies for products with strong network effects could influence the size of the user base if software prices are above the WTP of the consumer. Note that these results also occurred when an OSS/FS alternative was not revealed, indicating that the threat to commercial software market leaders may be further amplified if a viable competitor enters the market space.

The benefits of digital property such as software are difficult to restrict to only legal consumers. These benefits are typically associated with a public good. By strict definition, a public good is a commodity whose benefits are not depleted by an additional user and from which it is generally difficult or impossible to exclude people, even if the people are unwilling to pay for the benefits (Baumol and Blinder 2003). Although software mimics the properties of a public good, the reality of development costs incurred by software producers positions proprietary software as a private good legally protected by copyright law.

The implication is that software developers are faced with the dilemma of protecting a private investment in software that operates under the auspices of public property. Because software typically exhibits properties of a public information good, market leaders for commercial software are faced with the need to protect an investment in intellectual property without the appearance of engaging in “anticompetitive” or monopolistic practices. Firms and government agencies both struggle with defining what actions constitute monopoly intent (Baumol and Blinder 2003). The problem that exists in defining monopolistic behavior is that vigorous competition and strategies to protect digital property may appear very similar to acts that undermine competition and promote monopoly power.

## 6. Conclusions

Knowledge of consumers’ WTP is necessary in estimating demand and optimal pricing schedules. Results from this study indicate that commercial software producers should respond to new market realities (i.e., presence of OSS) and not rely solely on piracy control technologies and strategies to preserve revenue and market share.

This study suggests that both piracy prevention and deterrence controls individually increase WTP for proprietary software. The impacts of both of these approaches, however, vary in their effectiveness. This is especially visible with the introduction of OSS/FS alternatives available to consumers. Additionally, our findings suggest that prevention controls have a significantly greater impact on WTP. As a corollary, the absence of piracy controls leads to potential encouragement of piracy, and thereby significantly reduces WTP.

Interestingly, our results demonstrate that when both piracy prevention and deterrence controls are applied simultaneously by software producers it can cause a consumer backlash as suggested by the current copyright social norms and the moral development framework applied to piracy. The empirical finding in this study implies that postconventional moral development solutions are needed to change the social norms before further increases in piracy control measures. Microsoft’s recent decision to take legal action against open source vendors and users may be an attempt to further differentiate their product by guarding patent rights over comparable features in open source applications. However, in light of the findings from this study, these controls may only add to the negative image and lower a consumer’s willingness to pay.

The significant impact of perceived usefulness of software on the WTP is particularly encouraging for commercial software producers. Interestingly, it may also imply that open source efforts should continue to focus on feature enhancements and usability issues. If open source initiatives attempt to piggyback on commercial products to extend their product features, it could have an impact on consumer willingness to pay for a number of reasons. There is a small minority of the user population which has a zero WTP for software—such users may not wish to adopt open source products piggybacking on commercial products for advanced features. There is the possibility that such commercial allegiances may alienate staunch open source product supporters as well. As open source efforts expand, further research will be needed to clarify the impact of these new developments.

An established market share may help alleviate some of the impacts of the free alternatives on commercial software producers. Considering these results, the use of effective piracy controls and pricing strategies can present a true assessment of the value of proprietary software and establish a level of trust by the consumer. Although tolerating piracy has been shown to increase profitability for new product introductions, this study considers a product with established network externalities where the protection of an established user base is necessary to maintain firm profitability. In the context of network externalities, it can be conjectured that piracy control remains instrumental in protecting intellectual property investments, even in the presence of an OSS/FS alternative.
