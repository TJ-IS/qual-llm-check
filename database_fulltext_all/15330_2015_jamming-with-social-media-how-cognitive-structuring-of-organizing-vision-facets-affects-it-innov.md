# JAMMING WITH SOCIAL MEDIA: HOW COGNITIVE STRUCTURING OF ORGANIZING VISION FACETS AFFECTS IT INNOVATION DIFFUSION<sup>1</sup>

Shaila M. Miranda Division of MIS, Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK 73019 U.S.A. {shailamiranda@ou.edu}

Inchan Kim Division of MIS, Price College of Business, University of Oklahoma, Norman, OK 73019 U.S.A. {ick881@ou.edu}

Jama D. Summers Department of Accounting and Information Management, University of Tennessee, Knoxville, TN 37996 U.S.A. {jsumme21@utk.edu}

Organizing vision theory is an institutional alternative to the economic-rationality view of IT innovation diffusion. Institutional theorists have called for more attention to cognitive processes and structures in order to understand institutional mechanisms. Our objective was to unpack the cognitive structure of an organizing vision to understand its role in the diffusion of IT innovations. We focus on the know-why component of organizing visions and on social media as an IT innovation. In a two-stage study, Stage I leveraged schema theory, the “orders of worth” framework’s six justificatory principles, and relational class analysis to discover the hierarchical structure of the social media organizing vision. This resulted in a view of the organizing vision as comprised of four schemas, which we conceptualized as visions-in-use, and ten nested business use cases, each comprised of different combinations of the six principles. Based on this understanding, Stage II explored how community appropriations of visions-in-use and business use cases from the repertoire provided by an organizing vision shape four facets of an organizing vision—coherence, continuity, clarity, and diversity—and how these facets influence diffusion of the IT innovation. We found that the two vision facets we surfaced— clarity and diversity—are essential to understanding diffusion and how and why coherence and continuity matter to diffusion. Much as the “vision” of a musical jam session emerges from players’ multivocal performances, an organizing vision emerges from community members’ multivocal discourse about an IT innovation. Just as a jam session depends on a structure of rules and individual player creativity, diffusion of an IT innovation depends on an organizing vision that offers prospective adopters a well-defined repertoire of moves to choose from, yet affords them the freedom to improvise.

Keywords: IT innovation, organizing vision, social media, schema, relational class analysis, quantitative grounded theory

## Introduction

“In a jam session, the players cooperate in the formulation of a joint, emergent vision” (Eisenberg 1990, p. 151)

An organizing vision is a socially constructed “community idea for the application of information technology in organizations” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 460). Organizing vision theory complements economic-rationality views of diffusion by drawing on institutional theory to explain “how new technology for information systems (IS) comes to be applied and diffused among organizations” through discursive processes (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 458). Prospective adopters’ reception of the organizing vision as coherent, plausible, important, and (dis)continuous then affects diffusion (Marsan, Paré, and Beaudry 2012; Ramiller and Swanson 2003). Yet, beyond the broad knowledge categories of an organizing vision (i.e., know-what, know-how, and knowwhy; Wang and Ramiller 2009), we know little of what organizing visions contain that shapes the structure of the visions and how adopters come to respond to visions along dimensions such as coherence or continuity.

Institutional theorists (e.g., DiMaggio 1997) have recommended studying cognitive structures and processes to understand institutional mechanisms. To draw “attention to the construction of organizational issues (i.e., organizational innovations) at the institutional level,” Swanson and Ramiller (1997, p. 462) highlighted the role of community-level cognition in IT innovation diffusion. While their focus on community-level cognition was essential to developing the organizing vision concept, understanding how organizationallevel cognition informs community-level cognition can enhance our understanding of the organizing vision itself and its role in diffusion (Davidson 2006; Griffith 1999). The interaction of community member (e.g., organizational) cognition and community cognition (as an institutional constraint) in shaping action is captured by the metaphor of jamming, a musical performance requiring creativity and structure (Eisenberg 1990). In other words, visions are constituted through community members’ creative appropriations of institutionalized cognitive structures. These appropriations shape the facets of an organizing vision, influencing community members’ subsequent engagement with the focal innovation.

To understand what is contained in an organizing vision, we draw on schema theory (e.g., Fiske and Taylor 1991). As cognitive frameworks, schema theory and organizing vision theory are highly congruent and thus rife with opportunities to inform each other. Yet, for the most part, the two theories have developed independently. We draw on conceptualizations of the hierarchical nature of schemas to address the following questions regarding the underlying cognitive structure of an organizing vision and its possible effects on IT innovation diffusion:

RQ1: How is an organizing vision of an IT innovation structured?

RQ2: How does the uncovered structure of the organizing vision help us understand the early-stage diffusion of an IT innovation?

We employ a grounded theory method to uncover the underlying structure of an organizing vision and understand its effects on diffusion. To date, over 80 percent of investigations of organizing vision theory has been oriented toward developing propositions rather than hypothesis testing,<sup>2</sup> marking the areas as relatively nascent and consequently wellsuited to grounded theory (Glaser 1992). A grounded theory approach is especially viable given the paucity of theories to inform what constitutes an organizing vision and how it is constituted.

We study the organizing vision for social media, an IT innovation encompassing “a group of Internet-based applications…that allow the creation and exchange of User Generated Content” (Kaplan and Haenlein 2010, p. 61).<sup>3</sup> We focus on the period 2007–2012, when social media had diffused sufficiently across organizations (Chui et al. 2012) to be visible in published discourse, although still at the early stages of diffusion (Kane et al. 2014). Because usefulness is a primary determinant of technology adoption (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2007), we focus on the know-why knowledge category of an organizing vision, which speaks to “business benefits” (Wang and Ramiller 2009, p. 717). Because know-why is of paramount concern to prospective adopters, especially at early stages of diffusion (Swanson 2001; Wang and Ramiller 2009), we focus particularly on adopters. Thus, our research examines the social construction of the know-why component of an organizing vision by adopters at early-stage diffusion of an IT innovation and the subsequent effects of that organizing vision on diffusion.

Our study involved two stages corresponding to Glaser’s substantive coding, surfacing “the conceptual meanings given by generating categories and their properties,” and theoretical coding, discovering “conceptual models of relationships” (1992, p. 27). We address RQ1 through substantive coding at Stage I. To do so, we content-analyzed texts in which the top 50 Fortune firms discussed their initiatives involving social media. An initiative is an organization’s purposive appropriation of social media as described in a text. We then applied relational class analysis (RCA) (Goldberg 2011) to surface the lower-order schemas of a social media organizing vision represented in this discourse. We conceptualize the four lower-order schemas surfaced through RCA as visionsin-use, a term chosen to reflect the evolving nature of organizing visions (Swanson and Ramiller 2004). Nested within each vision-in-use are one or more business use cases—arguments for the IT innovation that address existing problematics or construct new ones. Community members activate (i.e., invoke from memory; Sedikides and Skowronski 1991)—visions-in-use from the repertoire furnished by the organizing vision and, in doing so, reproduce and reshape the organizing vision over time. Thus, just as individuals’ multivocal performance produces the collective experience in a musical jam session, community members’ multivocal discourse about an IT innovation produces the organizing vision.

We address RQ2 through theoretical coding at Stage II. We consider social media diffusion as the number of social media initiatives discussed by firms between 2007 and 2012. While surfacing the cognitive structure of the social media vision, we observed four organizing vision facets (i.e., coherence, continuity, clarity, and diversity), amounts of which differed based upon distinct combinations of cognitive structures (i.e., of visions-in-use and business use cases) over time. Coherence refers to internal consistency of meanings in a vision, continuity to the similarity of those meanings to meanings contained in visions associated with prior innovations, clarity to the transparency of a vision’s distinctive meanings, and diversity to the presence of different meanings. We explored effects of monthly variations in these facets—based on the community’s disparate combinations of the cognitive structures over time—on social media diffusion in four subsequent three-month periods. While extant literature offers some insights into how vision coherence and continuity influence IT innovation diffusion, we highlight the salience of vision clarity and diversity to explain diffusion and to explain the link between coherence/continuity and diffusion. Here too, the jamming metaphor holds. Just as musical jamming is based on and produces a harmony that pleases the audience, jamming with an IT innovation requires a cognitive structure (i.e., the organizing vision), which permits reproduction and improvisation and influences audiences’ receptivity to the IT innovation. Diversity and a measure of clarity in appropriation facilitates diffusion, but too much clarity decreases diffusion.

Our study makes two major contributions to the organizing vision literature. First, by addressing RQ1, we reveal the structure of an organizing vision. In deriving the vision structure, we highlight schema theory as a conceptual tool and RCA as a methodological tool for advancing organizing vision theory. Second, by addressing RQ2, we demonstrate how a detailed view of the vision structure allows us to understand IT innovation diffusion. This contribution addresses the primary goal of organizing vision theory: to understand how IT innovations come to be adopted and diffused (Swanson and Ramiller 1997; Wang and Swanson 2008). For prospective adopters, our work highlights 10 business use cases for social media and spotlights giving and getting attention as a novel problematic addressed by social media. For vendors, our work suggests that offering repertoires of meaning that evoke opportunities to “jam,” instead of repertoires of simple, familiar meanings, may enhance the traction of IT innovations.

In the following section, we address RQ1 through Stage I of our analyses. Next, we address RQ2 through Stage II of our analyses. Finally, we discuss the insights gleaned from both stages, the theoretical contributions of our work, and implications for practice.

## Stage I: Schema-Theoretic Decomposition of Organizing Vision

In order to understand the structure of an organizing vision, we now consider organizing visions in light of schema theory. We then describe our approach to surfacing the concrete cognitive elements of a vision and describe the elements thus identified.

## Organizing Visions as Schemas

A schema is “a cognitive structure that represents organized knowledge about a given concept or type of stimulus” (Fiske and Taylor 1984, p. 140). It consists of conceptual elements and the relationships among them. Schemas exist at multiple levels, including individual (Fiske and Taylor 1984), group (Chakraborty et al. 2010), organization (Labianca et al. 2000), and community (Bingham and Kahl 2013).

Organizing visions can be seen as a special type of community-level schema about IT innovations. Like other community-level schemas, such as the one for information systems in the early 1970s (Bingham and Kahl 2013), an organizing vision is a socially constructed, evolving knowledge structure held by a community (Gorgeon and Swanson 2011; Sun and Wang 2012). Similar to a schema, an organizing vision informs community members’ conceptualizations of IT innovations (Marsan, Paré, and Beaudry 2012; Reardon and Davidson 2007). Organizing vision and schema theories are similar also in their view of the relationship between the cognitive and the material worlds. As with visions, schemas are related to the tangible, that is, to material and human resources (Sewell 2005). Schemas give meaning and hence value to these resources (Sewell 2005, p. 216). Schema theory’s proposed relationship between cognition and material accumulation resonates with the notion that an organizing vision legitimates a focal resource and mobilizes actors’ accumulation of the resource (Swanson and Ramiller 1997).

While organizing vision theory has advanced at the level of an abstract idea about technologies, the literature suggests that such an idea consists of know-how, know-what, and knowwhy categories and develops in reference to multiple business problematics and rationales for innovation adoption (Barrett et al. 2013; Swanson and Ramiller 1997). This notion is consistent with schema theory, which postulates a hierarchical structure of cognition. For example, Taylor and Crocker (1981, p. 92) alluded to the “pyramidal structure” of schemas, in that they are “hierarchically organized with more abstract or general information at the top and categories of more specific information nested within the general categories.” James et al. (1978) viewed higher-order schemas as abstractions, generalizations of concrete, lower-order schemas, which themselves are comprised of distinct configurations of cognitive elements.

## Substantive Coding Methods

Our substantive coding began with preparing the data by coding texts. Thereafter, we surfaced categories through a multistep procedure combining qualitative and quantitative approaches as summarized in Figure 1. Appendix A explains our methodological choices.

## Coding of Texts

Our sample consisted of discourse texts by the top 50 Fortune firms in 2011. We sampled Fortune firms because larger organizations adopt innovations earlier (e.g., Tolbert and Zucker 1983) and “early adopters are more likely to espouse distinctive rationales” from which later adopters borrow (Swanson and Wang 2005, p. 21; see also Fligstein 1993; Han

1994). Further, because large firms are more likely to engage materially with organizational innovations early in their life cycle (e.g., Tolbert and Zucker 1983), they are more likely to be knowledgeable about the innovation. Then, since actors’ knowledgeability increases reliability of schemas surfaced (Smither and Reilly 1989), focusing on large firms enhanced the reliability of schemas surfaced. Finally, sampling top Fortune firms ensured heterogeneity in characteristics such as industry and reputation (Brown and Perry 1994), enhancing the generalizability of our findings to large firms.

Our sample of texts consisted of news releases, annual reports, and responsibility reports pertaining to social media published by these Fortune firms during the years 2007– 2012.<sup>4</sup> We developed search terms to identify these texts by reviewing operational definitions of social media in prior literature, subsequently augmented via trade press and news searches.<sup>5</sup> We searched LexisNexis for news releases using these keywords and firms’ annual and responsibility reports, obtained online. The news releases for each firm then were screened by one of the six members of the research team (comprised of the authors and three research assistants), who read each to confirm that it indeed discussed a social media initiative and eliminated those that did not from the dataset. Reasons for such elimination included use of social media terms in non-media contexts, for example, “forums” describing physical rather than online forums and “networking” unrelated to online social networking, inclusion of social media contact information in the standard company description at the end of all news releases, and duplicate news releases. Of almost 50,000 pages of news releases returned by the LexisNexis search, we retained only about 15 percent (approximaely 7,500 pages) for coding. We similarly screened annual reports for relevant content only. Only 46 of the top 50 Fortune firms had texts containing social media initiatives. Our final dataset consisted of 2,414 texts.

We then coded the texts using Boltanski and Thévenot’s (1999, 2006) “orders of worth” framework. While other value frameworks were available (e.g., Quinn and Rohrbaugh 1983), Boltanski and Thévenot’s comprehensive, theoretically rich framework has been espoused by sociologists for understanding actors’ “modes of justification” (DiMaggio 1997, p. 277). From their empirical observations of individuals’ justification efforts in a variety of contexts and from canonical texts by St. Augustine, Bossuet, Hobbes, Rousseau, Adam Smith, and Saint-Simon, Boltanski and Thévenot identified six prototypical social orders or worlds and their governing principles or values, which “are sufficient to describe justifications performed in the majority of ordinary situations” (1999, p. 369). Boltanski and Thévenot noted that actors consciously or unwittingly draw upon these prototypical worlds to justify or denounce actions.

The six prototypical worlds (and associated principles) are industrial, market, domestic, inspiration, civic, and renown.

The industrial world values “the efficiency of beings, their performance, their productivity, and their capacity to ensure normal operations and to respond usefully to needs” (Boltanski and Thévenot 2006, p. 204). The market world values “buyers and sellers” being “opportunistic in spotting and seizing the opportunities of the market, unhampered by any personal link,” and actors are deemed to be “worthy when they are rich” (Boltanski and Thévenot 1999, p. 372). The domestic world is concerned with personal relationships and assigns value “through reference to generation, tradition, and hierarchy” (Boltanski and Thévenot 2006: 165). The world of inspiration is inhabited by artists and other actors aspiring to be geniuses; its governing principle is creativity. The civic world is inhabited by “citizens” who subjugate their “selfish lusts” and “direct themselves exclusively towards the common good” (Boltanski and Thévenot 1999, p. 371). The world of renown focuses on fame, visibility, or public esteem. Boltanski and Thévenot (2006) noted that while certain principles dominate certain spheres (e.g., inspiration among artists and market among firms), actors in complex, modern society also draw upon other values to justify actions.

We began by content-analyzing the texts, coding each text for presence of the principles. To enhance coding validity, we began with keywords culled from the margin notes provided in Boltanski and Thévenot’s (2006) description of the six principles. We then refined the list by iteratively coding and discussing the keywords, deleting ambiguous words, and surfacing others that were either synonymous with Boltanski and Thévenot’s keywords or captured their intent with regard to the six principles. To code the texts, we read each text for the meanings firms attributed to social media and related those meanings to keywords associated with the principles. Because six different individuals performed the coding, we took two steps to ensure consistent understanding of the different principles across the different coders. First, we developed a codebook containing definitions of the principles and their associated keywords. Second, all three authors coded a set of texts with each of the three research assistants and met several times to reconcile and discuss the research assistants’ coding until we were satisfied that our understandings of the six principles had converged. We averaged about 10 hours training per assistant. Thereafter, each assistant coded another set of texts with one of the authors to ascertain training success. When inter-rater reliability was sufficiently high, assistants were permitted to code independently. Since six different individuals performed the coding, we confirmed consistent use of the coding protocol across all coders by having a random subset of 439 texts coded by two individual (i.e., two of the authors or an author and an RA). Inter-rater reliability— Cohen’s (1960) kappa—for civic, domestic, industrial, inspiration, market, and renown principles were 0.97, 0.99, 0.81, 0.96, 0.97, and 0.95 respectively.

Hereafter, our unit of analysis is the initiative, rather than text or firm. Because firms in the dataset represented multiple lines of business in multiple markets, we had no reason to anticipate singularity or homogeneity of meanings within firms. Because our data sources included annual reports that reported on heterogeneous social media initiatives undertaken across business lines and across the globe, we similarly ruled out the text as a unit of analysis. Our initiative-level analysis is congruent with Swanson and Ramiller’s observation that “discussions and interpretations are inexorably linked to ‘enactments’ in the form of social and technological initiatives relating to the innovation” (1997, p. 470).

## RCA as an Approach to Schema Discovery

Although grounded-theory proponents traditionally promote manual coding, discovering complex patterns manually is challenging. RCA, based on the notion of schematic similarity, is a quantitative approach to discovering underlying schemas.

Schema theory posits shared, but not universal or identical, meanings (Strauss and Quinn 1997). Similar experiences lead to shared schemas; dissimilar experiences lead to divergent schemas. Shared schemas imply “agreement on the structures of relevance and opposition that make actions and symbols meaningful” (Goldberg 2011, p. 1402, italics added). Thus, shared meanings can underlie both agreement and disagreement as “schematically overlapping” individuals, “who follow a similar relational pattern,” and “schematically different” individuals, “who follow opposite relational patterns but still agree on the dimensions along which meaning is defined,” employ the same underlying schema (Goldberg 2011, p. 1402, italics added). In this sense, schema theory resonates with Swanson and Ramiller’s view that in constructing an organizing vision, discourse “participants can ‘agree to disagree’ because their respective contributions to the discourse manifest a high degree of mutual intelligibility” (1997, p. 465). Schematic similarity entails comparability in how cognitive elements are organized. This may be a function of similar preferences for the elements or it may be a function of diametrically opposite preferences as long as the actors use the same rule to organize them (as explained in Appendix B).

Because schemas are interconnections among cognitive elements (Walsh 1995), analysis of schematic similarity can use connectionist models, premised on the tendency of cognitive units to cluster in consistent ways within structurally and/or experientially similar social groups (Strauss and Quinn 1997). Goldberg’s RCA, previously applied to surfacing communities of meaning underlying music tastes, offers an analytical approach to discovering shared schemas. Given our focus on know-why, the salient cognitive elements constituting schemas (Goldberg 2011) in our investigation are Boltanski and Thévenot’s justification principles.

For the RCA procedure, we computed a principle signaling rate for each initiative as the number of texts in which a principle was signaled divided by the total number of texts related to that initiative. This rendering of the data meets the four requirements for RCA: (1) the data are operationalized as scaled variables; (2) scales are ordinal and equidistant; (3) variables (here, the six principles) are comparably scaled; (4) the scales range from zero to one (Goldberg 2011).

RCA is a three-step process. In the first step, a relationality coefficient between each pair of observations is computed. This coefficient signifies the schematic similarity between pairs of observations and is analogous to a Pearson correlation coefficient (similarities and differences are discussed in Appendix C). In the second step, schematically similar groups of initiatives are identified. Schematic similarity is obtained from absolute values of the relationality coefficients, because very negatively and very positively related observations are treated similarly. The rationale is that when two initiatives are negatively related, they use different principles, but their use of the principles is organized in the same fashion (Goldberg 2011) (for details, see Appendix C). To identify schematically similar groups of initiatives, we first developed a square matrix of the absolute value of the relationality coefficients between each pair of initiatives. This square matrix may be visualized as a nondirected network, with each initiative represented by a node and each relationality coefficient an edge in the network. Because the relationality coefficient rarely equals 0 and its lower bound rarely equals -1, not all coefficients are meaningful and only those statistically different from the bootstrapped average (obtained with 1,000 resamples, at p # 0.05) were retained. To identify relationally similar groups, we then subjected the network to the Newman community detection algorithm. Unlike other clustering methods that can ignore peripheral vertices, the Newman algorithm tends to uncover more realistic community structures (Newman and Girvan 2004).

The third step involves representing schemas as graphs, depicting two schema properties. The first is correlation among nodes—here, principles—across initiatives in a schema: solid lines depict positive correlations between principles, dashed lines represent negative correlations; heavier, darker lines depict stronger, more significant correlations. The second is the extent to which each node binds together (or holds apart) other nodes in the schema, determined by the weighted clustering coefficient (CC) and depicted as the size of the nodes in the graph.<sup>6</sup> In these two properties, graphs reveal the distinct opposite relational patterns or groupings of principles signaling firms’ agreement on meanings but disagreement on preferences.

RCA revealed four schemas. The Newman modularity criterion used by RCA to partition the relationality network only indicates which multiple partition solution is best, not whether they are better than a single, unpartitioned network (Newman and Girvan 2004). ## Analyses of Schemas and Business Use Cases

The RCA procedure revealed four distinct schemas organizing the six principles in firms’ discourse about social media. Characteristics of these schemas are summarized in Table 2. The first and the fourth schemas accounted for the most number of initiatives. The first three schemas evince higher density than the fourth and therefore represent more homogeneous or internally consistent schemas. We now consider the principles constituting the four schemas.

Except for the second schema, which excluded inspiration and renown principles, the six principles appeared across all schemas. Yet, assembly of principles into schemas differed in two key ways. First, we observe distinct combinations of principles across the four schemas. For example, while the first, second, and fourth schemas combined industrial and market principles, when the principles appeared together in the first schema, they were coupled with inspiration; when appearing in the fourth schema, they were coupled with the renown. Second, schemas differed in the relative importance afforded to the six principles (depicted in Table 2 as the percentage of initiatives in which the principles are represented and the average signaling rate—that is, proportion of texts mentioning the principle—when the principle was invoked).

The RCA graphs for the four schemas (presented in Figure C2 in Appendix C) revealed distinct groupings of principles. Following Swanson and Wang, who noted that rationales for innovating with IT are “packaged as ‘business cases’” (2005, p. 21), we characterized these groupings of principles as the business use cases for social media. A business case speaks to “the value a proposed product or service would generate for your organization” (Maul 2011, p. 5). It addresses itself to a specific problematic, or, the innovation’s “relevance in the material economy” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 466). While a business use case is a specialized type of cognition within the organizing vision, a problematic is a material need that informs the vision, but also may be seeded by the vision as “a pertinent business problematic is not necessarily clear at the outset” and the organizing vision “may constitute a ‘solution in search of a problem’” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 466). We therefore define a business use case as an argument for the focal IT innovation that addresses an existing problematic or constructs a novel one.

Ideal types—here, schemas—within a typology are marked by core and peripheral components—here, principles; core components are more connected to other core and peripheral components within an ideal type (Fiss 2011). One or more grouping of peripheral components may complement the core component within an ideal type (Fiss 2011), constituting different business use cases. Schemas and business use cases represent idealizations of our data, where signaling rates of principles close to 100 percent are idealized as core principles, those around 50 percent are idealized as peripheral principles, and those close to zero as absent. ## The Technology-as-Efficiency-Engineer Schema

Focusing on the industrial principle, the problematic addressed by this schema was streamlining processes, a problematic dating back to the scientific management era (Taylor 2013). Firms’ technology use for cost efficiencies through automation or information has been noted in a range of studies (e.g., Zuboff 1988). Organizing vision researchers have linked this problematic to adoption of technologies such as videoconferencing, which “reduced costs due to travel” (Davidson et al. 2011, p. 4), and professional services automation, a financial reporting (i.e., efficiency) tool (Wang and Swanson 2007). Analyses of oppositional groupings of principles in this schema revealed three preference groupings.

The first, which we characterized as the GENERIC EFFICIENCY business use case and which dominated this schema, invoked the industrial principle alone; the second, characterized as the INSPIRED EFFICIENCY business use case, paired the industrial principle with inspiration; the third paired the industrial and domestic principles to represent the CULTURAL EFFICIENCY business use case.

## The Technology-as-Brand-Promoter Schema

Constituted by market and industrial principles, the focal problematic of this schema was increasing customer intimacy. Treacy and Wiersema (1993, p. 88) articulated this problematic as firms’ quest to develop long-term customer loyalty (i.e., of focusing on the customer’s lifetime value to the company, not the value of any single transaction). This problematic has been the purview of customer-relationship management (CRM) technology. For example, Wang and Swanson (2008) noted that the aim of CRM functionalities was to enhance customer service and marketing effectiveness. Discovery of this schema supports Deans’ (2011) recommendation that firms’ chief marketing officers be involved with social media adoption and implementation. It also supports marketing research demonstrating value of social media to word-of-mouth marketing, which was found to provide significantly longer-term effects than traditional marketing (Trusov et al. 2009). Analyses of oppositional groupings of principles in this schema revealed two preference groupings. The first grouping, characterized as the CONVENTIONAL PROMOTION business use case, invoked the industrial and market principles alone; the second, characterized as the CULTURAL PROMOTION business use case, paired these principles with domestic and/or civic principles.

## The Technology-as-Good-Citizen Schema

Defined by pairing civic and industrial principles, often in conjunction with the domestic principle, the focal problematic of this schema was garnering legitimacy by demonstrating corporate social responsibility, a long-standing business concern (Lim and Tsutsui 2012). Communicating social responsibility increasingly is viewed as an essential corporate function (McWilliams et al. 2006) and the Internet “has made it much easier for firms to target consumers who have social goals” (McWilliams and Siegel 2001, p. 125). We observed no appreciable groupings of preferences within this schema.<sup>8</sup>

## The Technology-as-Master-of-Ceremonies Schema

We base this schema name on the thematic principle associated with this schema. Although renown did not appear as a core principle in every business use case within this schema, its consistent absence from every other schema and consistent presence in this schema (see Table 3) marks it as a thematic element of this schema. We attribute its failure to attain status as a core principle in every business use case to the novelty of this principle to the business community, which gave rise to inconsistent appropriation and social construction of the schema. The manner in which organizations invoked this principle was both to draw attention to themselves as well as to confer attention to others. Anchored by the renown principle, this schema speaks to social media’s distinctive capability to enable firms to attract attention to themselves and to spotlight customers or members of the public strategically as does a master-of-ceremonies. Unlike the prior three schemas, the problematic underlying this schema (i.e., attracting and conferring attention) bears little resemblance to those associated with prior technologies. We do, however, see this schema’s distinctive renown principle reflected in social media phenomena such as Klout.com, which assesses and reports individuals’ social media influence (Stevenson 2012);

LinkedIn’s “Influencer” program, which algorithmically publicizes users’ posts beyond their immediate networks (Shih 2014); and the Wired/Kapitall “Social Index,” which tracks the stock price of nine “star” firms that have attracted the greatest number of Facebook “Likes” (Tate 2012).

This schema contained four combinations of principles. The first combination, which we termed GENERIC DISTINCTION, was of renown alone or combined with only the industrial principle. The second combination, termed MATERIAL DISTINCTION, was renown with inspiration and/or market principles. The third combination, termed CULTURAL DISTINCTION, included civic and/or domestic principles, sometimes with renown. The fourth combination, which we characterized as CAUSE-RELATED MARKETING, included civic and/or domestic and market principles. This business use case focused on increasing customers’ emotional engagement with the brand by facilitating consumers’ social goals (Baghi et al. 2009). Similar in function to Good-Citizen initiatives oriented toward engaging consumers in social causes and to Brand-Promoter initiatives promoting corporate brands, these initiatives differed by invoking the public’s prosocial motives together with asocial motives such as creativity and acquisitiveness.

## Stage I Discussion: How Is an Organizing Vision Structured?

There are two connotations to our first research question. The first is what is contained within the black box of an organizing vision? To answer this question, we drew upon schema theory, in conjunction with RCA, founded in schema theory. This enabled us to unpack the complex and abstract organizing vision. We highlighted justification principles (Boltanski and Thévenot 2006) as cognitive building blocks for the different schemas constituting the know-why component of a community’s organizing vision. Like Swanson and Ramiller (1997, p. 465), who noted that “discourse represents an important, extended occasion for the structuration of the community,” we find that structuration theory (Giddens 1979) helps to explain the concrete schemas surfaced and their relationship to an organizing vision. Specifically, we view an organizing vision as a sociocognitive structure that actors draw on to conceptualize an innovation (Gorgeon and Swanson 2011). While instantiating the vision through use, the actors also reproduce and reconfigure the organizing vision. We refer to the concrete schemas represented in actors’ instantiation of the community vision in discourse about an initiative as a vision-in-use and conceptualize organizing vision as a higher-order, abstract schema. The second connotation of our question is how do these components of an organizing vision come to be assembled? Structuration of an organizing vision happens at three levels. At each level, the organizing vision furnishes a repertoire of choices that inform community members’ decisions to engage in the discourse and how they justify an initiative. The repertoire includes resources (i.e., meanings about the focal innovation) and rules for deploying those resources. At each level, community members’ choices produce and reproduce the vision. At the first, most concrete level, community members make the principles more or less salient through use or disuse and the manner in which they assemble the principles strengthens or weakens the associations among principles constituting a business use case.<sup>9</sup> At the second level, community members’ appropriation of business use cases strengthens or weakens their relevance and increases the oppositions among them. At the third level, community members’ appropriations increase the relative salience of each vision-in-use to the community’s organizing vision and contribute to the oppositions among the visions-in-use.<sup>10</sup> Increasing oppositions among business use cases and among vision-in-use over time reflect a process of schematic differentiation (Strauss and Quinn 1997).

## Stage II: Discovering Organizing Vision Effects on Diffusion

Our substantive coding led us to discover the specific visionsin-use and business use cases that constitute the social media organizing vision. We oriented our theoretical coding toward discovering how variations in the nature of an organizing vision might affect diffusion. Two paths to discovering antecedents to IT innovation diffusion were available to us. First, we could investigate effects of the visions-in-use and/or business use cases on diffusion. Second, we could investigate effects of the facets of the organizing vision itself, shaped by community members’—here, adopting firms’— appropriations on diffusion. We pursued the second path for two reasons. First, organizing vision theory postulates distinctive visions and problematics for different IT innovations (Swanson and Ramiller 1997; Wang 2010). Finding a specific vision-in-use or a business use case to influence social media diffusion more than others would be unlikely to generalize to other IT innovations.<sup>11</sup> Second, discovering that appropriations of specific visions-in-use or business use cases influenced diffusion would be theoretically uninteresting because it would not shed light on why those types of cognitions encouraged diffusion.

The core theoretical construct at this stage of our study was diffusion, that is, “the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system” (Rogers 2010, p. 5). (A more detailed discussion of what we mean by diffusion appears in Appendix E.) Because firms are comprised of heterogeneous actors who engage with the focal IT innovation in different ways, we investigated diffusion as the number social media initiatives begun over time, rather than as the number of participating firms. To understand how an organizing vision relates to diffusion, we considered how community member appropriations of visions-in-use and business use cases shaped facets of an organizing vision over time. In the following sections, we describe facets of an organizing vision we observed to emerge concomitant with community members’ cognitive appropriations. We briefly review extant knowledge about these facets, describe how we investigated them, and report our findings.

## Surfacing and Defining Facets of an Organizing Vision

While validating the four-cluster solution relative to the nonpartitioned relationality network, we noticed initiatives were not equally schematically similar across the four visions-inuse (density in Table 2). Conceivably, this schematic similarity also varies as a function of community members appropriations from the repertoire of an organizing vision over time. We interpret this schematic similarity as vision coherence, which is the extent to which the visions-in-use within the organizing vision are internally consistent. Coherence is the first emergent facet of visions. Adopters produce the coherence of a vision at a point in time by justifying an initiative in a way that is similar to other justifications for that innovation at that point in time.

While surfacing the visions-in-use, we observed that problematics addressed by the first three visions-in-use resembled problematics associated with prior IT innovations. Specifically, the efficiency optimization teleology underlying the Efficiency-Engineer schema has been associated with technologies such as ERP (Swanson and Wang 2005), the customer-intimacy enhancing teleology underlying the Brand-Promoter schema has been associated with CRM (Wang and Swanson 2008), and the social responsibility orientation underlying the Good-Citizen schema has been associated with websites (McWilliams and Siegel 2001). In contrast, the Master-of-Ceremonies schema addressed a problematic (getting and giving attention or fame) with little precedent. As such, the first three visions-in-use socially construct social media as a continuous innovation while the last socially constructs social media as discontinuous, spotlighting vision continuity as a second vision facet (Ramiller and Swanson 2003). Vision continuity is the extent to which visions-in-use within the vision address problematics associated with prior IT innovations. Adopters produce the continuity of a vision at a point in time by justifying an initiative in a way that is similar to other justifications offered for prior IT innovations.

A third facet was derived from our observation of the varying extent to which community members appropriated business use cases in a manner consistent with the community’s custom or idealized use. In other words, some members appropriated business use cases more faithfully than did others. Whereas the concept of faithfulness as applied to technology feature use references the designers’ intention (DeSanctis and Poole 1994; Markus and Silver 2008), faithful appropriation of a schema references emergent custom within the community. Variations in faithful appropriation shape vision clarity, which is the extent to which ideal meanings and justifications are visible in the repertoire of visions-in-use and business use cases. Specifically, adopters produce the clarity of a vision at a point in time by justifying an initiative in a way that is similar to justifications associated with its ideal business use case.

A fourth facet that emerged while surfacing business use cases was the diversity of visions-in-use and business use cases appropriated by a community. We define vision diversity as the extent to which an organizing vision is comprised of different visions-in-use and business use cases. Research on material appropriation of IT innovations has noted that IT supporting firms’ administrative or technical cores becomes “increasingly elaborated” or diversified over time (Swanson 1994). ## Compilation of Dataset to Analyze Vision Effects on Diffusion

From the 1,183 initiatives discussed by the 46 firms, we compiled a 72-month dataset. Each data point captured the relative coherence, continuity, clarity, and diversity of the community vision for a given month and subsequent diffusion, as operationalized in Table 4.

Our analysis focused on diffusion in the three-month periods or quarters following each month’s vision. We selected a three-month period based on our assessment of the speed with which social media discourse can drive action, on one hand, and the time required for actors to perceive and respond to the evolving vision, on the other. With regard to speed, social media discourse can have a relatively quick impact on subsequent social media initiatives for three reasons. First, the discourse can increase community members’ confidence in their social media initiatives already under way, encouraging quicker release. Second, social media discourse can inform nonsocial media initiatives under way, stimulating a social media overlay. For example, Procter and Gamble established the “Children’s Safe Drinking Water” program seven years prior to launching it as a Facebook initiative (news release, 1/25/2012). Third, social media discourse may stimulate initiatives with shorter lead times. While coordinating an initiative across multiple social media sites or developing an app such as Ford’s “Augmented Reality App” may be time consuming, organizations can throw up a fan page or Twitter campaign relatively quickly. Sites like Mashable provide a quick how-to on setting up a Facebook site (Drell 2011). An example of a short lead-time initiative is the widely acclaimed, spur-of-the-moment Oreo Super Bowl “You can still dunk in the dark” Twitter campaign (Watercutter 2013).

With regard to time, while social media discourse can affect social media initiatives very quickly, community members still need time to process and respond to the evolving vision. Rather than assume an immediate response, we considered diffusion in the three-month periods or quarters following each month’s vision. Diffusion was measured as the average monthly initiatives for four quarterly periods (i.e., 1–3 months, 4–6 months, 7–9 months, and 10–12 months) following the organizing vision constituted by the initiatives discussed each month (n = 69, 66, 63, and 61, respectively). Averaging diffusion within three-month periods smooths random fluctuations in the number of initiatives begun each month and permits us to offer a longer perspective on the effects of variations in vision composition on diffusion.<sup>12</sup> Moreover, comparing findings across core indices helps the researcher ascertain whether saturation of insights gleaned has been achieved (Glaser 2008).

## Theoretical Elaboration

Glaser and Strauss (1980) recommended theoretical elaboration (i.e., discovering the structure of multivariate relationships) by examining partial correlations. Again, although significance tests are not essential in quantitative grounded theory (Glaser 2008), the benchmark we chose in considering the salience of partial correlations was 90 percent statistical confidence, coupled with consistency of evidence across the four indices of diffusion (i.e., across the four quarters). Appendix F details comparisons of simple and partial correlations through our theoretical elaboration journey. We reproduce the final step of the mediation analysis from Table F1 in panel 5 of Table 5. Each row in Table F1 and Table 5 depicts the baseline correlations between each facet and diffusion and the correlation between each facet and diffusion after partialing correlations between other facets in that step/panel and diffusion.

First, we were able to confirm the curvilinear relationships of coherence, continuity, and clarity observed in Figures 4 and 5. This is visible in the first three panels in Table 5. Specifically, we see that while none of the baseline (simple) correlations for these three vision facets with diffusion were significant, all partial correlations that concurrently considered the relationship of the linear and squared terms with diffusion were significant.

Second, our mediation analysis in Table F2 (Appendix F) highlighted the role of vision clarity and diversity in mediating effects of vision coherence on diffusion. Panel 5 of Table 5 shows that baseline coherence and diffusion correlations were significant, but partial correlations were insignificant. In Steps 1, 2, and 3 of Table F1, we see the partial correlations for coherence become insignificant in the presence of clarity and diversity, but not continuity. Thus, we conclude that clarity and diversity mediate effects of coherence on diffusion.

Similarly, Panel 5 of Table 5 shows that the baseline correlations between continuity and diffusion were significant, but their partial correlations were insignificant. In Steps 1, 4, and 5 of Table F1 (Appendix F), we note that the partial correlations for continuity diminish substantially in the presence of diversity, but not in the presence of coherence or clarity. Partial correlations between continuity and diffusion were insignificant in the presence of diversity in three out of the four subsequent quarters. Thus, we conclude that diversity, but not clarity or coherence, mediates effects of continuity on diffusion.

## Stage II Discussion: How Does the Vision Structure Help Us Understand Diffusion?

In the course of our substantive coding, we noticed community members’ appropriations of the social media organizing vision gave rise to different facets of the vision. The purpose of our theoretical coding was to discover how those vision facets might affect diffusion. We now formalize our observations as propositions, that is, statements that summarize our refinements of organizing vision theory and predict the relationship between the nature of organizing vision and IT innovation diffusion.

Our first observation during theoretical elaboration was to confirm the inverted-U-shaped form of the relationship between continuity and diffusion and between coherence and diffusion, visible in Figures 4 and 5 and anticipated by Swanson and Ramiller (1997). Similarly, we were able to conclude that an inverted-U-shaped relationship exists between clarity and diffusion. Thus, some clarity is desirable, but too much clarity stifles adoption. This may be because some ambiguity in meanings about an asset prompts its novel deployment (Stark 2009) as adopters fill in gaps in meaning in ways that connect with the needs of their organizations.

Proposition 1: At early stages of diffusion, vision clarity in one period has a curvilinear effect on diffusion of the IT innovation in subsequent periods: up to a moderate level, clarity increases diffusion; beyond a moderate level, further increases in clarity decrease diffusion.

Our theoretical elaboration process further confirmed the linear relationship between diversity and diffusion observed in Figures 4 and 5. Diverse meanings suggest a wider range of uses for the technology, facilitating wider adoption through the community (Bui 2014).

Proposition 2: At early stages of diffusion, vision diversity in one period has a linear effect on diffusion of the IT innovation in subsequent periods: diversity increases diffusion.

Our mediator analyses in Appendix F helped us situate clarity and diversity in a theoretical model that included the two previously identified vision facets (coherence and continuity) and IT innovation diffusion. Referring to Figures 4 and 5, we note continuity to have a curvilinear relationship with diversity, confirmed by comparing the simple and partial correlations in Appendix F. Specifically, at low to moderate levels of continuity, diversity increases, but then falls off as continuity increases. Too continuous or too discontinuous a vision constrains the set of business use cases available to prospective adopters. Thus, we see that continuity has a curvilinear relationship with diffusion because of its curvilinear relationship with diversity, which is related positively to diffusion.

## Proposition 3: At early stages of diffusion, the effect of vision continuity on diffusion of an IT innovation is mediated by vision diversity.

From Appendix F, we observe that effects of coherence on diffusion are mediated by both clarity and diversity. From Figures 4 and 5, we note the relationship between coherence and clarity is linear; the relationship between coherence and diffusion and between clarity and diffusion is generally curvilinear. Appendix F (Table F3) confirms that both the linear and squared clarity terms were essential to mediating effects of coherence on diffusion. We therefore conclude that coherence has a curvilinear relationship with diffusion partly because of its linear relationship to clarity, which has a curvilinear relationship with diffusion. The problematics underlying a coherent vision are apt to be transparent to community members, making it is easier for the community to appropriate visions-in-use and business use cases faithfully, enhancing the clarity of the vision they enact. From Figures 4 and 5, we observe a curvilinear relationship between coherence and diversity, which is linearly related to diffusion. Table F3 confirms the curvilinear relationship between coherence and diversity. In other words, moderate coherence facilitates adopters’ appropriation of diverse visions-in-use and business use cases, whereas too much or too little coherence impedes diverse meanings. This may be because moderate levels of coherence reveal the range of appropriation choices available to prospective adopters, yet necessitates improvisation to fill in meaning gaps. In contrast, too little coherence masks any specific meaning and too much coherence discourages experimentation with the meanings. We therefore conclude that coherence has a curvilinear relationship with diffusion also because of its curvilinear relationship with diversity, which is linearly associated with diffusion.

## Discussion

Analyses of the structure of the know-why category of the organizing vision for social media surfaced two cognitive constructs nested within an organizing vision: business use cases and visions-in-use. While Swanson and Ramiller (1997) noted that organizing visions evolve, substantive coding at Stage I led us to highlight community members appropriations of groupings of principles (i.e., business use cases) from a repertoire (i.e., organizing vision) of community meanings (i.e., visions-in-use) about the technology as the mechanism through which an organizing vision evolves. Jamming with technology entails such appropriations, through which community members produce a harmonious, yet multivocal, vision and shape its facets.

Theoretical coding at Stage II validated Swanson and Ramiller’s perspective that “the organizing vision’s production…has a particularly important relationship with processes of adoption and diffusion” (1997, p. 467). We confirmed the salience of two recognized facets of organizing visions (continuity and coherence) and demonstrated the salience of the two facets we identified (clarity and diversity) in predicting diffusion of an IT innovation. Reflecting our jamming metaphor, these findings suggest that structure (continuity, coherence, and moderate clarity) and improvisation (diversity and moderate lack of clarity) are essential if community members’ appropriations of meanings are to produce a harmonious, yet interesting, vision.

## Theoretical Contributions

Collectively, our findings validate the claim of organizing vision theory that

even as the community shapes the organizing vision through its discourse, the organizing-vision discourse reciprocally shapes the community as it exposes emergent opportunities that attract new participants (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 465).

We refine and extend organizing vision theory in two ways.

First, by addressing RQ1, we offer two lower-order cognitive constructs: visions-in-use, which emphasizes the disparate meanings contained within a vision, and business use cases, which emphasize the disparate problematics addressed by a vision. In noting their constitution by varied groupings of principles, we highlight Boltanski and Thévenot’s (2006) “orders of worth” framework as useful to understanding the community cognitions that influence adoption and diffusion of an IT innovation. In answering RQ1, we also highlight schema theory as a conceptual tool and RCA as a methodological tool that is meaningful to research on organizing visions. Casting organizing vision as a hierarchy of schemas allowed us to decompose the abstract community idea about an IT innovation into concrete cognitive elements used by the community to justify adoption of the innovation. This decomposition provides further insight into how an organizing vision is shaped through interactions “characterized by both agreement and disagreement about the vision’s content” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 462).

Second, by addressing RQ2, we enhance understanding of IT innovation diffusion, a key objective of organizing vision theory (Swanson and Ramiller 1997). In addition to validating the previously posited effects of two vision facets, continuity and coherence, on diffusion (Ramiller and Swanson 2003; Swanson and Ramiller 1997), we surfaced vision clarity and diversity as antecedents of diffusion. Further, by discovering that clarity and diversity mediate effects of coherence and continuity on diffusion of an IT innovation, we help explain why coherence and continuity are salient to diffusion. These dynamics highlight the need for a vision to balance structure and freedom to improvise, or jam, in order to enhance diffusion of IT innovations. Whereas structure provides the framework for coordinated action, freedom to improvise permits individual agency in relationship to one’s particular capabilities and needs.

Our study of the organizing vision of social media permits us to offer two contributions to schema theory. First, we highlight the mutability of collective schemas through individual use. Second, in contrast to extant wisdom on familiar and coherent schemas (e.g., Fiske and Linville 1980), we find schemas encompassing repertoires of meaning and thus disparate opportunities for action (i.e., opportunities to jam) in fact may be more appropriable.

## Practical Contributions

Extant literature suggests two conceptualizations of social media: “internally focused within the enterprise” or “externally focused toward customers, suppliers and partners” (Kane et al. 2014, p. 82). Our findings broaden extant views of social media. For example, a Good-Citizen meaning addresses a different set of actors (such as activists and regulators) within a firm’s political environment (Lim and Tsutsui 2012). We also surfaced the Master-of-Ceremonies schema that focuses on a novel IT problematic: giving and getting attention through IT. By identifying 10 business use cases, we also contribute to a more nuanced view of social media. Managers who have not perceived a business purpose for social media may be more likely to do so when presented with a wider range of meanings and business use cases. Mangers doubting the viability of a less visible business use case may be more apt to try it out when faced with evidence of its prevalence. For vendors participating in the discourse, our findings suggest that contributions encompassing repertoires of meaning offer prospective actors opportunities to jam, and may be more appropriable than simple, familiar schemas. In short, our work offers lessons that stakeholders can use to tailor their discourse about social media and to inform their understanding of how social media initiatives and social media discourse shape each other.

## Limitations and Future Research Directions

The multitude of methodological choice points suggests the need for programmatic research to determine the extent to which these findings hold up across different methodological choices.<sup>13</sup> We consider some choices with particularly important implications for the organizing vision literature. First, in contrast to previous researchers who interviewed prospective adopters to glean their understandings about IT innovations (Marsan, Paré, and Beaudry 2012; Ramiller and Swanson 2003), we examined texts describing adopters’ descriptions of their initiative. In other words, in contrast to earlier research that dimensionalized community members’ reception of the organizing vision, we address facets of the organizing vision itself. Therefore, our definition of coherence as internal consistency, while consistent with Swanson and Ramiller’s view of coherence as consistent meanings, differs from earlier connotations of coherence as subjective interpretability (e.g., Ramiller and Swanson 2003). Our definition of continuity as relatedness to prior IT innovations, while consistent with Swanson and Ramiller’s (1997, p. 472) view of it as “seemingly fixed reference points,” differs from earlier connotations of discontinuity as requiring a conceptual shift or implementation changes (Marsan, Paré, and Beaudry 2012). When and why community members’ subjective experiences along dimensions such as interpretability and need for conceptual and implementation changes relate to objective qualities of the vision are empirical questions worthy of future research. Such research may identify moderators and mediators of the relationship between an organizing vision and its reception.

Second, we focused on the know-why component of an organizing vision. This focus permitted us to offer a much more detailed perspective on this component than hitherto available. It does, however, raise a concern about the extent to which our findings would generalize to other knowledge categories of an organizing vision. With regard to the structure of an organizing vision, our conceptualization should remain unchanged. Were we to study the know-what knowledge component, we would expect specific social media tools or platforms to aggregate into know-what components of business use cases, some reflecting similar underlying knowwhat schemas or visions-in-use, and others reflecting different visions-in-use. Similarly, we would expect specific skills and capabilities to constitute know-how components of business use cases and visions-in-use. With regard to the effects on diffusion, the organizing vision literature has flagged knowwhy as especially important to adopters at early stages of diffusion (Wang and Ramiller 2009). This is unsurprising as extensive research points to the importance of perceived usefulness to IT adoption and use (Venkatesh et al. 2007). As know-why likely enhances perceived usefulness, diffusion effects possibly were higher for this knowledge category than they might be for other knowledge categories. Future research should investigate the extent to which effects of an organizing vision on diffusion that we noted generalize to other knowledge categories of the organizing vision.

Third, we limited our diffusion window to a year (four quarters) following the organizing vision socially constructed within a month. Larger-scale corporate social media initiatives require longer lead-times to move from idea to released initiative. Future research, therefore, should investigate longer cycle effects when studying diffusion of organizational innovations. Further, we focused exclusively on early-stage adoption of social media. Future research should consider whether our models generalize to other IT innovations and to other stages of diffusion.

Fourth, we note the distinctive nature of social media as an organizational innovation. In contrast to previously studied technologies, such as ERP, CRM, ASPs, and KM, where prospective adopters would depend on colleagues and vendors for information, business adopters are just as likely to have learned about social media from family and friends. The ability to rely on these informal sources may have decreased time-to-adoption, permitting us to observe shorter-term impacts of organizing vision facets. In contrast to previously studied technologies, which required CIO involvement because they targeted the IT function (e.g., CASE or OSS innovations) or entailed sizable outlays (e.g. ERP), the low cost, technological, and governance barriers-to-entry to social media enables organizational users to adopt the technology. In these ways, social media may have altered diffusion dynamics.

Finally, organizing vision theory has noted the salience of actors such as vendors, journalists, consultants, and academics. To what extent would social constructions of social media by these community members reflect the vision facets we identified? To what extent would the vision constructed by different categories of community members influence diffusion of the IT innovation differently? These issues merit further investigation.

## Conclusion

Organizing vision theory describes the organizing vision as a fluid idea that is constituted and transformed as community members agree and agree to disagree about a focal IT innovation. Our grounded theory approach allowed us to open up the organizing vision black box, leading us to identify its underlying structures that community members constitute and reconfigure through their discourse about corporate initiatives. The organizing vision structure we uncovered is hierarchical, entailing—from least to most concrete cognitive constructs— visions-in-use, business use cases, and justificatory principles. Much as the “vision” of a musical jam session emerges from players’ multivocal performances, an organizing vision emerges from the multiplicity of community members’ discursive engagements with an IT innovation.

Organizing vision theory highlighted an essential contradiction about the role of continuous and coherent cognitions around an IT innovation. Swanson and Ramiller (1997, p. 460) noted that discontinuity “enhances the salience of the innovation,” but also challenges sensemaking and knowledge transfer (Ramiller and Swanson 2003). Similarly, they state: “low coherence may actually be a plus, providing rhetorical flexibility….[but] low coherence will not be tolerated indefinitely” (Swanson and Ramiller 1997, p. 463). Our grounded theoretical approach permitted us to unpack this dilemma. Instead of viewing continuity/discontinuity and coherence/ incoherence as tensions that must be resolved or cause the vision to devolve, our work points to the need to recognize that moderate amounts of these facets are desirable. Our work further highlights cognitive clarity and diversity as two additional facets of an organizing vision that play a significant role in diffusion. By manipulating the faithfulness and the diversity of their appropriations, and hence clarity and diversity of the vision, actors enhance the viability of the organizing vision. “A key aspect of jamming, then, is its improvisational quality; it is forever interesting because the options for individual moves are unlimited” (Eisenberg 1990, p. 154). Yet, because “improvisational freedom is only possible against a well-defined…backdrop of rules” (Eisenberg 1990, p. 154) there are limits to the viability of faithless appropriations.

## Stage I: Discovering Meanings Attributed to Social Media

Our choice to use Boltanski and Thévenot’s (1999, 2006) “orders of worth” framework to study justification departs from some grounded theorists’ sentiment that the investigation should be grounded purely in data, blind to prior theories. Nonetheless, Strauss (1987, p. 306) said “there is no reason not to utilize extant theory from the outset.” Strauss (p. 306) articulated three conditions under which using prior theory is permissible, even desirable: first, “that it too [i.e., the prior theory] was carefully grounded in research,” that is, empirical data; second, that “entry into the research field follows immediately, or at least before a commitment is made to the research project”; third, that the researcher “be immediately sensitive to the new data and their potentials for new coding, conceptual densification, and integration.” Boltanski and Thévenot grounded their framework in decades of ethnographic and statistical investigations of how people justify distinctions among people and explain injustices in scientific, business, and lay contexts, thereby satisfying Strauss’s first condition. Our initial objective was to understand the meanings organizational actors attributed to social media. About two months into the study, after we had coded 541 texts from the first 15 Fortune companies, we elected to focus on understanding actors’ justifications for social media use. We therefore satisfy Strauss’s second condition. Finally, through the entire coding process, we remained alert to the possibility that the data might suggest novel justificatory principles not addressed by Boltanski and Thévenot. Although we were unable to discover any novel principles, our vigilance meets Strauss’s third criterion for acceptable use of prior theory by the grounded theory researcher. This approach is consistent also with Urquhart’s (2013, p. 29) labeling of the expectation that researchers investigate phenomena without invoking their prior knowledge as a “myth” and Urquhart and Fernandez’s (2013, p. 230) recommendation of a “phased literature review,” with the “understanding that the generated grounded theory will determine the relevance of the literature, never the converse.” Finally, as noted in Table F1, our grounded theory endeavors began after we developed the dataset of 1,183 initiatives and the signaling rates for the six principles across those initiatives. This is consistent with Suddaby’s (2006, p. 636) perspective that “in a grounded theory study, content analysis is only one of multiple contexts for acquiring data.” Thus, whereas we imposed Boltanski and Thévenot’s framework on the initial data, concepts other than principles and relationships among the concepts emerged through our engagement with the data.

Our second methodological choice at this stage (i.e., to use RCA) also may appear somewhat inconsistent with conventional grounded theory paradigms. Because of the connectionist structure of schemas (Strauss and Quinn 1997), discovering them can be an extremely complex task. For example, permitting signaling rates for the six principles to be either a 0 or a 1, there are 63 possible combinations of the principles or 63 possible schemas. With signaling rates varying continuously between 0 and 1, an infinite number of possible schemas exist. To deal with such complex phenomena, researchers have advocated leveraging advanced computational techniques in grounded theory research (e.g., Birks et al. 2013; Goes 2013).

RCA is particularly well suited to grounded theory as it performs constant comparisons at two stages of the procedure. First, computing relationality coefficients entails comparing the vector of values for all variables for each observation with the vector of values for all variables for every other observation (Goldberg 2011). Second, identification of relational clusters necessitates comparing each observation’s relationality coefficient with every other observation’s relationality coefficient (Goldberg 2011). In this fashion, RCA automates the process of constant comparison by leveraging advanced computational techniques

As an inductive, discovery approach, RCA may invite criticism that the knowledge discovered is incomplete (i.e., that saturation has not been attained). Mindful of this possibility, we conducted the analyses in stages to ascertain whether adding data changed our findings. Table A2 summarizes data and findings across analysis iterations, with the last two iterations adding firms and texts for April–December 2012, which was missing from the first two iterations.

As will be apparent from the table, the firms sampled became increasingly heterogeneous in industries represented, revenue, and profitability across the iterations. Discounting the first iteration, for which very little data was available, we observe two things. First, concomitant with increased sample heterogeneity, we see a steady decline in the average relationality coefficient, suggesting decreasing schematic similarity in the sample. Second, despite increasing sample and schematic heterogeneity, both the schemas identified and the relative distribution of initiatives across schemas remain consistent across the last three iterations. We therefore were satisfied that the social media schemas discovered were sufficiently comprehensive.

Having discovered the four schemas through RCA, we examined the graphs produced by RCA (see Appendix B, Figure B2) and initiatives associated with each schema for similarities and differences. This constant comparison of RCA output with the texts describing the initiatives enabled us to understand and label the schemas as well as identify the business use cases associated with the oppositional preferences patterns nested within the schemas.

## Unpacking Schematic Similarity

The premise underlying schematic similarity is that people with diametrically opposed attitudes toward a set of stimuli may have shared understanding about those stimuli because they organize their cognitions about the stimuli based on the same underlying rules (Goldberg 2011). In Table B1, Alex and Jamie clearly are schematically similar, given their preference for vegan foods. Bill and Bobby, who like foods associated with a country breakfast (eggs, bacon, and biscuits) also clearly are similar. The schematic similarity of Dean, who dislikes foods associated with a country breakfast and likes foods not associated with a country breakfast, to Bill and Bobby is less clear. Dean, who grew up with Bill and Bobby, organizes the foods into groupings identical to Bill and Bobby, retaining the cultural meanings associated with the foods, if not the taste for them. Thus, while individuals sharing the same preferences are schematically similar, individuals with oppositional patterns of preferences also are schematically similar.

Kelly and Mac, with shared preferences, are schematically similar to Sam and Taylor, with oppositional preferences, because all four manifest the same evaluation rule regarding the foods: protein versus carbs. In contrast, Alex and Jamie’s evaluation rule is whether or not the foods are vegan and Bill, Bobby, and Dean’s evaluation rule is a country breakfast meal. Thus, patterns of these nine individuals’ food preference reveal how their cognitions about the six foods are related to each other, revealing their underlying schemas about food.

## Explanation of Relational Class Analysis

Relational class analysis (RCA), advanced by Goldberg (2011) to detect shared understandings, is a knowledge discovery, rather than a hypothesis testing, technique that is useful in developing theories about meanings. Detecting schemas through RCA requires the researcher identify stimuli that are the building blocks of schemas. The foods in Table B1 are one type of schematic building block. The schematic building blocks in Goldberg’s study were musical genres. In our study, the focal stimuli were Boltanski and Thévenot’s (2006) six principles. Then, understanding how social entities organize those building blocks into schemas requires that the researcher elicit some signal of the meaning attributed to the individual stimuli (e.g., food or musical genre preferences). We derived signals from firms’ texts related to social media by coding for the different principles firms used to justify social media. Each text contained one-to-many initiatives and initiatives could be discussed across multiple texts. The resultant signaling metric was the rate at which each principle appeared across texts discussing each initiative. This metric fulfilled Goldberg’s requirement that the signaling metrics for the different variables be comparable, ordinal, equidistant, and scaled between zero and one. ## Step 1: Computing the Relationality Matrix

RCA begins by computing an $N \times N$ relationality matrix, where N = the number of observations. In this case, N was the 1,183 social media initiatives identified. For each pair of observations, a relationality coefficient is computed as follows (formulas from Goldberg 2011):

$$
R _ {i j} = \frac {2}{K (K - 1)} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K - 1} \sum_ {l = k + 1} ^ {K} (\lambda_ {i j} ^ {k l} \cdot \delta_ {i j} ^ {k l})
$$

where

$$
\delta_ {i j} ^ {k l} = 1 - \left| | \Delta X _ {i} ^ {k l} | - | \Delta X _ {j} ^ {k l} | \right|,
$$

$$
\Delta X _ {i} ^ {k l} = X _ {i} ^ {k} - X _ {i} ^ {l},
$$

$$
\lambda_ {i j} ^ {k l} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 & \Delta X _ {i} ^ {k l} \cdot \Delta X _ {j} ^ {k l} \geq 0 \\ - 1 & \Delta X _ {i} ^ {k l} \cdot \Delta X _ {j} ^ {k l} <   0 \end{array} \right.,
$$

K = total number of variables (or cognitive elements that constitute the schemas),

i, j = particular observations (here initiatives),

k, l = particular variables (here principles), and

X = set of data points, each ranging from 0 to 1, for K variables and N observations.

We automated these computations using Excel VBA to produce the N × N relationality matrix. The lower bound on the relationality coefficient depends on scale granularity and the number of variables, tending toward -1 as each increases, but rarely equaling -1 (in our data, the minimum relationality coefficient was -0.25). Consequently, expected values of relationality coefficients exceed zero. Goldberg therefore recommends ascertaining the significance of the relationality coefficients relative to their bootstrapped means. This was done in Stata (version 10.1) with 1,000 replications of 1,000 observations. As per Goldberg, coefficients with an absolute value significantly higher than the bootstrapped mean of 0.53 (SE = 0.0088) were retained. Using the relationality coefficients’ absolute value operationalizes the premise that positively and negatively related observations are schematically similar (Goldberg 2011).

The relationality coefficient bears some resemblance to the Pearson correlation coefficient. Like the correlation coefficient, the relationality coefficient can, in theory, range from -1 to 1. In reality, however, the lower bound on the relationality coefficient depends on scale granularity and the number of variables (i.e., cognitive elements that are organized into schemas), tending toward -1 as each increases. While correlation computes association based on two vectors of data points, treating the data points within each vector as independent, relationality is sensitive to interdependencies between the data points. Correlations are sensitive to differences in use of extreme values and cannot be computed in the presence of zero variance. Relationality, however, is less sensitive to differences in scale usage and can be computed even in the presence of zero variance. Sample relationality and correlation coefficients are shown in Table C1. As per Goldberg, the correlation between relationality and Pearson coefficients is high (r = 0.94 with our data).

## Step 2: Obtaining Relationality Clusters (Schemas)

To discover the distinct schemas or “islands of meaning” (i.e., clusters that organize cognitive elements in similar fashions), Goldberg recommended the Newman algorithm, which employs modularity maximization, where the optimal number of clusters is given by the largest modularity coefficient. The Newman community detection algorithm’s modularity index (using UCINet version 6.381) indicated optimal modularity for 4- to 14-cluster solutions. Because all clusters beyond the first four clusters in the 5- to 14-cluster solutions contained only single observations (initiatives), we retained the 4-cluster solution.

## Step 3: Representing the Schemas Detected

The graphical representation of schemas recommended by Goldberg depicts two characteristics of the relationships among variables in a schema. First, it depicts each variable's correlation with every other variable in the line joining pairs of nodes on a graph, where nodes represent variables (here, principles). Second, it depicts the extent to which each variable or node holds together other variables/nodes within the schema in the size of the nodes. The extent to which each variable holds together other variables is given by the weighted clustering coefficient (CC), which was computed using MS Excel as per Goldberg’s footnote 22:

$$
C C _ {i} = \frac {2}{k _ {i} (k _ {i} - 1)} \sum_ {j, k} (\tilde {\omega} _ {i j} \tilde {\omega} _ {j k} \tilde {\omega} _ {k i}) ^ {1 / 3}
$$

## What Is Meant By Diffusion?

There are two perspectives on what “diffusion” means. The original perspective focuses on adoption (Rogers 2010). Some researchers have argued this diffusion-as-adoption perspective is incomplete in the context of organizational innovations because it ignores post-adoptive use. For example, Fichman (1992, pp. 196-197) noted: “While much of classical diffusion theory is still applicable to adoption of innovations by organizations…the organizational adoption of an innovation is not typically a binary event, but rather, one stage in a process that unfolds over time.” Zhu and Kraemer (2005, p. 62) observed that “we need to view ebusiness diffusion as a multistage process that starts at adoption and extends to usage.” In this view, adoption and use are two essential steps in diffusion of organizational innovations. Table E1 summarizes MIS research based on this alternate diffusion-as-use perspective.

For the benefit of readers who strongly subscribe to the diffusion-as-adoption perspective, we attempt to quantify the initiatives in our dataset representing a diffusion-as-adoption perspective versus a diffusion-as-use perspective. To do so, we draw upon Cool et al.’s (1997, p. 552) approach of treating “each adopting unit as a social system where diffusion of the innovation occurs.” The key indicator of the extent to which the phenomenon underlying our study is diffusion-as-adoption versus diffusion-as-use lies in the extent to which distinct groups of social actors were involved with each initiative in our study. If social actors were associated with multiple initiatives, we would characterize their first use as adoption of the technology and subsequent use as use (Cooper and Zmud 1990). If more-or-less distinct sets of actors were associated with the initiatives, we would characterize each use as adoption. We develop these estimates by examining the networks of social actors surrounding a few initiatives in our sample.

## Theoretical Elaboration of Diffusion Antecedents

Glaser and Strauss (1980) recommended comparing simple and partial correlations to discover the structure of multivariate relationships that constitute a causal model (i.e., mediator and suppressor<sup>2</sup> effects) (Cheung and Lau 2008). First, we compared simple and partial correlations to confirm the curvilinear relationships of coherence, continuity, and clarity with diffusion observed in the scatterplot matrices. Table 5 in the article provides evidence of nonlinear effects: while none of the simple correlations between each of the three facets and diffusion was significant in any of the four quarterly periods, every partial correlation was significant when both the linear and squared terms were included simultaneously. Second, we wished to determine whether each facet mediated effects of other facets. A mediator is “the generative mechanism through which the focal independent variable is able to influence the dependent variable of interest” (Baron and Kenny 1986, p. 1173). Statistically, two facets related to each other and with diffusion (baseline correlations) and the relationship between one facet and diffusion diminishing considerably in the presence of the other (partial correlations) would be indicative of mediation. Because we wished to situate the two new facets within a theoretical model of the two previously identified facets and diffusion, we first considered coherence and continuity. In Step 1 in Table F1, we notice that partial correlations for both coherence and continuity diminish in the presence of the other, but in most cases remain significant. We therefore cannot conclude either coherence or continuity mediates the effect of the other on diffusion.

Next, we systematically investigated changes to the incumbent diffusion path model by introducing each of the two new facets with each of the incumbent facets separately and then together. In Step 2, we notice that the partial correlations for coherence diminish when accounting for clarity. This, together with the significant simple correlation between coherence and clarity (r = 0.69, p < 0.001), suggests that clarity might mediate the effects of coherence on diffusion. In Step 3, we notice that partial correlations of the linear and the squared coherence terms with diffusion across all quarters diminish when accounting for diversity. This suggests that diversity mediates the effects of coherence on diffusion.

In Step 4, when accounting for both sets of terms for continuity and clarity, we find all partial correlations with diffusion in the first and fourth subsequent quarters remain significant; the partial correlations for clarity with diffusion in the second quarter were insignificant; the partial correlation for the squared continuity term with diffusion in the third quarter was insignificant. In this step, we lack clear evidence of mediation of continuity by clarity or vice versa. In Step 5, the partial correlations of both continuity terms with diffusion in the first quarter diminished, but remained significant after accounting for diversity; the partial correlations between the continuity terms and diffusion in the next thre quarters was insignificant after accounting for diversity. This suggests diversity mediates the effects of continuity on diffusion.

In Step 6, once again we note that after accounting for clarity, the partial correlations between coherence and diffusion frequently diminish to insignificance, again suggestive of clarity mediating effects of coherence on diffusion. In Step 7, we observe all partial correlations for both coherence and continuity to diminish to insignificance in the presence of diversity. Together with the correlation between coherence and diversity (r = -0.63, p < 0.001) and continuity and diversity (r = -0.38, p < 0.01), this suggests that diversity mediates effects of both coherence and continuity on diffusion. Finally, in Step 8, we note the partial correlations for clarity and diversity generally remain significant in each other’s presence and in the presence of the remaining facets for two of the four quarterly periods, suggesting these two facets do not mediate each other’s effects on diffusion.

Because we observed diversity to mediate the effects of continuity on diffusion, we needed to confirm the curvilinear form of the association between continuity and diversity observed in Figures 4 and 5. In Table F2, we note that while the simple correlations between diversity and the linear and squared continuity terms were significant, the partial correlations were substantially higher. Further, the partial correlation between diversity and the linear continuity term changed from negative to positive. We therefore are able to confirm the curvilinear form of the association between continuity and diversity. Similarly, because we observed diversity to mediate the effects of coherence on diffusion, we needed to confirm the curvilinear association between coherence and diversity observed in Figures 4 and 5. In Table F2, we note that while the simple correlations between diversity and the linear and squared coherence terms were significant, the partial correlations were somewhat higher. Further, the partial correlation between diversity and the linear continuity term changed from negative to positive. Therefore, we are able to confirm the curvilinear form of the association between coherence and diversity.
