# Toward a component-based design framework of the international information technology standardization process

Theodora Ngosi<sup>1</sup>, Ashley Braganza<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computing, School of Informatics, City University, Northampton Square, London EC1V 0HB, UK; <sup>2</sup>Brunel Business School, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

## Abstract

This article is about a proposal to develop a component-based design (CBD) framework of the international information technology standardization (IITS) process. This process is criticized widely in information systems (IS) literatures for its complexity as a barrier to successful project development. These criticisms have not challenged this process itself or accede to proactive action toward positive changes. We stress the importance of reconstructing this process within a CBD framework, not only to reduce sources of complexity but also to give special prominence of project development. Primarily, our qualitative empirical case study of five projects is grounded in the organization information processing theory (OIPT) as a lens. Theorizing from the qualitative empirical evidence we define the IITS process, followed by its inductive analysis guided by OIPT as a lens through three levels: its environment, content and performance. Through this lens we articulate assumptions about IITS process complexity. From rich descriptions of this process, we combine selected dimensions of CBD and OIPT as a lens to develop the theoretical depth of concepts that add detail to our CBD framework. One implication for practice of this framework is for provisioning incisive IITS process change strategies drawing upon its dominating qualities. This article makes three contributions to the IS field: this CBD framework is a key contribution. We bridge the gap in knowledge by defining the IITS process, explicating its complexity toward proactive action. By using OIPT as a lens we extend its application.

## Introduction

his research article develops a component-based design (CBD) framework of the international information technology standardization (IITS) process. The core substance of this process is projects that develop or revise one or a cluster of related published standards (Cargill, 1989; De Vries and Verheul, 2003). A project leading to an IIT standard spans several years embracing construction of technical concepts, discussions, writing of draft standards and negotiations to establish international agreements on common solutions or requirements.

Interpretations about IITS have shown connections to the information systems (IS) field, because of its progression from basic research to development of technology and to innovation processes (cf. Hawkins, 1995; Spivak and Brenner, 2001). Another connection we can mention is the organizational and industry implementation impacts of IIT standards. This brings together different kinds of strategies for ensuring that the technology that is developed delivers value (Miller and Morris, 1999). These strategies often are defined through a combination of economic, governmental policies, social and technical dimensions. These dimensions are also supported when expressing IITS concepts in the development of standards, which bring technologies, applications and implementation practice into line.

We use two connections between the IS field and IITS as justification to focus our research. One, the IS field gives us the methodological direction to challenge analytically the IITS process. Two, our research is embedded in the body of knowledge on IT standardization by reference to the IITS process as the object of our study. With scanty IT standards literatures, this confluence between IS and IT standards body of knowledge is, in itself, a timely contribution. It gives us stronger concepts and arguments to make sense of the IITS process. It demonstrates how we create our accountability in the explication of IITS process challenges we examine, leading to clearer articulation of the practical importance of the CBD framework we present in this article.

Drawing upon this overview, the IS field has a growing body of standards literature criticizing the IITS process for lengthy time scales to produce IIT standards and because the process is considered inefficient (Severance, 1995; Rada, 1999; Jakobs, 2000; Ma¨ho¨nen, 2000; King and Lyytinen, 2003; Sherif, 2003). However, discussions in these literatures have not challenged the IITS process itself. We argue, successful project development is a critical strategic aim of the IITS process. A major obstacle to success is that this process has become too complex for any conventional solutions to be effective. We take these criticisms and advocate the need to reconstruct a defined IITS process within a CBD framework as the only means to reduce sources of its complexity. This contribution extends ‘calls’ to address IT standardization issues such as those mentioned in a report by Krechmer (2005: 87):

Carl Cargill spoke his concerns that the old standardization regimes were not prepared to address today’s problems and that a more innovative approach to standardization is required.

There are a number of themes emerging in various IS literatures which can explain why CBD could make a positive impact on IITS process effectiveness. Among the key strengths, CBD addresses: complex business or organization concepts and process functionality to meet requirements more effectively (Herzum and Sims, 2000), solutions for consistent operations (Veryard, 2000) and integrated technology (Adler, 1995; Davenport, 1998, 2000). Although these themes encourage our proposal, our position, in this article, is to generate factual knowledge about IITS project development. This leads to a definition of the IITS process and a critical review articulating its dominating features, performance and complexity.

Our proposal to develop a CBD framework builds from qualitative empirical case studies grounded in the organization information processing theory (OIPT) as a lens. We examined five projects. Theorizing from the qualitative case evidence we defined the IITS process, followed by its inductive analysis guided by OIPT as a lens through three levels: its environment, content and performance. OIPT criteria such as ambiguity and uncertainty (Galbraith, 1973, 1977; Daft and Lengel, 1986) facilitate theoretical focus and explication of assumptions underpinning IITS process complexity deeply embedded in project development. Findings from the analysis of the IITS process provide a clear appreciation of dominating features from which we connect our argument to the proposal. We believe that, by combining selected CBD dimensions and OIPT as a lens we can generate theoretical depth of rich sets of concepts that add detail to develop the CBD framework. With this framework we can reason the reconstruction of the IITS process aiming to give special prominence to project development. We describe this framework highlighting its dominating qualities.

This article makes three contributions to the IS field: One, we present a CBD framework as a key contribution addressing how the IITS process would appear when it is reconstructed. Two, we bridge a research gap in knowledge by demonstrating an empirical definition of the IITS process together with explicating its complexity toward proactive action. Three, by using OIPT as a lens in our study we extend its application (Goodman, 1978, 1988) to other limits involving theoretical grounding of our research design (Gregor, 2002, 2006; MISQ, 2004) and process analysis (Pettigrew, 1997, 2000).

The article is structured as follows. The next section, Conceptualisation, addresses literature review of the scope of IT standardization and gap in knowledge. Section ‘Theoretical dimensions’ introduces OIPT as a lens and engaging our conceptualization of its use in this article. We present the research design, case study and results. The IITS process has a section providing an insightful analytical discussion and findings. We move on to develop comprehensive concepts that have relevance in developing our CBD framework. We discuss the work presented in the article, followed by concluding remarks, implications for practice and future research directions.

## Conceptualization

## Scope of IT standardization

Three dominant strands in the body IS literatures help us to summarize of scope of IT standardization and to also link them to our research: representation, process of standardization and IIT standard.

The first strand is the representation of IT standardization. Cargill (1989), Schmidt and Werle (1998), Jakobs (2000) and Werle (2001) provide comprehensive representations of how IITS efforts are propelled through standards development organizations (SDOs), national standards bodies (NSBs) and committees. IITS is well known for its voluntary formality that reinforces the co-operation of different parties under specified conditions. For example, expertise, experience, representation and vested interest in activities or results (Cargill, 1989; Baron, 1995; Jakobs et al., 2001; Hallstro¨m, 2002). Consensus-based decision processes encourage individuals to express a position in any aspect of the IITS process.

The second strand is the process of IT standardization. Standards literatures offer three models explaining this process, namely: creation of industry standards (Cargill, 1989; Mansell and Hawkins, 1992), creation of national standards (Reilly, 1994) and creation and implementation of IT standards (Bonino and Spring, 1991). What we describe in this article is the process of IT standardization embracing the assessment of very complex systems. With the changes occurring naturally with the progression of technology, standardization assists in determining technical decisions to harmonize competing requirements and to provide common solutions, when there are divergent problems in a given context. It can include the development of technology as described in other case studies (such as Schmidt and Werle, 1992, 1998). It excludes implementation, which leads to other couplets such as processes of adoption and diffusion of published standards for the development of products or markets (Gabel, 1991; Fomin, 2003).

The third strand is the IIT standard. We define this as a technical specification describing actual or potential solutions to assessed technology problems. It can include essential requirements and instructive measures for the performance of a product or best practice for a process (cf. Hawkins, 1995: 1). If relevant, performance characteristics, compatibility of different systems products and conformity to binding instructions would be defined in the IIT standard (cf. Gabel, 1991; Hawkins, 1995; Spivak and Brenner, 2001). Indeed, an IIT standard, once published, is a binding technical specification on organizations and industries.

## Gaps in knowledge

Our contributions in this article demonstrate our accountabilities to fill knowledge gaps identified in the body of IS and IT standards literatures. The study of project development as a core of substance of the IITS process is a gap in knowledge. In IT standards literature (such as Cargill, 1989; Jakobs, 2000), there is general acceptance that an IITS process exists as a way to make sense of the elements underpinning standardization. SDOs implement a process for creating a wide spectrum of international standards of which IITS is one of its subject areas. Moreover, the models of the process of IT standardization mentioned above have yet to gain recognized approval in SDOs, so that they are applicable to define how IITS process projects are developed. Consequently, the combined strengths of these models and any formalities that SDOs choose to implement seem to fill a gap of ‘what this process might be’.

A pressing gap in knowledge is the number of interpretations of the IITS process emerging from various studies. The IS and standards literatures give attention to empirical studies within vendor-sponsored or sector IT standardization efforts. Banking and insurance (Michels and Morelli, 2001; Markus et al., 2006), information communication technology (Chen, 2003) and open standards (Krechmer, 2006; Zhu et al., 2006) are few example studies. And we should note, these empirical studies make valuable contribution to build the body of knowledge of IT standardization. Regrettably, however, many of these studies use the assumption that the creation of IIT standards (voluntary) and sector standards with de facto constructs have similar features. We expect that the IITS process has unique challenges that must be examined exclusively. Our empirical study creates accountability in developing knowledge about the IITS process. We believe this process requires an empirical definition that might help us to analytically challenge assumptions about its content, dynamic interplay of practices and performance taken in the context of the themes underpinning project development. This will lead us to a more focused search for solutions to its problems.

The lack of an agreeable definition of such a high-profile international process that is also debated widely mitigates another gap in knowledge. Studies by Axelrod et al. (1997) and Gabel (1987, 1991) suggest that, without internationally agreed IT standards many computing firms might not be able to sell their products or services competitively in global IT markets. IIT standards have been shown to guide the emergence of new technologies into huge iconic developments such as multimedia (Scha¨fer and Sikora, 1995; Chiariglione, 1998). Case studies on local area networks, UNIX Internet and World Wide Web argue that standards can expand markets, with an overall impact that can be measured in billions of dollars (Hurd and Isaak, 2005: 68–69). These market influences point to some of the fundamental reasons for defining of the IITS process as a necessity for its analysis and for taking appropriate problem-solving actions.

## Theoretical dimensions

In this section we focus on two objectives shaping the theoretical dimensions of our study of the IITS process and the basis from which we develop the idea of CBD. The two objectives are: (1) to examine an empirically defined IITS process through OIPT as a lens and (2) to develop concepts for defining a CBD framework of the IITS process.

## Theoretical grounding

In objective (1), Pettigrew (1997, 2000) reminds us to strengthen theoretical grounding in process studies. In particular, the IITS process has esoteric features that emerge during project development, over time. The content of the IITS process and the various layers of context in which the projects are developed raise the critical question of the effectiveness of performance. Therefore, the depth we seek to unravel this process increases the need to strengthen our theoretical and methodological mechanisms. The analysis of this process becomes one of understanding and explication that captures qualities, performance dynamics over time, to include particular meanings entrenched in project development.

In Gregor’s taxonomy (2002, 2006), the designation we assign to OIPT is a general theory with qualitative properties for analysing and describing. Analytical rigour and grounding in theory are assigned explanatory primacy in our research design and in the study of the IITS process. We define grounding as the trade-off between OIPT as theory of choice and theorizing we derive from its rich mix of features, such as guidelines and principles (cf. Weick, 1995: 388–390). Because of these features, in this article, we illustrate that OIPT is adaptable as a lens to facilitate analytic rigour and methodological theorizing.

The original context of use of the OIPT is to interpret views surrounding organization information needs and information processing in order to design solutions for co-ordinating or controlling its activities (Galbraith, 1973, 1987). To create accountability to grounding in theory, we customize the OIPT as a lens from four seminal models that are widely cited in IS literature. These are: model of organizational information processing (Daft and Lengel,

1986), information processing model of organization design (Galbraith, 1973, 1977), information processing model (Huber, 1982) and organization information model (Weick, 1969). These models suggest the OIPT has an interpretive framework with four guidelines, as follows:

(1) Understanding the processes by which an organization interprets its environment and functions.

(2) Determining information-processing alternatives for dealing with the complexity of organization functions.

(3) Determining information processing needs and information processing capabilities and the fit between the two to obtain optimal performance.

(4) Determining an organization’s capacity to cope with external and internal complexity.

Adapting OIPT guideline 1 frames preconditions qualifying analytic rigour and methodological reasoning. In both our research design and study of the IITS process, we apply contrasts that strengthen methodological reasoning to get to the ‘taken-for-granted elements’. Additionally, contrast helps us to cross-fertilize our insights from data gathering toward elaboration of elements that we seek to interpret in the results. Our primary contrast is macro- and microperspectives:

\- Macro-perspective to explore and to explicate environment factors together with relationships that might have an influence on project development (cf. Galbraith, 1973, 1977; Weick, 1969).

\- Micro-perspective for specificity of data gathering, indepth analysis, theorizing and interpretation of our findings. We can examine essential elements of project development for which data would be gathered, and examine the IITS process from different angles.

The next set is three contrast levels to differentiate our analysis of the IITS process: its environment, content and performance over time. Another challenge is to unravel assumptions underpinning IITS process complexity. We concur with Corning (1998: 1) that complexity can present different kinds of contexts that are unique in their meaning, such that they encompass a great variety of other phenomena. By determining the contexts of IITS process complexity, we can characterize it more accurately to decide how it impacts on CBD as our choice of action. Table 1 demonstrates our analytic grounding utilizing OIPT as a lens. Effectively, the combined elements frame our methodological reasoning yielding integrative angles from which to link findings from the macro- and microperspectives.

## Process reconstruction within a CBD framework

In objective (2), our empirically determined premise is that, the IITS process is difficult to describe or verify, because of the dynamic interplay of its features. Simplifying traditional contexts of this process or implementing layers of sophisticated systems is to automate the past. Reconstruction of this process within a CBD framework is a radical designoriented approach. CBD can help us to challenge the breath and depth of IITS process complexity, and move beyond soft solution options that can no longer be considered.

As a central theme of this article, we adopt CBD in the context of business and organization issues. A definition encouraging conceptual awareness of what might be included in our CBD framework is taken from Herzum and Sims (2000):

A business component represents and implements a business concept or business process. It doesn’t represent just any business concept, but those concepts that are relatively autonomous in the problem space y ‘Autonomous’ does not mean isolated. As business concepts in the problem space relate to other concepts, for example an order by a certain customer for one or more items, so business components mainly provide useful functionalities through collaboration with other business components (Herzum and Sims, 2000: 41).

## Research design

Here, our aim is to produce an empirical definition of the IITS process for in-depth analysis. Project development is the case. The social construction of IITS embracing human actions, practices and performance motivates our choice of a qualitative approach. We draw upon several strengths of IS research methods as instruments including: selection of projects to study (Patton, 1990); data gathering, analysis and explication of findings (Yin, 1993, 1994), leading to construction of empirical evidence and its meaning (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991; Bruner, 1993; Flick, 1998; Robson, 2002).

Our research design adopts a multiple-case inquiry carried out in a positivist stance to discover dynamic qualities of project development within real-life contexts (cf. Yin, 1994). One of the difficulties we face is that, at some stage in the investigation, an interpretive or a positivist paradigmatic stance is necessary for evidence building to then, express views about the reality of project development. We stress the importance of theory-driven empirical research utilizing OIPT as a lens for grounding our qualitative mechanisms toward these aims. We can refine underlying influences of different paradigmatic views as they arise in our investigation. Details of project development are unified hence, to develop stronger theoretical explication for defining of the IITS process as phenomenon of interest.

## Case study methodology

Four approaches supported our case study methodology: two for data collection, namely case description and qualitative longitudinal study; and two for addressing the case study aims from analysed qualitative data: case evidence building and explanations.

Drawing upon criteria from Patton (1990), we selected five projects: two from software engineering standardization (SES) and, three from the generic international IT standardization GIITS. Relevant selection criteria involved topical and critical projects, intensity, deviant, project contrast and convenience. The contrast between the projects influenced intense data collection from macroand micro-perspectives (Table 1). It provided a basis to compare the case evidence aiding explicitness in classifying qualities of project development to answer case study questions (cf. Flick, 1998; Strauss and Corbin, 1998). Concurrently, we examined committees, forums and secretariats entrusted with the development of the five projects. Together, these are case study items presented in Table 3.

The case description created our accountability to organize some priori knowledge about the five projects. Through source operational documents distributed widely in the selected forums, IEEE Computer Society and ISO JTC 1, we identified basic concepts of each project and relevance of their use in our study. In this case description, we developed profiles drawing attention to the question: What features, in broad terms, characterize these projects? In response, the profiles produced project development stages we considered universal across the five projects. Universal stages addressed common grounding for specificity of parameters, for controlled observations and data collection, and for case narration (Patton, 1990, 2002; Yin, 1994). Definition of these stages allowed comparison of evidence at some level of aggregation, so that similarities and differences could be identified. The revised case description is the case report of our documented evidence to be analysed.

A qualitative longitudinal approach was advantageous, because of the predetermined project development stages giving attention to our data collection methods. To date, a number of scholarly works have applied a positivist stance in qualitative longitudinal studies involving a multiple-case inquiry with varied phenomena. Our guidelines came from a multiple (dual) case study methodology (Leonard-Barton, 1990; Yin, 1994) and, processes and patterns of change (Pettigrew, 1989; Van de Ven and Poole, 1990). From another angle, Miles and Huberman (1994) suggest that the ‘case’ might not be monolithic; there might be issues embedded within it.

As a preference to add depth and detail to our data collection, our strategy of a multiple-case inquiry combined: (1) Macro- and micro-perspectives (Table 1) as units of analysis (Patton, 1990; Yin, 1994) to focus each project intensively by collecting qualitative data from one stage to the next. (2) Sub-units drawing attention to specified parameters, embedded items in the various layers of context in which project development occurred over time, and for which data was collected data.

Moreover, a qualitative longitudinal approach focuses on a time frame in which data is collected, and which leads to evidence building being given priority. In this case, the period of time that committees took to complete each specified project development stage, for each project under study, was the time frame underpinning our longitudinal approach. In this time frame we collected qualitative data through structured questionnaire surveys.

According to Stake (1995), individual accounts as ‘cases’ are of interest to researchers both for their uniqueness and their commonality. Thus, during our participant observations we used sub-units to also capture insights into possible commonalities across the projects, allowing replications in sampling similar parameters, and which save effort. Our participant observations also helped to draw attention to an array of context-sensitive elements that have influence on each project development stage, over time: such as committee actions, practices and social interactions.

We collected data from a variety of sources illustrated in Table 4. These data sources ensured the data was triangulated. Together, qualitative data collected from parameters and sub-units builds the body of case evidence to define dynamic qualities of project development and perceived phenomena (Boland, 1985; Walsham, 1995a, b).

## Data collection and questions

Ontology is the underpinning philosophy of our intention to unravel the real-life contexts of project development. Drawing upon OIPT as a lens, we applied two units of analysis to be associated, because our intention is to compare findings from GIITS and SES projects:

(a) Macro perspective giving attention to IITS environment with reference to committees, forums and secretariats for the five projects (Table 1).

(b) Micro-perspective focusing on the pre-determined project development stages linked to committee actions for which data would be collected. Committee actions, standardization approaches and practices are example parameters we sampled in each project development stage. Other parameters such as project information and events were developed inductively during intense data collection and analysis of findings from each stage, for explicating embedded items in the empirical evidence.

The case question to be answered with empirical evidence was posed as:

How is a project developed, and which leads to a definition of the IITS process?

With guidelines from Yin (1989, 1994), the ‘how’ gives particular attention to a positivist stance suited to inductive qualitative data collection using varied approaches. Knowing that we have an intense case with project contrast and deviant elements, the development of comprehensive data for evidence building is most suitable to the positivist stance. To collect comprehensive data we also questioned the ‘how’ and ‘why’ of embedded items and of the outcomes across and within real-life contexts of project development stages, over time (cf. Pettigrew, 1997: 342). ‘How’ and ‘why’ questions also help us to deal with insights into time and context of possible commonalities to be traced in the evidence.

## Analysis of qualitative case data

Our strategy for building case evidence involved inductive analysis carried out on qualitative data collected for each project at each development stage. Priority was given to answering few simple questions for critical reflection about the appropriateness of language describing particular technical aspects of findings as they were located in the accounts of the multiple focus group respondents. Following interpretive principles from Klein and Myers (1999) and Tesch (1990), this critical reflection also guided how we developed our accountability to discover the substance of the empirical evidence.

Our data analysis goal was to identify general themes related to how projects were developed. Stage by stage, we emphasized commonalities that could easily be associated, without cumbersome codes, across individual accounts of stages. Open coding (Miles and Huberman, 1994; Strauss and Corbin, 1998) proved useful as a tool for sorting and categorizing qualitative data into manageable categories in this interpretive inductive analysis. With manageable categories, we reapplied questions to encourage theorizing from the qualitative data. ‘What stage?’, ‘Which features?’, ‘Why are they connected’ and ‘how?’ are example theorizing questions devised from OIPT as a lens. In so doing, we theorized contextualized epistemological underpinnings of both the case study and findings seeking generalization of meanings located in the evidence.

Data were decontextualized when they were separated into units of meaning through sorting and, because they are separated from the individual cases in which they originated (cf. Tesch, 1990). The meaning of the case evidence, shifting in context was based on four principles: theory building (Galliers, 1992), focus of the case study and the case evidence (Orlikowski, 1993), narratives of unique commonalties (Stake, 1995) and constructed empirical explanations (Bruner, 1993; Robson, 1993, 2002; Flick, 1998).

Data analysis question 1: ‘What’ is the typical project development cycle (PDC)?

This question depended upon locating substance and meaning in the case evidence in order to explicate the reality of project development. Primarily, substance derived from categorized qualitative data defined the scope of each project development stage. For example, ‘Stage 1 (individual identity) has review of project proposal (objective) executed through committee actions to create technical information (meaning of evidence). When classifications of substance were identified, we expressed themes for elaborating the qualities of a particular project development stage including embedded items. Questions from OIPT as a lens strengthened our refinement and resolution of the meaning of evidence as we engaged in these data analysis steps.

Identified qualities of substance re-contextualized the evidence to a reduced data set, from which we easily integrated relationships that could be explained to reach a conclusion. The response to question 1 from our conclusion of compared evidence is: a typical PDC is evolutionary embracing a confluence of phases of standardization principles, leading to outcomes, such as draft standards (Figure 1).

Data analysis question 2: ‘Which’ features build powerful explication of the PDC and why?

Our definition of features relied upon similarities from a series of analytic comparisons of categorized evidence of projects in the same class, namely SES and GIITS. The compared evidence also forms the basis of interpretation and generalization.

The stage-by-stage inductive analysis proved useful to respond to the question ‘why’ certain featured would be similar. Consequently, we sought explicitness of content of features across the five projects (such as project inputs at a specified stage of development, project tasks and outcomes). Content included understanding subject matters as contexts in which identified features would be operationally linked to the execution of the project.

For ‘Stage 1-review of project proposal’, for example, scientific methods would be a core subject matter applicable to how a committee would abstract technical information from project inputs presented. When subject matters and features could be linked, the result was explicitness of content, namely an empirical representation of predominant relationships and similarities. Differences located in the compared case evidence identified with items that were less important than others were, insofar as they produced unsatisfactory relationships. With these differences, unnecessary items and biased views that may have been introduced in the inductive analysis of findings were eliminated from the evidence data set.

Case study question: how is a project developed, which leads to a definition of the IITS process?

This question brings us to where we started in shaping the case study. It is answered from the interpretive conclusions drawn from our constructed empirical representation of project development. The choice of conclusions depended upon strong confluence of similarities in the meaning of reality in the constructed evidence (Bruner, 1993; Flick, 1998). Our main conclusion reveals a definition of the IITS process embracing two main features: (1) A core process with seven stages. (2) A PDC with six stages (Figure 1). ## Analysis of IITS process

This inductive analysis is carried out through three contrast levels grounded in the OIPT as a lens (see Table 1): environment in which the IITS process exists, content and performance.

Macro level: environment in which the IITS process exists We define the macro level as the IITS environment (known hereafter as environment). In this analysis, we concur with Jakobs (2000: 8) of the significance of this environment:

The process of standardization cannot be regarded as a simple, one-dimensional activity to lay down technical rules and guidelines, taking place in a removed world of its own. Rather, it must be considered in conjunction with the environment within which it takes place. Very different facets need to be taken into account when trying to actually understand this process.

Our empirical evidence reveals that this environment embraces structural and operational views influencing IITS process practices. In the structural view are highly segmented organizations we highlighted earlier as SDOs and NSBs that function independently. International committees distinguished as technical committees (TCs), sub-committees (SCs) and working groups (WGs) develop approved projects. The ISO IEC Joint Technical Committee (JTC 1) is an exception; it currently provides a forum for the development of IIT standards for most areas concerned with IT applications, apart from public communications (http://www.isotc.iso.org/).

In the operational view, this environment deals with internal and external demands of IITS efforts. SDOs and NSBs also serve as functional components of this environment. SDOs give direction to separate subject areas of standardization. NSBs provide operational interchange and administrative support to both national and international committees. They bring together multiple stakeholders that contribute to project development in various committees such as, academic forums, government agencies and IT firms (Cargill, 1989; De Vries and Verheul, 2003).

## Environment as a source of complexity

According to Simon (1996: 184), complexity is a system that is made up of a large number of parts interacting in a nonsimple way. Findings from our analysis demonstrate environment structural complexity as an implication of the number of organizations forming IITS efforts. Their cooperation adopts hierarchical structures in direction of strategies and decision-making. Structural expansion is the most common, such as creating layers of committees with subject matters that overlap each other. Although these classical bureaucratic forms of structures are associated with stable environments of low complexity (Galbraith, 1973), they are inadequate in IITS efforts that experience complex and uncertain changes, from IT markets from which standardization input emerge. In the committees, these hierarchical structures do not address collaboration and co-ordination needs.

Functional complexity arises from these structures to include subject areas, functions, practices and problems that also overlap. This form of complexity places demand on adherence to practice conventions for inter-organization operational relationships, such as co-operation and collaboration needed to support IITS process performance effectively (cf. Keohane and Nye, 2001; Mackenzie, 2001). SDOs add layers of directives intended to guide project development (www.iso.org/directives). In reality and despite the requirements prescribed in the directives, many of the standards take considerably longer to complete because of the expansive nature of complex functional matters.

## Environment and uncertainty

This environment deals with unpredictable global challenges associated with the need to develop projects that can lead to technology solutions sought after in the IT market. Information from diffuse sources, such as market data, national legislation and technology concepts often come as requests to SDOs or NSBs to develop a standard. Information uncertainty arises then, from the amount of information that has to be processed to determine IITS process pursuits. Repetitive processing and evaluation distorts the content of the information created. Information sources can easily be abandoned based on cost of processing and maintenance (Arrow, 1974: 37). Lack of enough information rests attention on selecting the right decisions, such as polices guiding IITS process performance.

## Micro-level: content of IITS process

## Content features

Figure 1 presents two main features of our empirical definition of the IITS process: core process and PDC. In this side by side view, we use stages for the core process, because it is a formal feature that guides the development of a wide spectrum of other international standards (Ngosi and Jenkins, 1993). For the PDC, we use phases followed in the committees we examined, to develop standardization principles. This PDC resembles some aspects proposed in Jakobs (2002), whereby draft standards are validated by implementations before the IIT standard specifications are published. All the same, the PDC has equal importance in the successful completion of a project. The mutual use of the core process and PDC creates detail complexity as a source of uncertainty.

## Detail complexity

We describe detail complexity as the interdependency of the different elements (Senge, 1990) we locate in content of the IITS process. This complexity takes two forms: ambiguity and variety that expands the capacity of project development.

First is ambiguity. The IITS process is expected to provide committees with a relevant content that guides successful project development. Ambiguity arises from not having a formal framework defining this process. The committees we examined resorted to using the PDC to compose actions they believe would give interpretations of standardization. In this example, we concur with Holdsworth (1994) and Humphrey (1989) that a process that may have identifiable content, but has no performance framework also lacks definitive criteria to understand its substance, requirements and how it is expected to perform. The result of using the core process and PDC mutually is multiple interpretations or equivocality in those situations that become difficult to understand when performing tasks (Daft and Lengel, 1984, 1986).

Second is variety of features evolving from interpretations of project development. Documentation of draft standard, for example, has independent life cycles producing different sets of outcomes (Figure 1, stage 5, core process). Each cycle draws upon interpretations of actions and decisions from PDC phases 2, 3 and 4 (see Figure 1). Layers of sub-processes emerge hence from embedded elements and changes in conditions from one stage to the next. Prominent sub-processes, such as information gathering, collaborative writing of the draft standard and discussions present independent needs: such as inputs, procedures and decision-making (Ngosi and Braganza, 2006). Over time, variety of the sub-processes and their needs are grounds for uncertainty: not having enough information to execute actions or to produce timely results (cf. Galbraith, 1994).

Another kind of variety in features not to be ignored is intense stages. ‘Consensus-seeking’ (Figure 1, stage 2, core process) is an example of this. When we delineate its content, this stage embraces critical evaluation of a draft standard through NSBs, collection of comments, practices guiding intense decision-making, resolution leading to a ballot result. These features evolve together to the point where this stage can easily becomes a scenario. It can be defined by its independent content that includes actors, events, change conditions and outcomes (cf. Kazman et al., 1996; Leite et al., 2000). When ballot events become too large from the size of participating parties split-level sub-processes evolve and can become locked in practice conventions. This is an attempt to reduce different kinds of uncertainty.

To summarize IITS process content, our findings reveal 42 items qualifying as sub-processes, and 10 as scenarios all co-existing with other elements. Such content expands the capacity of the IITS process to handle operational matters that serve different ends such as information processing, information management and dissemination. And we should also note both sub-processes and scenarios present crosscutting issues that are difficult to define, manage or predict, because their impact is diffuse.

## Technical view

This illustrates more clearly standardization principles shaping IITS process performance. Eight dominant features are: project proposal, committee review, information gathering, project tasks, standardization approaches, draft standards, consensus-seeking and post-ballot draft revision.

A project proposal is the input that activates the IITS process (Figure 1, stage 1, core process). It is submitted to an SDO as a document presenting proposer(s) decisions of assessed need for a standard, problems or regulations to be addressed (Berg and Schummy, 1990). A proposal becomes a project, assigned to a committee, once it attains positive ballot results supported by other SDO criteria: such as its relevance, perceived technological benefits or societal implications (ISO Directives, 2004a, b).

A committee reviews the approved proposal as presented (Figure 1, PDC phases 1 and 2) to include comments from ballot results. This opens up an array of milestones leading to intensive phases of project development. Example milestones are conceptualization, problematization and mobilization (Baxter, 2000; Ngosi and Braganza, 2006).

Information gathering is key to project development, from proposal to the publication of the IIT standard. When a project is complex, committees require substantiated information from which to develop explicit criteria for evaluating inputs. Projects IEEE 1074 and ISO 12207, for example, presented a new topic on software life cycles (SLCs) that had not been examined previously in the SES community. Structured information gathering surveys were carried out with various organizations to gather information about SLC processes and software development practices. Results of such surveys help the committees to characterize technical information akin to evaluated technology concepts, methods, processes, requirements and organization implementation views that can be standardized (cf. Ngosi and Jenkins, 1993). Importantly, definition of the project development strategy (such as, scope, objectives and hypothesis for analysis) is determined from technical information as knowledge.

Standardization is about project tasks defined and executed with appropriate scientific methods (cf. IEEE, 2001). Projects ISO 10646, JPEG and MPEG-1, for example, required R&D activity for the development of concepts and techniques fitting the technical constraints of the project development strategy. Results of R&D activity presented theoretical and technical underpinnings for definition of project tasks as well as standardization principles. As shown in Figure 1, each PDC phase has unique objectives and problem contexts. Different sets of tasks would be designed and executed to fulfil the objectives for a particular phase. Cumulative results of project tasks across the PDC contribute to the testing and validation of the proposed project hypothesis.

Central to standardization are approaches categorized as scientific methods, engineering issues and technology aspects of how the project should be developed. Many approaches are developed through pre-standardization actions and industry practices (Cargill, 1989; Berg and Schummy, 1990). In defining the project development strategy, the committee would adopt concepts from standards published by other organizations. According to Updegrove (1995: 324–325) adoption provides a shorter method for establishing standardization approaches. It helps to reduce the risk of incompatible solutions brought on by the interdependence of different standards in the IT market. An example is project MPEG-1, whereby ITU allowed ISO to adopt the coding techniques specified in its de jure standard, ITU-T Recommendation H.261 (ITU, 1988). In this example, when required concepts and techniques have been adopted the committee would revise them to define relevant approaches. A project hypothesis for analysis would be proposed to make forward predictions of possible standardization actions, leading to a desired outcome.

The IITS process produces a series of draft standards interpreting the results of performance. Draft standards are written to present the best technical solutions from combined elements that validate the project hypothesis. Table 5 contains our empirical explications of draft standards. This table also illustrates that the documentation of each draft standard follows an independent life cycle that is only evident from the results.

Consensus-seeking is the due process of standardization (Baron, 1995; Jakobs, 2000) carried out when a committee produces a draft standard; this is submitted to the SDO (Figure 1, stage 2, core process). SDOs implement ballot rules that help to generate comments and votes as new information to which the committee needs to respond in order to validate the project hypothesis (cf. Chiao et al., 2005; Ngosi and Braganza, 2006). Negotiations and decisionmaking within a formal committee meeting help to identify and to resolve conflicting views presented in the comments and votes, toward consensus agreements.

## Dynamic complexity and implications

We draw from both the technical and social views of the project development strategy to explicate the contexts of dynamic complexity. Convenient concepts by Senge (1990) and Sterman (2001) suggest that, during project development is the interdependency of different elements (detail complexity). Reciprocal loops and changes in conditions evolve over time that may be fundamentally uncontrollable (dynamic complexity). Our findings demonstrate task uncertainty, task variability, information uncertainty and equivocality as prominent implications dynamic complexity.

The IITS process has a high degree of task uncertainty from its complex information needs. Galbraith’s definitions (1973, 1987) relevant to our empirical findings illustrates that task uncertainty is the difference between information necessary to perform a task at a high level and the information that is available to the organization.

The five projects we examined have complex dimensions requiring specialized inputs, such as technical information, technology products and scientific methods. As a project progress from one stage to the next, it becomes too complex to deal with limited amount of information. Creating more information from external sources is one approach. Moreover, the time taken to manually gather and evaluate new information impedes task performance.

Project complexity also creates task variability: the frequency with which unanticipated events occur during the execution of the project and requiring non-routine procedures (Bensaou and Venkatraman, 1995: 1495). Projects JPEG and MPEG-1, for example, employed intensive R&D activity, which involved varieties of task categories. They embraced concept development, analysis of problems and requirements. Each evolving task category may need independent treatment, ensuring that results derived from it provide input to a desired course of action in the PDC. The greater the task variety presented, more information is needed to explicate the results and to make decisions to resolve unanticipated events.

The IITS process relies on its environment (stakeholders, NSBs, SDOs and committees) and project development as key sources of information. SDOs and NSBs use information processing as a major operational activity for creating and documenting information received from internal as well as external sources. To cope with diverse and unpredictable dimensions of information processing, SDOs enforce operational procedures such as registration of processed documents, inventory management and dissemination of information to numerous memberships.

Information uncertainty occurs when SDOs and NSBs overload their ineffective operational capacity with procedures that do not guarantee availability or relevance of information to various projects. The IS resources in use do not include defining the specificity of complex technical information or its quality (cf. Wilson, 1997, 1999). Committees therefore face the problem of how to process, review and manage complex technical information in a synergistic or cost-effective manner.

Ballots present information-intense events that can lead to equivocality. The DIS ballot for ISO 10646, for example, produced well over 250,000 character sets of major scripts from global languages. Almost 200 pages of collated comments needed to be clarified in different contexts: editorial, technical and policies concerning the presentation of different language scripts. National comments presented conflict in the interpretation of the same character sets as non-compliant to major scripts. Committee resolution dynamics induced equivocality in the results from repeated evaluations, verification and intense editing, without favouring certain inputs from represented stakeholders. This equivocality can be resolved by reducing factual information (Daft and Lengel, 1986).

## Concepts of CBD framework

Findings from this inductive study have illustrated content and contexts of IITS process performance, and depth of the complexity we seek to resolve. From Table 2, we draw upon reconstruction assumptions and CDB dimensions to develop concepts for our CBD framework. We discuss five concepts: integrating framework, self-contained autonomous PDS, content creation, reconstruction and operational content.

## Concept 1: integrating framework

In this concept, we refer to assumption 1: IITS process content and performance contexts can not be verified. The challenge we face with reconstruction is detail and dynamic complexity that seems to emerge naturally with IITS process performance. Following Galbraith’s (1994: 14) proposition, we use design strategy as a workable structure within which we can organize IITS process features requiring separate attention to then, define how they fit together.

Our choice of design strategy is an integrating framework that helps to demystify this process by getting to the depth of different performance contexts and complexity. It is inherently less susceptible to failure, because it can create ‘a balanced view’ of how various features with multi-dimensional functions can fit together to enhance functionality. The absence of a good design strategy is an invitation to failure of both the reconstruction actions and performance expectations hence.

## Concept 2: self-contained autonomous PDS

In this concept, we use assumption 2 to draw attention to a design outcome that gives prominence to project development. As in Herzum and Sims (2000), Allen (2001) and Veryard (2000), business and organization CBD approaches place emphasis on autonomous operations, processes or services. Empirically, we determined that the mutual application of the core process and PDC (Figure 1) produces abtruse scenarios and subprocesses.

Documentation of draft standards and consensus-seeking are two examples we have mentioned. We advocate defining core aspects of the IITS process from such intensely volatile stages requiring distinctive treatment. Defined as core aspects, we clarify esoteric content and contexts. A CBD helps to tailor their functions and leads to prominence of particular aspects of project development; a self-contained component-based autonomous PDS. We can make distinctions between each PDS, so that they are dimensionally different by concept or functionality (cf. Herzum and Sims, 2000; Veryard, 2000).

PDS will be individualized in content such as actors, users, inputs, objectives and IS resources. They will have operational scope such as processes, practices, procedures and outcomes. As a design outcome, we give prominence to project development, because a PDS fulfils only the requirements of one core aspect of the IITS process, and not a combination of several ones.

## Concept 3: content creation

In this concept we revisit assumption 1. We have a design strategy; organization of features for content creation needs a design choice. Our preference is an open-layered CBD representation, because we are creating features from core aspects of the IITS process (cf. Herzum and Sims, 2000: 168–176). Open layering is able to deal with heterogeneous qualities we adapt from other areas of this process. Concurrently, content creation provides a basis for conceptualizing how to adequately resolve phenomena of interest, and how to construct the design outcome.

To deal with these two issues concomitantly, we select views from our empirical findings (Appendix A) for special attention to how project development evolves and for stronger analytic conceptualization of content creation considerations. Two views suitable for this open-layered representation are structural and operational. As illustrated in Figure 2, dynamic features might not be captured in a static or paper-based design. However, required layers are:

\- A component-based PDS will draw upon a structural view defining its layout scheme. For explicitness of content, we use a partitioning principle (Adler, 1995; Herzum and Sims, 2000) for defining influential operational boundaries in the PDS. Furthermore, partitioning helps to parameterize key features toward shaping the design representation hence.

\- In the operational view, open layering is adaptable not only for content or functional intent, but also considerations of those emergent contexts that will occur in new performance. Two or more layers can be used to address possible operational considerations. Function categories to include their contexts can be defined also drawing attention to inter-relationships within the PDS boundaries and, between other PDS across IITS process.

## Concept 4: reconstruction

In this concept, we focus on assumptions 1, 2 and 3 together (Table 2). When we have a design strategy (concept 1) we begin with defining the core aspects of the IITS process. This is achieved by outlining the core process and PDC together, so that their features can be reviewed on an equal basis. First-level reconstruction actions begin when we create the content of a desired component-based PDS (concept 2). Second-level reconstruction is achieved through definition of the open-layered representation, operational boundaries, assignment of selected features. This leads to elimination of excessive items (concept 3).

The third and most important level of reconstruction is structured clarification of the content of the PDS. From our reviewed list of methods, business dynamics diagrams and activity flow notations (Rock-Evans, 1992) facilitate development of models for structured definition of the PDS. Modelling one operational boundary at a time facilitates attention to detail through systematic analytic steps, clarification and refinement.

Moreover, in a structured model we reconstruct by excising the breadth and depth of contexts of complexity by degree of its detail, implications and addressing competing constructs. Refinement of detail needs adequate review of functional intent of the PDS, until the structured models are uncluttered. Drawing upon other scholarly works (such as Galbraith, 1977; Starkey, 1992; Doty et al., 1993) our refinement of the PDS is based on four criteria: relevance of specified features, their ‘fit’ to function independently or together, simplification and implications of expected functionality.

## Concept 5: operational content

This concept deals with assumption 3 for which we apply the design rationale dimension (Table 2). Reconstructing a dynamic process and fitting its core aspects into a design can easily dismantle its fundamental performance constructs. Operational content accords to different types of features, their functions, contextual constraints for the use of IS resource and interdependencies.

Lee (1997) and Moran and Carroll (1996) remind us that design rationale lays the foundation for defining operational content of the design outcome, to include managing decisions of the desired functionality. Operationally, the PDS must be realistic to support assessed constructs of a particular aspect of project development. Before making commitments to the operational content of the PDS and to adequately manage decisions, this is where we need to link CBD and OIPT as a lens. We can refine particular features isolating effects of proposed views in terms of intentions of operational contexts. To do this, we adapt OIPT guidelines 2, 3 and 4 defining decision criteria for determining essential constructs that must be transparent in the design. Four criteria are information-processing capability, increase operational capacity, increase performance capabil ities and IS capability.

## Conceptual CBD framework

Essential elements from these five concepts are associated to define our CBD framework. Figure 3 shows that an integrating framework is most adaptable, because we can organize the features around views that create differentiated contexts of component-based PDS.

Three empirically determined views that apply here are structural, functional and behavioural.

## Views of CBD framework

Structural view is a requirement to organize content features of this framework. While component-based PDS can truly become autonomous in this integrating framework, they can inter-operate dynamically in the same frame of reference.

Functional view will address high-level functions and methods governing performance of this framework. The functions will not include operational content of PDS that will be defined exclusively. We add three aspects that will be expected to enforce some of the policies governing successful project development: life cycle, ownership and micro-management.

\- Life cycle: Because component-based PDS would be created from core aspects of the IITS process, the life cycles in Figure 1 will no longer be viable for use. Hence, we advocate the need for a new IITS process formal life cycle to guide performance of the PDS.

\- Ownership: This integrating framework incorporates NSBs and SDOs. This is because these autonomous PDS are more likely to have different ownership patterns spread among SDO units and NSBs (cf. Kunda and Brooks, 2000; McGregor, 2000) that make progress towards rationalizing IITS process functions.

\- SDOs should be encouraged to adopt effective micromanagement approaches addressing successful project development. On the basis of our assessment of the impact of the PDS, two key approaches are: linking projects to IITS process strategy (Matheson and Matheson, 1998) to ensure understanding of performance needs and operational challenges. Another is customizing the PDS with solutions for creating relevant intellect, information and knowledge (Quinn et al., 1997: 514).

The behavioural view helps us to answer those questions that could be posed regarding our integrating framework. This view represents the next step toward the realities of project development to include architectures that are expected to enforce definition of the framework itself. Example architectures that can also help to rationalize a host of operational contexts supporting different types of PDS include:

\- Process architecture defining required operational scope of PDS, representations and practices. Harmon (2003) emphasises the importance of developing a process architecture prior to developing computer-based support. This is because content and context definitions capture different types of scenarios of the PDS (such as processes, functions) thereby aiding decision-making on resources needs.

\- Communication architecture support distributed computing environment in which this framework will perform. This will include information architecture to deal with integration issues and provisions of information (Laudon and Laudon, 2000).

\- Service architecture for creating operational interfaces providing key services. Connectivity through platform services increases the capacity to handle various operational aspects. For example, information manage ment (Kirk, 1999), workflow and sharing information (Kammer, 2000; Newell et al., 2000).

## Discussion

In this article we have described project development as a core substance of the IITS process. We touched on two key issues that are evolving in IS field regarding this process: the lack of empirical attention and widely debated concerns of its effectiveness.

Since 2002, the International Journal of IT Standards and Standardization Research (JITSSR) has encouraged the need to strengthen empirical research on the creation of IT standards. Its key studies include making sense of the phenomena surrounding IT standardization, relevance of standards to the IS field as well as how to improve both standards research and practice. Benbasat and Zmud (1999: 9) Recommendation 5 advocates ‘more proactive’ and ‘context rich bodies of research’, as insightful measures that strengthen empirical research. For research on IITS, what emerges from the reviewed literatures is the need for a more proactive confluence between IS and IT standards body of knowledge. This confluence is a necessity for understanding and for challenging the dominant process dynamics to which IT standardization is referenced. Without due attention to these dynamics, IT standardization will remain rooted in economic or policy justifications that are frequently used to spur the rush to standards (cf. Cargill, 1989: 4–5).

What makes our research article proactive is that, we have taken the first steps to present a comprehensive empirical rationale of project development that challenges theoretical conceptions underpinning the IITS process and its complexity. These three highlighted areas are underexplored in the IS field. What we claim as a contribution to IS literatures is our proactive empirical intervention that will stimulate critical thinking about conceptions (and misconceptions) about the IITS process. Indeed, our research article has significant relevance. The five projects we examined make a contribution to strengthen understanding of the content and performance of the IITS process. Empirical works on IT standardization can no longer pigeonhole the IITS process under the same labels as consortia-based studies, only because of similar representations.

In our literature review, we highlighted enduring criticisms about the IITS process that seem more copious than ‘calls’ for solutions. This research article connects successful project development to the reconstruction of this process within a CBD framework. We have therefore taken a step further to strengthen empirical research by focusing closely on what is important to influence IITS process effectiveness.

As a main contributions, this CBD framework demonstrates our accountability to close this gap in knowledge. It will overcome theoretical criticisms that need to be answered with specific solutions. Timeliness of IIT standards responding to global IT market needs (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 2001; Gosain, 2003), changing traditional institutional forms of standardizing in SDOs (King and Lyytinen, 2003) and old regimes (Krechmer, 2005) are example enduring criticisms that we touched on. Answers to these criticisms depend upon implementable solutions that might be derived from this CBD framework.

## Implications for practice

Earlier, we highlighted CBD approaches emerging as ISrelated developments encouraging how we can deal with complex process functionality to solve problems creatively. Other demonstrated impacts in e-commerce enterprises (Fingar et al., 2000; Allen, 2001) and finance sector (Sprott, 2000) strengthen the position we seek in reconstructing the IITS process. Instead of providing mechanisms for managing systemic IITS process matters in different directions, our CBD framework is a robust foundation for provisioning incisive IITS process change strategies, with attention to the qualities of the PDS. Matching chosen change strategies, performance expectations, requirements and technology toward desired solution details (cf. Galbraith, 1977; Doty et al., 1993; Sauer and Willcocks, 2003) assures justification of the implementation of this framework; if not more so, successful project development.

## Concluding remarks

The focus of our research article has been to develop concepts for a CBD framework from which to reason the reconstruction of the IITS process. We used OIPT as theory challenging this premise from rich descriptions of this process and presented an implementable design outcome. In these concluding remarks, therefore, it is fitting to revisit our theory-driven research design. Yin (2003) indicates that a multiple-case design should follow literal replication logic in the selection of cases such as: (a) conditions of the case lead to predicting the same results or (b) a theoretical replication logic whereby conditions of the case lead to predicting contrasting results.

Our multiple-case inquiry adopted a theoretical replication approach to study project development across five projects with deviant elements. The study is intensely analytical, because deviant elements make us question how and why actions, and embedded items differentially shaped in this universal life cycle. In terms of theoretical replication, our case (project development) with five projects leads to contrasting results. The degree of prediction of producing contrasting results cannot be determined accurately, because different types of methods used in the case study create irreconcilable positions. Inductive analysis of the case findings ensures robustness and confirmability underlying the philosophical assumptions of the case (cf. Flick, 1998; Strauss and Corbin, 1998). Since we compared these findings under equal conditions, this helps us to develop explanatory and integrative empirical evidence, and from which we define the IITS process with reasonable accuracy.

Our theory-driven research design grounded in OIPT as a lens is a distinctively different approach. It addresses this kind of theoretical replication, whereby we can reconcile different methods with particular paradigms. The findings obtained from projects can be examined independently. By grounding our research design in theory, this instils certainty about integrative multiple-case evidence (Yin, 2003), with more detailed specifications such as those we summarize in Appendix A. Thus, from seemingly separate classes of projects (GIITS and SES) our methodological reasoning grounded in OIPT as a lens produces an integrated set of results that we contribute to the IS field:

(1) Our theory-driven research design makes a theoretical contribution to requirements for IS research designs mentioned in Gregor (2002, 2006) and MISQ Roadmap (2004). (2) Empirical definition of the IITS process. (3) We were able to link CBD to OIPT as a lens strengthening theoretical focus in developing substantive concepts as well as details of our CBD framework. (4) Additionally, in Goodman’s philosophical views (1988: 71–72) OIPT now has an extended meaning and interpretation into a new schema as a lens, which meet the methodological requirements in our study of IITS process. It has acquired a new context of use, and which can improve with practice in other IITS studies, without changing its original characteristics or definitions.

## Agenda for further research work

We have not yet determined how complete a set of qualities is necessary for definition of the component-based PDS and for specification of their requirements. Another angle we are pursuing is that groups of people in the area of study tend to make our problem solving process fundamentally social. Markus (2004: 13) indicates how many technochange solutions cannot be adopted or used easily, because they conflict with existing organizational structures, cultures and practices. A case study by Kunda and Brooks (2000) also reminds us to look closely at factors affecting the introduction of CBD strategies in organizations. In particular, perceptions about environmental and organization level issues affect CBD implementation success.

Our case study surveys incorporated questions aimed at identifying the acceptance of our idea of a CBD of the IITS process. However, we need additional surveys with SDOs, NSBs and committees so that they can review our conceptual CBD framework together with the PDS designs. Without this broad survey intervention, we will have incomplete concepts. Our agenda for future research also needs publication in academic journals to contribute to knowledge; indeed, to encourage reciprocal interaction with practitioners of CBD process approaches and IT standardization. Our immediate agenda items are as follows:

(1) This reconstruction builds upon how we parameterize core aspects of the IITS process. We believe that these core aspects need to be qualitatively analysed in order to define the right kind of features that can be combined or eliminated entirely. The definition of core aspects requires in-depth surveys within the IITS environment to influence our decision-making. How we dimensionalize this CBD framework further depends on collected views suggesting its acceptance in the IITS environment. This avoids shortcoming associated with people’s resistance to change (cf. Markus, 2004).

(2) Our characterization of the IITS process shows that a theory-driven approach provides appropriate ‘connectedness’ theorized from: (a) empirical evidence to reasoning behind the analysis, (b) to specificity of our reconstruction actions and (c) to interpret the results hence. In light of Pettigrew (1997, 2000) this theorydriven connection in process-oriented studies is still rare. We are therefore taking this as an opportunity to develop an integrative process analytical framework defining our methodological reasoning. Exclusively, this analytical framework will make a contribution to organizational process studies.

## About the authors

Theodora Ngosi has a Ph.D. in Computer Science from City University of London. Her thesis investigated the reconstruction of the international IT standardization process embracing a component-based design perspective to project development. Her other publications on the international IT standardization process appear in the Journal of Information Technology and British Academy of Management. Her career interests are business process standards (enterprise, operational and management of IT strategies); organization-based project development; component-based design to drive organizational transformation toward process effectiveness; IT standardization; research methods, information systems and, managing change.
