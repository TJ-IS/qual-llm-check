## A B S T R A C T

Networks of communication are essential when managing corporate work and performing information exchange; the systems must allow them to be dynamic and well-structured. They help provide high organizational performance and innovative capacity in today’s knowledge intense corporations, and this means that organizations must manage the networks strategically. Despite the fact that practitioners are aware of the huge influence of informal communication on decision making, little is known about the underlying principles of efficient employee network collaboration, which is dynamic in nature, especially for complex environments resulting from steady innovation and high competitive pressure. We addressed this issue from a complexity perspective, using an agent based simulation to visualize the key elements of efficient, information-based, collaborative decision making. Our findings suggested that information and communication technologies (ICT) may not be able to leverage corporate performance of the increasingly complex adaptive organizations. There seem to be elementary natural constraints on the cognitive capacities of people dealing with and managing information. Rather than a better technica approach, a more ecologic one is therefore advocated as the best way to improve decision making.

## 1. Introduction

For businesses today, innovation is not a choice, but a question of survival. Shortened product lifecycles, intense global competition, and connectivity force companies to adopt new methods and technologies rapidly in order to stay ahead of the competition. Efficient information management and decision making performance become key success factors for management as flexible reaction to changes in the market have become vital.

But information managers must increasingly choose between advocating the use of more high quality information and recommending better tools for using it for better decision making. Three decades ago it seemed that organizations gathered more information than they used but seemed to still ask for more. But today the organization’s use of information is no longer in simple decision making: the use of information seems to be embedded in social norms that make it highly symbolic.

The positive impact of high quality analysis [15] and involvement, IS management effectiveness, etc. [16] on the quality of decision making is well documented. At the same time, the importance of a good alignment between IT and corporate strategy is not new but is regularly shown to improve corporate performance [5].

Undoubtedly employees and managers find it difficult to perform well in today’s fast changing and complex work environment [18]. Furthermore, focusing on alignment is not enough, or can even be counterproductive: the complexity of the systems often result in poor performance. On a personal level, even with better ISs, the quality of the organization’s decision-making is not improving; employees and managers are overwhelmed with information arriving in e-mails, reports, meetings, phone calls, video-conferences, letters, regulations, presentations and documents, and even blogs, resulting in information overload [7].

Thus we decided to study the complex character of the organization and the decision making processes as a possible cause of poor corporate decision making.

## 2. Complexity theory

Mainstream science is still mainly based on the paradigm of reductionism, which has been applied to thought about systems for centuries; but though algorithms and mathematical equations are powerful tools for describing linear and causal relationships like gravity and planetary motion, they are of little value in explaining complex behavior of the organization or an ant colony. When a phenomenon is encountered that seems complex it is generally assumed that it results from some underlying mechanism that itself is complex, but as Agent Based Simulations (ABS) show, simple programs can produce great complexity.

Complexity theory and its implication to management techniques and paradigms are not new [1,2]; it has its roots in disciplines such as physics, mathematics, biology, economics, and artificial intelligence. Whereas most mainstream approaches are concerned with specialized fields of research, they are less adequate in giving insight into more integrative and holistic approaches to understanding reality. In fact, they are less able to address interactions in networked structures. Embedded in the scientific paradigm of reductionism, they try to detect patterns that allow predictability, control, stability, and repeatability by identifying fundamental causal relationships amongst the systems elements. Complexity, in contrast, emerges in the non-linear interplay of a multitude of elements that need to be studied holistically. At different times, the same complex system can produce chaotic or orderly behavior.

To examine the emerging behavior of interacting units (people, bits of information, etc.) complexity theory needs research methods that allow researchers to model and simulate systems performing an ever evolving, non-linear dynamic behavior [3]. Artificial neural networks and agent based simulations are currently the two most popular approaches for the simulation of non-linear dynamics [8]. These methods can be applied to a wide variety of projects [6,9,13]. In order to examine the collaboration patterns of decision makers in organizational networks, we therefore decided to use agent-based simulations. The particular focus on efficiency of decision making as a surrogate for efficient information use was labeled as an ‘‘ecological’’ information approach; however it has not yet been studied in the frame of a changing company acting as a complex adaptive system. The information flows in a complex adaptive organization will here be termed an information ecology.

Corporate information ecology with less resource consumption and waste of energy requires analysis of the information environment of the organization as a diverse, interdependent, cultural, social, technical and political subsystem. In such complex systems the behavior of actors is often autonomous, and non-linear involving interactions with other actors, who therefore participate as key elements in a large organizational network of subsystems in which information is created, flows, and is being used.

## 3. The model

Models are often based on a few simple assumptions that still can generate complexity in the model close to that experienced in the real world. Drawing from the concepts of complexity and network theory, an artificial world had to be designed to allow us to study the intra-organizational communication schemes and their potential benefits to coordinated decision making.

## 3.1. Complex adaptive organizations and dynamic networks

We define dynamic networks as network-typologies that differentiate dynamic, stable, and internal networks. Networks are clusters of firms or units coordinated by market mechanisms instead of chains of commands. Stability and dynamics are characterized by the intensity and duration of the relations between the actors and the number of actors (i.e., its variability). Competitive companies are characterized by their ability to maintain a good organizational fit between the environmental requirements, capabilities, competitive strategy, and organizational structure. This corresponds to the theory of organizational consistency. In which managers make strategic choices based on their perception of the environment and their organization’s capabilities. The influence of the organizational environment is crucial, and the managers’ strategy is mainly to generate a fit. Networks may be more or less complex and dynamic, depending on the competitive environment.

## 3.2. The external environment

The world outside the organization may be modeled as an information-based competitive environment that requires its inhabitants to adapt to competitive pressure. Any market is a fitness-landscape in which organizations are placed on different levels, depending on their capacity to cope with restraints and exploit opportunities. We therefore modeled organizations as open systems that continuously interact with their environment to obtain information about the effect of their efforts to keep-up with or pass the competition. Thus the management derives appropriate strategic objectives expressed as dynamically changing knowledge-combinations. They represent the competitive organizational outputs, such as innovative products, services, processes, or business models.

## 3.3. The internal environment

Internal networks require the creation of an internal market in which decentralized processes coordinate the exchange-relations of the elements that make up the internal value chain. The internal environment consists of a networked structure of knowledgeworkers, modeled as virtual agents, a network of agents exchanging knowledge in order to ensure market efficiency by using internal resources to make decisions.

As decision making in organizations is a collective task, it depends on efficient synchronization among the decision makers; thus it needs to be network-based and involve collaboration. Networks consist of nodes and edges but as decisions are always made by people, we consider them as the elements or nodes connected by channels of exchange – edges. Exchanges include both tangible and intangible goods. When focusing on the qualitative dimension of decision making, a decision maker is mostly concerned with exchange of intangible goods, such as data, information, knowledge, and competencies. Quality of intangibles can be influenced by both people and ICT, both of which act as catalysts in the process. ICT adds various exchange channels to the basic one, which is face-to-face.

The quality of any channel depends on its capacity to transmit information of relevance to the decision process. Quality decisions depend on a combination of relevant knowledge and competencies, though it can be argued that other factors such as information spillover, actor education levels, and learning styles should be considered. However within our theoretical framework, we consider that decision making quality and efficiency depends basically on information synchronization and combination through exchange channels.

In order to measure the quality or efficiency of decision making, we adopted a concept proposed by Simons and Davila [19]: Return on Management (ROM), which derives an input–output relationship of management actions. . . It measures how efficiently a manager has chosen amongst available information sources, given his or her limited managerial time and attention:

$$
\mathrm{ROM} = \frac {\text { organizational   energy   released }}{\text { time   and   attention   invested }}
$$

In overloaded work conditions, Herb Simon’s bounded rationality prevails: team members with limited time and attention try to achieve satisfactory solutions rather than ones that are superior to all others. As information becomes increasingly abundant, the pressure to satisfice grows more acute.

We augmented the ROM ratio by adding the number of agents used in the decision (employees are costly, too). Thus we consider ROM to include a factor showing the efficient use of people in accomplishing the problem solving process. Thus in our simulation, ROM was defined as:

## 3.4. The agents

All agents have four characteristic properties, their: knowledge profiles (lists of all the knowledge domains in the organization); with each agent’s profile showing his or her level of expertise, capabilities, and competence.

Table 1 is an example of an agent’s knowledge profile. The top row knowledge area lists as columns the knowledge domains required to make managerial decisions. In this example we show eight distinct domains. Thus the knowledge profile is an ndimensional vector, where n is the number of different knowledge domains in a particular decision domain. The second row, competency level, lists the degree of expertise that a particular agent has acquired in that domain.

Aggregating the profiles of all agents in the agent network results in a knowledge matrix that shows the competencies of the agent population and models the organizational knowledge available for decision making (Table 2).

The knowledge matrix depicts in its top row the knowledge domains in the same way as an agents’ knowledge profile. In this case, n = 12, there are 12 different knowledge domains present in the organization. The rows below list Agents 1–14. Again the number of agents (=m, here 14) depends on the size of the organization being modeled. Finally, the numbers inside the table are the competency-levels of the agents. N = 3, M = 4 would therefore show us what Agent 4 knows about knowledge domain 3; in this exemplary table he is expert in this domain as he holds a competency level of 9 which no other agent has yet acquired. On the other hand this agent knows nothing about domains 9–11 and therefore relies on other agent’s expertise to be able to form a decision for that domain.

Not only knowledge is heterogeneously distributed throughout the agent network. Agents also communicate preferentially and thus they have individually different relationship profiles.

The individual ‘‘communication-vector’’ of an agent displays individual preferences in the form of probability values for communicating with other agents. The length of the communication vector shows the agents to whom an individual agent can connect (Table 3).

This is an exemplary communication vector of agent 3. It contains 8 positions. Thus Agent 3 is connected to 8 other agents with different probabilities, indicated by the numerical values. All individual relationships can be aggregated in an adjacency matrix which we term an ‘‘intra-organizational-communication-matrix’’. A sociogram is thus obtained which contains all probability values for agents inside the organization to contact each other. It is the equivalent of the network diagram that visualizes stronger (more probable) relations by thicker edges (Table 4).

Just as it is the case for the knowledge matrix the ‘‘preferential communication network’’ is a matrix. The numerical values inside are again the probability values for each agent to contact another agent. It should be noted that these are not necessarily identical. For example agent 5 contacts agent 2 with a probability of 5% whereas agent 2 contacts agent 5 with a 3% probability.

The process then involves an agent inside the organization being picked at random and activated to solve the problem of adaptation, i.e. generating the knowledge required to decide on a solution. If the agent is unable to solve it by matching his resources of knowledge, relations, time and attention, he relates to another agent in order to synchronize and combine their individual resources. Agents are allowed to communicate freely throughout the organization. If the required knowledge bundles are achieved, a decision is made and organizational energy is released. In this case, the organization stays competitive, while if it is not, it is falling behind its competitors. Accordingly, the behavior of an agent is controlled by a simple production system where each agent follows the same rules:

\- Receive problem statement.

\- Check time and attention level.

\- If zero: no action.

\- If positive: Match individual knowledge profile with the current problem statement.

\- If match 100% successful, decision is generated.

\- If not, relate to another agent of relationship profile.

\- Combine knowledge and reinitialize matching process.

\- If match 100% successful, decision is generated.

\- If not, repeat the whole process.

Once a decision is made, the next problem statement is given to a randomly picked agent. It requires different knowledge combinations and therefore re-triggers the process. Macro behavior in the form of specific patternsof collaboration and group dynamics are not programmed but emerge through the simple agent rules.

## 3.5. System adaptation: feedback loops and learning

Agents have initial knowledge and relationship values. As this is a dynamic model, these values evolve in the course of agent interaction. They are thus dynamic and increase or decrease depending on the outcome of each interaction. Efficient decision making, considered as desired outcome of collaborative information exchange, is positively related to both knowledge and relationship values. If no decisions can be formed, no organizational energy is released (ROM = 0). In this case knowledge and relations decline. Furthermore agents have limited time and attention span, and these are variables that are used up in the course of inter-agent communication. This results in a ‘‘cost’’ of communication; it consists of two elements: maintaining inter-agent connections (which act as transaction channels information flow) consumes the agent’s attention and exchange of information takes time. Inside the software, all decline rates can be varied to simulate different organizational structures, cultures, and competitive situations.

Agents can increase their individual knowledge base only when interacting with agents who possess higher value in any knowledge area. If they do not interact, no learning takes place and knowledge possessed declines. Interaction is necessary not only to expand but to maintain a solid knowledge base, as knowledge declines if it is not ‘‘used’’, i.e. shared. During interaction, agents strengthen their relations to other agents if their collaboration provided to a positive result. The values of connections that are not activated decline like the knowledge values if knowledge is not shared. Thus, decisionmaking provides the feedback to the participating agent population who adjust their ties to each other. This mechanism rewards successful collaboration patterns by strengthening relations. Moreover decisions generated create new knowledge which the participating agents gain in the course of communication in contrast to those agents who are not collaborating.

## 4.2. Observations in simulation runs

Given the multitude of variables and their possible constellations we have concentrated on observations that allowed us to identify general dynamics and characteristic patterns occurring in decision making processes inside networked organizations.

## 4.2.1. Knowledge

Common to almost all simulation runs is the development of the aggregated knowledge of the agent community. After rapid initial growth amongst all agents, corporate knowledge was maintained on a rather high level for a long period of time. But instead of remaining at this level it suddenly starts declining and decreases again. This emerging curve was characteristic to most runs, except for some special simulations run with certain group sizes. Even the rate of knowledge decline did not alter the typical form of this emerging learning curve’’ (Fig. 6).

## 4.2.2. Relations

Starting out with an equally connected agent community the simulation rapidly produced a biased network in which patterns of inter-agent communication emerged. There was a clear shift from a globally connected agent community to agent cliques, maintaining a few strong connections with one another. This of course depended on the relation decline rate (which could be fixed) but the simulation showed that the decline rate only affected the speed with which it happened.

This evolution seems to suggest that full connectivity through out the organization would not be the optimal structure for efficient decision making. It also raised the question: Which connection patterns are likely to be efficient and robust and ultimately lead an ideal cluster size?

Initially the path lengths in the agent networks were equally long – one step – as every agent was connected to all others.

## 4.2.4. Quality of decision making – return on management (ROM)

Decisions were taken once the requirements had been fulfilled. Thus there was no constant, linear progression of the development of the ROM. Instead the graph displaying the ROM was pulsing every time a decision was taken. No general observation can be made about the ROM. The performance was different in each run and each setting. One observable relation existed between the evolutions of the ROM and the corporate knowledge-base: a certain knowledge-level inside the agent-network was necessary before there was any ROM; i.e. before quality decisions could be made. Furthermore there seemed to be a correlation between the decision making performance of the agent community and their attention level. Once the levels were heavily loaded, the ROM stagnated and no decisions could be taken anymore. This disagrees with some authors who have stated that decision processes should be designed as systems of attention management rather than systems of information management. However, our research seemed to suggest that there were limits to the capacity of management of attention and negotiation.

Possibly even more interesting and surprising: there seemed to be a relation between the ROMs generated by different group sizes. Running simulations with groups of 4–7 agents, the ROM was flourishing extraordinarily. The same occurred for the development of the agents’ knowledge, which was constantly growing. In simulation runs with different group sizes these effects did not happen. Also for clusters of 4–7 people the path lengths did not rise.

## 5. Discussion

Our research seems to lead to the conclusion that complete connectivity, paradoxically is not able to improve the quality of decision making. Also that there should be reasonable doubt that 100% information can be provided in an economically or ecologically efficient way. Some authors [11] have nevertheless suggested an interesting concept that could solve this deadlock. They claim that companies like BMW, IBM, and Novartis are discovering what they label swarm businesses, which are successful in addressing the emerging era of increased collaboration, increased information exchange, and continuous learning. According to research on efficient collaboration, increased communication provides a positive contribution to team performance and team success [14]. Other experiments [12] lead by researchers in an innovative project around group size and performance in problem solving, found that the improved performance of small groups (5–10 people) was due to, their ‘‘ability to work together to generate and adopt correct responses, reject erroneous responses, and effectively process information . . . the group members combine their abilities and resources to perform better than the best of an equivalent number of individuals on the highly intellective complementary group task’’.

We found similar patterns: team-sized clusters of agents were capable of generating the highest ROM by efficiently making quality decisions; team sized collaboration clusters were more robust and efficient than structures characterized by complete connectivity. These findings are consistent with the ‘‘small world phenomenon.’’ It points out the efficiency of network structures that are characterized by the existence of local clusters in combination with bridging linkages in-between them. In such a ‘‘small world’’ all nodes are able to connect with path lengths of 6 steps. The results are equally consistent with Burd’s Structural Holes theory [4]. This does not only make the organizational network more robust and stable, but also offers a potential explanation of how even big organizations are able to synchronize efforts and resources: Information can flow quickly (in a few steps) to all other people in the organization.

But the question remains: why and how do team centered, decentralized systems outperform other forms of organizational structures?

There seem to be ecological, cognitive limits to information processing or problems with which teams must cope: members must devote time and attention to sorting through all the information, know-how, and feedback obtained to select content that is most useful for their project.

The more knowledge the team members gather, the more time and attention the processing demands, while sub-optimal satisficing behaviors are likely to increase. The time and attention available for other task-related activities is reduced too, further threatening team performance.

The processing requirements incurred as teams gather more knowledge thus can increase opportunity costs as well as satisficing behavior in overloaded environments.

Thus managing information can lead to saturation, and thus information overload and deadlocks.

Maintaining transaction channels always involves transaction costs- in the case of this simulation mainly the loss of time and attention of the interacting agents. The issue of efficient information management and the related decision making performance is the relation of group size and number of transaction channels, which is exponential. It follows the combinatory relation of nchoose-r, which leads to the formula n(n  1)/2 that explains how many pairs of communicating people can possibly exist. These effects offer a potential explanation of how information overload emerges, and therefore, why decentralized management of information makes sense. If the size of the communicating and information-exchanging group exceeds the optimal region of 4–7 people, complexity and costs rise. Indeed, observation of large groups has shown that they tend to break up into smaller (4–7 people) sub-groups.

The evolution of clusters, resulting from managing information overload, leads to decreased connectivity (protecting the individual agents from information overload), the overall network remains stable and network distances between agents still remain small (average 1–7 steps). This allows overall network cohesion while avoiding information deadlocks created by exorbitant communication costs.

The one most important limitation of our research is the possible difference between these simulations and reality. The similarity between Salton’s team size distribution and our results was a relevance test. It suggested that our simulations stand the test of reality. But as in all agent based simulations, one can never be really sure that they provide a real life result.

## 5.1. Implications for managers

If efficiency is embedded in relations, management should concentrate on facilitating group-collaboration. This requires a loosening of the control driven management paradigm in favor of allowing for decentralized decision making and auto-organization among co-workers. Interesting examples for an efficient approach is Amazon’s ‘‘2-Pizza’’ policy: No team should be larger than 2 pizzas can feed. An alternative example is Toyota’s ‘‘Obeya’’, which means ‘‘Big room’’. All critical decision makers gather in one room and make important decisions together. Teams are only as large as the room allows to and meet every morning for 15 min or less. In Toyotas words:’’ The obeya group is like a cross functional team. But instead of weekly meetings, it is an ongoing session. The group would typically include engineers, design stylists, suppliers, assembly workers and members of our marketing team. Teams in these war-rooms showed a doubling of productivity. Why? Among other things, teams had easy access to each other for both coordination of their work and for learning, and the work artifacts they posted on the walls remained visible to all. The result is fewer changes, saved time and reduced cost. ‘‘Some will claim the emergence of an informal organizational structure that circumvents management control. But this does not necessarily mean that informal organizations are problems to their managers, on the contrary, companies like eBay, Starbucks, or Bell Canada profit by allowing decentralized decision making.
