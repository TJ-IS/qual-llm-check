This study examines how individuals filter knowledge encountered in online forums, a common platform for knowledge exchange in an electronic network of practice. ## Introduction

In today’s networked environment, the Internet has become a ubiquitous platform for supporting both personal and organizational knowledge exchange (Jarche 2010, Rodgers and Negash 2007). One of the most rapidly growing mechanisms for such exchange is the electronic network of practice (ENP), which is a collection of loosely connected and geographically distributed individuals who engage in similar activities and communicate via computer-mediated channels (Wasko et al. 2009). Participants in an electronic network of practice commonly exchange knowledge through specialized, publicly available Internet forums where individuals can share their own knowledge and seek solutions to their problems (Wasko and Faraj 2005). For example, a computer programmer might post a solution on an online forum, such as www.experts-exchange.com, in response to the question of another network participant. The same programmer might then return to the forum to seek potential solutions to his or her own problem. Researchers have begun to explore factors that influence individuals’ willingness to contribute knowledge to an electronic network of practice (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Wasko et al. 2009); however, less is understood about knowledge evaluation and adoption within such networks. Most searches on a network forum produce several potentially competing candidate solutions that vary in completeness, accuracy, and utility for solving a given problem. Knowledge seekers thus face the task of filtering these solutions and deciding which, if any, they will adopt. Because adoption of invalid, specious, or otherwise flawed knowledge can be costly to both individuals and firms, there is significant practical and theoretical incentive to better understand how knowledge encountered in an electronic network of practice is evaluated as part of the knowledge exchange process.

Over the past decade, a handful of studies in the information systems (IS) domain have begun to explore how knowledge seekers adopt knowledge through technology-mediated channels (Fadel et al. 2009, Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). Most of this work draws from dual process theories of human information processing. These theories posit that knowledge adoption is governed by two possible processing routes, moderated by the degree to which the individual engages in cognitive elaboration of the knowledge being considered (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b). These studies have laid the groundwork for understanding how knowledge exchanged through technology-mediated channels is ultimately adopted; however, this work has typically limited its focus to evaluating knowledge seekers’ adoption of a single piece of knowledge. This perspective, while valuable, does not address how knowledge seekers first filter through multiple possible alternatives before arriving at a final adoption choice. This filtering stage is particularly important in technology-mediated contexts such as electronic networks of practice, where knowledge seekers are likely to encounter multiple competing solutions contributed by an array of loosely connected network participants.

In this study, we seek to advance current research on electronic networks of practice and technologymediated knowledge exchange by exploring the process by which knowledge seekers evaluate and filter knowledge found on Internet forums associated with electronic networks of practice. Our work contributes to existing IS knowledge evaluation research in two primary ways. First, we focus our investigation on the filtering component of knowledge evaluation as opposed to post-hoc evaluation of a single adopted piece of knowledge. In doing so, we draw on decision-making theory (i.e., staged models of choice) to understand when filtering occurs. We then employ dual process theory to develop hypotheses of how filtering occurs when individuals must consider multiple pieces of knowledge they encounter in an electronic network of practice forum. Specifically, we hypothesize and quantify a moderating role of elaboration—the degree to which the knowledge seeker “scrutinizes the issue-relevant” knowledge content (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, p. 7)— in determining the relative strength of the dual information processing routes during filtering.

Second, although prior knowledge adoption research has employed field survey methodologies (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008) or laboratory experiment methodologies (Fadel et al. 2009), we test our hypotheses by conducting a field experiment in which professional programmers consider candidate solutions to a programming problem using a simulated online forum. Data gathered during the experiment include participants’ answers to survey questions, ranking of candidate solutions, and a proximal measure of elaboration using gaze patterns captured by an eye-tracking device. This approach leverages the methodological strengths of prior work by combining the external validity afforded by professional field participants with the rigors of experimental control. Additionally, use of an eye-tracking device permits detailed examination of elaboration while participants filter multiple candidate solutions. The results of our experiment advance theoretical understanding of knowledge evaluation in electronic networks of practice and offer practical implications to both knowledge-producing and knowledge-seeking practitioners.

## Theoretical Background

An electronic network of practice is a large, loosely knit, geographically distributed group of individuals that relies on electronic means as its primary form of communication. ENP participants engage in a shared practice, but they typically do not know each other or expect to meet face-to-face (Brown and Dugid 2001, Wasko et al. 2009). ENPs are characterized by several features that distinguish them from other types of groups such as communities of practice or virtual teams. First, participation in an ENP is voluntary and self-organizing. Individuals autonomously determine how, when, and to what extent they participate in the network. Importantly, this means that “knowledge seekers have no control over who voluntarily responds to their questions, or the helpfulness or relevance of the responses to the current problem at hand” (Wasko et al. 2009, p. 257). Second, participation in the network is open to anyone, regardless of personal acquaintance with other users or familiarity with the network. This distinguishes ENPs from more cohesive internal organizational work groups or communities of practice in which participation is often regulated by established norms, expectations, or strong social ties. Third, network participants are linked through their shared engagement in practice. As opposed to more static knowledge repositories or databases, knowledge exchange in ENPs centers on problem solving and often produces an exchange between two or more anonymous participants. Finally, as noted above, ENPs rely on asynchronous computer-mediated communication and exist primarily in electronic space such as openly accessible Internet forums (Wasko and Faraj 2005, Wasko et al. 2009). Consequently, communication among participants is frequently void of innate social or other nonverbal cues inherent in other forms of group or interpersonal communication.

One of the most common platforms used by ENPs is the online discussion forum, an Internet-based, virtual bulletin board where network participants can share knowledge with each other. Such knowledge sharing typically occurs in a question-answer format, wherein network participants post questions about specific problems they are facing and other participants offer potential solutions. The openness and searchability of online forums create the potential to support rapid knowledge sharing among thousands of individuals at a relatively low cost, thus making them a popular platform for knowledge exchange in an ENP. However, these same characteristics also present unique challenges to ENP knowledge seekers as compared with other previously studied forms of technology-mediated knowledge transfer. For example, knowledge received via a solicited email originates from a limited number of known sources about whom credibility indicators (e.g., organizational position, tenure, etc.) are available (Sussman and Siegal 2003). The same might be true of knowledge retrieved from an internal knowledge management system (Fadel et al. 2009), which also commonly undergoes an organizational validation process to ensure its level of quality (Durcikova and Gray 2009). Even broader online communities are likely to self-regulate their content via norms and expectations that arise from more cohesive, sustained community membership (Brown and Dugid 1991, Zhang and Watts 2008). In contrast, an ENP forum rarely imposes constraints on who is allowed to submit knowledge or what quality of knowledge is allowable. Instead, ENPs are often orbited by a constellation of participants ranging from active contributors to lurkers to even saboteurs (Wasko and Faraj 2005). This lack of formalized control, coupled with the number and anonymity of knowledge contributors, complicates the task of the ENP knowledge seeker, who must filter and evaluate a potentially large number of candidate solutions before deciding which, if any, to adopt.

The process of filtering multiple candidate solutions can be conceptualized as part of a multistage model of decision making that results in adoption of a candidate solution. Among the first to formalize such a model was Simon (1957), who proposed three basic stages of decision making: intelligence, design, and choice. Figure 1 adapts Simon’s original model for filtering and evaluation in the ENP context. The first stage is search, in which the individual seeks information that informs his or her decision. This is followed by the filtering and evaluation stage, in which a decision-making model is applied to the information gathered during search. Finally, in the adoption stage, the individual makes a choice based on the outcome of applying the decision-making model to the information gathered. Applying these stages to an ENP forum, the knowledge seeker first searches the forum for a solution to a problem, then filters and evaluates the resulting solution set, discarding some solutions and retaining others for further consideration, and ultimately chooses which, if any, solution to adopt.

In the IS domain, research has largely focused on the search and adoption stages of the knowledge seeking and adoption process. With respect to search, researchers have sought to understand how and why individuals search for solutions and when they terminate the search process. Broder (2002) developed a taxonomy of three types of Web searches (informational, navigational, and transactional) and asserted that informational search was used most frequently, a finding confirmed by other studies (Rose and Levinson 2004). Research has also identified different types of search tasks (e.g., simple versus complex, specific versus general, exploratory versus lookup) and has investigated the impact of task type on the search process (Wildemuth and Freund 2009). Finally, scholars have recognized that there are limits to an individuals’ searching ability because of a variety of factors (e.g., amount of information to search and knowing what to search for), and have explored how and when people stop searching for information online (Browne et al. 2007).

IS research has also investigated how knowledge seekers adopt knowledge through mediated channels (e.g., Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). Much of this work has drawn on dual process theory (Eagly and Chaiken 1984, Petty and Cacioppo 1986a) to understand knowledge seekers’ adoption choices. For example, Sussman and Siegal (2003) utilized the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) to explore factors that influenced whether accounting consultants adopted email advice they had solicited from known colleagues (see Petty and Cacioppo 1986b). Zhang and Watts (2008) employed the heuristic systematic model (Chaiken 1987, Eagly and Chaiken 1984) to explore how online community members evaluated a single message (the last message read) on a community forum. Similarly, Fadel et al. (2009) used the ELM to examine how undergraduate business students evaluated the perceived usefulness of a single problem solution obtained from a mock internal knowledge management system. These studies have begun to shed light on how various forms of technology-mediated knowledge is ultimately adopted. However, one important characteristic of this work is its focus on the adoption of a single piece of knowledge that was either solicited by a knowledge seeker from a known source (e.g., Sussman and Siegal 2003) or already identified by the researcher as the focal knowledge candidate (e.g., Fadel et al. 2009, Zhang and Watts 2008). Although certainly valuable for understanding the ultimate adoption choice, this approach assumes that any initial filtering of multiple knowledge candidates is either unnecessary or has already occurred. To our knowledge, the IS discipline has yet to thoroughly explore the process by which a knowledge seeker filters multiple, competing candidate solutions en route to a final adoption choice. As noted earlier, this filtering stage is particularly important in ENPs, where knowledge seekers are likely to encounter multiple candidate solutions from which they must choose. Therefore, as shown in Figure 2, the goal of this study is to address this research gap by exploring how knowledge seekers evaluate and filter multiple solutions found on an ENP forum before adopting one particular solution.

## Theoretical Development and Hypotheses

## Dual Process Filtering

Filtering is the process by which a set of possible alternatives is reduced prior to making a final choice. When the universe of possible alternatives is large, a knowledge seeker cannot realistically evaluate each one. Instead, the individual forms a consideration set, a subset of alternatives of which he or she is aware and is willing to evaluate further (Roberts and Lattin 1991). The filtering process proceeds as the consideration set is further reduced to a smaller choice set, or “the set of alternatives considered immediately prior to choice” (Shocker et al. 1991, p. 183). It is from this choice set that a final alternative is selected. For example, in the context of an ENP, a knowledge seeker may receive a number of possible solutions to a problem posted on a forum. Assuming the knowledge seeker is willing to consider each answer posted, these answers constitute his or her consideration set. The person must then evaluate the solutions in the consideration set to decide which, if any, will enter the choice set as candidates for final adoption.

Theory suggests that knowledge seekers have two competing objectives when reducing their consideration set to a choice set: (1) perform the reduction quickly and (2) optimize the decision outcome by retaining high-quality candidate solutions that are likely to ultimately be adopted (e.g., Chakravarti and Janiszewski 2003). These competing objectives present a classic trade-off between speed (i.e., quick reduction) and accuracy (i.e., high-quality reduction), which is a common feature in human information processing (Kahneman 2011, Payne et al. 1993, Stanovich and West 2000). To achieve speed, an individual is likely to rely on heuristic-based decision rules to quickly arrive at a reduced choice set (Payne et al. 1993). However, such a strategy may come at the expense of accuracy, which often requires more deliberate evaluation of the merits of each alternative (Kahneman 2011). This trade-off is evocative of the core tenet of dual process theories of information processing (Chaiken 1980; Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b), which suggests that information evaluation occurs through two processing routes: central and peripheral. Central route processing focuses on analyzing the quality of the knowledge content itself, and peripheral route processing focuses on peripheral cues accompanying the knowledge (e.g., the credibility of the source). Both the central and peripheral routes are involved in most knowledge evaluation; however, in a particular circumstance the more dominant route will depend on the likelihood of elaboration, or the degree to which the knowledge seeker systematically examines the content of the knowledge to determine its value. Theory suggests that when individuals are motivated and able to scrutinize knowledge content, the likelihood of elaboration is high and central route processing will be dominant (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b). Central processing requires time as well as cognitive and attentional resources, yet it is likely to produce high-quality assessments of knowledge content (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a). When individuals are not motivated or are unable to elaborate and engage in central processing, they tend to rely on peripheral processing by considering other cues (e.g., source credibility) aside from the knowledge content itself (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994). Because peripheral processing relies on simple heuristics (e.g., credible sources provide high-quality knowledge), it requires less time and fewer cognitive resources than central processing.

As noted earlier, prior IS research has employed dual process theory to explore how a single piece of knowledge is adopted; however, we believe that dual process theory also provides a useful framework for understanding the process of consideration set reduction, particularly as it relates to multiple solution candidates encountered in an ENP forum. Previous consideration set research demonstrates that multiple characteristics of a choice set candidate may be evaluated during the process of consideration set reduction (Roberts and Lattin 1991); however, current theory offers little guidance on how this process takes place. We hypothesize that knowledge seekers who encounter many knowledge candidates (as found in ENP forums) employ dual process filtering as they balance the competing goals of speed and accuracy in reducing their consideration sets.

## Hypotheses

To form our hypotheses, we first address the central route of knowledge evaluation. Prior research has shown that the perceived quality of computermediated knowledge (interpreted through central processing) influences the degree to which it is perceived as useful and, consequently, the likelihood that it will be adopted by the knowledge seeker (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). Because the goal of filtering is to identify high-quality solutions as candidates for final adoption, it stands to reason that central route processing should play a role in identifying high-quality solutions to be retained in the final choice set. For example, programmers evaluating the quality of potentially reusable code are more likely to retain the code under consideration if it is perceived as comprehensible, syntactically functional, relevant to the problem, flexible enough to meet varying requirements, and functionally large enough to justify reuse (Biggerstaff and Richter 1987). Intuitively, knowledge perceived to have higher-quality content according to criteria such as these should be more likely to be retained in the choice set than knowledge perceived to have lower-quality content. Stated formally,

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> An ENP forum solution of higher perceived content quality is more likely to be retained in the choice set than a solution of lower perceived content quality.

As argued above, dual process theory also predicts that peripheral cues play a role in knowledge evaluation. One of the most frequently studied peripheral cues in dual process research is source credibility, defined as the “perception of the credibility of a message source, reflecting nothing about the message itself” (Sussman and Siegal 2003, p. 51). Source credibility is generally conceptualized in terms of expertise and trustworthiness (Hovland et al. 1953, McCracken 1989). Because posts to an online forum are voluntary and are typically not rewarded with tangible remuneration, it is probable that contributors post submissions in a good-faith effort to solve a problem (Wasko and Faraj 2005). However, despite good intentions, the contributor may lack the experience or skill necessary to create a valid solution. Thus, in an ENP forum context, we expect source expertise to be the more operant dimension of source credibility. Logically, knowledge that originates from a source thought to possess high expertise is more likely to be retained in the choice set than knowledge from a source thought to possess dubious or unproven expertise. This position is corroborated by studies demonstrating that higher perceived source credibility leads to higher perceptions of computermediated knowledge usefulness and ultimately to adoption (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). Consequently, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> An ENP forum solution contributed by a source of perceived high expertise is more likely to be retained in the choice set than one contributed by a source of perceived low expertise.

In addition to content quality and source credibility, we also hypothesize effects of an additional peripheral cue that has received less attention in dual process research but is commonly found in online forums: validation. Validation indicates the usefulness or accuracy of a proposed solution and usually consists of an endorsement by a user or community of users (Durcikova and Gray 2009). In some ENPs (e.g., www.experts-exchange.com), validation takes the form of a single “accepted solution” to a problem posted by a forum user. In other cases, validation may be manifest by comments from a user who successfully implemented the solution. Validation can be an important peripheral cue in deciding whether or not to adopt knowledge (Poston and Speier 2005). Because validation offers evidence of knowledge utility, validated knowledge should be more frequently retained in the choice set than unvalidated knowledge. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> An ENP forum solution that is validated (i.e., has an indicator that the solution has worked for at least one other person) is more likely to be retained in the choice set than one that has not been validated.

The preceding hypotheses concern solution characteristics that are evaluated during central processing (evaluation of content quality) and peripheral processing (evaluation of source expertise and validation) and their influence on choice set retention. However, according to dual process theory, the relative influence of each processing route depends on whether the knowledge seeker elaborates on the knowledge candidates. As defined above, elaboration refers to whether an individual engages in systematic examination of the knowledge content (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a). Dual process theory predicts that elaboration likelihood depends on an individual possessing the ability to process the information as well as the motivation to do so (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b). When an individual has the ability and motivation to scrutinize knowledge content, elaboration likelihood is high. Under these conditions, the individual will evaluate the knowledge by relying primarily on the content of the knowledge itself (content quality) and less on peripheral cues (source expertise and validation) (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, Sussman and Siegal 2003). However, when ability or motivation is low, the individual is more likely to rely on peripheral cues rather than engaging in intensive scrutiny of the knowledge content. In other words, elaboration is expected to moderate the relative influence of such peripheral cues in determining whether a solution will remain under consideration. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> In the context of an ENP forum, elaboration positively moderates the influence of content quality on choice set retention (H4a), negatively moderates the influence of source expertise on choice set retention (H4b), and negatively moderates the influence of validation on choice set retention (H4c).

Our fifth and final hypothesis concerns the relative strength of content quality versus peripheral cues in knowledge evaluation in ENP forums. Prior studies have found that content quality is a stronger determinant of knowledge adoption than peripheral cues in computer-mediated contexts such as email (Sussman and Siegal 2003) and online communities (Zhang and Watts 2008). However, when users gather knowledge from an ENP forum where many solution candidates exist, there are several reasons why peripheral cues could be more influential than content quality during the filtering stage.

First, simplifying the choice process is a major concern to knowledge seekers as they form their choice sets (Chakravarti and Janiszewski 2003). Easily interpreted peripheral cues can help to quickly distinguish between knowledge candidates, thus simplifying the choice process. Second, research indicates that exposure to many options during knowledge evaluation can decrease a user’s ability and motivation to scrutinize content, thus leading to increased reliance on peripheral cues (Freeman and Spyridakis 2004). As noted by Browne et al. (2007), the presence of many candidate solutions may impair a person’s ability to engage in focused analysis and to consider all available information (Grisé and Gallupe 1999, Miranda and Saunders 2003, Schultze and Vandenbosch 1998), particularly in an online environment (Nadkarni and Gupta 2004, Rowley 2000). Finally, research in decision making has supported the notion that decisionmaking strategies are very sensitive to the number of available choices (Payne et al. 1993, Schwartz 2004). The knowledge seeker, constrained by bounded rationality, must effectively filter candidate solutions, and peripheral cues offer an accessible mechanism to help him or her more easily identify those that seem most promising. This prompts the final hypothesis:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> In an ENP forum where multiple potential solutions are available, peripheral cues (e.g., source credibility, validation) have a stronger influence on choice set retention than content quality.

## Stimulus Materials

We developed a specialized experimental instrument that simulated popular online ENP forums where software developers might go to seek solutions to systems development problems (e.g., www.expertsexchange.com, www.dreamincode.net, www.stackoverflow .com). The instrument presented eight candidate solutions to an array-sorting problem. The candidate solutions were presented simultaneously on a single screen. Each of the eight candidate solutions consisted of three components: (1) the solution code (see Appendix A in the online appendix, available as supplemental material at http://dx.doi.org/10.1287/ isre.2013.0502), (2) the source’s name and level of expertise, and (3) an indication of whether the solution had been validated by someone other than the contributor.

The array-sorting problem required the programmer to sort objects contained in an array. The selection of the array-sorting problem for this study was the result of several considerations. First, there had to be multiple ways to accomplish the task so that participants could review many plausible solutions. Second, the problem had to be understandable and relevant to potential participants. Finally, the problem had to be difficult enough that professional coders might seek solutions for the problem online.<sup>2</sup> The array-sorting problem satisfied all of these considerations.

To create high-quality solutions, sorting code samples were selected from actual online knowledge bases and were translated by one of the authors into consistent C# syntax. C# is similar to Java and C++, all of which have similar control flow and syntactic properties. Additionally, these languages are currently among the most popular software development languages (Tiobe\_Software 2011). Four high-quality and four low-quality solutions were developed for the array- sorting problem. High-quality solutions were logically correct and would produce the desired result if implemented. Low-quality solutions were logically incorrect, lacking essential portions of code, or seeded with steps that would not generate the correct result.

To ensure that the quality of the candidate solution content could be accurately perceived, the solutions were pilot tested by six professional software developers who had an average of 13.2 years of programming experience. The developers were asked to evaluate each solution on a seven-point scale with high quality and low quality as the endpoints. A paired-samples t-test revealed a significant difference (t455 = 40709, p = 00005) in the perceived quality of high (M = 3083, SD = 1027) and low (M = 1037, SD = 0052) quality solutions, indicating that the quality of the solutions could be accurately differentiated.<sup>3</sup>

## Procedure

After providing their consent, participants were seated in front of an eye tracker that was calibrated based on the position of each participant’s head. Participants were then instructed that they would be presented with two different problems and eight candidate solutions for solving each problem. The first problem asked participants to consider solutions for computing the mathematic factorial of a number. This problem was intended to acquaint them with the data collection instrument. The second problem was an array-sorting task in which participants were asked to provide a rank ordering of the candidate solutions, specifying the order in which they would try the solutions if faced with that specific problem. After participants completed the second problem, the eye tracker was turned off and participants answered questions in an online survey. Data from the second task was analyzed for this study.

## Measurement

Elaboration. The classical definition of elaboration is the amount of message-relevant thinking that an individual engages in while evaluating a message (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a). As noted earlier, elaboration focuses on the content of the message rather than its surrounding peripheral cues. Research in cognitive psychology has operationalized elaboration as the amount of effort exerted by an individual to evaluate a message (Cacioppo et al. 1983), as evidenced by such techniques as thought listing (Cacioppo and Petty 1981) or measuring the number of high-quality arguments a participant can recall from a message (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994). In management information systems (MIS) research, elaboration has been largely replaced by its antecedents (e.g., ability and motivation; Sussman and Siegal 2003) or captured as the number of sample trials a subject requested (Tam and Ho 2005).

In our study, we return to the definition of elaboration as distinguished from its antecedents. Elaboration was operationalized as the length of time the participant spent scrutinizing the content (code) of each candidate solution (apart from consulting peripheral cues like expertise and validation). Underlying this operationalization is the assumption that when a participant scrutinizes the code, he or she is engaging in message-relevant thinking. Although this operationalization is a proxy for true elaboration, it is consistent with approaches taken in other disciplines that use gaze patterns to infer elaboration from attention and to link elaboration to memory and recall (Calvo 2001, Christianson et al. 1991, Pieters et al. 2002). However, in contrast to past work in which gaze was captured across the entire stimulus message, we focused on the amount of time participants evaluated only certain components of each candidate solution (i.e., the code). The precise, real-time gaze data captured by the eye tracker allowed us to measure scrutiny of content quality in situ rather than relying on post-hoc reports or surrogate variables, the accuracy of which may attenuate because of recall limitations and unrecognized switching between central and peripheral processing (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994).

Eye tracking was performed using the Tobii T60 Eye Tracker, which is capable of capturing 60 coordinates per second. We captured gaze data for all participants using a two-layered approach that included (1) Tobii Studio, a proprietary software package created by the manufacturer of the eye tracker, and (2) a custom research instrument designed for this study that linked the gaze data generated by the eye tracker to a specific component of the candidate solution on the screen (e.g., high-quality code, a label indicating no validation, a score indicating a high level of expertise). The custom research instrument was necessary because of the random creation and ordering of the candidate solutions. The amount of data captured by the instrument was dependent on numerous factors, including the ability of the eye tracker to track the participants’ eyes and whether the participant was looking at components of the candidate solutions during the task. In all, the instrument captured over 1.3 million points of gaze data, averaging 35.2 data points per second. To reduce the number of data points, multiple consecutive gaze data points for a single solution component were consolidated into a duration measure that represented the length of time the participant looked at that component. Elaboration for a single candidate solution was calculated by summing the length of time a participant spent gazing at the content (code) of the candidate solution. Candidate Solutions in the Choice Set. Participants’ retention of a candidate solution in the choice set was operationalized using rankings. Each participant ranked each solution in the order that the participant would try the solution. The ranking task helped to ensure that participants initially included all solutions as part of the consideration set, thus ensuring that all solutions received some level of evaluation. Although each candidate solution received a rank, our primary interest was to understand how solutions received a high probability of being retained as part of the choice set (i.e., those that were highly ranked). Therefore we dichotomized the rankings into two groups: solutions ranked in the top three (topranked) and solutions ranked below the top three (bottom-ranked). Three solutions were selected as the threshold for top ranked solutions for two reasons. First, permitting three solutions to be top ranked accounts for the fact that multiple solutions may comprise the choice set prior to knowledge adoption. This issue is especially salient in the experiment because multiple candidate solutions actually solve the array-sorting problem. Second, a choice set of three solutions is consistent with past consumer behavior research that shows consumers’ tendency to seriously consider approximately three alternatives (e.g., Ranjbarian and Kia 2010).

## Model Specification

A repeated generalized linear model (GLM) that uses a quasilikelihood function to model predictor variables’ effects (McCullagh and Nelder 1999) was used to test the hypotheses for three reasons. First, GLMs permit the fitting of regression models for dependent variables that follow a bimodal distribution (Myers et al. 2010). Additionally, GLMs permit modeling of interactions between categorical and continuous explanatory variables. Finally, repeated GLMs can account for correlated measures taken from the same participant by modeling random effects from the correlated responses (Myers et al. 2010). Thus, a repeated GLM is ideally suited to our data.

Consistent with our hypotheses, the model for retention of candidate solutions in the choice set takes the following form:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Pr(Choice Set Retention)} \\ = \beta_ {0} + (\beta_ {1} * \text {Solution Length}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {2} * \text {Years of Experience}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {3} * \text {Elaboration}) + (\beta_ {4} * \text {Content Quality}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {5} * \text {Source Expertise}) + (\beta_ {6} * \text {Validation}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {7} * \text {Content Quality*Elaboration}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {8} * \text {Source Expertise*Elaboration}) \\ \quad + (\beta_ {9} * \text {Validation*Elaboration}) + \varepsilon . \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

This model accounts for any potential main effect from elaboration, the length of the solution (i.e., number of characters; see Appendix A in the online appendix), and years of programming experience. It also accounts for the hypothesized main effects of content quality, source expertise, and validation, as well as the expected interaction effects between elaboration and each of the factors for content quality, source expertise, and validation.

Creating a suitable model is traditionally an iterative process unless prescribed by theory (Hosmer and Lemeshow 2000). In our case, the factors included in our model are determined by theory. Therefore, we created several competing models for the evaluation of comparative model fit. This approach is consistent with the multistep process of evaluating fit advocated by Hosmer and Lemeshow (2000) and recently employed in MIS research (Zmud et al. 2010). The first model included only the control variables (model 1; Table 4); the second model included only the main effects (model 2; Table 4); the third model was our hypothesized model as described by Equation (1) (model 3; Table 4); and the last model was a fully specified model containing all possible combinations of the independent variables (model 4; Table 4). Fit was evaluated using the corrected quasilikelihood under independence model criterion (QICC; Pan 2001). Rather than relying on loglikelihood as does Akaike’s (1973) information criterion (AIC), the QICC uses quasilog likelihood to handle correlated response data. The QICC penalizes unnecessary model complexity and, similar to the AIC, a smaller value of the QICC indicates a better model fit. The model containing the main and interaction effects prescribed by theory (model 3) offered a better fit than any of the alternative models. The coefficient estimates for model 3 are shown in Table 5.

In this model, coefficients must be interpreted carefully because of the presence of interaction terms and because of the logit link function. A common approach to interpreting main and interaction effects in logit models is to examine conditional odds ratios (Afifi et al. 2004). Odds ratios are simple to calculate; however, they are frequently misinterpreted because they do not imply a constant change in the estimated probability of the target event. The change in probability that results from a change in a single variable depends on the values of the other variables in the model (Hoetker 2007). Further, the effect of a single variable (e.g., elaboration) may have opposing effects on probability depending on the other variables in the model (Hoetker 2007). This phenomenon is especially salient when interaction terms are present (Ai and Norton 2003). Therefore, following Hoetker’s recommendations, we focus on graphical representations to interpret marginal effects of significant main and interaction relationships (also see Kaufman 1996, Norton et al. 2004). The method of calculating marginal effects is presented in Appendix C in the online appendix.

Dual process theory suggests that as individuals form their choice sets, greater elaboration will increase the relative importance of content quality (H4a) and decrease the relative importance of peripheral cues such as source expertise (H4b) and validation (H4c). Our results provide mixed support for these hypotheses. First, as shown in Table 5, our analysis did not show a significant decrease in the importance of source expertise and validation with more elaboration; therefore, H4b and H4c are not supported. In contrast, greater elaboration did increase the importance of content quality (H4a), as evidenced by the significant coefficient for the content quality ∗ elaboration term (Table 5). However, because the nature of this interaction effect is difficult to ascertain from the sign and magnitude of the coefficient (Hoetker 2007), we present its graphical representation. Figure 7 shows how increased elaboration magnifies the effect of content quality on the probability of retaining a given solution in the choice set. Specifically, as more time is spent elaborating solutions of high quality (conditions HHA, HHN, HLA, and HLN), the predicted probability of retention increases, whereas greater elaboration of low-quality solutions (conditions LHA, LHN, LLA, and LLN) decreases their likelihood of retention.

Finally, H5 predicted that as individuals evaluate multiple competing solutions in an ENP forum, the influence of peripheral cues on choice set retention would be stronger than content quality. To compare the magnitude of these effects, we calculated the predicted change in retention probability that would result from adding high content quality, high source expertise, or validation to a solution that was previously lacking these qualities. However, in logit models the predicted probability varies nonlinearly with changes in the independent variables, and the effect of a single independent variable depends on the values of the other independent variables. Therefore, following Kaufman’s (1996) recommendations, we examined the change in predicted probability from each independent variable across a range of reference probabilities (from 0.05 to 0.95, in increments of 0.05). Results showed that the effect of source expertise on choice set retention was between 1.94 and 2.12 times larger than that of content quality, and the effect of validation was between 2.43 and 2.87 times larger than that of content quality (see Appendix D in the online appendix for plots of predicted changes in probabilities from content quality, expertise, and validation).

If a knowledge seeker is unmotivated, he or she will not expend cognitive effort to evaluate the content of a solution, but will depend more on peripheral cues, such as contributor expertise and validation. ## Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore how knowledge found on an ENP forum is filtered by knowledge

seekers. Drawing from dual process theory (Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b) and literature on consideration sets (Chakravarti and Janiszewski 2003, Roberts and Lattin 1991, Shocker et al. 1991), we hypothesized that retention of a forum solution in a knowledge seeker’s choice set would be influenced by the content quality and peripheral cues indicating the expertise of the source and external validation. We also expected that elaboration would strengthen the effect of content quality on adoption and weaken the effect of source expertise and validation on adoption. ## Theoretical Contributions

The first theoretical implication of our results is that dual processing occurs earlier in the knowledge seeking and adoption process than has previously been shown. Previous research (e.g., Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008) has applied dual process theory to the adoption stage in which knowledge seekers choose whether or not to apply a single piece of knowledge. Our findings suggest that dual processing (evaluation through central and peripheral routes) also occurs during filtering as knowledge seekers evaluate multiple candidate solutions to form a choice set. However, the relative strength of the two routes employed during filtering differs from that observed during adoption. Both Sussman and Siegal (2003) and Zhang and Watts (2008) found that argument quality was the single strongest predictor of perceived knowledge usefulness and/or knowledge adoption.

![](/api/attachments/W73V7PT4/fulltext/images/bb6f6eeec05fd0677be3c3f15595549a624ce8b88dbeffb02ef2686c20d25b73.jpg)

In contrast, even when controlling for solution length, our analysis failed to support the hypothesis that higher content quality of a solution found in an ENP forum has any direct influence on whether or not the solution is retained in the choice set (H1). During the filtering stage, both high-quality and low-quality candidate solutions were filtered out of the choice set. Although these results are not significant, the lack of support for H1 may imply that the objective quality of an argument matters less during filtering than it does during adoption, especially when the knowledge seeker must process several competing candidate solutions. In light of past research (e.g., Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008), this suggests that future theory building should carefully explore conditions under which content quality influences filtering decisions.

Although content quality alone does not exert a significant influence on retention in the choice set, elaboration does positively moderate the relationship between content quality and retention in the choice set (H4a). This result is consistent with the findings of other dual process research in the knowledge adoption stage (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). However, as noted earlier, none of these studies operationalized elaboration directly, but instead relied on antecedents of ability and motivation as proxies for this construct. Our results contribute a theoretically refined perspective of the knowledge-seeking literature by showing that, as measured herein, elaboration itself makes content quality more influential in the filtering process. Because the quality of a solution cannot be determined without at least some scrutiny of its content, this result seems obvious. However, it also highlights a subtle assumption made here and in prior research that high-quality knowledge alone will promote its likelihood of adoption (Petty and Cacioppo 1986b, Sussman and Siegal 2003). The combined results of H1 and H4a indicate that objective content quality is neither self-evident nor sufficient for a candidate solution to be retained in the choice set. Only when individuals expend the effort to scrutinize the solution does its quality become apparent.

With regard to peripheral cues, the results of our study offer at least four major theoretical implications. First, the significant results observed for H2 and H3 demonstrate that knowledge seekers rely heavily on peripheral cues such as source expertise and validation when forming their choice set. Moreover, as predicted by H5, these cues increase the probability of a solution being retained in the choice set more than the objective quality of the content. Although this might be expected from knowledge seekers who are novices or who lack experience in the topic they are investigating, our results show that peripheral cues also factor prominently in the filtering decisions of professionals with significant experience and expertise. For research, this highlights a theoretical nuance that may differentiate ENP forums from other modes of technology-mediated knowledge transfer such as email. Namely, our results suggest that the importance of peripheral cues that accompany competing candidate solutions in an ENP should not be discounted, even under conditions in which knowledge seekers might be capable of and interested in analyzing the solution content itself.

Second, dual process theory suggests that greater elaboration should decrease the relative importance of accompanying peripheral cues in determining the knowledge adoption choice (Eagly and Chaiken 1993; Petty and Cacioppo 1986a, b). Extant studies in the IS domain support this hypothesis, finding that greater expertise and/or involvement (surrogates for elaboration) diminished the influence of source credibility (Sussman and Siegal 2003) or validation (Fadel et al. 2009) on the adoption choice. However, we failed to find evidence that the impact of source expertise and validation during filtering was attenuated with greater elaboration (H4b and H4c). Though not conclusive, this seems to imply that when filtering multiple solutions in ENP forums, peripheral cues play a critical role in knowledge evaluation regardless of the degree to which the knowledge seeker elaborates on the content of the knowledge itself. To illustrate the durability of peripheral cues, consider the LHA solution series (low-quality content, high level of source credibility, accepted solution) shown in Figure ??. At low levels of elaboration, the probability of retaining this solution in the choice set is quite high (near 0.75), presumably due to the influence of peripheral cues. With increased elaboration, the probability of retention drops as the low quality of the solution content becomes apparent. However, nearly 60 seconds of elaboration are required before the predicted probability drops below that of the next highest candidate solution (HLA). If participants had spent 60 seconds evaluating each solution, we might have observed more balance between the central and peripheral route. However, 60 seconds is nearly three times more than participants’ mean elaboration time (20.16 sec.) and greater than 89.6% of all observed elaboration times. Thus, it appears from the data that once peripheral cues characterize online knowledge as valuable (or not valuable), that characterization is difficult to alter. Although this finding contrasts with prior dual process work, it corroborates other studies that suggest an enhanced role for peripheral cues in an online setting. For instance, Tam and Ho (2005) found that in an e-commerce context, sorting on an e-commerce webpage (a peripheral cue) directly impacted a product adoption choice across all stages of information processing. This prompted them to suggest that “traditional persuasion theories, including the ELM, have focused on attitude changes brought about by elaborating the merits of arguments embodied in a persuasive message. Their focus on message argument quality, although sufficient for contexts studied before, may fail to offer a complete account of the mechanism underlying persuasion tasks that involve choice outcome” (p. 286). Our results seem to support this conclusion by showing that peripheral cues exert a notable and persistent effect on evaluation of ENP forum knowledge even when elaboration is undertaken.

It is important to reiterate that the pattern of results we observed with respect to elaboration may be related to the means by which elaboration is measured, an important dimension of this study’s methodological contribution. Prior empirical testing of dual processing models has frequently been simplified because of the difficulty of capturing elaboration during an experimental task, resulting in antecedents of elaboration (e.g., the experiment participant’s ability and motivation) substituting for elaboration itself. The methodological improvement offered by using the eye tracker to capture elaboration during the task allows us to separate the measurement of elaboration from its antecedents, thereby offering a clearer description of how elaboration influences (or fails to influence) central and peripheral processing routes in an online setting.

Third, prior IS dual process research has emphasized source credibility as an important peripheral cue (Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008), but not all studies have found it to be a significant predictor of knowledge adoption (Fadel et al. 2009). Our results lend support to the notion that during the filtering stage, the perceived expertise of the knowledge source does play a role in choice set formation. However, our study also showed that validation seemed to exert a stronger effect on ultimate retention in the choice set than did source expertise, as suggested by the relative magnitude of the coefficients in Table 5 and the higher marginal effects for validation shown in Figure 6. This implies that validation should not be overlooked in ongoing theoretical development surrounding various forms of technology-mediated knowledge evaluation and adoption. This finding also speaks to a question raised by Eagly and Chaiken (1993) in their review of how peripheral cues impact persuasion: “Are all peripheral mechanisms equally powerful determinants of persuasion when elaboration likelihood is low, and equally ineffective in conferring attitudinal persistence?” (p. 323). Although the question of attitudinal persistence is unanswered by our data, it is clear that the peripheral mechanisms examined in this study have unequal effects that, as discussed above, are not limited to low elaboration likelihood. In the context of ENP forums, our findings suggest that peripheral cues that reflect on the knowledge contribution itself (e.g., validation) may be more potent than cues that reflect on the contributor of the knowledge (i.e., source expertise).

Finally, most prior IS dual process research has limited its examination to a single peripheral cue— source credibility (e.g., Sussman and Siegal 2003, Zhang and Watts 2008). By incorporating the additional cue of validation, our study offers additional insight into how cues might operate in conjunction with each other. Examining the effects of peripheral cues at the median and first and third elaboration quartiles (Table 6) reveals that peripheral cues wield the greatest influence on choice set retention when they are congruent in their indication. To illustrate, at the median level of elaboration for the LLN condition (low-quality content, low level of source credibility, not validated), the marginal effect of adding expertise is a 0.09 increase in the probability of retention, and the marginal effect of adding validation is a 0.13 increase in the probability of retention. However, when validation is present (condition LLA), the marginal effect of adding expertise is 0.30. Similarly, when expertise is present (condition LHN), the marginal effect of validation is 0.36. Thus, the additive impact of having congruent peripheral cues that both indicate high content quality exceeds the summed incremental impact of adding each peripheral cue individually. The notion of additivity, in which message components reinforce each other, has been hypothesized and shown before (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994); however, the phenomenon has traditionally been explored with peripheral cues reinforcing message quality. Our results indicate that additivity also occurs among peripheral cues. In other words, when peripheral cues are congruent (e.g., high source expertise and accepted solution), the effect is much greater than the sum of the individual effects of source expertise and validation.

## Implications for Practice

For practitioners, this study reveals characteristics of knowledge filtering in ENPs that have practical implications for the design and support of online ENP forums. The most significant implication is that peripheral cues are highly salient to people who must filter solutions on a forum—perhaps even more salient than the quality of the solution itself. Surprisingly, this observation appears to hold even for skilled professionals who presumably possess the capability and motivation to analyze the solution content. Although content quality did become operant with elaboration, the peripheral cues had an immediate and persistent impact on developers’ evaluations of online knowledge. At least two implications can be drawn from these observations. First, practitioners should not assume that objective quality of online solutions alone will influence filtering decisions, even when the target audience is relatively sophisticated. Although quality does factor heavily in the choice to adopt, it is much less prominent in the preceding stage of filtering, which governs what is considered for adoption. This recommendation is likely to be particularly salient for online knowledge bases designed to provide a “quick fix” for knowledge seekers who lack the time or inclination to elaborate extensively on the possible solutions. Second, because peripheral cues play an important role in the filtering process, designers and supporters of ENP-associated online forums would be well advised to implement these cues as a valuable guide to knowledge seekers.

A related implication for practice concerns the degree of agreement between central and peripheral cues in ENP forums. If knowledge seekers rely heavily on peripheral cues as our data suggest, they risk being misled by potentially faulty peripheral cues (e.g., those that erroneously endorse low-quality solutions or undermine high-quality solutions). However, our results (specifically the positive interaction between elaboration and content quality in H4a) indicate that effort invested in elaborating the solution content can promote retention of objectively higherquality solutions. As one would expect, this suggests that knowledge seekers would likely achieve better decision outcomes by engaging in increased scrutiny of proposed solutions, especially in situations in which peripheral cues may not have been audited or verified. The incremental time required to further scrutinize a set of solutions will likely be significantly less than the amount of time spent implementing and fixing a single low-quality solution.

Beyond the negative consequences to the individual who adopts faulty knowledge, the possibility of choosing faulty solutions also poses a risk to forum owners and stakeholders because a knowledge seeker who is misled once by inaccurate peripheral cues may be less likely to return to that same knowledge base for other solutions. Although knowledge seekers’ tolerance for faulty or misleading peripheral cues is currently unknown, our results suggest that forum owners should ensure that the peripheral cues accurately reflect what they are intended to capture. This could be accomplished by auditing validation and source expertise to increase the accuracy of these cues.

Finally, our results identify validation as a peripheral cue that factors prominently in knowledge evaluation. Validation was shown to be the single most influential feature of candidate solutions, outweighing both objective content quality and source expertise. Validation is unique because it typically relies on the experience of others (often other knowledge seekers) who have tested a given solution. Thus, forum owners who wish to provide validation to knowledge seekers must entice past knowledge seekers to return to the knowledge base and report on the success or failure of their attempts to implement recommended solutions. Although mechanisms for accomplishing this are not yet well understood, possibilities may include fostering attitudes of altruistic validation efforts, reducing barriers to providing feedback, or providing incentives for validation.

## Limitations and Future Research

As with all research, this study has limitations that should be considered. First, because elaboration is a phenomenon that occurs within individuals’ minds, it is difficult to devise a measure that captures it entirely. Although we believe that our use of an eye-tracking device is an improvement over past methods (e.g., measuring time spent performing a task, self-reports of effort or recall), we cannot definitively discern an individual’s thoughts at a given point in time. For example, there may be cases when a participant is considering the contents of a solution but is looking away from that solution (e.g., off screen). However, even techniques that capture brain activity (e.g., fMRI, EEG) cannot definitively identify the object of a participant’s thoughts. Gaze patterns primarily reflect attention, which is a necessary precursor of elaboration (Greenwald and Leavitt 1984, Tam and Ho 2005). Therefore, we believe that the eye tracker provides, at minimum, a useful lower bound on the amount of elaboration occurring during solution evaluation.

Second, some of our results may be influenced by the contextual/experimental artifacts employed in the study. For example, the lack of support for H1 could be attributed to the relative complexity of the arraysorting solutions presented to participants. Although our pilot study participants were able to differentiate between high- and low-quality code, the mean quality rating of both types of solutions was below the midpoint of the scale. In other contexts where the content is more easily assessed or there are fewer candidate solutions to evaluate, differences in content quality may be more pronounced and its effect on choice set retention may be significant. The peripheral cues used in our experiment were explicit and therefore easy to interpret. Future work should investigate situations in which peripheral cues are more ambiguous or difficult to infer. Additionally, participants may have formed differing assumptions about the reliability of peripheral cues. Future research should investigate perceptions of cue reliability, including such factors as the contributors’ trustworthiness. Moreover, patterns exhibited when evaluating knowledge for a programming task may be distinct from those that would be observed in other domains in which the stakes might be higher or more dramatic (e.g., evaluating alternative surgical procedures or long-term investment strategies). Future research should explore how variations in context and knowledge complexity might alter the pattern of results observed here.

Third, as with any experimental research, our results could be subject to external validity threats. Specifically, we recognize the possibility that programmers’ motivation and experience as measured in a field experiment may not fully represent these variables in an actual ENP knowledge-seeking scenario. However, we believe that the efforts made to rule out low motivation and to control for experience as alternative explanations offer a reasonable assurance of the robustness of our findings.

Finally, this work does not speak to the sequential process by which candidate solutions are evaluated (e.g., temporal scanning of solution components). For example, individuals who alternate their attention between peripheral cues and knowledge content may emerge with different choice set retention patterns than those who focus on one after the other. With the methodological improvement provided by the eye tracker, it should be possible to more clearly observe the process that knowledge seekers follow in evaluating candidate solutions—a process that, when fully mapped, may reveal additional insights into how ENP knowledge bases can best be designed to support knowledge exchange.

## Conclusion

Knowledge seekers increasingly turn to electronic networks of practice for solutions to their problems and inquiries, yet relatively little is understood about how knowledge encountered on an ENP online forum is filtered and evaluated prior to the final adoption choice. This study shows that although many tenets of dual processing theory hold in the context ENP forum filtering, others do not. Specifically, our results indicate that peripheral cues have a stronger influence on filtering decisions than does the objective quality of the knowledge content, which becomes operant only when substantial elaboration takes place. This study contributes to the information systems literature by empirically assessing the relative impact of commonly used cues in online forums. We believe that the rich context of the study, including the use of an eye tracker to measure elaboration, provides enhanced theoretical insight into how knowledge is evaluated in an electronic network of practice.
