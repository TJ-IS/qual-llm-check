## Abstract

The 3DayCar programme reveals the principle source of delay during the order fulfilment process in the automotive industry as information processing not manufacturing. This suggests that information and communication technologies (ICT) are crucial in re-shaping industry structure towards customer responsiveness and building to order. This paper reports on the findings of a longitudinal study during a critical period for the industry where firms adopting web-enabled ICT seek to increase inter-organizational collaboration and gain competitive advantage over other supply chains. In all, 75 interviews across 28 firms examine the adoption of business-to-business (B2B) e-procurement and supply systems by vehicle manufacturers (VMs) and suppliers. Forms of organization are represented as a map of industry structure showing supply relationships and firm affiliation to B2B applications. VMs have tended to adopt a short-term price-driven approach using e-hubs and webEDI (electronic data interchange) in their attempts to control cost. Suppliers are concerned over an apparent disregard for firm autonomy, particularly those who have launched ICT projects of their own. Recommendations for the future are considered in light of what such large-scale efforts imply for design, deployment, and uses of ICT in industry settings.

## Introduction

he longevity of the automotive sector has twice led the world to a new production paradigm, from craft to mass production in the 1920s, and then lean production in the 1970s and 1980s (Fine, 1997). Although heralded in the past as a world-leader in management theory and new technology (Drucker, 1946; Chandler, 1977; Womack et al., 1990), the current era of vehicle manufacture (VM) finds an industry in crisis. Automaker giants are beset by 40–80 days of unsold inventory, share value reduced to junk bond status, and one-fifth of European customers driving home cars that are not what they intended to buy (Lambe, 2003; Holweg and Pil, 2004). Investigation by the 3DayCar programme reveals the principal source of unresponsiveness during customer order fulfilment as information systems – order entry, processing, and scheduling – and only relatively minor delays attributable to actual vehicle production (3DayCar, 2002). ‘3DayCar’ is a unique research programme that not only examines the process of customer order fulfilment but also explores a total system view of the forces governing the adoption of technology. It is argued here that information and communication technologies (ICT) play an emergent role in reshaping the automotive manufacturing sector towards the goal of a 3DayCar: that is, only building new vehicles to customer order. Few other studies capture either a whole industry perspective or the complexity of structural change over time.

This paper reports on the findings at the mid-phase of a 9-year longitudinal study into the role and impact of ICT during a critical period for the automotive industry. The car business is ripe for revolution: poorly equipped to thrive in the 21st century and with chronic debt and overcapacity problems, where ‘yabout half of the industry is regularly incapable of earning a decent return on its invested capital’ (Economist, 2004a: 12). Yet, several false summits have already been encountered as industry partners have attempted to speed data exchange by adopting ICT within and between their organizations. First, the arrival of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) in the 1990s has not proved as successful in manufacturing as first anticipated, with around half of customers across all sectors believing they paid more than the system was worth (Vance, 2003).

Second, the promise of the e-commerce revolution – driven by visions of the virtual organization and a digital economy (Lapidus, 2000; Tapscott et al., 2000) – is taking longer to materialize than expected. Despite growing optimism after the 2000 dotcom crash of an e-commerce revival in sectors such as retail, groceries, and music (Rabinovich et al., 2003; Economist, 2004b), the adoption of business-to-business (B2B) systems by firms in less information-intensive manufacturing industries face considerable barriers from institutional hierarchy and other forms of resistance (Bakos, 1998; Hagel, 2002; Howard et $a l . ,$ 2003). The aim of this paper is to explore the role of ICT in reshaping automotive industry structures. The phenomenon under investigation is the adoption of ICT and the unit of analysis is the industry. The research poses three questions: Why are ICT adopted in the automotive industry? What is the role of ICT within and between supply partners implementing build-to-order? How are ICT reshaping industry structures?

The paper is presented as follows. The next section reviews the adoption and role of ICT in industry, examining the impact on structure by discussing inter-organizational collaboration and competitive advantage. The subsequent sections provide a brief overview of the 3DayCar programme, presents the research strategy and design, the findings in their consecutive phases, and also discusses the findings in relation to theory and practice. The last section concludes with pointers to focus future work and insights towards the design, deployment, and uses of ICT in this and other industry settings.

## The adoption and role of ICT

The first section begins by examining the origins of ICT across multiple sectors and concludes with the adoption of ICT in the automotive industry. The second section reviews the impact of ICT on industry structure from the perspectives of inter-organizational collaboration and competition.

## Origins of ICT

The study of structure as market and hierarchical forms of organization underpins considerable 20th century research (Chandler, 1962; Arrow, 1974; Williamson, 1985). This includes the diffusion of electronic networks in the transition from institutional hierarchies towards electronic markets (Malone et al., 1987). The origins of ICT and electronic business are closely connected in information systems literature as enablers to competitive advantage and inter-organizational collaboration (Porter and Millar, 1985; Kumar and van Dissel, 1996). Historically ICT-enabled competition and collaboration are viewed as opposite ends of a single continuum, evolving from the growing field of information systems and the study of computer science in the 1950s. ICT or ‘information communication technologies’ provides a collective term for computer-based information systems that support the interdisciplinary coordination of groups, markets or hierarchies (Ciborra, 1993). While ICT is often used interchangeably with IT or information technology, it reflects the more recent growth in computer applications that support information exchange at all levels within and between firms. For instance,

McKinsey & Co. (2002) present a common perspective of ICT that corresponds with four core operations across the organization (Figure 1). This includes both terrestrial and web-enabled ICT applications that connect to the enterprise by means of an ERP ‘backbone’ with external electronic linkages to sales outlets and suppliers.

The role of ICT is well documented across product and service sector operations where the benefits of sharing information are attributed to exploiting opportunities in global markets, reducing inventory, and transaction costs, and improving cash flow (Lee and Whang, 1998; Li and Williams, 1999; Jayaram et al., 2000). Buhalis (2004) argues that airlines are early adopters of ICT in comparison to travel and tourism, where the industry is now using the Internet to improve its distribution strategy and reduce costs. The airline industry has adopted intranets to develop tactical and strategic management, as well as extranets for communicating with partners and to support B2B relationships. Sigala (2003: 1224) studying the hotel sector finds that productivity gains do not accrue from ICT investments alone, but from ‘the full exploitation of ICT networking and informational capabilities’. Other studies of ICT focus on the determinants of technology adoption by the firm (Lucchetti and Sterlacchini, 2004; Hollenstein, 2004) and the role of ICT in structural change and regional economics (Laursen, 2004). Hollenstein argues that to explain the timing and intensity of organizational ICT adoption, the anticipated benefits and costs must be understood beforehand. IT has grown over the decades because successive innovations have reduced the costs of coordination in terms of processing data and communicating information (Malone et al., 1987). However, while it is now 40 years since the advent of modern IT, few organizations do it well and often struggle to turn ‘a costly mess into a powerful weapon’ (Feld and Stoddard, 2004: 72).

The introduction of ICT in the automotive industry begins with the emergence of electronic data interchange (EDI) in the 1960s and 1970s, derived from road and rail transport businesses who realized the benefit of document standardization and replacing paper-based methods with electronic communication (Threlkel and Kavan, 1999). EDI represents the earliest example of an inter-organizational system in the industry, reflecting the pattern of buyer– supplier relationships supported by a bespoke transaction processing system that operates across organizational boundaries (Swatman and Swatman, 1992). Figure 2 presents the origins of automotive ICT in the context of technology ‘eras’, for example, data processing (DP) and management information systems (MIS). Earl (1989) argues that the DP era included many disappointments over failed computer systems, concerns with the cost of computing, outdated concepts of technology, anxieties about human impacts, and very patchy management involvement, commitment, and experience of computing across organizations. The automotive industry is little different in this respect to other vertically integrated manufacturing sectors at the time. New information technologies in the 1970s such as material requirements planning and computer-aided design were often adopted as ‘stand-alone’ systems in the firm, supporting only localized functional requirements and reinforcing hierarchical structures based on principles of organizational planning that originated from the US military (Wernle, 2003). This was a common feature of contemporary vehicle manufacturing execution systems at the time, involving a piecemeal, ‘islands of technology’ approach to shopfloor operations that resulted in a disjointed information system between plant workstations and production control.

The adoption and use of inter-organizational systems – including the role of IT – is ranked as one of the most popular topics for research by the IS community (Galliers, 2003). Yet, despite exemplary studies by Markus (1983), little longitudinal research exists that describes both adoption; the initial phase of introduction into the firm, and use: the long-term outcome resulting in either system acceptance or rejection. These distinctions are derived from Hart and Saunders (1997) whose study of EDI in interorganizational theory raises over 20 propositions concerning issues related to power and trust. The completion of such long-term studies represents a practical problem to researchers where large-scale global IT projects can require 5 years or more to integrate multiple stakeholders, often across several world regions. The automotive industry is a particular offender in this regard with examples of ERP projects taking over 10 years before becoming operational, for instance: General Motors’s MGO project (Material for Global Optimization). While the advances in ICT has increased opportunities for information sharing and shifted the nature of electronic activity from basic ordering and invoicing towards shared production scheduling and collaborative computer-aided design (Lee and Whang, 1998; Li and Williams, 1999), the problems of adoption persist. A further example is Collaborative Planning and Forecasting – ‘CPFR’ – which emerged in 1996 as both a retail and manufacturing information sharing concept to increase supply chain efficiency, but is now strongly criticized for ‘slow progress’ because of firm collaboration and IT legacy issues (Computerworld, 2002).

Growing recognition of the problems over communication, information exchange, and computer integration in the 1980s led to initiatives across the world by the Automotive Industry Action Group in the US, the Ministry of International Trade and Industry in Japan, and the Odette Committee in Europe. Independent and statesponsored bodies encouraged the use of digital data and emphasized the importance of standardizing methods of data exchange and clarification over the critical issues for electronic business (Kuroiwa, 1999; Lauer, 2000). The Computer-Aided Logistic Support (CALS) programme by the US Department of Defence was one of the earliest initiatives attempting to overcome the serious problems in product development, procurement, and supply of burgeoning inventory of hundreds of millions of hard copy engineering drawings, outdated technical manuals, and high annual investment in technical information (Ross, 1994). The Toyota Motor Corporation was similarly motivated and developed their own ‘open business environment’ based on the principles of information sharing using common ICT tools to exploit collaborative activity with multiple partners and suppliers (Kuroiwa,

1999). As the benefits from a standardized approach to EDI diffusion were acknowledged in terms of the impact on supply chain performance (Mackay and Rosier, 1996), a new debate emerged over the transformative effects on industry structure by ICT-enabled cooperation and competition using Internet-based information systems (Threlkel and Kavan, 1999; Helper and MacDuffie, 2004).

## ICT as competitive weapon or collaborative platform?

The role of ICT across industry supply chains has traditionally focused on the fields of economics, co-ordination, and competition (Porter and Millar, 1985; Konsynski and McFarlan, 1990; Clemons and Row, 1992). Economic benefits are related to the reduction in transmission charges and administrative costs, such as \$200 million savings by the Chrysler Corporation (Mukhopadhyay et al., 1995). Co-ordination benefits arise from the tight coupling and speed of exchanging information leading to efficiencies between firms such as reduced inventory (Clemons and Row, 1992). Competitive advantage stems from gaining access through ICT to diversified markets and leveraging key resources in new areas using economies of scale and scope (Porter and Millar, 1985). One of the best-known examples is American Airline’s Sabre system that provided flight queries and reservations for American and other subscriber airlines, where the system displayed its owner’s flights preferentially. Following anti-trust suits from other airlines petitioning against American Airlines newfound competitive advantage, greater access to reservation data was granted in return for the operators of the system being able to charge other carriers for transaction costs. When used in this manner, IT not only changes the way a firm competes but also enables technology to be used as a ‘competitive weapon’ against other firms (Ives and Learmonth, 1984: 1193). Webster (1995) provides a classic description of this phenomenon in the automotive industry. The case reveals that the high costs of investing in bespoke EDI is used as a competitive weapon to preserve the power of dominant stakeholders, that is, the VM, thereby locking suppliers into procurement relationships and keeping competitors out of them.

An alternative perspective is the role of ICT as an enabler of cooperation that provides a stable platform for interorganizational collaboration. Ciborra (1993) proposes reforms of the relationships with suppliers, customers, and other partners based on a mix of organizational forms: teams, markets, and hierarchies where technology can be harnessed to shape businesses and increase market transparency. Supply chain relationships have advanced from competitive to collaborative (Bowersox and Closs, 1996). This is reflected by a shift in inter-organizational systems literature in the 1990s that describes ‘the role of information technology in enabling the transition from inter-firm competition to cooperation’ (Kumar and van Dissel, 1996: 279). Terms such as collaboration and cooperation combine the concepts of multiple stakeholders, shared benefits, and the creation of value between firms (Kanter, 1994; Lee and Whang, 1998; Bowersox et al., 2000). Kumar and van Dissel describe these inter-organizational systems as information and communication technologybased systems that transcend legal enterprise boundaries and imply a level of cooperation and coordination well beyond traditional arms-length relationships. Their classification of system types identifies the arguments for potential conflict and hence possible strategies for containing conflict in these systems. study electronic relationships between organizations, particularly the role of power and trust in EDI adoption in order to understand how electronic media supports strategic alliances that firms create to advance mutual goals. They argue because computer networks are increasingly adopted to support the flow of information between and within organizations ‘their use both influences and has consequences for inter-organizational relationships’.

Collaborative supply chain relationships are extensively researched where much of the generic buyer–supplier relationship literature originates from the automotive industry (Sako, 1992; Lamming, 1993; Hines, 1994). The shift from adversarial price-driven relationships towards collaborative partnership-driven relationships is widely discussed where a common assumption is the trend towards closer collaborative relationships has a positive impact on firm performance (Croom, 2001). Yet, the arguments for inter-organizational collaboration and gaining competitive advantage over one’s rivals cannot be considered in isolation from one another. Success in today’s business world often requires that firms pursue both competitive and collaborative strategies simultaneously (Lado et al., 1997). A concept to emerge in organizational research that reflects this is ‘coopetition’ where the effectiveness of coordination mechanisms on knowledge sharing in intra-organizational networks consists both of collaborative and competitive ties (Tsai, 2002). ICT is used in inter-firm coopetition to share knowledge between groups that under certain circumstances can also be considered a source of competitive advantage (Levy et al., 2001). The question over what constitutes a collaborative relationship has important ramifications for industry structure and the role of ICT. As the spread of Internet-enabled networks continues, complex issues emerge over how buyers and suppliers manage relationships in less information-intensive manufacturing industries, where powerful stakeholders attempt a transition from electronic hierarchies towards electronic markets. Figure 3 illustrates a common view held by automotive analysts in 2000 – a period of considerable speculative investment in Internet technology – over the impact of webenabled ICT on industry structure.

Rubin and Fogarty’s illustration of web-enabled industrial organization represents the level of understanding of ICT such as Internet trade exchanges or ‘e-hubs’ before the e-commerce downturn in April 2000. They argue that VMs, tier 1 and 2 suppliers shift from a hierarchical top-down structure (Figure 3a), towards a central hub structure providing both an online marketplace and one-stop communication point for commercial trading and new product collaboration (Figure 3b). Yet, this represents an over-simplification of the impact of ICT on industry structure that ignores the conflict of interest between procurement systems focusing on price and those fostering supply chain collaboration. Industry leaders at the time such as Jac Nasser (Ford CEO, 1999–2001) overestimated the transformative capabilities of web-enabled ICT and ignored the potentially damaging impact introducing such technology would have on supply chain relations. Component suppliers feared the introduction of consortium ehubs such as ‘Covisint’ whose electronic auctions reversed long-term partnerships overnight due to VMs using ICT instead of conventional face-to-face methods to negotiate ‘the lowest price from suppliers and solicit electronic requests for quotes, parts, and services’ (Kisiel, 2002: 3).

Williams et al. (2002) offer an alternative perspective to competition vs collaboration where, with the advent of the Internet, the impact of the electronic supply chain on strategic partnerships and alliances enables an increase in flexibility to adjust to market preferences. They argue that ICT provide the capability to decouple from unproductive alliances and reconnect to more suitable partners. The relationships in this flexible type of organizational structure are based on productivity, not the establishment of long-term partnerships where sustaining relationships means they establish a significance or ‘entity’ of their own (Cousins, 2002). This represents a departure from the idea of fostering supply chain relationships in order to increase efficiency, streamline processes, and enhance customer service through long-term collaboration. Williams et al. speculate that new types of leadership will be required in future organizational structures, where the management of electronic supply chains requires an engagement in both short-term arms length and long-term collaborative relationships, and where planning is flexible and dependent on the needs of the focal organization. This supports the coopetition argument and suggests the role of ICT as either competitive weapon or collaborative platform requires updating, assisted by the increasingly ubiquitous nature of generic technology. In a similar vein, Helper and MacDuffie (2004: 1) argue that the debate about whether New Economy firms powered by new business models will displace Old Economy firms is now largely closed: ‘Instead, attention can be focused on a broader enquiry into the ways the capabilities of the Internet can alter the modes of exchange among firms.

This study demonstrates that the impact of ICT on automotive supply chain structure is more complex than that suggested by literature (Figure 3). Hence, understanding their role in creating benefit (or disbenefit) is more elusive. 3DayCar research is driven by fundamental questions over why ICT are adopted by industry, their role in terms of inter-organizational collaboration during implementation of build-to-order, and the impact on automotive industry structure. This presents a considerable challenge in terms of methods, not just from the scale of an industry study, but also coping with the issue of multiple levels (intra, inter, meta) and complex sociotechnical environments. A brief overview of 3DayCar is presented next.

## The 3DayCar programme

The 3DayCar programme was launched in 1999 by the University of Bath, Cardiff University, and the International Car Distribution Programme to investigate the feasibility of building new vehicles to customer order in the United Kingdom (UK). Six research streams: Marketing, Finance, System, Organization, Environment, and Technology worked with 25 industrial sponsors representing dealers, VMs, suppliers, logistics, trade associations, credit & finance, government, and IT management consultancy. 3DayCar is a vision of a system in which every customer receives a car produced exactly to specification and delivery date (3DayCar, 2002). For some people this means getting the car as quickly as possible – in 3 days. In terms of benefit for the automotive industry, the capability to build-to-order in short lead times offers a considerable prize: the elimination of wasteful vehicle stock and reliance on retail discounting that erodes profitability. While the concept of the ‘x-day’ car has now become popularised, it originates from the mid-1980s as one of nine challenges for the Japanese motor vehicle industry in the 21st century (Hall, 1993). The spiritual predecessor to the 3DayCar was the study by the International Motor Vehicle Programme at MIT, which culminated in the renowned ‘2 to 1 productivity gap’ finding between Western and Japanese auto manufacturers (Womack et al., 1990), with subsequent developments in lean thinking (Womack and Jones, 1996). 3DayCar proposes that while the lean approach over the past 20 years has ‘succeeded’ at the level of the vehicle assembly plant in terms of either eliminating component stock or pushing it upstream to suppliers, it has failed to address waste on a far broader scale in terms of supply chain delay through information processing. The programme has grown from a UK-based study in 1999, to the third and present phase of research, which examines innovative technologies and their impact on supply chain logistics across Europe and other world regions. A persistent theme throughout has been the role of ICT and the impact on industry structure towards improving flexibility and responsiveness.

## Methods

Case analyses covering the initial periods of adoption for six primary and 22 secondary organizations form the empirical core of an attempt to understand the role and impact of ICT on industry structure. Case studies are useful when exploring new areas of research (Eisenhardt, 1989), particularly in information systems because they cope with technical situations with many variables of interest (Yin, 1994). The rich qualitative and quantitative data sets generated (Yin, 1994) are particularly important because the measurement of intangible phenomena (e.g. roles and relationships) was a central concern of this work. The data were collected using multiple methods (Miles and Huberman, 1994), principally: preliminary interviews in phase I to map the current state of physical and information flow during the order fulfilment process, followed by semi-structured interviews and focus groups in phase II to uncover the motivations and barriers to collaborating through ICT applications. Phase III commenced in 2004 and is at present gathering data to model the supply chain from customer to supplier using computer simulations to gauge the effects of new concepts in interoperability and flexible network technologies (Bischoff and Esterhazy, 2005).

Longitudinal or temporal studies of multiple organizations are popular in management research, despite the practical difficulties over resources, difficulties in generalizing results, and the subjectivity that may arise during data collection and analysis (Pettigrew, 1988; Walsham, 1993; Brown, 1998; Das and Van de Ven, 2000; Perlow et al., 2002; Lewis et al., 2004). Case research alleviates these difficulties by employing iterative triangulation, using multiple data sources and analytical techniques to improve representational accuracy of resultant theory (McCutcheon and Meredith, 1993; Lewis, 1998). Multiple case studies are potentially a rich source for comparing disparate settings, where more cases can increase generalization when applied to complex settings (Miles and Huberman, 1994).

A total of 75 interviews and eight focus groups were completed from the start of the 3DayCar programme (Appendix A, Table A1). A case protocol guided questioning during the semi-structured interviews (Appendix B). Over the first two phases, a wide range of methods of data collection was employed, including semi-structured interviews, site workshops, and survey/questionnaires. In the interests of brevity, a more detailed description of methods is omitted here, but can be reviewed further from the following publications (Howard and Holweg, 2004; Howard et al., 2004; Miemczyk et al., 2005). Despite considerable support for 3DayCar, which attracted sponsorship from six major VMs, an issue raised early on in the programme by investigators was ‘what constitutes industry research?’ Studies at industry level differ from inter-organizational research because they seek a higher order of representation that waive the usual caveats over generalizations from single or limited samples of cases (e.g. Lewis et al., 2004). In undertaking the largest study of its kind into automotive build-to-order, 3DayCar also embarked on what was subsequently to emerge as research of some significance over the role of information, technology, and organizations (Holweg and Pil, 2001), and the impact of IT on industry stakeholder dynamics (Howard et al., 2003). While Table 1 presents a retrospective and therefore ‘rational’ view of the programme, it is important to remember that the findings over delay caused by information processing were unexpected, particularly considering the tenets of the original research founders and their strong pedigree in manufacturing (Womack et al., 1990; Womack and Jones, 1996).

## Empirical material

The empirical material is presented from phases I and II of the 3DayCar programme. Phase I describes the total order fulfilment process in the UK, from the customer placing the initial order at the dealership, to final hand-over of the vehicle. Phase II describes the adoption of B2B e-procurement and supply systems by VMs and suppliers in Europe.

## Phase 1: Customer order fulfilment in the UK

A key finding is of the average 40 days it takes to deliver a new vehicle in the UK, around 85% of the delay is derived from upstream processes dependent on information processing: customer order entry, order processing, and scheduling, and only 15% from manufacturing and distribution (Holweg and Pil, 2001; 3DayCar, 2002). The average industry lead time of the total order fulfilment process is based on data provided by the dealers, VMs, component suppliers, and logistics carriers. Figure 4 forces the investigator to consider the causes of delay in terms of what or who is obstructing the flow of information. Further examination of the current order-to-delivery process reveals the following barriers:

\- ‘Order entry’ is usually daily or weekly from the dealer to the manufacturer, but orders are often assembled and relayed by the National Sales Company only on a weekly basis. Purchasing a new car involves excessive computer hand keying and duplication of information where dealer management systems are not integrated with communication systems linked to the manufacturer. Attempts to change the whole process from a manual to an electronic system requires considerable thought as to integration, because there are still around 20 hard copy documents required before a vehicle can be sold.

\- The ‘Order bank’ (often termed the Sales Order Bank) is held centrally at the VM. This is typically the first filter of orders to match them to production constraints. This stage represents one of the major barriers to a 3DayCar where large numbers of customer orders are processed prior to production at a set time every 24 h. The configuration of VM systems typically results in individual mainframe systems updating overnight, processing batches of orders in time intensive cycles that adds 4 to 5 days to the order lead time. A further problem concerns legacy systems, originally built for a ‘different world’ of IT capability, specific tasks (not integrated) and where technology was associated with management control. These systems are still driven by in-bound logistics and push by production, rather than by order demand. During the interviews some resistance was encountered to non-mainframe, non-batch systems models, where VMs believed that mainframe/batch offered greater control and performance. Managers involved in the original installation were now reluctant to switch off legacy ICT, which may explain why the average plant maintains around 200 systems.

\- ‘Order scheduling’ refers to the orders that have been sent to the factory in weekly batches in line with the production programme. This schedule is also given to suppliers, but they rarely rely on this information for production planning because it is generally too unreliable. While the VMs and tier 1 suppliers exchange data such as schedules and shipping notices using conventional EDI, it is recognized that ‘past tier 1 there aren’t any system standards’. Suppliers perceive the lack of adherence to bespoke EDI standards by VMs – in terms of data format and protocol – to be a major barrier to the order to delivery process. Industry opinion is divided as to whether ‘EDI is dead’ (i.e. bespoke, dedicated communications links between two businesses) or merely whether the emergence of Internet technology has rejuvenated existing EDI formats by allowing them to be posted on the web. Yet, uncertainty exists particularly amongst tier 2 suppliers over the issue of what ICT should be adopted, resulting in a haphazard uptake across the industry of technologies such as Odette, Edifact, web-enabled PCs and e-hubs.

\- ‘Production sequence’ is where orders are selected from the schedule on a daily or weekly basis, and are assigned a day of build. Component suppliers are informed of daily requirements anything from 1 day to 1 week in advance. Even though manufacturers aim not to change the day of build once the sequence is set, in practice as little as two-thirds of vehicles actually get built on the day as originally planned. Shortages of components and quality issues during vehicle assembly are the main reasons for this shortfall.

\- ‘Manufacturing’, ‘Loading’ and ‘Vehicle distribution’ combined take on average only 6 days. The delivery lead time of the vehicle to the dealer includes the time required to build economic loads for the high-capacity car transporters. Yet, here even a lack of system integration and poor-quality data exists that impedes order fulfilment. In-bound logistics IT systems for raw materials and components are more developed than outbound vehicle distribution, but this contradicts the value of the goods carried. The lack of contractual commitment given to logistics providers by the VMs on out-bound delivery promotes a short-termism that hinders longterm investment and the development of robust, electronic inter-organizational links.

Phase I reveals that information processing accounts for the high proportion of delay that occurs before an order reaches production. While manufacturing accounts for only 1 day of the average 40 day delivery lead time in the UK (Figure 4), processes such as vehicle painting add to the unreliability and inflexibility which impact on downstream operations. Overall, the 1999–2001 investigation reveals poor levels of supply chain responsiveness. Hence, many VMs are now exploring the possibilities of reducing lead time to the customer through their own initiatives, that is, BMW – ‘Customer-Orientated Sales and Production Process’; Ford – ‘Order Fulfilment’; Renault – ‘Project Nouvelle Distribution’; and Volvo – ‘Customer Order Production’. The challenge is a whole system change away from ‘shifting stock’, that is, selling cars that have been already built, to ‘selling slots’, that is, cars before they are built. This requires high information visibility across the supply chain, involves considerable effort in integrating ICT within and between firms, and depends on the collaboration of supply chain partners. Ultimately, the concept of building to order offers huge rewards, where ‘ythose who implement stockless supply first will gain a significant competitive advantage’ (3DayCar, 2002: 4).

Phase 2: Collaboration and the role of B2B e-procurement and supply

Phase 2 shifts from a process-based view of total order fulfilment, to the adoption of B2B e-procurement and supply systems. It builds on the findings in phase I over shortcomings in information processing capability across the supply chain. Six cases are selected as representative of as wide selection of firms as possible – that is, US ‘Big 3’, European niche, Japanese transplant – while subject to the usual research budget constraints. They consist of VMs: Ford, Volvo, and Nissan; tier 1 suppliers: Dana and Delphi; and tier 2 supplier: Hella. All cases focus on collaborative issues during the adoption and use of web-enabled ICT.

Ford is the third largest VM worldwide, but is undergoing major restructuring following losses of \$4.5 billion in 2002. Senior management is seeking significant cost reductions across all global operations and has already cut 17,000 jobs. The plan to return to profitability involves replacing the firm’s intranet with a large-scale e-procurement hub supported by the auction tool from Covisint. This is an ambitious project that seeks to create electronic interactive links between the North America headquarters, European subsidiaries, and suppliers. It aims to streamline operational costs through material price reduction, transaction efficiency, and supplier rationalization. The system is expected to control the purchasing process worldwide, improve manufacturing plant stock control, and promote communication with suppliers. However, the e-hub is already facing delays from differences in organizational structure (e.g. responsibility for managing inventory) between US and Ford Europe, and supplier suspicions over component price cuts.

Volvo is a premium VM in Northern Europe acquired by Ford in 1999. It is now obliged to integrate a large-scale eprocurement hub into its business, but expects to achieve benefits in process efficiency, more strategic buyers, and full integration with new product development. Volvo acknowledges its processes are based on ‘paper and personality’ involving time-intensive transactions using legacy IT systems. The culture of autonomy and corporate citizenship is diametrically opposed to the Detroit parent organization. As a Ford Premier Auto Group (PAG) partner, Volvo must adopt the e-hub despite the design for universal American processes and hierarchical structures that are very different to its own, which are based on flexibility and flat management. The next step is the most critical – production parts – this directly affects manufacturing, distribution, and the links with dealers. However, top management has underestimated the resources needed because of implications for business change at Volvo due to differences in process, structure, and culture. It is feared that ICT developed originally for Ford will create considerable disruption for Volvo.

Nissan is the seventh largest VM whose European research and development facility is based in the UK. It aims to launch an Internet-based buyer–supplier portal for standardizing the purchasing, supply, and design process. Currently, 70% of components are outsourced and developed by suppliers, yet only 20% of the data are submitted digitally. Nissan is seeking significant cost reductions from its electronic purchasing and design engineering initiatives, yet it is only at the beginning of the project, where savings are so far limited to the purchase of indirect materials (e.g. office furniture) through e-catalogues. Nissan’s adoption of e-procurement is underdeveloped partly because of supplier suspicions of large-scale e-hub projects and their failure to deliver value. It is also due to concerns from management over the impact of web-enabled ICT tools such as auctions damaging long-term buyer–supplier relations. While Japanese manufacturers perfected the practice of Just-in-Time operations using technology such as MRP as a key element of success, there are fears that the move towards e-procurement will be viewed as a backward step, motivated solely by considerations of cost. A further issue is the lack of industry consensus to recognize one platform for purchasing, supply, and design, which uses standard software, formats, and protocols.

Dana is a tier 1 supplier that manufactures drive shafts, chassis, and engine components. It has already adopted indirect materials e-procurement and plans to extend this to direct materials across all plants in Europe. ‘eSource’ is an electronic database of tier 2 and 3 suppliers that holds data on supplier metrics, visible to all sites. ‘Ariba’ is a third-party provider of an electronic marketplace that generates and receives bids for non-production parts. Dana’s decision to adopt a private hub managed by an application service provider reflects the concerns over joining Covisint. There is fear over the scale of the e-hub and suspicion of the lack of readiness to support suppliers at any level in the supply chain. Even the decision to outsource ICT adoption meant difficulties gaining support across all departments at Dana, because of newly decentralized structure and issues over resolving who should pay for the system. Further, there is a tendency for senior management to be ‘too near the forefront’ of e-business development while resistance persists in other areas of the organization over possible elimination of traditional roles such as the purchasing manager.

Delphi is one of the biggest tier 1 suppliers worldwide and operates in two sectors: vehicle propulsion and interiors/electrical. It has recently implemented a homegrown webEDI project in the UK to increase the efficiency of sequenced in-line supply operations. This provides access to up-to-date delivery information and reduces transaction costs. webEDI creates visibility across a supply chain by introducing real-time interaction between tier 1 and 2 supply partners using standard PCs equipped with web browsers. Delphi is now able to anticipate changes in production schedules, thereby preventing material shortfalls and reducing risk of premium freight charges. It offers a considerable advantage over bespoke EDI systems by providing a cheap data transfer format that is common to all suppliers. The most significant benefit to all participants is the reduction of component stocks through reliable information exchange, and eliminating unnecessary communication via fax or phone.

While Delphi has achieved localized benefits with its ICT in the UK, it remains concerned over the implications of system complexity industry-wide, not only for formats and protocols but also the growing number of e-hubs. Further, the increase in supply chain transparency and tier 1 suppliers with proven ICT capabilities means more responsibility for some operations are being transferred from the VM: ‘a lot of the supply problems come to us’.

Hella is a tier 2 supplier of lighting systems in Europe. It is exploring the options of using ICT to improve efficiency, such as adopting webEDI or e-hubs for stock movement reporting quality control. The supplier has recently been requested by several manufacturers to use webEDI to exchange component pick-up sheets and advance shipping notes, and Covisint for purchasing and stock control. webEDI has improved communication with tier 3 suppliers and eliminated information reliability issues from faxing hundreds of pages of component schedules to dozens of suppliers. However, the benefit of Covisint is less clear. The purpose of the consortium e-hub is to host e-auctions and provide an electronic component release system that is connected to supplier performance metrics. Yet, the time needed to log-on, navigate the system, and for the VM to respond is very slow. At a time of burgeoning ICT complexity across the industry, Hella cannot afford to support systems that add little value and charge for training. There is increasing concern in purchasing at the prospect of further developments: ‘we won’t be in a position to support everything they throw at us!’

The findings reflect the conflicting motivations and barriers during ICT adoption by the industry. VMs’ expect B2B e-procurement to increase transaction efficiency by facilitating a strategic approach to purchasing, supply, and design. The anticipated increase in information visibility is expected to improve supplier selection, monitor spending, create a common source of information, enhance communication, and eliminate paperwork. Yet, very few of these benefits have been realised because of the ambitious scale of the projects. Ford’s e-hub project involves a huge challenge, not only in replacing many IT legacy systems but also attempting to connect firms of very different culture and structure in regions around the world. The management at Volvo has real concerns over reducing their dependence on interpersonal skills, which they feel is needed to maintain current long-standing relationships with suppliers. Nissan is restrained by its cautious approach to e-business, but must now meet ambitious new digital data performance targets to retain its design capability in Europe. While all manufacturers perceive the primary benefit of ICT in terms of cost reduction, they are increasingly daunted by the scale of resources needed for ongoing support of these projects.

Suppliers have already begun to realize some of the expected benefits from their web-enabled ICT projects. This includes improved purchasing performance, reduced IT complexity, cheaper communication links, common data formats, and the ability to handle larger quantities of data. They remain suspicious of VM e-hubs because they are not fully functional, introduce an additional layer of complexity into the current system, and require training for which they – the supplier – must pay. There is also strong resistance to the use of electronic auctions beyond commodity components. Despite initial delays during logging on and navigation, suppliers are beginning to benefit from e-hubs in terms of supply chain visibility and audit capability. It is significant that most e-procurement systems founded by tier 1 suppliers are now operational, for instance: the Dana/ Ariba e-hub, and the supplier hub SupplyOn (founded by Bosch) specializing in online communication and collaborative engineering. Tier 2 suppliers such as Hella also expect to gain transaction efficiencies from systems such as webEDI, but they have minimal resources at their disposal to manage the high service levels demanded by VMs and tier 1 suppliers. ## Discussion

This discussion addresses the questions posed in Introduction section over the role and adoption of ICT and how ICT are reshaping industry structures. The map of industry structure presents the role of e-hubs, webEDI, and other forms of ICT in terms of firm affiliation and supply relationships (Figure 5). It presents the structure and flow across all tiers in the supply chain by classifying electronic applications in terms of membership and relationship. Membership is represented by the affiliation to e-hubs by VMs and suppliers. The relationship between industry stakeholders is represented by the flows – shown as solid and dotted arrows – within and between the e-hubs, driven by price/market, and collaboration/knowledge. Price relationships are short-term, based on considerations of market forces. Collaborative relationships involve long-term exchanges and include information or explicit knowledge sharing that support new products and services. Figure 5 illustrates the complexity of electronic networks, where VMs have tended to adopt a short-term view of ICT, viewing the opportunity to reinforce collaborative ties using e-hubs to significantly reduce supply chain costs. This ‘one size fits all’ approach towards ICT disregards suppliers concerns over firm autonomy and control (Howard et al., 2003), especially those who have successfully launched projects of their own.

The earliest automotive ICT were based on price optimization and resulted in vertically integrated control for the VM. Firm competitive advantage was achieved through the differentiation of products and business processes, and included the design of bespoke communication links to the supplier. In this sense, EDI has remained a reliable (albeit high-cost) ICT application with which to support buyer–supplier interactions, despite the transition from short-term/adversarial to more long-term/ contractual relations. Yet, the same systems approach perceived to support firm competitive advantage during the era of mass production now represents a barrier to supply chains attempting build-to-order. For instance, phase I of 3DayCar reveals the considerable delay in current order fulfilment systems caused by information processing during order entry and scheduling, high volume order batch size, and bespoke legacy systems. Phase II shows the introduction of e-procurement and supply to the industry as treated largely in the manner of a traditional ICT adoption, often disregarding elements of supplier relationships that ensure a more sustainable delivery of process and product-related benefits. The introduction of B2B applications has resulted in further complication of the arrangement of poorly integrated electronic networks, hence an early observation that ‘e-commerce simply speeds up the mess’ (Howard et al., 2002: 1525). Significantly, the 1990s vision of an Internet ‘industry transformation’ has now subsided, with web-enabled ICT re-emerging as part of a ‘second wave’ that is more evolutionary in outlook.

In terms of build-to-order, the role of ICT within and between firms is to provide a stable platform for responsive information flow. Supply partners upstream of production require visibility of daily component schedules and advance warning of downstream demand variability from the market. Hence, industry has shifted from traditional notions of technology as competitive weapon on the basis of ‘firm vs firm’, towards ICT as a collaborative tool for competing on the basis of ‘supply chain vs supply chain’. While the standardization of business language, formats, and software protocols will create benefits through industry collaborating as a whole, the key source of competitive advantage is the capability of supply chains to integrate ICT between partners, linking customers with production. For BTO, this means connecting front-end, customer facing systems such as online vehicle configurators with back-end systems such as direct order booking and component scheduling.

Finally, the impact of web-enabled ICT on automotive structures suggests a complicated arrangement of overlapping networks competing for limited membership across the industry, resulting in isolated pockets of collaboration and isolated information flow (Figure 5). It is argued a ‘mid-phase’ has been reached in ICT evolution, which either points towards e-hub consolidation or firms opting to outsource their information processing to third-party service providers. Are e-hubs really just speeding up the mess? There is now evidence of pockets of value adding activity emerging elsewhere in the industry. This involves a consortium of UK distributors in the automotive component aftermarket using e-hub and document translation technology to automate order-invoice matching between sites (Howard and Vidgen, 2004).

## Conclusion

This research highlights the value of industry-level analysis of ICT effects. It points towards the debate over the role of ICT as either competitive or collaborative as now redundant for industry in the 21st century, replaced by the need for greater network flexibility and supply chain responsiveness to decouple firms and adjust to market preferences. This represents a challenge for all manufacturing sectors – not just automotive – to consider the implications of traditional supply chain management (e.g. physical assets, forecasts, and inventory) in terms of how this applies in future to the principle of building to order and the virtual organization. Further research is required over new forms of organization, particularly for Western firms seeking to respond to emerging markets and realize benefits from more dynamic supply relations while avoiding conflict caused by short-termism and ICT complexity. Recommendations for the future are considered below in light of what such large-scale efforts imply for design, deployment, and uses of ICT in industry settings:

(1) Studies of inter-organizational system adoption increasingly span across whole supply chains, regions, or sectors in order to capture the impact of ICT on industry structures. This is particularly the case when exploring barriers, constraints or the reasons for business failure. Hence, tools for mapping implementation such as business process mapping notations must be extended to capture increasingly multi-dimensional configurations such as the identification of participants and non-human actors, and categorization of process activities, that is, manual, automated, or human–computer activity (Fedorowicz et al., 2005).

(2) The role of ICT and its impact on industry stakeholder interaction represents a rich area for research in automotive (Howard et al., 2003) as well as other sectors. While this research gained access to most prime manufacturers and suppliers, the level of the supply chain from tier 2 and below is less explored. For instance, how does the argument for disintermediation apply here? Further, as information flows improve, conventional understanding of supply chains as ‘tiers’ may need to be reconceived using emergent theories of networks.

(3) Building to order represents a significant step for industries such as automotive and aerospace that in many cases is vital for firm survival. Future 3DayCar research aims to examine supply chain performance in relation to centralised, distributive, and adaptive ICT systems. This future landscape will be explored in relation to aspects such as the governance of ICT architecture standards by industry leaders, ICT design for responsive manufacturing, and exploitation of the system by core users including the end customer.

## (2) Barriers to adoption

(i) How many systems are already used by your firm for B2B communication?

(ii) Does the system introduce a new threat to the firm or any of its partners (e.g. inventory, price transparency, competitive advantage)?

(iii) In current business relationships are there any issues over the system adoption over interfirm decisionmaking, culture, or control?

(iv) How does departmental structure (e.g. hierarchy, centralized decisions) affect communication during the adoption of the system?

(v) How does availability of resources affect system integration?

(vi) How significant is trust during system implementation?

(vii) To what extent are top management (i.e. CEO and Directors) aware of the current opportunities and threats from the Internet and web-enabled ICT?

(viii) Does user resistance to systems occur, and if so why?

(ix) Is learning encouraged in the organization such as training to develop IT skills?

(x) Do current systems reflect the needs of the business: are they secure, scalable, or easily switched off and replaced?

(xi) How accurate, consistent and complete is data transmitted and received?

(xii) To what extent are any shortcomings in data exchange related to computer or human error?
