# The dynamic nature of trust transfer: Measurement and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reciprocity

Keywords: Trust transfer Dynamic nature Reciprocity Measurement Decision making Experimental analysis

## a b s t r a c t

Previous trust literature has provided a limited context for the study of how trust is transferred among multiple agents and how the relations among agents evolve. This paper investigates whether reciprocity helps determine future trust transfers. Furthermore, we propose that the update of trustors' expectations derived from previous interactions may moderate the relationship between reciprocity and future transfers of trust. In this context, we also address the previous literature's calls to reinforce the trust measurement; to that aim, we present two robust and comparable indexes for measuring trust transfer and its reciprocity. Our methodology relies on an experimental design with two rounds of decision making and multiple triads of agents. Our <sup>fi</sup>nal results show evidence supporting the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reciprocity and the existence of a moderating effect resulting from the update of trustors' expectations.

## 1. Introduction

Trust has become a major issue in a number of disciplines, such as psychology [27,59,60], sociology [3,43,65] and economics [36,75]. In management speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, trust is increasingly demanded within [22,35] and between organizations [51] as well as in domestic and international developments [49]. Rousseau et al. [61] delimitated trust as the intention of one agent (trustor) to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of another agent (trustee). Despite a growing interest in the academic literature and practical implications of trust, the previous literature has paid limited attention to measuring and understanding how trust changes after previous interactions.

According to Williamson [76], we can distinguish three types of trust—calculative, institutional, and personal trust. Each type of trust yields different explanations regarding why people trust [2]. To explain why people trust, calculative trust relies on a subjective assessment of the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that cheating gives to the other party [54 (p. 528), 75]. Institutional trust analyzes structures that encourage con<sup>fi</sup>dence in trustworthy behavior and in goodwill to explain why people trust. Personal trust focuses on the trustor's assessment of the trustee's potential behavior depending on his personal features and previous behavior. In our study, we investigate personal trust generated by the trustor's assessment through trust transfer.

A trust transfer occurs when an agent (i.e., the trustor) trusts an unknown agent (i.e., the trustee) because the unknown agent is related to a trusted third agent (i.e., an agent whom the trustor trusts). In this work, we delimitate a triad of relations based on the existence of three agents in the trust process: the agent who trusts (the trustor), the agent whom the trustor trusts (the trustee), and a third party who is concurrently linked to the trustor and the trustee. This delimitation is useful for analyzing if the third party can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the trust relation between trustor and trustee, keeping in mind that “two people who have little or no knowledge of each other can develop trust for each other relatively quickly when they share trust in a common third party” [46, p. 94].

The trust transfer has exponentially gained attention in recent years among practitioners and academics due to its importance in current business practice and to the challenges in understanding the nature of the multiple relations involved [15,67,68]. Firms' managers may interact with third parties – such as independent certi<sup>fi</sup>cation agencies, sports and movie stars, employee coaches, or online media users – to increase the trust placed in the managers (the trustee) by customers, stockholders, or other stakeholders (the trustors).

Previous literature on trust has mainly relied on static theoretical perspectives [19,42] and focused on online contexts [47,67], with only a few studies addressing the transfer of trust in interpersonal contexts [69,72]. These studies have generally assumed symmetry in the agents' behavior. Considering symmetric trust, researchers not only take for granted that if A trusts B, then B must trust A [11], but they also assume that each party has approximately the same level of trust for the other party; that is, counterparts display equivalent trust behaviors toward each other [64]. However, recent studies have highlighted that trust is not necessarily symmetric [11] and that static symmetric analyses have led to a neglect of the potential role of reciprocity and the update of trustors' expectations in the phenomenon of trust transfer. In our analysis, we do not assume that trust transfers are necessarily symmetric. Examining trust transfers from this perspective aligns with calls for research [64] that performs additional empirical analyses examining how one party's trust behavior affects the other party's reciprocal trust behavior rather than assuming them to be equivalent [62]. Finally, in this context, prior studies have also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed a research lacuna in trust measurement [21,42] and, as a direct consequence, in trust transfer measurement. Although there have been suggestions in the literature for additional empirical analyses [46], studies aimed at <sup>fi</sup>lling these gaps remain scarce. Therefore, our interest in this topic answers the call to develop this “fruitful area for future research” [62, p. 347].

This work aims to contribute to the research on trust transfer at the interpersonal level by investigating the dynamicity of the phenomenon, its measurement from both the trustor's and trustee's perspectives, and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence that reciprocity and the update of trustor's expectations play in future transferences of trust. We argue that the existence of a common trusted third party can also generate trust in relations between a trustor and an unknown trustee by promoting the transfer of trust. We build and empirically test two comparable indexes for the measurement of trust transfer and its reciprocity, and we further investigate the moderating effect of the update of trustor's expectations on the occurrence of future trust transfers. This study was conducted by examining an experimental context where (a) symmetry in agents' trust behavioral patterns may arise but is not assumed and where (b) the transfer of trust evolves over time. The study tests this evolution by running a second round of decision making after a previous interaction with the same agents.

Five sections follow this introduction. The paper's second section explores the theoretical background of trust and trust transfer. The third section presents the research hypotheses. In the fourth section, we introduce the methodology used and our measurement methods. We then report and interpret the results in the <sup>fi</sup>fth section. Finally, the sixth section summarizes the conclusions, limitations, and implications of our results.

## 2. Theoretical background

De<sup>fi</sup>nitions of trust abound and rely on different aspects [28]. Trust has been de<sup>fi</sup>ned as an expectation of positive (or non-negative) outcomes that an individual receives based on the expected action of another party in an interaction characterized by uncertainty [6]. Mayer et al. [45] proposed that trust is the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other party will perform a particular action important to the trustor. This expectation exists regardless of the trustor's ability to monitor or control the other party. This concept introduces willingness to be vulnerable as an aspect of trust and incorporates expectations about the other party's behavior. Later, Rousseau et al. [61] delimitated trust as a psychological state comprising the intention to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations for the intentions or behavior of another. Herein, we adopt this de<sup>fi</sup>nition of trust.

The literature on trust proposes that decisions made by certain people (i.e., the trustors) involve a risk level that depends on other people to whom the decision is related (i.e., the trustees). Thus, trustors are prepared to act – to place some trust in the trustee – if they perceive that trustees present a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient degree of trustworthiness (i.e., a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient degree of ability, integrity, and benevolence) for the trustor to perceive the decision's inherent risk as lower than the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [45,62]. Even when different factors have been suggested to explain the reasons for these decisions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ calculative, institutional, and personal factors), most previous literature has analyzed the direct connections between trustor and trustee as well as the trustors' direct analysis of the trustees to evaluate their trustworthiness.

With the purpose of achieving a higher degree of precision and control in these analyses, previous scholars have often treated trust as a dyadic and static decision. From this viewpoint, researchers have argued that one person either completely trusts or completely distrusts another [19,42]. In this view, the level of trust re<sup>fl</sup>ects a single point rather than a distribution along an intra- or interpersonal continuum. However, a stream of literature posits that trust changes over time (e.g., [8,56,77]). This approach has usually assumed that trust starts at a low level and gradually increases [48]. Lewicki and Bunker [41, p. 118] state that “this is a fundamentally different perspective on trust from the view that the essence of trust can be captured by a single, ‘static’ de<sup>fi</sup>nition of its key elements and attributes”. Along these lines, recent works have stressed the importance of carrying out more empirical analyses that examine the dynamic nature of trust [42,71].

Most previous studies have also focused on the study of trust in dyads (i.e., pairs consisting of a trustor and a trustee) without giving much consideration to the social context that surrounds the dyad [18]. However, different works have delimitated at least three ways in which a trustor and a trustee may be linked to each other via third parties: network closure, structural equivalence, and trust transfer. Network closure considers the extent to which dyads within a society are connected by mutually known third parties [13]. Structural equivalence captures the extent to which a trustor and a trustee are similar in terms of the formal and informal relationships that they have with others and in terms of the relationships that they do not have with others [9]. Finally, the basic premise for trust transfer is that rather than being based on direct experience with the object of trust (i.e., the trustee), trust is often based on a third agent trusted by both members of the dyad [67]. To date, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of third parties on interpersonal trust via trust transferability has only been analyzed by Ferrin et al. [18], who adopted a social network approach in which trust transferability is assumed to be linked to the number of commonly trusted third parties.

Based on previous studies [15,16,24,67,69], we posit that a trust transfer occurs when one party (the trustor) trusts an unknown agent (the trustee) based on that agent's association with a trusted third party. Unlike the work of Ferrin et al. [18], our analysis does not focus on the number of common third parties in social networks; instead, we focus our analysis on triads in which trustors and trustees have a single common trusted third party of varying nature controlling the effects on each of the relations in the social network.

Notably, whereas the action of trusting entails the trustor's decision to place himself into a vulnerable position based on positive expectations regarding the intentions of a certain trustee [31], the explanations of trust refer to the set of possible motivations (i.e., drivers of trust) leading a trustor to place his trust in a given trustee. Trust transfer is directly connected to the concept of transitive trust, i.e., one party (the trustor) trusts an unknown agent (the trustee) based on that agent's association with a trusted third party [29,74,79]. Thus, the trust in an agent (i.e., the third party) leads the trustor to trust an unknown trustee.

## 3. Hypotheses

## 3.1. Trust transfer and the existence of a common trusted third party

The trustor faces problems in evaluating the trustworthiness of completely unknown trustees (or of trustees with whom he has a very limited history or no history of interactions). Indeed, the prior literature states that “trust begins at zero when no prior information is available about the trustees” [42, p. 994]. We claim that the existence of a common trusted third party can serve as an effective tool for promoting the transfer of trust from a trustor to an unknown trustee at the interpersonal level.

Previous studies have shown that trust may emerge from “consistent behaviors of trading partners that allow another trading partner to make predictions and judgments due to past experiences and is measured via a predictive process” [54, p. 531]. Kumar and Becerra-Fernández [39] stated that increases in trust between parties, which are produced through an accumulation of prior interactions that were judged by the parties as being ef<sup>fi</sup>cient and equitable, increase the likelihood that parties may be willing to make more signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and risky investments in future transactions [16]. Finally, another study found that the number of shared trusted third parties increases the probability that trust transfer will occur between a trustor and an unknown trustee [18].

We propose that the trustor, by being aware of the existence of a trusted third party in common with the unknown trustee, infers the trustee's trustworthiness. Consequently, the shared trusted agent between a trustor and an unknown trustee reduces the perceived risk associated with the decision of trusting an unknown trustee, leading to a greater trust by the trustor in the unknown trustee. Based on this assumption, we postulate the following hypothesis:

H1. The trust placed by a trustor in an unknown trustee increases when both agents keep a common trusted third party.

## 3.2. The dynamic nature of trust transfer

Gathering lessons from past experiences results in an accumulation of knowledge about the other party, which facilitates the prediction of the other's future behavior [40] and fosters future trust interactions among acquainted individuals [25]. Lewicki and Bunker [41,42] claim that trust is dynamic and distinct in character at different stages of any relationship (professional or personal). They state that “trust is viewed as a dynamic phenomenon that takes on a different character in the early, developing, and ‘mature’ stages of a relationship” [41, p. 118].

Trust thus changes with the passage of time as individuals begin to feel more (or less) comfortable with one another and develop more speci<sup>fi</sup>c awareness of others' integrity, ability, and benevolence. Recent works have stressed the importance of conducting more empirical analyses considering the evolution of trust [42,71].

Trust dynamicity may be linked with the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reciprocity. In this sense, the concept of reciprocity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the trust that results when one observes the actions of another and considers one's subsequent trust-related behavior based on those observations [64]. In an extension of the model presented by Mayer et al. [45], Brower et al. [10] argued that trust is not necessarily mutual: A may trust B, but B may not trust A. However, empirical studies examining this reciprocal linkage, in which one party's trust affects the other party's trust in return (as opposed to assuming that they are equivalent), are rare [63].

In this context, several works have described trustors as decision makers who use information from their history of interactions with a particular partner to draw inferences about the partner's trustworthiness [34,41]. Additionally, previous authors have proposed that trust relies on information about the other party that can be developed through interactions over time [20,53,54]. The assumption here is that when an individual has more information about others based on experience, the individual is better able to predict the actions of others. This predictability decreases the perceived risks inherent in the trustor's decision to trust a trustee.

Taking this approach as a basis, we propose that the behavior displayed by the receiver of a trust transfer plays a key role in future transfers of trust. Despite the scant attention paid in the literature to the dynamic nature of trust transfers, it is of great importance to know how the trust that a trustor is willing to transfer to an unknown trustee varies depending on past behavior in the trust process.

In this sense, reciprocity may have a relevant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on trust transfer. For instance, positive responses from the trustee (e.g., rewarding the trustor's transfer of trust with an equal or even higher degree of transfer) increase the trustor's expectations about how the trustee may act in the future, which may result in a greater willingness of the trustor to conduct more interactions with this trustee. Taking this approach as a foundation, we propose that the behavior displayed by the receiver of a trust transfer plays a key role in future transfers of trust:

H2. The reciprocity obtained by a trustor after a trust transfer to a trustee positively determines future transfers of trust between them.

The amount of information collected by a trustor about the trustee's behavior may depend on multiple factors (e.g., the characteristics of the trustor and the relational context). In any case, a greater accumulation of experiences makes the trustor more able to forecast the other party's trust behavior, thus reducing the perceived risk inherent in the decision to transfer trust to an unknown trustee. Previous studies have analyzed the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the length of a relationship and its impact on trust, and they concluded that short relationships lead to less trust, whereas long relationships yield more trust [17]. Trustors interact with multiple unknown trustees (e.g., new workmates, sellers, and buyers) throughout their lives in different contexts (e.g., at work, in the supermarket, and in their free-time activities). These interactions provide opportunities for accumulating information and knowledge about the behavior exhibited by trustees. This knowledge allows trustors to make more accurate estimations of how each trustee will act in the future, which reduces the perceived risk inherent in trustors decisions to transfer their trust.

We contend that a context of repeated interactions with a trustee provides a trustor with an opportunity to collect additional information for predicting the trustee's expected behavior. As a consequence, the trustor is able to con<sup>fi</sup>gure expectations for interpreting and understanding the behavior displayed by such a trustee. The update of trustor's expectations regarding future trustee behavior is based on the trustor's assessment of the trustee's response in previous interactions.

Prior studies have stressed the importance of an individual's beliefs in in<sup>fl</sup>uencing dynamic trust games after observing the <sup>fi</sup>rst-period behavior of the counterpart [58]. A trustor does not know whether he faces a trustee willing to trust, but he might have a belief or expectation about how likely it is that the trustee is willing. If his initial expectation is suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently high, the trustor will choose to trust such a trustee. After making that decision regarding trust, the trustor may observe that the trustee has provided a level of trust higher or lower than the trustor's original expectations, and thus, the trustor will update his expectations regarding future trustee behavior.

For example, if the trustor observes that his trust has not gotten the expected feedback from the trustee, the trustor will update his expectations regarding the trustee, and given this negative experience, he will very likely choose to reduce (or cancel) his future level of trust in such a trustee. We emphasize that the update may materially affect the trustor's behavior. The same level of reciprocity from the trustee may not generate the same effect in future trust transfer, depending on how greatly the trustee's behavior deviated from the trustor's original expectations.

In this work, we propose that the update of trustors' expectations may moderate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of past trust transfer reciprocity on the occurrence of future trust transfers. This proposed in<sup>fl</sup>uence leads to the following hypothesis:

H3. The update of a trustor's expectations moderates the relationship between the reciprocity obtained by this trustor after a trust transfer to a trustee and future trust transfers between them.

## 4. Methodology

Given the impossibility of developing an experiment of general application to any trust-based relations in the business world, “experiments allow creating settings that are similar to their realworld analogs” by “emulating real-world conditions in the laboratory” [32, p. 216]. Therefore, the use of experiments to draw conclusions that are applicable to the real-world business environment has found strong support in the literature [1,12,55,57]. We <sup>fi</sup>rst describe the subjects participating in this study. Then, we discuss the experimental design, followed by the task and the procedures applied, and <sup>fi</sup>nally, the measures used.

## 4.1. Experimental design

The experimental design is a crucial element in determining what strategies people will employ in certain situations under study. Trust has been measured through surveys and via experimental analyses. Kreps' work constitutes a pioneering study in the measurement of personal trust in dyads with a physical experimental methodology [36]. In this study, because we wanted to measure the amount of trust transferred between agents, we chose to use a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed trust game. Fig. 1 illustrates the structure of the traditional trust game [5,37,38].

The trust game is usually played by pairs of individuals. Each pair consists of a trustor and a trustee. Trustors are assigned an initial endowment before starting the game; this amount is the same for every trustor. Then, each trustor chooses between the action Send (S) or Do not Send (D). If the trustor chooses D, trustors receive their endowment at the end of the game, whereas trustees get nothing. If the trustor chooses S, the amount the trustor passes to the trustee (s euros) is tripled and given to the trustee (to represent a return on an investment). The trustee then chooses between the action Keep (K) or Return (R). If the trustee chooses K, the trustee receives triple the amount that the trustor sent (3×s euros). Furthermore, the trustor receives the initial endowment minus the amount he passes to the trustee. If the trustee chooses R, the trustee receives triple the amount sent by the trustor, minus the amount that the trustee decided to return to the trustor (r euros).

The structure of this game re<sup>fl</sup>ects, in an easily observable way, the central element of trusting acts—making oneself vulnerable based on positive expectations of the intentions or behavior of another [61]. Any money sent to the trustee makes the trustor immediately vulnerable because the trustee can reciprocate as little as he wishes. The trustee acts as a dictator with no economic incentive to return anything. In <sup>fi</sup>nitely repeated games of complete information, the last interaction has the same characteristics as in the isolated encounter game. When backward induction is applied, game theory states that co-operative behavior is only possible in <sup>fi</sup>nitely repeated games when it is also an equilibrium in the isolated encounter game [52].

The situation changes when the dilemma game is transformed into a game of incomplete information. In their groundbreaking work, Kreps et al. [37] and Kreps and Wilson [38] show that rational co-operation is also possible in a <sup>fi</sup>nitely repeated game when the opponent knows that there is a (small) probability that he is interacting with a player who prefers to co-operate.

Fig. 1. Extensive form of the trust game.

In their experiment with this game scenario, Berg et al. [5] reported that trustors sent an average of \$5.16 (out of \$10). The average amount returned by trustees was \$4.66 (of \$15.48), leaving trustors with an average of 50 cents less than they had sent. In a second experiment, participants who knew the results of the <sup>fi</sup>rst experiment sent slightly more (\$5.36) and also returned more (\$6.46), providing trustors with an average gain.

Because our purpose was to investigate the trust transferred by trustors and the trust transfer returned by trustees, we designed a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed trust game. In our setting, each trustor plays the trust game with three types of trustees: a trustor's friend, a friend of a trustor's friend, and a stranger. These three types of trustees represent different levels of distance between the trustor and the trustee: a trustee whom the trustor knows and trusts (i.e., the friend), a trustee whom the trustor does not know directly but who is referenced by a third party (i.e., the friend of a friend), and a trustee whom the trustor does not know directly and from whom the trustor does not have any references (i.e., the stranger). Each trustor received an initial endowment of 4 euros to play with each one of the three trustee types. Moreover, participants were allowed to send and return only whole euros.

A trust transfer occurs when a trustor sends money to a trustee who is the friend of the trustor's friend. Moreover. this trust transfer reaches its maximal level when the trustee who is the friend of the trustor's friend receives the same amount as the trustee who is the trustor's friend. At this point, the friend of a friend is treated as a friend. Symmetrically, each trustee plays the game with three kinds of trustor: the trustee's friend, the friend of a trustee's friend, and a stranger. In our paper, a trust transfer reciprocation is maximal when the trustee who is the friend of a trustor's friend returns at least the same portion of the trustee's money as the trustor sent him.

## 4.2. Participants

Participants were recruited from a large pool of full-time undergraduate students in a Bachelor of Economics and Business program. The participants were all enrolled in the same course when the experiment was developed but attended different classes, providing a broad range of previous relationships. Their ages were also quite homogeneous, ranging from 19 to 21 years old. To ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the conditions required by the experimental design, we obtained a total of 99 observations. Because the experiment was run twice, the <sup>fi</sup>nal number of observations was 198. Participants received their earnings during the week after the experiment.

## 4.3. Task and procedures

Prior to the day of the experiment, participants were required to submit the names of a maximum of four people from the class whom they considered to be their friends and four persons in the class whom they did not know (we distributed a list of the class members from which to select these names). From this information, we were able to classify participants into the three different categories.

Participants were divided into trustors and trustees and allocated into two separate classrooms. It is important to note that communication among trustors and trustees was not allowed at any time in our experimental design. Thus, unlike previous studies examining trust in iterative experimental contexts (e.g., [36]), our trust game did not allow individuals to establish honor-trust arrangements that act as self-reinforcing mechanisms for trust. In fact, the instructions provided to the participants followed the usual scheme in experimental settings to ensure that participants properly comprehended the game before playing: the instructions provided a general description of the process and a brief delimitation of all of the situations that could emerge from our modi<sup>fi</sup>ed trust game (including some numerical examples) to ensure the subjects' understanding of the game.

The game started in the trustors' room. Trustors received three envelops (labeled “friend”, “friend of a friend”, and “stranger”), and they were asked to write down the amounts that they wanted to send to each type of trustee. Furthermore, they were required to write on a separate sheet of paper the amounts they expected to receive back from them. Then, the instructor collected the sheets containing the trustors' expectations, and the envelopes were delivered to the trustees' room. Before distributing the envelopes, we instructed the trustees to write the amounts that they expected to receive from each type of trustor.

After the sheets containing the trustees' expectations were collected by the instructor, each trustee received the corresponding three envelopes, one from each type of trustor. After reading the amounts that each trustor had sent, the trustees were asked to write down the amounts that they wanted to return to each type of trustor. The envelopes were then sent back to the trustors' room. Each trustor checked the amounts returned by the trustees, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of the game was then complete.

We conducted two rounds of the game and, to control the evolution of trust transfer across agents, every trustor (trustee) played the second round with the same three trustees (trustors). The <sup>fi</sup>rst round was called “Round A” and the second round was named “Round B”.

## 4.4. Indexes and measures

One of the greatest lacuna in the research agenda on trust and trust transfer is measurement methodology [21,42]. Previous studies in several areas have based the measurement of trust transfers on interpersonal contexts and face-to-face interactions between agents [50,69,72] or on ad hoc designs of laboratory experiments [67]. However, no consensus has been achieved regarding a common measure for trust transfer across disciplines. As a consequence, several measures are used in different contexts relying on alternative trust approaches, providing results that are hardly comparable. Hence, we propose two indexes for the measurement of trust transfer and its reciprocity; we then present the variables and describe how they were measured.

## 4.4.1. Indexes of trust transfer and trust transfer reciprocity

## 4.4.2. Measures

4.4.2.1. Trust. This variable is measured as the amount sent by trustors to each type of trustee. It enters the analysis as a dependent variable for testing Hypothesis 1. We asked trustors to report how much they wanted to send to each type of trustee in each round of the game.

4.4.2.2. Future trust transfer. This variable is measured by the trust transfer index, which re<sup>fl</sup>ects in a given round of the game how much trust a trustor transfers to a trustee who is a friend of a friend compared with how much trust the trustor transfers to a trustee who is a friend. We asked trustors to write down how much they sent in each round. With this information, we calculated the trust transfer index corresponding to each trustor according to the index developed above. The trust transfer index in the second round is the dependent variable for testing Hypotheses 2 and 3.

4.4.2.3. Reciprocity. This variable is measured by the trust transfer reciprocation index. This index measures how much of a trust transfer the trustee returns to the trustor. We asked trustees to record how much they returned in each round. With this information, we calculated the trust transfer reciprocation index corresponding to each trustee according to the index developed above. The trust transfer reciprocation index in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round is the independent variable for testing Hypotheses 2 and 3.

4.4.2.4. Update of trustors' expectations. Trustors undertake updates in their expectations about trustees' behavior across rounds. This update of trustors' expectations enters into the analysis as moderating variable for testing Hypothesis 3. We asked trustors to note how much they expected to receive from each type of trustee (i.e., friend, friend of a friend, and stranger) in each round of the game. From this information, we selected the data referring to the cases in which the trustees were friends of the trustors' friends. The update of a trustor's expectations was calculated as the difference between the trustor's expectations about the trustee's trust behavior in the second round and the trustor's expectations about the trustee's trust behavior in the <sup>fi</sup>rst round.

4.4.2.5. Trustor's average propensity to trust. This variable was used as a control variable for testing Hypotheses 2 and 3. It represents a trustor's inherent tendency to trust. A trustor's propensity to trust was measured as the arithmetic mean of the amounts that the trustor sent to each of the three types of trustee. Prior studies have shown that people differ in their propensity to trust. Propensity can be interpreted as a general willingness to trust others [35,44]. Kee and Knox [30] argued that trust depends not just on past experience but also on dispositional factors, such as personality. This personality-based form of trust has been referred to by other scholars as dispositional trust [34], generalized trust [66], and trust propensity [45]. McKnight et al. [48] argued that trust propensity has recently taken on new importance because cross-functional teams, structural reorganizations, and joint ventures create new working relationships more frequently; it is likely that trust propensity is the most relevant trust antecedent in contexts involving unfamiliar actors [7].

## 6. Conclusions, managerial implications, limitations, and future research

## 6.1. Conclusions

This study aimed to investigate the dynamic nature of trust transfer and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reciprocity and the update of trustors' expectations. In addition, we have developed robust measures regarding trust transfer and its reciprocity. These indexes are relevant because several recent studies have pointed out that trust is not necessarily symmetric [11]. Prior analyses of trust have assumed symmetry between the parties involved. The study was conducted using a laboratory experiment consisting of a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed trust game. Our design emphasized interactions among multiple triads of agents who maintain different kinds of relationships with one another.

Our results provided three relevant contributions to the delimitation of trust transfer and its reciprocity. First, previous studies have shown that individuals tend to trust in trust games played in dyads, con<sup>fi</sup>rming that they are open to trust even when the other party has no incentive to offer reciprocity to the trustor (e.g., [5]). Our results extend these prior works by showing that the existence of a commonly trusted third party linking the trustor and the trustee fosters a stronger willingness to trust in a modi<sup>fi</sup>ed anonymous trust game.

Second, this study serves to address the role and implications of dynamicity in trust transfer. Despite scant attention paid in the literature to the dynamic nature of trust transfers, it is of great importance to know how the trust that a trustor is willing to transfer to an unknown trustee varies depending on the behavior the trustee previously displayed.

Third, although trust literature has called for additional empirical analyses of these issues [46], studies aimed at <sup>fi</sup>lling these gaps remain scarce. In this study, we empirically show that trust transfer is not necessarily symmetric between a trustor and a trustee. We showed that trustors are positively and signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the reciprocity exhibited by trustees during past interactions with such trustors. Moreover, the trust transferred from a trustor to a trustee is negatively and weakly moderated by the update of trustor's expectations. Additionally, this work contributes to the research lacuna on trust measurement [21,42] by building and empirically testing two indexes for the measurement of trust transfer and its reciprocity. These indexes allow the measurement of both sides of a trust transfer: the trustor and the trustee. Moreover, they represent robust and comparable measures that can serve as effective tools for establishing comparisons across disciplines. It is important to highlight the wide range of applicability of the measures proposed, which can be applied to the measurement of trust transfer and its reciprocity at interpersonal as well as intra- and inter-organizational levels.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

The relevance of our work is linked to the growing importance of “trust transfer” in recent years via the managerial use of multiple and heterogeneous systems to increase trust in a <sup>fi</sup>rm through the use of third parties. Many managers have used certi<sup>fi</sup>cations from independent third parties (e.g., ISO<sup>1</sup> 14001) or famous sports or movie stars in their commercials. Both systems try to gain trust from customers, investors, regulators or other stakeholders via the third party's endorsement of the <sup>fi</sup>rm (considering the trustworthiness of the independent certi<sup>fi</sup>catory agency or the movie and sports stars). More recently, the emergence of multiple websites that rate business services and provide comments from other customers (e.g., TripAdvisor or Yelp) or assurance seals [26] also re<sup>fl</sup>ects that customers trust <sup>fi</sup>rms based on the global experience of third parties with these <sup>fi</sup>rms.

In our paper, we have used trustees labeled as friends, friends of friends, and strangers to generate some variance in the trustor's original expectations and behavior. Even when our hypotheses are independent of the nature of the involved agents, we believe that analyzing trust between friends is important for the business world. People stick to their friends' opinions when making decisions such as which books to read and which <sup>fi</sup>lms to watch. Friends suggest restaurants, books, insurance providers, and doctors based on their previous experiences. In this sense, decisions based on trust transfer from friends often have relevant implications in the business world [23,70,73,78].

Our work supports the importance of trust transfer in real life and clari<sup>fi</sup>es some hidden aspects of that process. First, although it is true that an association with trusted third parties may generate customer trust in an unknown <sup>fi</sup>rm, it is important to note that such customers gather knowledge through repeated interactions, which provides useful information leading the consumer to update his expectations about a <sup>fi</sup>rm's future behavior. Thus, although the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of trusted third parties continues, the update of expectations also acquires a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in determining future interactions between the customer and the company. Our results suggest that trust transfer may even generate negative consequences for the trustee if trustors' initial expectations were too high as a consequence of the endorsement of a third party; our results show that the differences between a trustor's expectations and a trustee's behavior reduce trust in subsequent interactions.

Second, the dynamic analysis employed in this work better re-<sup>fl</sup>ects how individuals use repeated interactions to make trust decisions. The generation of trust through repeated interactions may be especially relevant in organizational environments (e.g., working teams and leader–subordinate situations). At the organizational level, an awareness of trust dynamics can improve our understanding of how professional relationships change and evolve over time [71].

Third, our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that trust is not necessarily symmetric. Thus, research that examines trust relationships must analyze all of the parties involved in these interactions. Moreover, considering the relevance of trust transfer to the trustors' behavior, regulators should examine the nature and activities of agents providing support to third parties (e.g., certi<sup>fi</sup>cation agencies or “independent” websites).

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

As with any behavioral research, this work has several potential limitations. First, the scenario used in the experiment may appear to be simplistic and not necessarily match the complexity of the real world. However, such an approach is necessary, as the use of scenarios provides experimental control [33]. The control of external variables is critical for behavioral research [4]. Furthermore, many researchers (e.g., [14]) have used similar scenarios in their experiments in related research areas. In this sense, we designed a study based on a laboratory experiment involving interactions not only among strangers but also among friends. Because the latter group has a very speci<sup>fi</sup>c nature which may raise issues of generalizability, there is a need for caution when extrapolating our conclusions to the real-world business environments. Additionally, as the sample composition relies on students from a Bachelor of Economics and Business program, more extensive studies are recommended to extend the generalizability of the results.

Second, our results regarding the moderating role of the update of trustors' expectations on the relationship between reciprocity and future trust transfers exhibit a low level of signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Hence, additional studies are encouraged to test the robustness of this effect at alternative settings and samples.

Third, the trust game was played only twice. Though prior literature has noted that simple dynamic models, when initiated with <sup>fi</sup>rst period observed behavior, constitute appropriate settings for predicting how observed behavior will evolve [58], it would be interesting to develop further empirical studies that include a greater number of interactions among the agents to determine the longterm evolution of trust-transfer dynamics. We intend to extend our study using a larger number of iterations to address the evolution over time of the update of trustors' expectations in trust-transfer decision-making processes.
