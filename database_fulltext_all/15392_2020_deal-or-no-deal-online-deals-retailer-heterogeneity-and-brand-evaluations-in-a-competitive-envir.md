# Deal or No Deal? Online Deals, Retailer Heterogeneity, and Brand Evaluations in a Competitive Environment

Jorge Mejia,<sup>a</sup> Anandasivam Gopal,<sup>b</sup> Michael Trusov<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405; <sup>b</sup> Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742

Abstract. Daily deals platforms have emerged as an integral part of the marketing mix for retailers and have enjoyed a wide acceptance among consumers. However, there is considerable ambiguity about the effects of deals on brand evaluations, in particular, the effects on preconsumption brand evaluations. This ambiguity can influence the decisions by retailers to join daily deals platforms, which operate as double-sided networked markets. We perform a series of laboratory experiments to test the effect of offering a deal in these platforms on consumers’ preconsumption brand evaluations. Our research shows that brand evaluations are contingent on retailer characteristics (price segment and age), customarily reported deal-performance statistics (number of views and purchases of the focal deal), and competitive deal intensity. Furthermore, the paper finds that the effect of daily deals on brand evaluation differs substantially from that associated with regular prin coupons, which operate without the benefits of a platform-based model. Finally, loca competition from deals in the platform is shown to have a significant negative spillover effect on neighboring retailers who do not offer deals. Our work thus informs retailers considering joining deals platforms about potential costs associated with such a decision, while also providing insights for platform owners on how they may help mitigate some of these losses in brand value for members of their ecosystem. Our research thus provides for a fuller and more nuanced evaluation of daily deals’ effects on brand evaluations.

## Introduction

The need to create and sustain a digital conversation with current and prospective customers has become an imperative for firms operating in today’s internetbased economy. Viewed through the lens of digital marketing, firms invest in a plethora of internet-based technologies that help foster engagement with consumers across all stages of the customer lifecycle (Yoo et al. 2010, Kannan and Li 2017). Early work in customer-relationship management (CRM) provided evidence for how information technology could be deployed to enhance the firm’s demand-side performance (Mithas et al. 2005). In today’s digital marketing context, firms invest in several technologies to engage with customers—industry data show that firms’ investments in online marketing, such as search engine optimization, display advertising, social media, usergenerated content management, and mobile marketing, will surpass offline marketing in 2019 (Enberg 2019). Within this portfolio of digital marketing initiatives, one particular component that has become highly popular among small and local businesses pertains to the use of daily deals. Groupon, the leading daily deal platform, has processed over \$20 billion in transactions for over 1 million local merchants, has approximately 47.2 million active customers, and has reported selling over 1.5 billion deals thus far globally (Groupon 2019), clearly indicating that daily deal platforms have transitioned into the mainstream for many service providers.

Daily deal firms effectively operate as double-sided platforms, similar to many other technology firms that operate within the digital marketing space (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005, Bakos and Katsamakas 2008) One side of the platform consists of consumers who receive information about daily deals—that is, deep price discounts offered by retailers—while the other side of the platform is composed of retailers who offer these deals. The platform benefits from attracting as many retailers as possible and receiving a proportion of the discounts offered by these retailers to customers on the other side of the platform (Cao et al 2018). Common to most platforms, there are crossside network effects that are important; the more retailers participate with the platform, the greater are the chances that customers will sign on to the platform (Li et al. 2017). Similarly, the higher the number of customers, the more attractive the platform is to retailers. Thus, for platforms like Groupon, it is imperative that retailers and consumers continue to join the platform. However, the incentives at play for consumers and retailers show interesting nuances not fully addressed in the extant literature.

Customers who receive information about daily deals on the platform should find these deals attractive, as these deals commonly offer deep price discounts of approximately 50% value (Bai et al. 2015, Li 2016). However, it is also possible for consumers to view such deep discounts with suspicion; the literature suggests that customers may view large price discounts as indicative of retailer weakness or low quality (Biswas et al. 2013, Chen and Kirmani 2015). Moreover, prior work has shown that the effects of daily deals on the online reputation of retailers offering them is, on average, negative (Byers et al. 2012a, b), referred to as the “Groupon effect.” Furthermore, Cao et al. (2018) show that the presence of deep discounts from daily deals can have a depressive effect on sales, attributable to quality concerns about the service or product that is being discounted. Indeed, the central role of quality perceptions on daily deals platforms can lead to significant changes in user behavior on the platform over time, as shown by Hu et al. (2019). Viewed together, the potential costs of joining deals platforms for retailers, arising from perceptions of quality and value on the part of consumers, may be considerable; retailers could run the risk of having their brand value or reputation harmed.

These costs notwithstanding, the literature on platforms has largely focused on the benefits of being part of the platform ecosystem for most retailers. The broader set of consumers reached through digital platforms and exposed to deep price discounts allows the retailer to potentially acquire new customers and increase sales (Clifford and Miller 2012, Gupta et al. 2012). Furthermore, a daily deal platform’s technology allows the retailer to collect more information on customer preferences, purchase behavior, and engagement (Tiwana et al. 2010). However, the extant literature on platforms has not fully enunciated the potential costs or drawbacks associated with being on a platform. In particular, the costs of being on a daily deals platform have been less directly addressed in the literature. Retailers thus face a striking tension when considering the option of offering daily deals: Although the benefits are well-established, does the offering of a daily deal through a deal platform lower the brand perception of the retailer, even before a consumer purchases the deal? We tackle this unaddressed, yet important, question in this paper.

The stress on preconsumption effects from deals is particularly relevant here. Although the negative postconsumption effect of the deal on consumer perceptions has been addressed in prior work (Byers et al. 2012a), the impact of deals on preconsumption brand value remains unexplored. We argue that this is a crucial factor for retailers because it informs the decision to join the platform itself—if the retailer’s brand value takes a hit by merely offering a deal, the retailer needs to understand this particular effect as well as consider factors that may mitigate this preconsumption effect on the retailer’s brand value. Given the popularity of deals within retail and services sectors, arguably not all retailers or service providers offering deals experience this negative effect (Li 2016). Hence, it is key to understand what are the contingency factors that affect such preconsumption effects.

In this paper, we tackle these questions by focusing on the preconsumption effects of offering daily deals on the retailer’s brand reputation, thereby complementing work focused on the effects of deals on ex post outcomes (Li 2016, Cao et al. 2018, Li and Wu 2018). In order to rule out the confounding effects of actual deal consumption, we use experiments to explore how the decision to offer a deal may affect the retailer’s ex ante brand evaluation. We perform our analysis in stages. First, recognizing that daily deals are platform-based, whereby the benefits of platforms should accrue (Tiwana et al. 2010), we contrast the preconsumption brand effects of deals platforms from traditional print-based price-discount coupons. We test whether there are indeed preconsumption effects on the retailer’s brand value as perceived by customers in the presence of a daily deal. Subsequently, we examine several retailer-specific and deal-specific contingency factors that may moderate the relationship between offering a deal and preconsumption brand evaluation (Dholakia 2011b, Li 2016). We consider their impact on how brand impressions may be formed at the point where the daily deal is offered. In summary, we address the following open questions:

1. How do daily deals affect the brand evaluation of retailers offering them? Do these effects differ significantly from those related to traditional pricediscount coupons?

2. How do retailer-specific factors moderate the effect of daily deals on preconsumption brand evaluation?

3. How do deal-specific factors moderate the effect of daily deals on preconsumption brand evaluation?

Briefly, we first examine how deals differ from traditional discount coupons in their effect on brand value, predicating these differences on the fact that daily deals operate on the platform-based model. As part of a platform, deals combine aspects of price promotions and opt-in advertisements, designed to attract attention on the platform’s consumer side (Shivendu and Zhang 2013, Edelman et al. 2016). Having thus established that daily deals do indeed affect brand value differently compared with printed discount coupons, we then study factors that may moderate the influence of daily deals on brand value. One clear source of moderation pertains to the price segment in which the retailer operates. Higher-priced retailers may experience systematically different consumer perceptions from daily deals than lowerpriced retailers because the magnitude of the discount is higher (Biswas et al. 2013). Furthermore, highpriced retailers offering deals may appear to operate from a position of high quality and confidence (Kirmani 1997, Volckner and Sattler¨ 2005), compared with lower-priced retailers who are likely to be perceived as low-quality and desperate. A similar argument can be used for newly established retailers as well, where offering deals can be viewed as legitimate strategies to acquire customers. In turn, established retailers may appear desperate to acquire new customers, indicating weakness. Thus, we consider the moderating effects of the retailer’s price segment and age on the effect of daily deals on brand value.

Beyond retailer heterogeneity, the competitive environment around the retailer can also influence how customers respond to deal offers. Indeed, one of the benefits offered by platforms to consumers is access to a broader set of sellers (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005), which makes competition more salient for service providers. Prior work in reference prices argues that if all local competitors offer price discounts, leading to high deal intensity, then the consumer’s reference price is adjusted downward (Bell and Lattin 2000). If this occurs, then the effect of the deal on brand value should arguably be muted. Furthermore, in this situation, retailers not offering a deal are likely to see a spillover effect, because they would appear to be priced higher, thereby being less appealing to pricesensitive consumers. We thus consider the moderating influence of deal competition around the focal retailer.

Finally, we also study the extent to which a retailer’s brand evaluation is influenced by how successful the offered deal is on the platform. In studying these effects, we take one additional step from simply considering the offering of a deal for the focal retailer—we consider how the actual performance of a deal (if offered) may affect brand perceptions. However, these perceptions are still preconsumption because the consumers here are still influenced by the presence of a deal, rather than based on the purchase and use of the deal’s promotion. Research on observational learning (Banerjee 1992, Cai et al. 2009) argues that when individuals observe decisions made by others, they are likely to discount their own private signals and herd. The presence of the platform helps make observational learning more salient because data on buying behavior by others are easily accessible (Tiwana et al. 2010). We, therefore, posit that preconsumption brand evaluations of retailers offering deals may be moderated by the extent to which the deal is perceived as attractive to other consumers (captured by the number of views and deals sold on the platform).

We conduct our study through a series of experiments using the restaurant sector, which is particularly suitable because deals are common here (Dholakia 2011b), and brand value is highly important in this sector (Lu et al. 2013). Using Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT), we examine the conditions under which offering a deal affects the restaurant’s preconsumption brand evaluation. AMT-based experiments allow us to identify the effects of deals through randomization, although limiting the generalizability of the results because the stimuli are based on a particular daily deal interface (Yelp). We report here on a series of eight experiments conducted on AMT, with over 2,900 individual subjects. As a baseline, our results show that the presence of a daily deal negatively affects the preconsumption brand evaluations of a restaurant, consistent with the Groupon effect, albeit associated with ex post online reviews (Byers et al. 2012a). We observe, however, that this negative effect is lower in magnitude when compared with regular discount coupons more common in offline settings, providing evidence for why platform based deals continue to be popular with service providers, relative to traditional coupons. Further testing for mechanisms underlying these differences point to consumer effort (i.e., deals require less effort from consumers) as well as trust in the platformbased model (i.e., the terms of the deal are guaranteed by the platform) as being fundamental here.

In further testing, we show that the negative effect of deals on brand evaluation is moderated by the restaurant’s price segment and age. In combination, higher-priced, new restaurants can mitigate the negative effect of a deal on brand value to a large extent in our experiments. Further, we observe the moderating effect emerging from the “confidence versus desperation” mechanism (Chen and Kirmani 2015). Newer and high-priced restaurants are viewed as being more confident, relative to older restaurants and those in lower price segments. Additionally, restaurants operating in areas with high competitive daily deal intensity are less affected by deals, because they become the “norm” within these local markets. Intriguingly, restaurants that do not offer deals in such contexts are negatively affected, showing clear spillover effects on preconsumption brand value.

Finally, our results show that the degree of visibility and sales of the deal (as signals of success) also moderates the negative effect on brand evaluation for the restaurant offering the deal, consistent with herding. We find that although low impressions— that is, fewer “views”—do not seem to moderate the negative effect of offering a deal on preconsumption brand evaluations, deals with higher views appear to boost evaluations. Remarkably, showing deal traction in terms of sales can both hurt or boost evaluations significantly—when consumers observe that the offered deal experiences low sales, the negative effect on brand evaluation is exacerbated, while showing high sales significantly boosts brand evaluation.

Our study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, we document how and why platformbased daily deals are different from typical price promotions in the eyes of the consumer, in terms of trust, lower transaction and search costs, and competition (Bakos and Katsamakas 2008, Tiwana et al. 2010). Indeed, our work helps answer a standing question about the popularity of daily deals, given the Groupon effect. We provide evidence that daily deals, in spite of their downsides, are still better than their offline counterparts in terms of their perception by consumers. The platform-based model behind daily deals provides clear benefits that are hard to replicate with traditional, offline coupons.

Second, we contribute to the literature on the effects of deals, albeit their preconsumption effects (Kimes and Dholakia 2011). Preconsumption effects are important here because they inform decisions by retailers to join the deals platform. Furthermore, we provide evidence for the moderating influence of restaurant and deal characteristics, showing how specific conditions may mitigate the adverse effects on brand value prior to consumption (Chen and Kirmani 2015, Li and Wu 2018). Finally, we show that the effects of deals can permeate the local market space by affecting the brand value of retailers that choose not to offer them, potentially sparking price wars (Bai et al. 2017). These spillover effects are particularly noteworthy, given their implications for retailers operating in dense competitive environments where price promotions may be a common phenomenon, as discussed later in the paper.

## Background and Theory

## Preconsumption Effects of Daily Deal Platforms

Online daily deals, also referred to as group-buying deals, social coupons, or group discount vouchers (Kumar and Rajan 2012, Luo et al. 2013, Bai et al. 2015), are coupons posted online through firms such as Groupon. These sites operate two-sided platforms (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005) on which retailers offer deals (on one side of the platform), and consumers buy the offered deals (on the other side of the plat form). Deal platforms operate through the presence of cross-side positive network effects (Cao et al. 2018), thereby making it imperative that both retailers and consumers stay on the platform and transact with each other. Such a model, therefore, implies that each entity receives positive utility from remaining on the platform—customers receive price discounts on products and services that they want, while sellers are able to generate positive business value from staying on the platform and offering deals. Platform owners like Groupon therefore work toward ensuring the viability of the platform (Rysman 2009). They work with retailers to offer deals for a specific period of time (usually two weeks). Retailers benefit from the platform by expanding the reach of their markets to new customers more easily (Tiwana et al. 2010), while also gaining from lower transaction and search costs on the platform (Bakos and Katsamakas 2008). Customers benefit from deep price discounts (typically 50%), which can be redeemed at the retailer over a longer time period (typically three months after the deal is posted) (Dholakia 2010). The consumer is generally assured of the discount by the platform owner, thereby ensuring trust on the platform. Once the coupon expires, consumers can still redeem the original dollar value of the coupon without the discount, thereby increasing the value for the consumer from remaining on the platform. The platform-based model has led to the use of deals to become accepted as an integral part of the online and marketing mix within the retail industry (Integreon 2012, Krasnova et al. 2013).

Within the academic literature, research on daily deals can be divided into two streams. The first stream tackles questions about why retailers may offer such deals, identifying important antecedent factors. Bai et al. (2017) study the decision to offer daily deals and argue that retailers with higher online reviews are more likely to offer deals because deals can help expand their addressable market. Further, the presence of local competition moderates these effects. In more recent work, Li et al. (2017) use a structural model to show that localized competition and the presence of observational learning—that is—herding behavior, can lead to the decision to offer deals. Word of mouth on the consumer side can add cross-side effects, thus increasing the tendency to offer deals. Indeed, the extent to which vendors operate in clusters of activity is more likely to opt for deals, which can increase price competition (Kitchens et al. 2018). In analytical work, Shivendu and Zhang (2013) use similar logic and show how newer firms that need to differentiate themselves are more likely to use deal platforms.

The second stream studies the downstream effects of deals on outcomes for retailers offering them as well as the eventual consumers. For retailers offering deals, a primary objective is clearly to increase demand. The literature is, however, ambivalent about whether offering daily deals leads to higher revenues per se. Although some papers show a positive effect on retailer revenues (Bai et al. 2015, Eisenbeiss et al. 2015, Edelman et al. 2016, Li 2016), others argue that most retailers may not see a net increase in revenues, given the deep price discounts typically offered (Dholakia 2011a, b). Survey-based data suggest that only 50% of retailers offering deals actually made any surplus because consumers do not spend more than the deal amount, leaving retailers to face the costs of inadequate inventory and demand planning (Marinesi et al. 2017).

Beyond revenues, the question of how offering a daily deal affects the focal retailers’ brand evaluation has also been examined in some detail, with some ambiguity in terms of the relationship’s true form. Because daily deals are, at their core, deep price discounts, it is conceivable that they influence consumer perceptions of brand and service quality (Jamieson and Bass 1989; Kirmani and Wright 1989; Kirmani 1990, 1997). Early work found no significant effect of daily deals on the retailer’s brand equity (Kimes and Dholakia 2011). However, more recently, research indicates a significant effect on brand and quality perceptions, in particular, on the retailer’s online reviews. In studying the relationship between daily deals and the resulting online reviews, Byers et al. (2012a, b) found that offering a Groupon deal led to an average 0.2-star-rating decrease in Yelp (the Groupon effect). Their study followed approximately 5,000 Groupon vouchers from several categories in the United States, which were then linked to online reviews for that retailer on Yelp. The Groupon effect may, however, be mitigated by some contextual factors—indeed, Li (2016) showed that this effect is positive for retailers with lower ratings prior to offering the deal. Furthermore, lesser-known retailers with no prior reputation actually benefit from deals in terms of their online reputations (Kitchens et al. 2018).

In this paper, we add nuance to this perspective by examining how daily deals affect the retailer’s brand value in three specific ways, each capturing consumer perceptions before the deal is actually consummated and, therefore, representing preconsumption effects. We first start with differentiating daily deals from traditional coupons in terms of preconsumption perceptions, thereby establishing as a baseline for whether platform-based deals are viewed as more attractive for retailers. Second, we study the impact of the deal on the customer’s perceptions as a function of the retailer’s characteristics. Building on prior work showing how retailer-level characteristics may moderate the Groupon effect (Bai et al. 2015, Li 2016, Kitchens et al. 2018), we identify three specific factors that may moderate the deals’ effects on the focal retailer’s brand value—the retailer’s age and price segment as well as the competitive deal intensity in the local market around the retailer. These factors allow decision makers within the retail segment to potentially gauge how the average consumer may respond to the offer of a deal from them, but do so before consumption.

Third, we consider one additional step here and study how consumers may respond when a deal is offered on the platform before the consumer has purchased it. In this scenario, the retailer has arguably chosen to offer a deal, but the specifics of the deal may drive brand perceptions. In this case, we focus on aspects of the deal per se, rather than the retailer. We focus on the deal performance factors—views and sales of the deal. Studying these factors provides the retailer manager with insight into how the performance of the deal itself, controlling for retailer characteristics, may also influence brand perceptions. We start with differentiating deals from traditional coupons below.

## Are Daily Deal Platforms Viewed Differently than Traditional Coupons by Consumers?

Daily deals also represent a mix of price promotions and opt-in advertisements (Edelman et al. 2016), presented through a platform. Daily deal subscribers sign up with the platform and receive email messages with the equivalent of advertising copy, containing positively framed images and endorsements (Shivendu and Zhang 2013). In addition to these advertisements, daily deals offer deep price promotions. We use these two aspects to theoretically motivate (a) the differences between deals and regular coupons, and (b) the moderating effects of restaurantand deal-specific factors in this setting.

As a first step, it is important to understand how daily deals differ from coupons in the eyes of consumers, and prior work in digital platforms provides some clear evidence for why this may be the case. The fact that deals are offered on a platform reduces the transaction and search costs for shoppers significantly (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005, Tiwana et al. 2010). Specifically, the costs are reduced by requiring lesser effort from consumer to redeem price discounts—technology makes this process relatively seamless and easy. In addition, the process of searching, comparing, and purchasing multiple deals is centralized in one location, further contributing to a reduction in search and transaction costs (Kimes and Dholakia 2011). This platform model can result in several differences in how the consumer views online deals, relative to traditional coupons. First, there is less uncertainty in the redemption process because the deal platform owner is also party to the transaction and provides a form of insurance, which is absent in cases where coupons are offered directly by the retailer. Second, customers may trust the centralized daily deal platform even if they are not acquainted with the individual retailer offering deal. That is, they may trust Groupon more than a new retailer, knowing that the platform guarantees their investment. Indeed, trust is an essential component of the platform model (Tiwana et al. 2010). Finally, daily deals platforms offer the possibility of using mobile phones to redeem the coupon, which should compare favorably in terms of effort (Li and Wu 2018). These differences in the redemption process, trust in platforms, and effort required to acquire the voucher can shape customer responses to deals vis-a-vis tra- \` ditional coupons preconsumption.

In addition to the reduction of transaction costs described above, deals can also lead to greater engagement of the consumer with the retailer, which can benefit the retailer’s brand relative to traditional discount coupons. Daily deals require full upfront payment on the platform, whereas regular coupons often do not (Dholakia 2011a). Paying for the deal up front can, on the one hand, increase the customer’s intention to consummate the purchase and engagement with the retailer. Because deal platforms publicize deals using a combination of advertising copy (Wang 2006, Mollen and Wilson 2010) and price promotions (Wirtz et al. 2013, Grewal et al. 2017), purchasing a deal could increase the customer’s engagement and emotional attachment to the retailer, relative to the impersonal and transactional nature of the coupon. Access to more information on other deals, retailers, and options on the platform can also help consumers believe that they are making informed choices when they purchase deals (Li and Wu 2018). On the other hand, having to pay beforehand could induce a negative response, potentially leading to lower brand evaluations relative to traditional coupons, where no up-front effort is required, other than the effort of retaining the coupon. However, on the basis of the arguments made above, the platformbased deals model is likely to be better received by consumers relative to traditional discount coupons, allowing us to conjecture that preconsumption brand valuation from deals may be less negative than that associated with traditional coupons for retailers. We next describe the moderating effects of how deals affect brand value ex ante.

## Sources of Heterogeneity in Daily Deals Platforms: Retailer-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Heterogeneity

Prior work has established that retailers’ promotional activities shape consumer expectations attitudes (Lynch and Ariely 2000, Mazumdar et al. 2005, Lee and Tsai 2014). In the case of deals, however, these promotions are sporadic and episodic—retailers opt to offer them at specific times, with redemption periods marked clearly. Therefore, we are specifically interested in how the marginal effect of the daily deal on the resulting brand evaluations may change depending on the retailer’s characteristics. One such characteristic is the retailer’s price segment—that is, the average price for the offered service or product at the retailer. Retailers vary significantly in their price segments, leading to price anchoring for the average consumer (Grewal et al. 1998). Therefore, we question if the retailer’s price segment moderates the relationship between deals and brand evaluation.

The literature provides two reasons for why such an effect may manifest. The first pertains to consumer perceptions of offer attractiveness driven by the actual discounted dollar amount (Blattberg et al. 1995). Winer (1986) proposed that the larger the difference between the initial and final purchase price, the higher the resulting consumer utility and the more positive the consumer attitude toward the offer. He argued that this effect is predicated on the discounted dollar value rather than the discount rate because consumers develop personal forecasting rules for prices based on gains or losses on absolute dollar amounts rather than on discounted rates. A similar mechanism has been observed in other product contexts as well (Della Bitta et al. 1981, Grewal et al. 1998, Wu et al. 2004, Biswas et al. 2013). Using this rationale, the discounts offered on the deal platform by retailers in the low-price segment are likely to create lower brand evaluations by consumers because the resulting discount dollar value is smaller Retailers in high-price segments deliver discounts that are higher in dollar terms and may generate more positive preconsumption perceptions (Biswas et al. 2013).

A second reason for a moderating effect comes from the features that deal platforms share with opt-in advertisements. In the persuasion knowledge model, Friestad and Wright (1994) study when advertised promotions lead to positive versus negative effects for retailers and find that the motivations behind promotional efforts can matter. Those that are perceived as appropriate and reflecting confidence in the service create positive evaluations, whereas promotional, or advertising efforts that smack of desperation produce negative brand evaluations (Kirmani 1997, Chen and Kirmani 2015). Extending this argument to deals suggests that retailers offering them under conditions that reflect confidence may not receive negative brand evaluations. Because consumers make strong associations between price and quality (Erickson and Johansson 1985, Rao and Monroe 1988, Volckner¨ and Sattler 2005), the perception of a deal from a low-price retailer without the slack to offer such discounts is rooted in desperation rather than a well-crafted marketing strategy (Rao and Monroe 1989, Kirmani 1997, Smith and Natesan 1999). Thus, the effects of deals on ex ante brand evaluations are likely to be more detrimental for lower-priced retailers, but not for high-priced retailers.

We also consider the role of the retailer’s age. Regardless of the price segment, consumers expect new retailers to market their services aggressively. Thus, offering deep price promotions as well as investing in advertising efforts when the business is new is viewed as legitimate (McDougall and Robinson 1990, Carter et al. 1994). From a persuasion knowledge perspective (Chen and Kirmani 2015), new retailers may offer deep discounts as long as the signal that the retailer is new is provided alongside. Within a price segment, new retailers may, therefore, be less penalized for offering a deal in terms of their brand evaluation. Established retailers, on the other hand, are likely to be perceived as struggling because the discounted price is compared with the earlier reference price anchors (Kirmani and Wright 1989, Li and Hitt 2010). Anecdotal evidence also shows that new businesses find it advantageous to offer deals to recruit new customers (Silverstein 2013, Fields 2014, Entrepreneur 2015). Established retailers offering deals can lead to perceptions of weakness and desperation (e.g., “is the retailer having problems?”). Whether the retailer is, indeed, in such a state may not matter. Thus, the age of the retailer may moderate the extent to which daily deals affect preconsumption brand evaluations.

## Daily Deal Intensity and Competition Also Vary

We shift our focus to the competitive environment around the focal retailer offering a deal in a daily deal platform. The premise of the platform architecture is that it should increase options for consumers on one side of the platform and the competition among sellers on the other side of the platform (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005, Boudreau 2010). Moreover, competition is a source of heterogeneity that has been implicated in prior work in deals (Bai et al. 2017, Li et al. 2017). However, and in the words of Tiwana (2015, p. 266), “Prior studies [. . .] focus almost exclusively on competition among but not within platforms,” thereby failing to capture the competitive intensity faced by many retailers when they join platforms, which we address here.

In our context, we use the reference prices literature (Kalyanaram and Winer 1995, Mazumdar et al. 2005) to understand how deal intensity within the local competition may affect preconsumption brand evaluations. Price promotions lead to higher sales and demand (Raju 1992, Mulhern and Padgett 1995), increased purchasing by current customers, stockpiling, and category switching by other consumers (Walters 1991, Sethuraman and Tellis 2002). Price promotions can also affect competitor products negatively, due to brand substitution and reduction in sales (Kumar and Leone 1988, Ehrenberg et al. 1994, Kannan and Yim 2001). However, frequent and consistent price promotions within the same category of products lead to a significant lowering of reference prices for that product class (Winer 1986, Greenleaf 1995) Consumers make purchasing decisions by comparing posted prices to reference prices that provide reasonable anchors for valuation. The competitive environment provides price anchors that consumers can use as external reference prices in valuing the foca product (Yadav and Seiders 1998). When price promotions become frequent in the competitive marketplace, they reduce external reference prices for most consumers (Mazumdar et al. 2005).

In the context of deals, therefore, increased use of deals (saturation) within a local market dampens expected prices that consumers are willing to pay, through lowered reference prices. This reduction in reference prices, caused by high local deal intensity, has implications for brand evaluation for the focal retailer. Lower reference prices are associated, in general, with lower brand equity and weaker reputations (Raghubir and Corfman 1999, Valette-Florence et al. 2011, Yoon et al. 2014). Products routinely offered at deep discounts tend to be valued less by consumers. However, a retailer offering a daily deal in an environment where daily deal intensity is exceptionally high faces a marketplace where external reference prices for that service have been reduced systematically. If so, the negative effects on brand evaluation from offering a deal are likely to be less severe. We propose that higher localized deal intensity, by reducing external reference prices, is likely to moderate the relationship between the daily deal offered by a retailer and the resulting ex ante brand evaluation.

The high deal intensity in the local competition may, however, cause an opposite effect on those service providers who choose not to offer a deal. First, because of the direct effect of the deal, they are likely to lose some customers to those service providers who do offer deals. In addition, because external reference prices are lower for those services, the original prices retained by the retailer will lead to consumers’ negative perceptions, all else being equal (Kalyanaram and Winer 1995). These negative perceptions are likely to spill over to preconsumption brand evaluations as well (Duan et al. 2008). Indeed, if external reference prices are systematically lower due to high deal intensity, retailers who retain their original prices are viewed with confusion by consumers who tend to react to these original prices as price increases (Greenleaf 1995, Lowengart 2002). Thus, retailers who do not offer deals in local markets with high deal intensity are likely to see their brand evaluations suffer.

## Daily Deals-Speci<sup>fi</sup>c Heterogeneity

In addition to retailer-specific factors, we argue that consumers will respond more positively to the retailer when they observe that the specific deal being offered is well-received in the marketplace. In other words, if and when a retailer offers a deal that attracts a significant number of views as well as sales (i.e., as customarily displayed by a deal platform), the negative brand evaluation associated with the deal could be partially mitigated. Previous research on herding behavior may help explain this process. Models of herding (Banerjee 1992, Bikhchandani et al. 1998) argue that individuals tend to imitate decisions made by others, even in the face of their private signals, leading to conforming behavior. Within the restaurant setting, Cai et al. (2009) show how individuals are influenced by the rankings of dishes on the menu and are more likely to order those from the popular lists, leading to higher perceived satisfaction with the meal—the authors term this observational learning. In general, the tendency of individuals to herd has been observed across domains (Debo and Veeraraghavan 2009, Duan et al. 2009, Lee et al. 2015), particularly in the restaurant sector (Raz and Ert 2008) and in groupbuying offers requiring a threshold (Wu et al. 2014).

Extrapolating from this literature, recent work on daily deals by Li and Wu (2018) argues that herding behavior can be seen when individuals observe specific deals to be highly popular, in terms of sales. After controlling for other factors, the higher the perceived popularity of a specific deal is (as signaled by the number of views and sales), the more likely the consumer to find the deal attractive and purchase the voucher. We extend this reasoning to the brand evaluation of the retailer offering a deal; if a highly popular deal is offered by a retailer, in terms of deal views and purchases, the consumer is more likely to be positively inclined toward that retailer. Therefore, the negative association of the deal with the retailer’s brand, if any, could be reduced even before the purchase of the deal. We posit that the brand evaluation of the retailer offering a deal may be moderated by information about the number of times the deal is viewed and purchased by other customers.

In summary, we argue that the preconsumption effects of daily deals on brand evaluations are moderated by retailer heterogeneity (price segment and retailer age), deal intensity in the competitive environment, and deal-specific heterogeneity (deal views and deal sales). These preconsumption effects, if any, add to the decision-making calculus that firms need to use when they consider joining deal platforms, in addition to other factors that have been identified in the literature (Anderson et al. 2013). In lieu of formal hypotheses, we allow the experiments, described in the next section, to provide us with guidance.

## Testing the Preconsumption Effects of Daily Deals in the Laboratory Context and Setting

We chose the context of restaurants offering daily deals. Daily deal platforms are popular with consumers and readily available in the restaurant sector (Dholakia 2010, Farahat et al. 2012). The restaurant sector is also an apt context to study how brand evaluations matter (Kimes and Dholakia 2011) due to being clearly segmented by price, age, and other characteristics that make the process of measuring consumer perceptions easier (Gu et al. 2011, Lu et al. 2013). Most consumers are familiar with the restaurant sector, making it feasible to conduct experiments. In our experiments, we use a fictional restaurant to elicit responses from subjects. In order to retain some level of familiarity with selecting and evaluating restaurants for our subjects, we use experimental stimuli designed to look like an official Yelp page for the restaurant. Consumers often check or refer to Yelp when they are in the process of selecting restaurants, being the most common site for accessing online reviews (BrightLocal 2017). The use of a standard Yelp interface allows us to retain a closer link between stimuli used in the experiments. In creating experi mental conditions, we were careful to use the user interface of Yelp.com by adopting the same fonts and colors (see examples in Figure 1 and Online Appendix C).<sup>1</sup> Specific treatments were applied to the Yelp interface, described below for each experiment. We first start with contrasting deals with traditiona coupons, to examine whether these are indeed viewed differently by consumers. Subsequently, we describe the individual experiments to test the moderating effects of the three factors—retailer-specific heterogeneity, deal intensity in the local market, and dealspecific heterogeneity.

## Study 1: Contrasting Daily Deals and Traditional Coupons

Procedure, Data, and Measures. The objective of Study 1 is twofold. First, we test whether consumers brand evaluations of a restaurant’s services following the offering of a deal through a platform is different from a traditional printed coupon while holding the case of no coupon or deal as a baseline. Second, we investigate the mechanisms that may explain the differences between daily deals and coupons, if any, in terms of brand evaluation. We test two proposed mechanisms: perceived effort to claim the deal and perceived trust in redeeming the deal through a platform. Additionally, we test several alternative explanations, such as reduced uncertainty from higher transparency, increased level of engagement, and greater attachment to the brand, that may also be influential here.

A particular spillover effect so far unreported in the literature is noteworthy—even restaurants who choose to offer no deals are affected when nearby competitors offer them. For those restaurants that choose to offer deals, the consequences are also significant. For restaurants without nearby deal competition, offering a deal leads to a significant lower brand evaluations. However, when there is high daily deal competition, there is no evidence of any change in brand evaluations as a result of offering a deal. This is in line with prior work suggesting that when promotions are attributable to external factors (i.e., high deal competition), the negative effect of offering a promotion on the brand is muted (Raghubir and Corfman 1995, 1999).

Furthermore, the results corroborate research showing how localized deals competition influences consumer expectations as well as the decision to offer deals on the part of focal restaurants (Li et al. 2017). In environments with high deal intensity, the negative effects associated with offering a deal are attenuated— a deal here may be perceived as standard practice in the marketplace with little penalty imposed from consumers’ perspective. The high deal intensity in a neighborhood likely reduces reference prices for that service, leading to a spillover effect even on those retailers who choose not to offer such deals. Thus, the presence of competition on the platform can, paradoxically, benefit retailers by lessening the extent to which they are negatively affected by offering deals. On the other hand, for nonparticipants on such deal platforms, there appear to be significant negative consequences from the decision to not join the deals platform.

Although we have thus far explored heterogeneity among merchants offering deals and local competition, and their moderating effects, clearly not all deals are successful—some deals may receive more attention from consumers (Li and Wu 2018), leading to varying effects on brand evaluations. We explore these in the next series of experiments. We note that, unlike the experiments described thus far, Studies 3A and 3B below focus only on deal restaurants—that is, there are no “no-deal” conditions in these experiments.

## Study 3A: Daily Deals and the Moderating Effect of Deal Impressions

Procedure, Data, and Measures. Study 3A examines whether consumers’ brand evaluations of a restaurant offering a deal are moderated by the popularity of the deal with other consumers in terms of deal impressions or views—that is, the number of consumers visiting the website where the deal is sold. We recruited 300 respondents (110 women, $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 6 )$ from AMT for this experiment. These subjects were different from those in previous studies and participated for pay (\$0.35). They were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: deal without impressions information, which served as a baseline; deal with low impressions; and deal with high impressions. The experimental procedures were identical to the previous studies, except for the treatments, which now include information about the number of visits (i.e., impressions) to the deal page. Adopting the same user interface used by Groupon to convey this information, subjects in the low-impressions condition were shown the following added information: “+10 viewed,” on the deal page. In the high-impressions condition, subjects were shown the following: “+100 viewed.” The “no impression” treatment showed no information other than the deal parameters, which were common across all settings. The stimuli for each condition are shown in Online Appendix C. Similar to the previous studies, we retained an average value for other factors. Participants were asked to review the information in the simulated web page and then assess the quality of the restaurant. We performed manipulation checks on the popularity of the restaurant. We asked respondents to rate the restaurant popularity $( 1 ~ = ~ ^ { \prime \prime } \mathrm { v e r y }$ $\mathrm { p o p u l a r } ^ { \prime \prime }$ to $7 ~ = ~ \mathrm { ^ { \prime \prime } n o t }$ very popular”). As before, we measure brand evaluation, composite of purchase intention, and perceived quality $( r = 0 . 8 9 )$ , used as the dependent variable.

Results and Discussion. Manipulation checks confirmed that participants in the “high-impressions” condition (mean = 5.01) find the restaurant more popular than those in the “low-impressions” and “no impressions” conditions (mean = 3.12; F(1, 299) = 4.08, $p \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Additionally, responses to brand evaluation are well balanced across subject characteristics. Our objective is to understand better how the effect of offering a deal affects preconsumption brand evaluation differently when the deal is more (or less) popular in terms of views. For the “no impressions” condition, the mean brand evaluation is 4.76, which is indistinguishable (using pairwise t-tests) from that for the “low-impressions” condition, 4.66. Thus, consumers do not appear to punish deals with low impressions in any systematic manner. However, for the “high-impressions” condition, the mean brand evaluation is 5.85, significantly higher than both other conditions (pairwise t-tests, $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for both comparisons). Using ANOVA, we confirm that treatment assignment significantly changes subject’s brand evaluation $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ; the details are available in Online Appendix B. These results are shown in Figure 8 and attest to the effect that successful deals, measured by using impressions, may have on mitigating the negative effects of the deal per se.

## Study 3B: Daily Deals and the Moderating Effect of Deal Sales

Procedure, Data, and Measures. Study 3B examines whether consumers’ brand evaluations of a restaurant offering a deal are moderated by the popularity of the deal with other consumers in terms of deal sales. We recruited 300 respondents (115 women, $\mathrm { M e a n } _ { \mathrm { a g e } } = 3 5 )$ from AMT. These subjects were different from those in previous studies and participated for pay (\$0.35). Similar to Study 3A, respondents were randomly assigned to one of three conditions: deal without sales information, deal with low sales, and deal with high sales. The experimental procedures were identical to study 3A except for the treatments. Adopting the same user interface used by Groupon, we showed subjects in the “low sales” condition the following: “+10 bought,” while the “high sales” condition showed $^ { \prime \prime } { + } \bar { 1 } 0 0$ bought” (shown in Online Appendix C). Similar to previous studies, we retained an average value for other factors. We performed manipulation checks on restaurant popularity by asking respondents to rate the restaurant popularity (1 = “very popular” to $7 = { } ^ { \prime }$ “not very popular”). As before, we measured brand evaluation, composite of purchase intention, and perceived quality $( r = 0 . 8 9 )$ as the dependent variable.

Results and Discussion. Manipulation checks confirmed that participants in the “high sales” condition (mean = 5.71) find the restaurant more popular than participants in the “low sales” and “no impressions” conditions (mean = 3.31; F(1, 299) = 3.67, p < 0.001) Additionally, responses to brand evaluation are well balanced across subject characteristics. ## Discussion and Managerial Implications

A recent Wall Street Journal article declared: “For years, small businesses had a love-hate relationship with daily-deal sites” (Dizik 2017). Such ambivalence toward daily deals and the platforms that offer them extends beyond businesses to include customers and researchers. This ambivalence notwithstanding, their presence in the digital marketing portfolio for businesses in the retail sector, in particular, remains undisputed, as evident in market size statistics. The daily deals industry has experienced robust growth in the last few years, with revenues at an annual growth rate of approximately 12.5% (IBIS 2019). To research the daily deals phenomenon, recent academic work has examined the downstream effects of offering daily deals to customers and retailers (Li et al. 2017, Li and Wu 2018). A notable aspect of this research pertains to the so-called Groupon effect, wherein the online reviews of retailers offering a deal are negatively affected (Byers et al. 2012a). Despite this stream of research, there remain open questions about how deals may affect retailers and consumers. One issue has remained unresolved in the literature that has clear implications for why retailers may choose to join the deals platform as a seller (Bakos and Katsamakas 2008): How does offering a daily deal influence the retailer’s brand perception before the purchase decision is made? Clearly, this question has direct implications for the return on investment that retailers may enjoy from joining deals platform, while also complementing earlier work studying the ex post effects of deals (Byers et al. 2012b, Li et al. 2017).

Using a series of experiments conducted on AMT, we show that the preconsumption effects of deals on brand valuation do manifest, complementing earlier work that showed how deals affect ex post online reviews (Byers et al. 2012b). However, these adverse effects are less damaging to the focal retailer compared with print coupons, which represent the traditional way of offering price promotions. Clearly, the platform-based model of deals helps by reducing the uncertainty, transaction costs, and effort in buying and redeeming these deals. Thus, our work explains, in part, why, despite the ongoing ambivalence about deal platforms in general, they remain consistently used by both retailers and consumers, especially within the retail sector.

Going beyond this baseline effect of deals on preconsumption brand value, we ask the following: Are there underlying variations in terms of how these preconsumption effects may manifest across retailers and deals per se? Indeed, there is awareness even in practitioner circles that a platform like Groupon “May Not Be Ideal for All Businesses” (LaPonsie 2019). Our study sheds some light on this issue as well. Indeed, we show that the price segment, restaurant age, and the deal intensity in local competi tion significantly moderates the preconsumption effect hitherto presented. Beyond documenting these moderators, we also explore some mechanisms that drive this heterogeneity in the observed preconsumption effects. A series of follow-up experiments show one mechanism at play here: whether retailers are perceived to be operating with confidence or desperation when offering deep price discounts (Kirmani and Wright 1989). Thus, the participation on the deal platform itself, regardless of the benefits of the platform model, can signal confidence or desperation to buyers on the other side of the platform, thereby influencing the extent to which cross-side demand effects may kick in (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). Additionally, we show how the success of the deal, made visible through the platform’s technology and design in terms of deal views and sales, moderates the effect of offering a deal on brand evaluation.

Taken together, the research presented here provides one clear insight of relevance for retailers considering deals and deal platform firms: Under certain conditions, offering deals can lead to positive outcomes for the retailer in terms of preconsumption brand evaluation, even though the overall effect of offering deals is still negative. From a platform’s perspective, our work adds nuance to the question of why retailers would choose to join two-sided platform markets as the revenue-generating side for the platform owner (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005). Research on platforms has mainly focused on governance issues on the platform (Tiwana et al. 2010), as well as economic issues about the management of these platforms (Bakos and Katsamakas 2008). However, the access to a large number of consumers on the other side of the platform through cross-side network effects has remained the main attraction of platforms within the literature (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005, Cao et al. 2018). We show that the very act of joining such a platform itself (i.e., by offering a deal) can convey information to consumers that can undercut the benefits of the platform. Thus, platform management entails not only the governance of the platform ecosystem and its constituents, but also mitigating any shifts in consumer expectations coming from merely offering a product or service on a platform.

Our work here provides several open questions for future research. First, although we document the ex ante brand impacts of deals platforms for retailers, there are other ex ante features that may also affect the returns on investments that retailers can face from joining platforms. Additional investments in service recovery may be needed for retailers joining deal platforms, because the actual consumption process may continue to be influenced by the lowered brand value and quality expectations. Because the preconsumption brand evaluation resulting from offering a deal can “stick,” service recovery can become a critical capability. Deep price promotions can lead to congestion during consumption that can have downstream negative effects, as discussed by Byers et al. (2012b); to ensure that this does not happen, further ex ante investments will be needed by retailers to safeguard their brand. Second, although we consider how retailer-specific, deal-specific, and environmentspecific characteristics of restaurants separately affect preconsumption perceptions of merchants offering daily deals, considering some of these characteristics together would be an interesting extension of our work.

Another implication of our work pertains to the effects of deals on those restaurants that choose not to offer them. Although prior research has highlighted the benefits of two-sided platforms (Parker and Van Alstyne 2005, Tiwana et al. 2010), we focus attention on a related, yet unexplored question: How are firms that choose not to join the platform affected by their proximal competitors joining the platform? In the context of daily deals platforms, many practitioners have conjectured that the prevalence of deals in lo calized neighborhoods has significant spillover effects, even on those that choose not to offer them. This is not a minor problem for the restaurant industry. Despite the growth of daily deals in recent years, the majority of retailers in the United States have not offered them. We find that these spillovers are significant; nondeal restaurants may be adversely af fected by the presence of intense deal activity among their local competition, made salient through the platform’s reach. As a consequence, these cumulative effects can easily lead to “price wars” in local markets and the observed localized antipathy to deals in the business press (Edwards 2011, McArdle 2012). Even more interesting is the observation that the effects of deals on brand evaluations are not solely attributable to the poor (or good) performance of service providers at the point of redemption, as expected. In our experiments, subjects do not experience any service from these nonparticipating restaurants, yet their preconsumption evaluations are significantly affected when a daily deal is offered. These results pose exciting implications for all retailers who operate in con texts where deals have become competitive necessities.

As a whole, our results present a more fine-grained view of how daily deals affect consumer brand evaluations. In terms of theoretical contributions to the literature, we provide specific conditions under which online deals affect preconsumption brand perceptions. These nuances are particularly important in being able to assess the full value of deal platforms, while also augmenting the recent work in this area (Bai et al. 2015, Li 2016, Kitchens et al 2018). Our work also extends the consumer behavior work of Friestad and Wright (1994), Kirmani and Wright (1989), Chen and Kirmani (2015), and Li and Wu (2018) in understanding how consumers interpret a marketer’s efforts, motives, and tactics. We show that in the information-rich environment tha platforms commonly provide, consumers do form quality attributions based on cues found in the deals themselves, retailer-specific factors, and the local competitive environment.

Our study also has implications for practice, especially for restaurants (retailers in general) and platforms. For platforms, our work has implications for how they influence the brands of members of their ecosystems. If, as we show, consumers appear to be interpreting daily deals either as a signal of confidence or desperation, the platform should be especially sensitive to how information about retailers is presented to consumers on the platform, so as to minimize any inadvertent effects on the retailer’s brand. Alternatively, platforms should strategically target only those retailers whose participation on the platform does not affect their brands. Inadequate attention to these issues could lead to retailers defecting from the deals platform and the eventual unraveling of the platform. Our discussions with managers at a leading deal platform indicate that the firm is indeed aware of these broader effects and has started to consider targeting specific retailers strategically so as to avoid these externalities.

Indeed, Groupon recently announced plans to acquire an artificial intelligence startup to offer automated voice and chat solutions to improve the customer experience of ordering services to reduce any brand hits on retailers ex ante (Trefis Team and Great Speculations 2019). Whether the deployment of these technologies influences customers in terms of their preconsumption impressions remains to be studied. Furthermore, for retailers considering daily deals, understanding how their customers may perceive their motivations for offering such discounts and their competitive environment is critical. Managing their participation on deals platforms requires being aware of any preconsumption effects and actively combating them strategically. As Bharadwaj et al. (2013) argue, many firms have tried to use, and indeed understand, social media and other marketing tools in isolation. An effective business strategy should consider how platform-based daily deals affect the social media standing of retailers, while also ensuring a positive return on investment for their investments in joining platforms. We believe that our research will help contribute toward a broader understanding of these factors that affect firms’ strategic decision making.
