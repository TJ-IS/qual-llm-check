# Vertical Integration and Zero-Rating Interplay: An Economic Analysis of Ad-Supported and Ad-Free Digital Content

Soohyun Cho<sup>a</sup>, Liangfei Qiu <sup>b</sup>, and Subhajyoti Bandyopadhyay<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Rutgers Business School, Rutgers, The State University of New Jersey, Newark, New Jersey, USA; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems and Operations Management, Warrington College of Business, University of Florida, Gainesville, Florida, USA

## ABSTRACT

We discuss the economic impacts of the interplay between vertical integration and zero-rating for digital content. To this end, we develop a game-theoretic model that involves an Internet Service Provider (ISP), two competing content providers (CPs)—one an adsupported CP, the other ad-free—and consumers. While previous studies have separately examined vertical integration and zerorated content, none to our knowledge has explored the simultaneous interaction of both phenomena, which became apparent after the historic merger of AT&T and Time Warner. This vertical integration of an ISP and a CP has sparked an intense debate among policymakers and practitioners alike over the economic implications and consequent business practices (e.g., zero-rated content). The results of our analysis show that an ISP can generate higher profits after the merger with zero-rated content. Surprisingly, we find that the consumer valuation of the ad-free content significantly afects how the ISP will decide to maximize its profit. We extend our model to additionally consider the CPs’ partial sponsorship of their consumers’ data usage as well as the ISP’s diferent pricing schemes for its consumers and the CPs. Our analysis, which illustrates the various arrangements between vertical integration and zero-rated content, and our findings on consumer surplus and social welfare should be helpful to policymakers as well as to the ISPs, CPs, and consumers of digital content.

## KEYWORDS

Vertical integration; zero rating; game-theoretic model; Internet Service Provider; ISP; content provider; ad-supported content; ad-free content

## Introduction

## Revolution in the Digital Content Market: Vertical Integration

AT&T’s recent acquisition of Time Warner represents the telecom giant’s most ambitious bid to date to compete with technology companies, such as Netflix and Amazon, which are focused on distributing premium content and, according to the ruling by the U.S. District Court [8], are already vertically integrated in the digital content market [23]. AT&T and Time Warner, which have been struggling of late to retain their customers, believe that the merger would enable them to avoid redundant investment and maintenance costs while improving their revenue streams by selling targeted advertisements at a higher rate using AT&T’s infrastructure and Time Warner’s content [20]. However, the U.S. Justice

Department has come out against the merger, ostensibly due to concerns that the deal could hamper competition among Internet service providers (ISPs) and content providers (CPs) and ultimately harm consumers via higher prices and restricted access to certain content [15]. In contrast, University of Chicago economics professor Dennis Carlton, a former Assistant Attorney General for Economic Analysis who testified on behalf of AT&T and Time Warner during the government’s antitrust suit, argues that the eficiency derived from vertical integration should remain a major consideration in light of the changing media landscape [3]. Indeed, even when considering its potential economic benefits for customers, vertical integration remains a highly controversial issue and, as exemplified by the recent merger of JioFiber and JioTV in India, will continue to remain so all over the world [31].

## Advertising Revenue: Intensifying Competition with the Digital Advertising Platforms

In terms of advertising revenue, AT&T and Time Warner are also experiencing increased competition from web giants Facebook and Google. Generally, traditional CPs (e.g., Time Warner) employ the “spray and pray” strategy in television advertising, based on demographic information about the viewers of their content. However, Facebook and Google can provide highly targeted digital ads using granular data by observing consumers viewing behavior for specific online content and discerning viewer preferences and interests. The rise of digital advertising that eficiently attracts consumers based on their preferences has upped the pressure on traditional CPs, with the share of U.S. spending on digital advertising exceeding spending on television advertising for the first time in 2016 [12]. As a result, traditional CPs, which rely on advertising as a major source of revenue, will have to charge additional fees to users of their content, and might ultimately lose the more cost-conscious consumers.

Conversely, households are likely to continue the recent trend of cord-cutting that promises to reduce the price of their network bundles. The consumers’ intent to cut the cord diminishes the connectivity-derived revenue of ISPs like AT&T, making it critical for the company to increase viewership by attracting high-quality content without losing its bargaining power in negotiations with the CPs. Additionally, a failure to agree upon renewal terms with the CPs will result in a blackout of those contents and may ultimately harm the ability of the ISP’s consumers to access their preferred content [1].

Consumers’ cord-cutting behaviors also afect traditional CPs, such as Time Warner. Since CPs rely heavily on ad revenue—which is positively related to the number of consumers the CP attracts—a decrease in consumers would decrease revenue. Compounding the efects of cord-cutting, web giants such as Netflix and Amazon can also use their massive budgets to siphon of consumers by ofering improved user interfaces and relatively low subscription fees, which can accelerate the decrease in the number of consumers of the traditional CPs [24].

The increasingly competitive business environment in online streaming has compelled AT&T and Time Warner to pursue vertical integration in an efort to increase ad revenue by exploiting their complementary assets. The companies claim that the higher ad revenue allows them to charge consumers less for video distribution while safeguarding the benefits of their existing customers [8]. In addition to the higher ad revenue, vertical integration provides the companies with more flexibility to allocate and use their budgets for strategies (e.g., zero-rated content) that can attract more consumers [2, 30].

## Changing Digital Content Market: Zero-Rated Content

AT&T recently introduced its DirecTV Now service to provide free data (a practice known as sponsored data or zero-rating) for customers who use DirecTV digital content, which is proprietary to AT&T. Under the terms of the service, AT&T wireless customers who watch DirectTV on their mobile devices are exempt from the resulting data usage being applied to their monthly data cap. Customers of a rival carrier such as Verizon and Sprint would be at a disadvantage, since watching streaming video on mobile devices rapidly eats through data [38]. If AT&T made good on its plan to acquire Time Warner, the digital content of the latter would similarly be zero-rated for AT&T customers. Some consumers, in order to lower their spending on online services, might then opt for such zerorated content in place of their traditional favorites. Consequently, rival digital content providers, such as Netflix or Hulu, might have to ofer zero-rated content to their own customers if they wish to dissuade them from switching to content from, say, Time Warner.

In this paper, we look at four diferent cases of the interplay between an ISP and CPs against the backdrop of ISP integration and zero-rated content, and explore the concerns of regulatory agencies [14]. For example, if consumers were to choose the CP that ofers zero-rated content, it would harm the profitability of other CPs, which would be detrimental to the consumers in the long run. Our study employs a game-theoretical model to examine the evolving dynamics among Internet service providers, content providers, and consumers. For the purposes of our analysis, we assume the existence of one ISP (often a reality in many geographical areas within the United States), such as AT&T, and two competing CPs, such as Time Warner and Netflix.

One of the primary goals of this research is to combine the concept of zero-rating to vertical integration. This idea enables ISPs to technically avoid violating the principles of net neutrality (in the sense that the ISPs do not prioritize certain content over others) while providing them with a potential strategy to increase their profits in the changing media landscape. As we explore in the model section, using diferent zero-rating arrangements between the ISP and the CPs, we analyze the possible strategies of the diferent players within our modeling framework. We then find the dominant strategies of the ISP under diferent market scenarios and thereby compare the welfare outcomes. These comparisons allow us to suggest possible courses of action for regulatory agencies like the FCC as they debate pricing schemes about digital content delivery and future mergers in the media landscape.

The remainder of this paper is arranged as follows. In the second section, we review the literature that motivated our research. We then explain our model, analyze it under four diferent arrangements between an ISP and CPs (third section), and discuss our results (fourth section). In conclusion, we discuss the policy implications for corporate management as well as the potential economic consequences for consumers and provide some possible directions for future research.

## Literature Review

## Vertical Integration

Vertical integration refers to the business model in which a company expands its control over its entire business framework by coordinating companies at diferent stages of the value chain. This type of business structure is generally more prevalent when there is a disparity in the balance of power between two companies [27]. Previous studies have shown that companies generally pursue integration due to considerations of uncertainty in demand, lack of market flexibility, and risk aversion [30, 37]; an integration strategy would enable these companies to provide better-priced and higher-quality products and services for its end-users [2, 17]. Regulators, meanwhile, are wary of the practice, seeking to ensure that vertical integration does not hinder competition in the market and flexibility for consumers. An earlier study by Economides [10] found that a vertically integrated monopolist could increase its profits by efectively raising the costs of its downstream rivals, regardless of the costs imposed on its own downstream subsidiary. Conversely, the study also found that if the monopolist increased costs for all downstream firms, including its own subsidiary, its profits decreased. While the former finding aligns well with our own results, the latter of Economides’ conclusions diverges from ours since our model’s scenario (e.g., a downstream rival maximizing its profit by optimizing the amount of the subscription fee charged to its consumers) difers from his scenario. We will examine this comparison alongside our findings in greater detail in the results and conclusions sections.

According to Guo et al. [18], vertical integration can increase the overall welfare efects for consumers if Internet service providers (ISPs) integrate with more efective content providers (CPs) and grant these CPs prioritized delivery of their content. However, their study also found that the prioritizing of CPs in the vertical integration scenario could potentially harm consumers by limiting their freedom to access contents from less efective CPs, thus violating the concept of net neutrality. Aïd et al. [2], on the other hand, argued that vertical integration could benefit firms by eliminating asymmetric risk structures and implementing risk diversification, especially in industries that face high market uncertainty and less flexible market conditions.

One recent study examining vertical integration in the telecom industry [5] discusses the strategies of a vertically integrated ISP and a pure-play CP (which is not vertically integrated) when there are multiple ISPs and CPs. The authors find that, depending on its market share, the ISP could reap benefits by delivering the CP’s contents, while the CP could increase its profit by enabling the ISP to deliver contents. The observation of mutual benefits arising from the interactions between the two diferent entities in this study aligns with our own findings and provides new insights into the special arrangement between an ISP and a CP that exists in our model.

## Sponsored Data

Following AT&T’s January 2014 introduction of sponsored data (also called zero-rating), which allows CPs to subsidize their consumers’ Internet connectivity, researchers from various fields have examined the potential impacts of sponsored data on CPs and consumers as well as ISPs [6, 22, 29, 36]. Wang et al. [42], for example, developed a twolevel Stackelberg game (multi-leader-multi-follower game) to describe the interactions between multiple content/service providers (leaders) and a large population of consumers (followers) in a non-cooperative manner. They found that when the service provider imposed a low subscription fee, CPs were more likely to provide full sponsorship to attract more consumers. However, if the fee increased, CPs were observed to reduce and ultimately end subsidies because their revenue could not cover the costs arising from the sponsorship. Even though they stopped the sponsorship, CPs still realized positive revenue because consumers needed to choose at least one of the CPs. Lotfi et al. [26], meanwhile, examined the impact of sponsored data within a non-neutral network by applying sequential and bargaining game frameworks. Their study assumed that the payof of an ISP is determined by two factors—monetary value and consumer satisfaction—since the demand of end-users depends on service quality as well as the amount of sponsored data. The researchers found that if a content provider’s consumers were sensitive to quality issues, but the ISP and CP were short-sighted in their strategy, the CP would decide not to sponsor. However, if the data resources in the market increased (i.e., if the ISP can deliver more data through its network), a CP operating under the same conditions cited above might choose to sponsor a large amount of resources since the ISP charges a lower sponsoring fee to the CP.

Other researchers considered the impact of data caps in situations where sponsored data is allowed. Data caps impose more restrictive conditions on consumers, forcing them to pay high overage fees or to seek to cut back on data usage. Wood [44] pointed out that the use of sponsored data in conjunction with a data cap would efectively double-charge consumers; moreover, the cap is not reasonable from a network-management perspective because it does not contribute to alleviating trafic congestion. Mei et al. [29], meanwhile, claimed that an ISP could maximize its profit by changing the data cap and imposing a fixed upfront fee and unit transportation cost under diferent sponsored data plans. If no sponsoring data were allowed, they found, the ISP could maximize its profit by charging only the unit transportation cost with no data cap. If the ISP allowed either one CP or both CPs to sponsor its consumers, the ISP’s optimal strategy would be to charge two diferent fees—a fixed up-front fee and unit transportation cost—with no data cap.

The aforementioned papers and research articles rigorously examine the impacts that vertical integration or sponsored data (including data on the Internet network) can exert on businesses with limited resources. However, only a few of them discuss the simultaneous and combined impact of vertical integration and sponsored data, both of which comprise viable approaches for resolving problems arising from limited resources.

In this article, we propose a comprehensive framework for an ISP’s network management options by considering the dynamics of vertical integration and sponsored data simultaneously. Current Internet service industry trends show that ISPs are increasingly seeking to merge with CPs, which in turn are willing to afiliate themselves with ISPs; at the same time, some of these merged entities are controlling the content that reaches their consumers. Keeping in mind such recent tendencies, we identify the conditions under which the ISP’s network management decisions would result in a maximization of their profits. However, any such decision by the ISPs might have detrimental efects on the consumers or the society as a whole. Accordingly, this study on sponsored data within a vertical integration framework seeks to establish criteria under which policymakers should regulate ISPs in order to maximize social welfare while also considering the potential benefits for consumers.

## Internet Service Provider

We assume that the monopolist ISP covers the complete market and delivers digital content from a CP to consumers by charging a usage-based price, ${ \boldsymbol { p } } _ { I }$ . This assumption about the monopolist ISP is based on examples from real-world situations. While there are many providers for Internet access, we can often observe geographical monopolies in terms of Internet services. For example, customers in many geographical zones of the United States, specifically suburban and rural areas, currently have access to only one wired ISP option [4]. Indeed, many ofice and residential buildings in urban areas operate under the same limitation [33]. We also assume that the ISP can acquire a CP (from among two CPs) as a subsidiary. If it merges with the CP, the relation between the ISP and the subsidiary CP is an example of vertical integration. Specifically, we assume that the ISP vertically integrates with only one CP (either $C P _ { T }$ or $C P _ { N } )$ . There are two reasons why this assumption is reasonable: first, vertical integration is a common strategy that two relatively less competitive companies can pursue as they seek to gain an edge over a more competitive company. For example, the vertical integration of AT&T and Time Warner withstood a state-led legal challenge because the companies claimed, successfully, that the merger would allow them to compete with web giants such as Facebook and Google. Combining with more than one CP would almost inevitably invite antitrust suits from the government. Secondly, for the purposes of our research, we can observe how the vertical integration and zero-rated content would afect the ISP’s profit if it considered integrating with one of two possible CPs while allowing the CPs to ofer zero-rated contents. Consequently, we believe that our paper will facilitate discussions for examples in the real world, and would be of interest to academicians, regulators, and practitioners alike.

## Content Provider

The CPs in our model compete to reach out to consumers. As in prior literature, we assume that the digital contents ofered by the diferent CPs are nearly identical [5]. Furthermore, based on the findings of previous studies [28, 30], we diferentiate the two competing CPs in the following two aspects: 1) revenue generation schemes (ad revenue vs. consumer subscription fee revenue) and 2) commercial interruptions (i.e., adsupported content and ad-free content).<sup>3</sup> Typically, CPs do not charge the consumers directly and generate revenue by selling ads from merchants who want to reach these consumers at a price that is related to the number of the CPs’ subscribers or to the CPs’ performances [16, 32]; the revenue from the ads, meanwhile, depends on the revenue generation rate, r, and the volume of contents delivered to consumers, λ [11]. Accordingly, the number of consumers of CPs is an important factor in generating revenue. Moreover, CPs may seek to attract more consumers by ofering them free content with commercial ads [13]. Along the same lines, the merger between AT&T and Time Warner was initiated after the latter sought to place ads in front of a broader audience with the ultimate aim of generating more ad revenue. In other words, through the merger, Time Warner intends to access consumer data and sell more targeted (personalized) ads to provide free or costefective content because, as the number of Pay-TV consumers declines, high-priced targeted ads can compensate for the decreased revenue from the Pay-TV users [34, 39]. In particular, according to the digital team at AT&T, sales prices for data-driven ads (which are more targeted and more lucrative ads) are 60 percent higher than those for demographic-driven ads based on geographic information [8].

However, in today’s content market, people are more likely to pay subscription fees for the ad-free content ofered by CPs such as Netflix and Amazon as consumers grow increasingly impatient with commercial interruptions [41]. This lower tolerance for interruptions was a major factor in Apple’s decision to launch its own ad-free content subscription service [43]. However, even though consumers of ad-free CPs prefer viewing content without commercials, they still have high price sensitivity. For example, when Netflix increased monthly subscription fees by \$3, 33 percent of its subscribers downgraded their services, and 10 percent canceled their subscriptions outright [35]. This type of price-sensitivity led Hulu to ofer both ad-supported and ad-free plans, with 70 percent of its customers sticking with the ad-supported plan [40].

In sum, the current digital content market is led by two types of CPs. The first type generates its revenue from ad sales, with revenue levels determined by the number of viewers. The other type obtains revenue from subscription fees charged to consumers.

## Consumers

In our model, we assume that the end consumers are charged an Internet access cost to access digital content. More specifically, the Internet access cost is determined by the multiplication of the usage-based price per packet, $\displaystyle { \mathit { p } } _ { I { : } }$ , by the consumer’s data usage, λ.

Our model assumes a unit mass of consumers, who decide whether to access the content based on their net utilities. The consumer makes this decision by considering her own utility as it relates to 1) the Internet access cost paid to the ISP in order to use digital content from CPs; 2) the subscription fee paid to $C P _ { N } ;$ and 3) the deviation from their “ideal” content on a Hotelling line (which is parametrized as the fit cost t) [19]. Since we also assume that the consumers are heterogeneous to the CPs (which provide similar content), consumers are uniformly distributed on a line segment between 0 and 1, with the assumption that one $\mathrm { C P } \ ( C P _ { T } )$ is located at 0 and the other $( C P _ { N } )$ at 1.

Therefore, if a consumer accesses content from $C P _ { T }$ , the utility function for an arbitrary consumer is derived as follows: $U _ { T } = V - t x - p _ { I } \lambda + I p _ { I } \lambda _ { T }$ . Meanwhile, the utility function for an arbitrary consumer who accesses the content of $C P _ { N }$ is expressed as $U _ { N } =$ $V + V _ { A } - t ( 1 - x ) - p _ { I } \lambda - p _ { N } \lambda + I p _ { I } \lambda _ { N }$ (because a customer of $C P _ { N }$ must pay the subscription fee in addition to the Internet access cost in order to consume digital content). Since some consumers tend to value the convenience of ad-free content [9, 41, 43] and because the customers of $C P _ { N }$ are likely to similarly value ad-free services, we introduce the term $V _ { A }$ in the utility function of a consumer of $C P _ { N }$ in order to capture the additional value derived from ad-free convenience.<sup>4</sup> For the utility function, we use the indicator (I) in the function based on whether the ISP decides to allow $C P _ { T }$ or $C P _ { N }$ to ofer zero-rated content to their customers. In practice, ISPs generally allow CPs – specifically subsidiaries CPs – to fully sponsor their consumers if ISPs decide to allow CPs to ofer zero-rated content. We assume, therefore, without loss of generality, that the amount of data that $C P _ { T }$ or $C P _ { N }$ sponsors for their consumers is the same as the consumers’ data usage (full sponsorship): $\lambda _ { T } = \lambda _ { N } = \lambda$ . In our extended model, we relax the assumption by enabling CPs to partially sponsor their consumers’ usage $( 0 \leq \lambda _ { T } , \ \lambda _ { N } \leq \lambda )$

## Introducing Four Cases of Vertical Integration with Zero-rating

The ISP determines whether it will let the CPs ofer zero-rated content to their consumers and whether it will vertically integrate with $C P _ { T }$ . The CPs, meanwhile, continue to ofer their content, which their consumers can access through the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ network. Figure 1 presents the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ four network management options, which are developed by considering both the vertical integration between the ISP and $C P _ { T }$ and the zero-rated content. Among the four options, the ISP will choose the option that maximizes its profit.

Case 1 describes a baseline scenario wherein a monopoly ISP plays a role as an independent entity that delivers digital content from CPs to consumers. In addition, the ISP does not allow any CPs to ofer zero-rated content to their consumers. In other words, consumers need to pay the ISP for Internet connectivity (and optionally subscription fees) in order to access digital content.

Cases 2, 3, and 4 reflect diferent scenarios in the zero-rating realm, mirroring diferent possibilities in the media landscape after a merger like the one between AT&T and Time Warner. In this setup, AT&T charges the content delivery cost (i.e., the Internet connectivity cost, from the perspective of CPs and consumers) to either CPs or consumers; Time Warner, meanwhile, generates its revenue mainly through advertising rather than consumer subscription fees. In developing our model, we assume that an ISP merges with a CP that generates its revenue through ad sales (as in the AT&T-Time Warner merger) and follow the pricing strategies that mirror that reality.

The primary diference between Cases 2 through 4 is the ISP’s strategy as it relates to zero-rated content. Cases 2 and 3 are similar to current ISP pricing strategies: the ISP in Case 2 allows only its subsidiary CP $( C P _ { T } )$ to ofer zero-rated content, while the ISP in Case 3 allows both CPs to do so. Case 4 is a potential setup that ofers interesting insights into the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ future business direction, even though an analogous case does not yet exist in the real world. Here, the ISP allows only its rival CP $( C P _ { N } )$ —which is not its subsidiary and generates its revenue through subscription fees—to ofer zero-rated content.

As a further comparison to Case 4, we propose a Case 5, in which the ISP merges with $C P _ { N }$ rather than $C P _ { T } ,$ , despite the low probability of such an occurrence. In the real world, $C P _ { N }$ outcompetes $C P _ { T }$ and thus has less incentive to merge with the ISP. The ISP may also be reluctant to acquire $C P _ { N }$ as its subsidiary since $C P _ { N }$ (e.g., online behemoths such as Netflix and Amazon) is likely to have a much larger market capitalization than that of $C P _ { T }$ and the ISP may have limited funds (such a merger would also invite heavier scrutiny from regulators on anti-competitive grounds). However, since a prospective merger between the ISP and the more competitive $C P _ { N }$ could provide fertile ground for future research, we submit Case 5 as a thought experiment that complements our model.

## Game-Theoretical Model for Analyzing the Cases

To analyze the five diferent cases, we develop a game-theoretical model with three stages. The sequence of decisions is illustrated in Figure 2.

In the first stage, the ISP announces the Internet access fee, $\displaystyle { \mathit { p } } _ { I { : } }$ , to the consumers and the CPs (who may want to sponsor data). Since it provides its own network, the ISP, in order to maximize its profits, would determine whether or not to permit the CPs to ofer zero-rated content to their consumers. In the next stage, $C P _ { N }$ announces its subscription fee $\left( { p _ { N } } \right)$ to its consumers that maximizes its own profit, after taking into account the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ decision on zero-rated content. Finally, in stage 3, after observing the respective decisions of the ISP and the CP, consumers choose between the two competing CPs.

In order to deduce the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) of the player’s actions in each stage, we use backward induction from Stage 3 to Stage 1. In Stage 3, consumers choose their preferred CP by considering the Internet connectivity price $\left( { p _ { I } } \right)$ the subscription fee $\left( { p _ { N } } \right)$ incurred by choosing $C P _ { N }$ , and the ofering of zero-rated content, as well as their intrinsic preference for each CP. The consumers’ decisions can be expressed by the marginal consumer $x ^ { * }$ who is indiferent between $C P _ { T }$ and $C P _ { N }$ . In other words, the consumers located in $[ 0 , x ^ { * } ]$ choose $C P _ { T }$ and the consumers located in $[ x ^ { * } , \ 1 ]$ choose $C P _ { N }$ . In the equilibrium, the demand for each CP is $D _ { T } = x ^ { * }$ , and $D _ { N } =$ $1 - x ^ { * }$ . ## Extended Model III: Diferent Internet Connectivity Prices for Consumers and CPs

Extended Model III discusses pricing schemes by considering the balance of bargaining power that exists between ISP and its rival CP, $C P _ { N }$ . If $C P _ { N }$ has suficient bargaining power over the ISP, it will negotiate the Internet connectivity price with the ISP in order to pay a lower price than the consumers. Ostensibly, the ideal scenario for $C P _ { N }$ would be to pay no connectivity price for zero-rated content. However, in the real world, ISPs configure their price schemes based on zero-rated content and decide whether or not to allow specific CPs to ofer such content. We illustrate this possibility by assuming that the ISP charges $C P _ { N }$ half the price paid by consumers $( p _ { Z } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ , where $\hbar z$ is the Internet connectivity price for $C P _ { N } )$

We can also consider an alternative scenario concerning the bargaining power between the ISP and $C P _ { N }$ by assuming that the ISP has the bargaining edge over $C P _ { N }$ and that the

ISP charges $C P _ { N }$ a higher Internet connectivity price than it charges consumers. In this case, the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ ideal scenario would involve charging $C P _ { N }$ the highest possible connectivity price for zero-rated content. However, once again, in the real world, ISPs must settle on an afordable price to charge CPs so that the latter can ofer zero-rated content to their consumers. We illustrate this possibility by assuming that the ISP charges $C P _ { N }$ 1.5 times the price paid by consumers $( p _ { Z } = \frac { 3 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ , where $\hbar z$ is the Internet connectivity price for $C P _ { N } )$

## Lemma 5 (Impacts of Price Changes Related to Bargaining Power)

If the ISP charges its rival CP a price (either higher or lower) that difers from the price it charges consumers, the price diferential afects the $I S P { ^ { \circ } s }$ profit, consumer surplus, and social welfare in Case 4 (where the ISP allows only its rival CP to ofer zero-rated content).

In Case 4, if the balance of bargaining power between the ISP and $C P _ { N }$ results in the ISP charging $C P _ { N }$ a price that difers from the price it charges consumers $\left( { p _ { I } } \right)$ (i.e., either ${ \scriptstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } } p _ { I }$ or ${ \scriptstyle { \frac { 3 } { 2 } } } p _ { I } )$ , these diferences in pricing, in turn, afect consumer surplus and social welfare as well as the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ profit. These diferences in pricing do not afect the other cases. In Cases 1 and 2, $C P _ { N }$ does not pay the Internet connectivity price to the ISP because $C P _ { N }$ does not ofer zero-rated content. Thus, the diferent prices do not result in any changes from the original setup of the primary model in Cases 1 and 2. In Case 3 of the extended model, depending on the price that it charges $C P _ { N } ( \mathrm { e i t h e r } \frac { 1 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ or ${ \frac { 3 } { 2 } } p _ { I } )$ , the ISP changes its Internet connectivity price accordingly and then generates the same amount of its profit as it does in Case 3 of the primary model. In line with the $\mathrm { I S P } { \boldsymbol { s } }$ changing prices and unchanged profits, the values for consumer surplus and social welfare in the extended models are the same as those in the primary model (additional details about these comparisons are provided in Online Supplemental Appendix 5).

In Case 4, the ISP can consider the sum of the profit derived from both Internet connectivity fees paid by $C P _ { T } { ^ { \circ } } s$ consumers and from $\boldsymbol { C P _ { N } } ^ { \mathrm { ~ ~ } }$ s zero-rated content. Our analysis shows that, depending on the fit cost (t) and revenue generation rate (r), the ISP can generate higher or lower profits than it does in Case 4 of the primary model by charging $C P _ { N }$ diferent prices. However, if we compare the two diferent pricing schemes in the extended model $( p _ { Z } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { Z } = \frac { 3 } { 2 } p _ { I } ) } \end{array}$ , charging $C P _ { N }$ the higher price $( p _ { Z } = \frac { 3 } { 2 } p _ { I } )$ generates higher profits for the ISP and lower consumer surplus than charging it the lower price $( p _ { Z } = { \textstyle \frac { 1 } { 2 } } p _ { I } )$ . In terms of social welfare, since the higher ISP profit in the scenario where $p _ { Z } = \frac { 3 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ impacts social welfare more than the consumer surplus, the scenario where $p _ { Z } = \frac { 3 } { 2 } p _ { I }$ produces greater gains in social welfare than the scenario where $p _ { Z } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } p _ { I }$

## Extended Model IV: Fixed Prices for Consumers and Variable Prices for CPs

In the extended model IV, we assume that the ISP charges consumers a fixed price (F) (i.e., flat-rate pricing) while charging CPs a variable price (i.e., pay-per-data). Since the data usage of some consumers does not exceed the data limit, they would simply pay the fixed price with no additional charges. More specifically, if consumers use zero-rated content, they do not need to pay the fixed price to the ISP. However, the CPs, which ofer the zero-rated content, must pay the ISP the variable price according to the data usage of consumers.

## Lemma 6 (Comparisons between Fixed Prices and Pay-per-Data for Consumers)

When the ISP changes its pricing scheme by charging its consumers a fixed price for Internet connectivity and charging the CPs a pay-per-data price for zero-rated content, the changed pricing scheme increases the ISP’s profit if consumers’ data usage is high in Case 4 (where the ISP allows only its rival CP to ofer zero-rated content).

Our analysis shows that a fixed price for consumers afects the ISP’s profit in only Case 4. In Cases 1, 2, and 3, the fixed price for the consumer in the extended model is the same as the amount charged to consumers in the primary model under the pay-per-data plan $( F = p _ { I } \lambda )$ ). However, in Case 4, the ISP charges $C P _ { T } { ^ { \circ } } s$ consumers the fixed price and $C P _ { N } { ' } s$ consumers the variable price for zero-rated content. By comparing Case 4 in the extended model with Case 4 in the primary model, we see that if consumers use more data, more consumers will choose $C P _ { T }$ rather than $C P _ { N }$ under the fixed pricing scheme. The results imply that if the consumers pay a fixed price for a relatively large amount of data, they have less incentive to choose the CP that ofers zero-rated content. The ISP, in turn, generates more revenue from the increased market share of $C P _ { T }$ . However, if consumers use less data, $C P _ { T }$ would cede some of its market share, and the ISP would generate less revenue from its subsidiary. Further details of the analysis and results appear in Online Supplemental Appendix 6.

## Discussion

Despite a U.S. federal court’s approval of the historic merger of AT&T and Time Warner, the vertical integration between ISPs and CPs remains a controversial topic. One reason for the ongoing debate stems from the inherent nature of vertical integration. It is clear that a horizontal merger would likely inhibit competition between players in the same business sector and harm consumers due to the resulting increase in prices [3, 8, 15]. However, there is no clear evidence showing that vertical integration of two entities practicing their trade in complementary business sectors would cause the same efects as a horizontal merger. In addition to vertical mergers between ISPs and CPs, ISPs have recently introduced zero-rating, which is a new payment scheme for CPs and consumers that reflects shifting consumer preferences, both in terms of their increasing consumption of high-definition videos, as well as their desire to spend less for their online access. Zero-rating schemes from ISPs allow CPs to ofer their content to consumers without increasing the latter’s budget for online access.

Taking into account the rapidly evolving business dynamics of vertical integration and zero-rated contents, we develop a game-theoretic model to determine the best strategy for ISPs that will maximize their profits, as well as the resultant consumer surplus and social welfare efects of such a strategy. In order to simplify the model and consider real-world business structures, we assume that the players include a monopoly ISP, two CPs $( C P _ { T }$ and $C P _ { N } )$ that have diferent pricing strategies for generating revenue, and consumers. We study the efect of the vertical integration between the ISP and one of the CPs, as well as the influence of zerorated content, which is one potentially applicable price mechanism of the vertical integration. In our primary model, we assume that, ceteris paribus, consumers prefer ad-free digital content: in other words, consumers put a positive valuation for ad-free content. Our results show that the ISP will always choose to vertically integrate with a CP. Surprisingly, however, the consumer valuation of the ad-free content significantly afects how the ISP will decide to maximize its profit. Moreover, the deviations between the $\mathrm { I S P } ^ { \prime } s$ strategies for profit maximization strategy and for maximizing consumer surplus or social welfare carry significant policy implications for telecom regulators since it will be incumbent upon them to decide how to implement zero-rated content when they approve of a vertical merger [7, 21, 45]. We augment our analysis by considering partial data sponsorship by the CPs and diferent Internet connectivity prices for consumers and the CPs. There are several potential ways to expand upon our research. Since CPs like Netflix or Apple are already active in producing their own content [43], future analyses can consider the implications of a CP producing its own content in the context of vertical integration and zero-rating. Another potential area of research is the horizontal integration between wired-ISPs and wireless ISPs (such as AT&T U-verse and AT&T’s wireless service) in addition to the vertical integration between an ISP and CPs.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze the economic impacts stemming from the vertical integration of an ISP with a CP based on the real-world example of the AT&T-Time Warner merger and the ensuing business practices and debates that arose in the wake of that integration. Among the various theoretical implications of our paper, we developed a game-theoretic model to demonstrate that vertical integration can be jointly analyzed with zero-rating in order to analyze real-world phenomena and business practices, such as consumer valuation for ad-free content, the disparate levels of bargaining power between an ISP and the CPs, and an ISP’s various pricing schemes. Our modeling and analysis procedures may also prove applicable to developing and expanding analytical models based on other real-world issues in the rapidly-changing landscape of accessing online content.

Our findings also have important managerial implications. First, if an ISP considers vertical integration with a CP, it must consider various external factors, such as consumer valuation for ad-free content and revenue generation rates. For example, our results show that an ISP’s preferred strategies will change depending on whether consumer valuation for ad-free content is high or low. While our paper assumes that the valuation variable is exogenously determined, it could be considered as a decision variable in future research. Second, we identify the deviations from the ISP’s preferred strategies and those that would maximize consumer surplus and social welfare. The results of our analysis should be useful to policymakers to develop regulation that would incentivize ISPs to consider not only their own profit but the welfare of consumers and other participants (e.g., CPs) as well.
