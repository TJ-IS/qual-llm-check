Abs tr act: This research examines the role of diversification on incumbent firms response to the threat of new entry. When faced with threats posed by new technologies, incumbent firms in the information technology (IT) industry can either perform research and development (R&D), or acquire the new entrants who are successful at innovating. We use a two-stage game-theoretic framework to model the relation between diversification and the decision to acquire versus perform R&D. We also collect data on financial indicators for firms in the IT industry using the Compustat database to empirically test the propositions from the analytical model. Our results suggest that firms with a higher degree of diversification are more likely to innovate through acquisition than through R&D. Moreover, diversification has a positive effect on investment in acquisitions, as well as a negative effect on investment in R&D.

How to r esp ond apr opr iately to the p otential thr eat posed by new entrants is a perennial dilemma facing incumbent information technology (IT) firms. While this threat exists for incumbents in all industries, it is more pronounced in the IT industry for several reasons, including the fast pace of technological change, short product life cycles, low cost of entry, and technical and market uncertainty surrounding new innovation. The fast pace of technological change can lead to the development of new products that pose a threat to existing products. The low cost of entry in IT industries makes it feasible for start-ups (e.g., Cisco Systems and Google) with intellectual capital to emerge as industry leaders in a short time. Innovations in IT are also characterized by technical and market-based uncertainty. Technical uncertainty arises due to concerns about the feasibility of the new technology, and market uncertainty arises from the lack of knowledge about whether customers will ultimately prefer the product [28]. As Christensen [13] points out, these factors can cause incumbent firms to be displaced from their market leadership position by new start-ups with disruptive technologies. When facing this dilemma, incumbent firms can respond in the following ways: perform research and development (R&D), acquire other firms, or both [10]. While prior research has mainly focused on the antecedents and consequences of R&D and acquisition separately, little research discusses how firms decide between choosing acquisition versus R&D as the preferred response to the threat of new entry.

Both R&D and acquisition strategies are common in the IT industry. Firms can pursue innovations internally—that is, they can invest in R&D and launch new products in the market. For example, Intel’s line of microprocessors has been the staple of the personal computer (PC) industry for over two decades. Intel conducted R&D to build the Atom processor in 2008, which became immensely successful in the netbook market (one of the fastest-growing segments in the PC industry in 2008–9). Firms such as Cogent (which provides fingerprint and biometric solutions) and Maxim (which designs and manufactures linear and mixed-signal integrated systems) innovate primarily through investments in R&D. An alternative strategy that firms can follow is to strategically acquire other companies. By acquiring new entrants, incumbents can gain access to the new technologies and the market opportunities these may generate. As Veugelers and Cassiman [48] suggest, acquiring can lead to time gains, lower technology and market acquisition costs, and access to specialized knowledge. Firms such as Cisco have been highly successful in leveraging acquisitions as a competitive strategy. For example, in the early 1990s when Cisco realized that customers started preferring switches to routers, it acquired switch-maker Crescendo. Since then, Cisco has completed dozens of acquisitions to enter new markets or to complement its existing products [32]. Similarly, Google acquired a small start-up named Writely, which enabled it to the launch its online office suite GoogleDocs. Incumbents can also acquire a small stake in an entrant initially, and later decide to do a full acquisition. For example, in 1996, Microsoft invested in WebTV, which makes devices that display the Internet on a television. When the idea caught on with television viewers, Microsoft bought the rest of the firm [44].

In this paper, we examine the decision of a firm to invest in R&D versus acquisitions in the context of firm diversification. Anecdotal evidence suggests that the number of markets that a firm operates in (or diversification) is likely to be a key factor in determining its innovation strategy. Less diversified firms, which focus on a narrow product line, mainly invest in R&D, whereas more diversified companies, which offer a broad product range, mainly follow a strategy of investing in acquisitions. For example, firms such as Intel and Lexmark are based around a core product and most of their revenues are tied to that product. Both firms innovate mainly through investments in R&D. Intel invests heavily in R&D primarily aimed at its microprocessor line of products. This enables Intel to be in a better position to predict technological changes and adapt its products accordingly. Baysinger and Hoskisson [6] suggest that R&D intensity is higher for dominant business firms than diversified firms. On the other hand, Oracle has a broad product portfolio based around several technologies such as database systems, middleware, and enterprise systems, and primarily follows an acquisition strategy. VeriSign, which mainly provides Internet infrastructure services but also has a diverse product portfolio ranging from Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) certificates, domain name services, and enterprise security, spends around three times more on acquisitions than on R&D.

Therefore, while anecdotal evidence and past literature point to a link between diversification and mode of innovation followed by the firm, there has been little research on how diversification relates to the investments made by firms in acquisitions relative to R&D. With this motivation, we propose our main research question: How does the extent of diversification impact the innovation strategy of a firm? In other words, are diversified firms more likely to invest in acquisition, or are they more likely to invest in R&D?

To answer this question, we propose an analytical framework where an incumbent decides between acquiring a start-up or investing in its own R&D in a two-stage game-theoretic model. Our main result is that the extent of diversification is positively related to the probability of a firm choosing acquisition over R&D as a mode of innovation. The model also yields the intuitive result that the incumbent is more likely to invest in R&D when consumers prefer the new technology over existing products in the market. We also test the predictions of our analytical model by collecting data on the relevant financial indicators for IT firms in the United States from the Compustat database. Our regression analysis provides empirical support for our proposition that the degree of diversification is positively associated with the probability of a firm choosing acquisition over R&D. Our results also suggest that unrelated diversification is associated with a higher propensity to acquire, and that related diversification has no effect on this choice. We also estimate a simultaneous equation model with R&D and acquisitions as endogenous variables, and find that diversification has a positive effect on investment in acquisitions and a negative effect on investment in R&D. Finally, our results indicate that R&D and acquisitions are complements in IT firms—that is, higher investments in R&D are associated with higher investments in acquisitions.

## Literature Review

The imp act of R&D on firm p er form ance is a well-researched topic [20]. The learning literature identifies an important benefit of R&D to be the fact that it helps firms to develop the absorptive capacity to generate new knowledge [14]. A firm’s absorptive capacity for learning, however, depends on its endowment of relevant technology-based capabilities [35]. R&D investment is the necessary condition for the creation of absorptive capacity [46]. The knowledge management literature suggests that innovation occurs when a firm identifies the potential opportunities to fill the gaps in the industry positioning map, such as new customer segments, new customer needs, or new production methods [30]. Therefore, efficient knowledge exchange among functions internal and external to the firm is critically important to success [34]. Related research [42] also suggests that R&D is the key to developing new knowledge within a firm.

One of the main reasons for diversification by firms is the availability of slack resources [39]. When slack resources become available, a firm is likely to go the acquisition route [45]. Acquisitions become possible when nonowners of an asset value it more than the owners [19], and usually involve an “acquirer with a high asset valuation purchasing a target with a low asset valuation” [40]. Blonigen and Taylor [10] study whether acquisition and R&D are positively or negatively related in high-tech industries in the United States. While the decision to pursue innovation through acquisition versus R&D seems like an “either or” decision, in reality the two may be complementary [4]. For example, the theory of absorptive capacity [14] predicts that firms that have an internal knowledge base will be in a better position to absorb external knowledge generated through acquisitions. Such firms are also in a better position to adapt external knowledge gained through acquisitions to local conditions [15]. Cassiman and Veugelers [11] provide empirical evidence of complementarities between R&D and external acquisitions by measuring the impact of different innovation strategies on new product sales. While prior literature has examined the determinants of R&D [21] and acquisitions [10] separately, we know of no prior work where these are jointly estimated. Moreover, prior research has also not examined how diversification relates to the probability to investing in acquisitions versus R&D.

Research has also examined competition between incumbents and new entrants in the context of firm diversification. One of the common strategies that new entrants follow when competing with incumbents is targeting the “blind spot” of the market leaders. “Blind spot” is defined as the flaws that can result from a company’s mistaken or incomplete view of the industry and competition [52]. It is conceivable that a firm that operates in many different markets has a bigger blind spot than firms that operate in a single market. Acs and Audretsch [1] suggest that innovations are likely to come from smaller firms in industry sectors with a low concentration and fast-changing technology. For example, the threat of new entrants can come to Cisco in any of the 16 product markets that it operates in. In such scenarios, incumbent players are often blindsided by entrants who introduce products to occupy the incumbents’ blind spot because the incumbents fail to anticipate all possible threats. Once new entrants are successful in launching a new product ahead of the incumbent, the incumbent can respond by licensing the new technology or acquiring the new entrant [18].

In management information systems (MIS) literature, Nault and Vandenbosch [36] studied competition between an incumbent and entrant in IT industries on the optimal timing of new product introduction. Liu et al. [27] modeled competition in IT industries in the presence of network externalities, and tested their hypotheses using data from the flash memory markets. Researchers have also examined other issues related to IT-producing industries such as returns to human capital for IT professionals [3], and the impact of training on firm performance among software firms [33]. Our research extends the work of Nault and Vandenbosch [36] by examining how diversification is related to the mode of innovation followed by incumbent IT firms when faced with threat of new entry. Our paper also contributes to the discussion on “make versus buy” in the strategic management literature [11] by studying the choice of a firm to invest in R&D versus acquisitions in an analytical model, as well as performing rigorous empirical analysis to validate the same.

## Analytical Model

We cons id er an incumb ent comp eting in n product markets against a competitive fringe composed of m firms. The incumbent is considering entering a new market. This may be achieved via on of two means: explicit R&D efforts undertaken by the incumbent or the acquisition of an entrant that successfully performed the requisite innovation. Furthermore, the incumbent may obtain a stake in the entrant as an alternative to performing R&D. The benefits of obtaining a stake are twofold, reflecting potential synergies between the entrant and the incumbent. First, the incumbent learns inside information about the entrant. Second, the incumbent can steer the entrant toward research and strategic areas that are of greater interest to the incumbent. In the analysis below, we let a “hat” above a variable denote the payoff to the incumbent contingent on having a stake. The timing of the game is as follows. The incumbent decides in the first stage of the game whether to perform R&D at cost R or obtain a stake in the entrant at cost S. In the second stage of the game, the incumbent decides whether or not to acquire the entrant at cost E, which is the same regardless of having a stake or not.

If the R&D cost R exceeds the stake cost S, then obtaining a stake strictly dominates performing R&D because, all other things being equal, the incumbent is more profitable with a stake in the entrant. Thus, to render the game nontrivial, we assume

$$
S > R.\tag{A.1}
$$

Firms compete in monopolistic fashion, which has the following features [49, ch. 6.4]. First, demand is differentiated and derived from consumers being endowed with constant elasticity of substitution (CES) preferences, specified below. This enables the consumer to have differentiated demand across a collection of products. An advantage of this specification is that the demand function is effectively log-linear, whereas with Bertrand or Cournot models of differentiated competition, demand is typically restricted to be linear to ensure tractability (which is less realistic from an empirical standpoint). A second feature of monopolistic competition is that each firm has a monopoly in its own product market, thereby choosing the price (or quantity) of its product subject to a downward-sloping demand function (generated from CES preferences). However, consumers take into account that the product offered is substitutable with the products being offered by all other monopolists (the elasticity of substitution determining the degree to which they are substitutable). Furthermore, consumer demand for the entire market is determined by their income and willingness to pay. By contrast, with differentiated Cournot or Bertrand competition, there is no direct link between the demand for one product versus another. The pricing policy of a firm affects the demand for its product and that of its competitors.

Our demand framework is built on the work of Dixit and Stiglitz [17] and Benassy [7], the latter of which extends the Dixit–Stiglitz framework to endow consumers with a taste for variety. The innovation component of our model is entirely new. The closest innovation framework to ours is due to Aghion and Howitt [2] and Romer [41], who model endogenous macroeconomic growth as a process by which new products are invented as the result of an explicit R&D endeavor. Such models endow consumers either with a preference for variety (as we have) or a preference for higher quality.

## Basic Model

Sup os e ther e ar e n products in the market, the demand for which stems from a representative consumer. Let $Q _ { i , n }$ denote the consumer’s demand for product i, and $P _ { i , n }$ the product price, for $i = 1 , . . . , n$ . The consumer has CES preferences $U _ { _ n } = ( \Sigma _ { _ { i = 1 } } ^ { n } Q _ { i , n } ^ { \theta } ) ^ { 1 / \theta }$ where $\theta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ . With these preferences, the elasticity of substitution across any pair of products is $\sigma = 1 / ( 1 - \theta )$ , which lies in the range $\sigma \in \left( 1 , \infty \right)$ . The consumer allocates the income $Y _ { _ n }$ toward consumption. In the spirit of Benassy [7], consumers have a taste for variety. The greater the number of products available to purchase, the more utility is derived by the consumer from consumption. In our framework, this effect is manifested by an increase in the allotment by the consumer toward expenditures in response to an increase in the number of products; thus, $Y _ { \mathfrak { n } }$ is increasing in the span n of the market: $Y _ { n } = n ^ { \nu } Y$ , where $\nu > 0$ represents the extent to which the consumer values variety.

This specification endowing consumers with a taste for variety stems from quasilinear utility of the type studied in Varian [47]. An increase in income originating from an enhancement of the product portfolio is technically equivalent to an increase in utility (by virtue of an enhanced product portfolio) while holding constant the consumer’s disposable income. This equivalence is proved in Mas‑C olell et al. [31, ch. 2].

To reflect the fact that a stake enhances the payoff to the incumbent, let ${ \hat { Y } } _ { n } = n ^ { \nu } { \hat { Y } }$ denote the consumer’s expenditures when the incumbent has a stake in the entrant, where the premium

$$
\hat {Y} > Y\tag{A.2}
$$

reflects the synergies enjoyed by the incumbent and entrant as manifested in the enhanced consumer demand. In practice, synergies are also reflected in terms of reduced costs. However, for ease of exposition, we solely consider the case in which synergies lead to enhanced demand.

The consumer solves the problem

$$
\max _ {\left\{Q _ {i, n} \right\} _ {i = 1} ^ {n}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {i, n} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {1 / \theta} \text {   subject   to   } \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P _ {i, n} Q _ {i, n} \leq Y _ {n}.\tag{1}

$$

$$

where $\lambda _ { _ n }$ is the Lagrange multiplier.

$$

$$

is a price index.

We infer that $\mathfrak { E } = 1 / ( 1 - \theta )$ is the price elasticity of demand under the standard Dixit–Stiglitz assumption that the price index ${ \bar { P } } _ { n }$ is taken as given by the producers of each product line. Because $\theta \in ( 0 , 1 )$ , we find that $\mathfrak { E } \in ( 1 , \infty )$ , implying that demand is elastic. Thus, the larger is θ , the greater is the ease with which the consumer substitutes one product for another, and the more responsive is the demand for a product to changes in its price (holding $\bar { P } _ { { } _ { n } }$ constant).

The demand for the new product is unit elastic. As before, $\mathfrak { E } = 1 / ( 1 - \theta )$ is the price elasticity of demand of existing products, assuming producers take the price index as given. The demand for the new product does not depend on the demand for existing products. Furthermore, the demand for an existing product does not depend on the demand for the new product. Existing products and the new product are linked only via the budget constraint of the consumer. This follows from the fact that the elasticity of substitution is one between the new product and the collection of existing products, which in turn follows from the Cobb–Douglas relationship between the new product and the collection of existing products.

The consumer spends the share $1 - \alpha$ of his or her budget on existing products, $\Sigma _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } P _ { _ { i , n + 1 } } Q _ { _ { i , n + 1 } } = ( 1 - \alpha ) Y _ { _ { n + 1 } }$ , and the share α on the new product, $P _ { 0 , n + 1 } Q _ { 0 , n + 1 } = \alpha Y _ { n + 1 } .$ In this sense, the parameter α represents the importance the consumer assigns to the new product relative to the collection of existing products.

There are competing effects relating how the expenditures of the consumer on existing products change in response to the innovation. Prior to the innovation, the consumer spends $Y _ { n }$ on the collection of existing products. After the innovation, the consumer spends $( 1 - \alpha ) Y _ { n + 1 }$ on the same. On the one hand, because the consumer has a taste for variety, the total expenditures of the consumer increase in response to the innovation: $Y _ { _ { n + 1 } } > Y _ { _ { n } }$ . On the other hand, the share α of the consumer’s budget is now allocated toward the new product. Which effect dominates is ambiguous.

## The Market Equilibrium Prior to the Innovation

In each of the n product markets, there is an incumbent competing in Cournot fashion against a competitive fringe of m identical firms. The marginal cost of producing any of the existing products is $c _ { \scriptscriptstyle I }$ for the incumbent and $c _ { { } _ { F } }$ for a fringe firm. Being larger and more established, the incumbent has a cost advantage: $c _ { _ I } { < } c _ { _ F }$ . The inverse demand function for product i is

$$
P _ {i, n} = \left(\frac {Y _ {n}}{Q _ {i , n} \overline {{P}} _ {n}}\right) ^ {1 - \theta}.
$$

Because each product enters the utility function of the representative consumer symmetrically, and costs of production are identical across product lines, each market is identical. Thus, we study the problem of the typical market, the inverse demand for which is

$$
P _ {n} = \left(\frac {Y _ {n}}{Q _ {n} \overline {{P}} _ {n}}\right) ^ {1 - \theta}.
$$

Let $q _ { I , n }$ denote the output of the incumbent and $q _ { F , n }$ that of the typical fringe firm. The problem of the incumbent in the typical market is

$$
\max _ {\left\{q _ {I} \right\}} \left(\frac {Y _ {n}}{\overline {{P}} _ {n}}\right) ^ {1 - \theta} \frac {q _ {I , n}}{\left(q _ {I , n} + Q _ {- I , n}\right) ^ {1 - \theta}} - c _ {I} q _ {I, n},\tag{3}
$$

where $Q _ { - I , n }$ is the aggregate output of the incumbent’s competitors. Following Dixit and Stiglitz [17] and Benassy [7], the incumbent takes as given the price index ${ \bar { P } } _ { n }$ in formulating its strategy.

The following lemma derives the Nash equilibrium:

Lemma 2a: Consider the markets prior to the innovation. The profits of the typical fringe firm and incumbent (derived from all n markets) are

$$

$$

$$

$$

respectively. With a stake in the entrant, the profit of the incumbent from all n markets is

$$
\hat {\Pi} _ {I, n} = \left(\frac {\hat {Y} _ {n}}{1 - \theta}\right) \left(\frac {(1 - \theta) c _ {I} + m (c _ {F} - c _ {I})}{m c _ {F} + c _ {I}}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

Proof: See the Appendix.

A fringe firm is inactive if $c _ { { \scriptscriptstyle F } } \ge c _ { { \scriptscriptstyle I } } / \Theta$ . Thus, the competitive fringe is driven out of the market if the marginal cost of production of a fringe firm is sufficiently large relative to that of the incumbent. If the competitive fringe is inactive, such that $m = 0$ , then the aggregate profits of the incumbent simplify to $\Pi _ { { \cal I } , n } = Y _ { n } / \varepsilon$ , and the price becomes the usual monopoly markup over marginal cost,

$$
P _ {n} = \frac {c _ {I}}{\theta} = \frac {c _ {I}}{1 - 1 / \varepsilon}.
$$

As in the case of consumer demand, there are competing effects relating how the profits of the incumbent derived from all n markets of existing products change in response to the innovation. The ratio of profits post- and preinnovation is $\Pi _ { { n + 1 } } / \Pi _ { n } = ( 1 - \alpha ) Y _ { { n + 1 } } / Y _ { n }$ Because the consumer has a taste for variety, the total expenditures of the consumer increase after the innovation, $Y _ { _ { n + 1 } } > Y _ { _ { n } }$ , but the share α of the consumer’s new budget is allocated toward the new product. The typical fringe firm in the typical market faces the same trade-off: $\pi _ { { } _ { F , n + 1 } } / \pi _ { { } _ { F , n } } = ( 1 - \alpha ) Y _ { { } _ { n + 1 } } / Y _ { { } _ { n } }$ . The price of an existing product remains the same, $P _ { _ { n + 1 } } = P _ { _ n } = ( m c _ { _ F } + c _ { _ I } ) / ( m + \Theta )$ , since the cost structure does not change after the innovation. Thus, the output and sales of the incumbent and typical fringe firm in each product market are subject to the same trade-off. A similar trade-off arises when the incumbent has a stake in the entrant.

## The Innovation Game Between the Incumbent and Entrant

In this subsection, we present the details of the game and derive conditions under which the incumbent finds it optimal to invest in R&D versus acquisitions. The game has two stages, as shown in Figure 1.

The incumbent decides in the first stage of the game whether to perform R&D at cost R or obtain a stake in the entrant at cost S. In the second stage of the game, the incumbent decides whether or not to acquire the entrant at cost $E ,$ which is the same regardless of having a stake or not. This assumption is reasonable because the price that an incumbent pays for the entrant depends on the market’s valuation of the entrant, which does not change whether the incumbent has a stake or not.<sup>1</sup> Moreover, we make this assumption for tractability purposes and relaxing it does not change our results.<sup>2</sup> If the incumbent performs R&D in the first stage, then an innovation race ensues between the incumbent and entrant, which the incumbent wins with probability $1 - p$ and the entrant wins with probability $p .$ If the incumbent performs R&D and wins the race, then there is no need for the incumbent to consider acquiring the entrant. However, if the incumbent performs R&D and loses the race, then the incumbent considers acquiring the entrant. If the incumbent obtains a stake in the entrant, then the entrant invents the new product for certain, after which point the incumbent considers acquiring the entrant. We solve for the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE) of the game. To implement backward induction so as to derive the SPNE, we begin by solving stage 2.

## Stage 2: The Decision of the Incumbent to Acquire the Entrant

Suppose the incumbent performed R&D in stage 1 but the entrant was successful at inventing the new product. The incumbent decides whether to acquire the entrant or not. If the incumbent acquires the entrant, then the payoff is monopoly profits from the new product minus the cost of acquisition, $\Pi _ { \tiny I , n + 1 } + \pi _ { \tiny I , 0 } ^ { \tiny M } - E - R$ . If the incumbent does not acquire the entrant, then it earns $\Pi _ { I , n + 1 } - R .$ . Thus, the incumbent acquires the entrant if monopoly profits from the new product exceed the cost of acquisition, $\pi _ { I , 0 } ^ { M } \geq E$ . Applying the result p $\mathbf { \Phi } _ { I , 0 } ^ { M } = \mathbf { \Phi } \propto Y _ { n + 1 }$ and consumer income $Y _ { _ { n + 1 } } = ( n + 1 ) ^ { \nu } Y$ this condition becomes

$$
n + 1 \geq \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v}.\tag{4}
$$

Suppose the incumbent did not perform R&D and obtained a stake in the entrant. Acquiring the entrant yields $\begin{array} { r } { \hat { \Pi } _ { { \cal I } , n + 1 } + \hat { \pi } _ { { \cal I } , 0 } ^ { M } - { \cal E } - S . } \end{array}$ , whereas not acquiring the entrant yields $\hat { \Pi } _ { I , n + 1 } - \mathsf { S }$ . Thus, the incumbent acquires the entrant if

$$
n + 1 \geq \left(\frac {E}{\alpha \hat {Y}}\right) ^ {1 / v}.\tag{5}
$$

Because of the synergies enjoyed by the incumbent and entrant, we have ${ \hat { Y } } > Y ,$ implying that $( E / \alpha \hat { Y } ) _ { 1 / \nu } < \left( E / \alpha Y \right) _ { 1 / \nu }$ . Note that this comparison presumes that the cost of acquiring the entrant is the same irrespective of whether the incumbent has a stake in the entrant.

The following lemma summarizes the decision of the incumbent to acquire the entrant, which gives rise to three cases. The cases are presented in order of increasing degree of diversification:

Lemma 3: Suppose the new product is invented. The SPNE exhibits the following outcomes in stage 2 of the game:

Case 1: Suppose the incumbent is focused: $n + I < ( E / \alpha \hat { Y } ) ^ { I / \nu } .$ . Then the incumbent does not acquire the entrant.

Case 2: Suppose the incumbent is average: $( E / \alpha \hat { Y } ) ^ { I / \nu } < n + I \leq ( E / \alpha Y ) ^ { I / \nu }$ . Then the incumbent acquires the entrant only if it has a stake.

Case 3: Suppose the incumbent is diversified: $( E / \alpha Y ) ^ { l / \nu } \leq n + I .$ . Then the incumbent acquires the entrant.

Proof: Follows directly by examining Equations (4) and (5).

The intuition for these results is as follows: suppose the incumbent is focused, having few products in its portfolio with narrow scope. Then the monopoly profits it would attain upon acquiring the entrant are not sufficiently great to outweigh the cost of acquisition. Suppose the incumbent is diversified, having a large product portfolio with broad scope. Then the span of the market is large enough to warrant acquiring a successful entrant, irrespective of whether or not the incumbent performed R&D or obtained a stake. Since $( E / \alpha \hat { Y } ) _ { 1 / \nu } < ( E / \alpha Y ) ^ { 1 / \nu }$ , if the incumbent has obtained a stake in the entrant, then it does not need to be as diversified to acquire a successful entrant. In other words, the incumbent is more likely to acquire the entrant if it has a stake.

## Stage 1: The Decision of the Incumbent to Obtain a Stake or Perform R&D

Consider stage 1 of the game. The incumbent must decide whether to perform R&D, incurring cost R, or obtain a stake at cost S. We perform the analysis for each of the above cases separately. Within each case, we obtain two subcases (labeled a and b).

As with the three cases, the two subcases are presented in order of increasing degree of diversification.

## Case 1: Incumbent Is Focused

The expected payoff of performing R&D is $\Pi _ { { } _ { I , n + 1 } } + ( 1 - p ) \pi _ { { } _ { I , 0 } } ^ { { } _ { M } } - R$ because the incumbent does not acquire the entrant if the incumbent loses the race. The payoff of obtaining a stake is $\Pi _ { \scriptscriptstyle { I , n + 1 } } - S$ because the incumbent does not acquire the entrant in this case. Define the term

$$
\Pi \equiv \left(\frac {1 - \alpha}{1 - \theta}\right) \left(\frac {(1 - \theta) c _ {I} + m (c _ {F} - c _ {I})}{m c _ {F} + c _ {I}}\right) ^ {2}.
$$

It follows that the incumbent performs R&D instead of obtaining a stake if and only if

$$
n + 1 \leq \left(\frac {S - R}{\Pi (\hat {Y} - Y) - (1 - p) \alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v}.\tag{6}
$$

Under assumption (A.1), this condition presumes that the synergies associated with having a stake in the entrant are sufficiently great to satisfy

$$
\Pi \Big (\hat {Y} - Y \Big) - (1 - p) \alpha Y > 0.\tag{A.3}
$$

Otherwise, the incumbent would strictly prefer performing R&D, rendering the model unrealistic.

The following lemma derives the SPNE:

Lemma 4a: If the incumbent is focused (i.e., Case 1 holds), the SPNE of the game is the following:

Case 1a:

$$
n + 1 \leq \min \left\{\left(\frac {S - R}{\Pi (Y - Y) - (1 - p) \alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v}, \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} \right\}.
$$

The incumbent performs R&D in the first stage and does not acquire the entrant in the second stage (if the incumbent loses the innovation race).

Case 1b:

$$
\left(\frac {S - R}{\Pi (\hat {Y} - Y) - (1 - p) \alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} <   n + 1 <   \left(\frac {E}{\alpha \hat {Y}}\right) ^ {1 / v}.
$$

The incumbent obtains a stake in the first stage and does not acquire the entrant in the second stage.

Proof: Follows from combining the conditions in Case 1 $( n + l < ( E / a \hat { Y } ) ^ { l / \nu } )$ and Equation (6). The result in Case 1b in Lemma 4a is especially interesting because it suggests that an equilibrium where the incumbent obtains a stake in stage 1, but does not acquire the entrant in stage 2.

## Case 2: Incumbent Is Average

The expected payoff of performing R&D is $\Pi _ { { } _ { I , n + 1 } } + ( 1 - p ) \pi _ { { } _ { I , 0 } } ^ { { } _ { M } } - R$ because the incumbent acquires the entrant only if it has a stake. The payoff of obtaining a stake is $\hat { \Pi } _ { { \cal I } , n + 1 } + \hat { \pi } _ { { \cal I } , 0 } ^ { M } - E - S$ because the incumbent acquires the entrant in this case. It follows that the incumbent performs R&D instead of obtaining a stake if and only if

$$
n + 1 \leq \left(\frac {S + E - R}{\Pi (\hat {Y} - Y) + \alpha (\hat {Y} - (1 - p) Y)}\right) ^ {1 / v}.\tag{7}
$$

Lemma 4b: If the incumbent is average (i.e., Case 2 holds), the SPNE of the game is the following:

Case 2a:

$$
\left(\frac {E}{\alpha \hat {Y}}\right) ^ {1 / v} <   n + 1 \leq \min \left\{\left(\frac {S + E - R}{\Pi (\hat {Y} - Y) + \alpha (\hat {Y} - (1 - p) Y)}\right) ^ {1 / v}, \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} \right\}.
$$

The incumbent performs R&D in the first stage and does not acquire the entrant in the second stage (if the incumbent loses the innovation race).

Case 2b:

$$
\max \left\{\left(\frac {S + E - R}{\Pi (Y - Y) + \alpha (Y - (1 - p) Y)}\right) ^ {1 / v}, \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} \right\} <   n + 1 \leq \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v}.
$$

The incumbent obtains a stake in the first stage and acquires the entrant in the second stage.

Proof: Follows from combining the conditions for Case $2 ( ( E / \alpha Y ) ^ { I / \nu } < n + I \le ( E / a Y ) ^ { I / \nu } )$ and Equation (7).

## Case 3: Incumbent Is Diversified

The expected payoff of performing R&D is $\Pi _ { { } _ { I , n + 1 } } + \pi _ { { } _ { I , 0 } } ^ { M } - R - p E$ because the incumbent acquires the entrant only if the incumbent loses the innovation race. The payoff of obtaining a stake is $\hat { \Pi } _ { { \cal I } , n + 1 } + \hat { \pi } _ { { \cal I } , 0 } ^ { M } - E - S$ because the incumbent acquires the entrant in this case. It follows that the incumbent performs R&D instead of obtaining a stake if and only if

$$
\left(\frac {S - R + (1 - p) E}{(\hat {Y} - Y) (\Pi + \alpha)}\right) ^ {1 / v} \geq n + 1.\tag{8}
$$

The following lemma derives the SPNE:

Lemma 4c: If the incumbent is diversified (i.e., Case 3 holds), the SPNE of the game is the following:

Case 3a:

$$
\left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} \leq n + 1 \leq \left(\frac {S - R + (1 - p) E}{(Y - Y) (\Pi + \alpha)}\right) ^ {1 / v}.
$$

The incumbent performs R&D in the first stage and acquires the entrant in the second stage (if the incumbent loses the innovation race).

Case 3b:

$$
\max \left\{\left(\frac {S - R + (1 - p) E}{(Y - Y) (\Pi + \alpha)}\right) ^ {1 / v}, \left(\frac {E}{\alpha Y}\right) ^ {1 / v} \right\} <   n + 1.
$$

The incumbent obtains a stake in the first stage and acquires the entrant in the second stage.

Proof: Follows from combining the conditions in Case 3 $( ( E / a Y ) ^ { l / \nu } \leq n + l )$ and Equation (8).

Overall, we find that the more diversified the incumbent, the more likely it is to obtain a stake and acquire the entrant, and the less likely it is to perform R&D. For example, in Case 1a, which is the region in which the incumbent is the most heavily focused, the incumbent performs R&D in the first stage and does not acquire the entrant in the second stage; and in Case 3b, which is the region in which the incumbent is the most heavily diversified, the incumbent obtains a stake in the entrant in the first stage (instead of performing R&D) and acquires the entrant in the second stage.

We can combine the results from Lemmas 3, 4a, 4b, and 4c to calculate the SPNE. The results can be summarized as follows:

Proposition 1a: Ceteris paribus, the propensity of a firm to invest in acquisitions compared to R&D increases with the degree of diversification of the firm.

Proposition 1b: Diversification has a positive effect on firms’ investment in acquisition, as well as a negative effect on investment in R&D.

We also find that for a given level of diversification, a firm’s decision to invest in R&D increases as consumers attach more importance to the new product compared to the existing products. These results are shown graphically in Figure 2. The x-axis denotes the degree of diversification and the y-axis denotes the preference for new technology. For any given value of α , we find that the probability to invest in acquisitions relative to R&D increases with the degree of diversification n. We also observe that the probability of investing in acquisitions increases on the lower-right side of the graph. But the probability to invest in acquisitions decreases on the topleft side of the graph. The dominant strategy space consists of (1) taking stake in stage 1 and acquisition in stage 2 (Region R IV), and (2) doing R&D in stage 1 and no acquisition in stage 2 (Region R I). However, we also find that investing in both R&D and acquisition in stage 1 and stage 2, respectively (Region R III), and taking a stake in stage 1 and not investing in acquisition in stage 2 (Region R II) are also feasible strategies.

Propositions 1a and 1b provide empirically testable propositions that we develop in the next section. Proposition 1a suggests that diversification makes a firm more likely to invest in acquisitions relative to R&D. Proposition 1b suggests that diversification leads to an increase in investments in acquisitions and a decrease in investment in acquisitions.

## Alternative Specification

The empirical results confirm our findings in the analytical model that diversification is associated with a higher tendency to invest in acquisitions as compared to R&D. To shed light on the mechanism of how diversification affects a firm’s propensity to invest in acquisitions versus R&D, we test Proposition 1b by proposing separate models to check the effect of diversification on R&D and acquisitions respectively. The independent variables are based on prior literature as mentioned above. Further, prior research [21] has also identified acquisitions as an important determinant of R&D investments. R&D investments by a firm have also been identified as important determinants of acquisitions [10]. Therefore, we propose the following system of equations:

$$
R N D = \alpha_ {1} + \alpha_ {2} D I V + \alpha_ {3} S I Z E + \alpha_ {4} P R O F + \alpha_ {5} L I Q + \alpha_ {6} A C Q + \alpha_ {7} D E B T + \varepsilon_ {r}\tag{E2}
$$

$$
A C Q = \beta_ {1} + \beta_ {2} D I V + \beta_ {3} S I Z E + \beta_ {4} P R O F + \beta_ {5} L I Q + \beta_ {6} R N D + \beta_ {7} T O B I N + \varepsilon_ {a}.\tag{E3}
$$

Equations (E2) and (E3) constitute a system of equations where the dependent variables RND and ACQ, respectively, are endogenously determined. ## Related Versus Unrelated Diversification

To shed more light on how diversification affects the decision of a firm to invest in R&D versus acquisitions, we consider different forms of diversification and test their impact on our dependent variable PROP. Prior literature has considered two main forms of diversification—related and unrelated [38]. In related diversification, a firm operates in businesses related to its existing products, whereas in unrelated diversification, a firm operates in business areas that may not be related. The intuition behind using related and unrelated diversification is that firms that operate in related businesses are in a better position to exploit their “core skills” to achieve economies of scale and scope, as well as exploit technical and managerial skills [43]. Bettis [8] points out that R&D also fits in Rumelt’s [43] concept of “core skills.” Thus, one may expect that related diversification may be associated with a higher tendency to invest in R&D. Prior research suggests that firms pursue unrelated diversification to increase firm size and the managers’ power rather than leveraging of shared core skills [12]. In the absence of synergy with existing “core skills,” one would expect that firms perform unrelated diversification using acquisitions. We measure related and unrelated diversification using the entropy measure suggested by Palepu [38]. Suppose a firm operated in N different market segments in J industry groups (such that $N > J )$ . Two or more market segments being part of the same industry group implies related diversification. Suppose $P _ { _ i }$ denotes the sales of the firm in market segment i and $P _ { j }$ denotes the sales of the firm in industry group j. If the firm operates in only one market segment i in industry group $j ,$ then $P _ { _ i } = P _ { _ i }$ . But if the firm operates in market segments h and i in industry group $j ,$ then $P _ { i } + P _ { h } = P _ { j }$ . Related diversification is measured as

$$
R E L = \sum_ {j} \sum_ {i} P _ {i} ^ {j} \cdot \ln \left(\frac {1}{P _ {i} ^ {j}}\right),
$$

where $P _ { i } ^ { j }$ is the share of sales in market segment i of industry group j in the sales of that group. Unrelated diversification is measured as

$$
U N R E L = \sum_ {j} P _ {j} \cdot \ln \left(\frac {1}{P _ {j}}\right).
$$

We expect that it is the unrelated diversification that is positively related to the relative investments on acquisitions, whereas firms that are high only on related diversification should not affect the decision to spend more on acquisitions, relative to R&D. The model we estimate is

$$
\begin{array}{c} P R O P = \omega_ {1} + \omega_ {2} R E L + \omega_ {3} U N R E L + \omega_ {4} S I Z E + \omega_ {5} P R O F + \omega_ {6} L I Q \\ + \omega_ {7} D E B T + \omega_ {8} T O B I N + \varepsilon_ {\omega}. \end{array}\tag{E5}
$$

The results shown in Table 7 support these expectations.

The coefficient of REL is negative as expected, although it is not statistically significant. In other words, firms that are diversified in nonrelated areas may not achieve synergies in internal innovation due to sharing of resources such as technical capabilities. Such firms prefer to innovate through external acquisitions. However, the coefficient of UNREL is positive, which suggests that unrelated diversification is associated with a higher propensity to invest in acquisitions over R&D.

## IT Versus Non-IT Firms

The dilemma about which innovation strategy to follow is especially important in the IT industry because of the following reasons: first, the IT industry is fast paced and rapid technological change can disrupt the industry status quo every few years. Second, the low start-up costs ensure a plethora of start-ups that try to build business models around new technologies. For example, in the mid-1990s, a new start-up company named Yahoo! emerged as the leading search engine provider on the Internet, only to have its dominance usurped by a newer start-up named Google in the early 2000s. Incumbent firms in the IT industry need to be cognizant of the emerging technology landscape as well as of the shifting consumer preferences. ## Concluding Remarks

Pr ior r es ear ch has focus ed on the anteced ents and cons equences of firms’ investments in R&D and acquisitions, as well as the relation between R&D investments and acquisitions. In this paper, we examine a hitherto unexplored area—how an incumbent firm’s choice of mode of innovation depends on its degree of diversification. This issue is of particular interest in the context of IT industries since the fast pace of technological change and low start-up costs makes it imperative for incumbents to devise new ways to keep entrants at bay and preserve their market leadership position.

To address this question, we use game theory to model an incumbent firm’s decision about the mode of innovation (R&D versus acquisition) in a two-stage game. In the first stage, the incumbent decides whether to perform its own R&D or not, and in the second stage, the incumbent decides whether to acquire an innovative start-up that is successful. We also consider the possibility that an incumbent can acquire a strategic stake in the entrant at the beginning to hedge against uncertainty in the outcome of innovation. We find that firms that are more diversified are more likely to innovate through acquisitions than perform R&D. We also find that the incumbent firm’s investment in R&D increases if consumers prefer the new product over existing products.

We augment our analysis by testing the results from our analytical model using firm-level empirical data collected from Compustat. Our dependent variable is the proportion of innovation investments made in acquisitions as a fraction of the overall innovation budget (investments in both R&D and acquisitions), and the independent variable of interest is the degree of diversification. We also control for the firm size, debt, profitability, Tobin’s q, and liquidity.

Our results confirm that diversification is associated with a higher tendency to acquire than perform R&D. We also show that diversification leads to an increase in investments in acquisitions as well as a decrease in investments in R&D. We also perform a variety of robustness checks to demonstrate the validity of our results, as well as uncover additional insights about the mechanisms on how diversification affects firms’ innovation strategy. We also find that unrelated diversification is associated with an increase in investment in acquisitions compared to R&D, whereas related diversification does not have any significant effect. Finally, we also show that firms in the IT industry consider R&D and acquisitions as substitutes, and that an increase in one type of investment is associated with an increase in the other type as well. Our results provide insights to managers at IT firms on the optimal response to threat of new entry based on the degree and type of diversification of the firm.

The results of our study need to be interpreted recognizing some of its limitations. For example, in our analytical model, we assume that the entrant does not act strategically, and that the acquisition price and probability of success are exogenously determined. While this can be generalized, the analysis becomes much more complex and the intuition less transparent. Also, our data on acquisitions are limited to the aggregate investments made by firms, and do not capture details such as the nature of the acquired companies, and the stage of acquisition (preemptive versus controlling stake).

Despite these limitations, this paper makes a contribution to the study of innovation in IT firms. It extends prior work on competition in IT industries (in MIS literature) and “make versus buy” (in strategic management literature) by investigating how diversification affects IT firms’ incentives to choose between R&D and acquisitions as alternative modes of innovation. Both the analytical model and empirical evidence support a positive relation between a firm’s diversification and propensity to acquire successful innovators rather than perform R&D.

$$
\lambda_ {n} = U _ {n} / Y _ {n}.\tag{A1}
$$

Applying Equation (A1) to (2), we obtain the inverse demand function in terms of the demand for each good:

$$

Inverting (A2), we have

Q _ {i, n} ^ {\theta} = \left(Y _ {n} \left(P _ {i, n} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {- 1}\right) ^ {\theta / (1 - \theta)}.\tag{A3}
$$

Summing (A3) over all i, we obtain

$$

Applying this to Equation (A3), we derive the demand function for product i:

$$

where $\bar { P } _ { n } \equiv \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n } P _ { j , n } ^ { \ - \theta / ( 1 - \theta ) }$ is the price index. ## Basic Model—New Product Introduction and Proof of Lemma 1b

Let $Q _ { 0 , n + 1 }$ denote the representative consumer’s demand for the new good and $P _ { 0 , n + 1 }$ denotes its price. In the spirit of Benassy [7], the new product and the collection of existing products have a Cobb–Douglas relationship: the consumer’s preferences are

$$
U _ {n + 1} = Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \mathrm{Q} _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta},
$$

where $\alpha \in \left( 0 , 1 \right)$ represents the extent to which the new product is a substitute for the collection of existing products. With these preferences, the elasticity of substitution across any pair of existing products remains $\sigma = 1 / ( 1 - \theta )$ , and the elasticity of substitution between the new product and the collection of existing products is 1 (due to the Cobb–Douglas structure). The consumer allocates the income $Y _ { n + 1 }$ toward the consumption of new and existing products. Because the consumer has a taste for variety, his or her overall expenditures on consumption increase in response to the innovation, $Y _ { _ { n + 1 } } > Y _ { _ { n } }$

The consumer solves the problem

$$
\max _ {\left\{Q _ {i, n + 1} \right\} _ {i = 0} ^ {n}} Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta} \text {   subject   to   } \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {n} P _ {i, n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1} \leq Y _ {n + 1}.\tag{A5}
$$

The first-order condition with respect to the demand for an existing product is

$$
\big (1 - \alpha \big) Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta - 1} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta - 1} = \lambda_ {n + 1} P _ {i, n + 1} \text { for } i = 1,..., n,\tag{A6}
$$

where $\lambda _ { n + 1 }$ is the Lagrange multiplier. The first-order condition with respect to the demand for the new product is

$$
\alpha Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha - 1} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta} = \lambda_ {n + 1} P _ {0, n + 1}.\tag{A7}
$$

From Equation (A6), we have

$$
\big (1 - \alpha \big) Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta - 1} = \lambda_ {n + 1} P _ {i, n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1}.
$$

Summing this over all $i = 1 , . . . , n ,$ we find

$$
\big (1 - \alpha \big) Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta} = \lambda_ {n + 1} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P _ {i, n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1}.\tag{A8}
$$

From Equation (A7), we have

$$
\alpha Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta} = \lambda_ {n + 1} P _ {0, n + 1} Q _ {0, n + 1}.\tag{A9}
$$

From Equations (A8) and (A9), we obtain

$$
U _ {n + 1} = Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {\alpha} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {(1 - \alpha) / \theta} = \lambda_ {n + 1} \sum_ {i = 0} ^ {n} P _ {i, n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1} = \lambda_ {n + 1} Y _ {n + 1},\tag{A10}
$$

where the last equality follows from the binding budget constraint. This yields the expression

$$
\lambda_ {n + 1} = U _ {n + 1} / Y _ {n + 1}.\tag{A11}
$$

Applying Equation (A11) to Equation (5), we obtain the inverse demand function for an existing product:

$$
P _ {i, n + 1} = (1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {- (1 - \theta)} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {- 1} \text {for} i = 1,..., n.\tag{A12}
$$

Applying Equation (A11) to Equation (6), we obtain the inverse demand function for the new product:

$$
P _ {0, n + 1} = \alpha Y _ {n + 1} Q _ {0, n + 1} ^ {- 1}.\tag{A13}
$$

From Equation (A12), we have

$$
P _ {i, n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1} = (1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta} \left(\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {- 1}.
$$

Summing this over all $i = 1 , . . . , n$ , we find that total expenditures for the existing products are

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P _ {i, n + 1} \mathrm{Q} _ {i, n + 1} = (1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1}.\tag{A14}
$$

Similarly, from Equation (A13), we have that expenditures for the new product are

$$
P _ {0, n + 1} Q _ {0, n + 1} = \alpha Y _ {n + 1}.\tag{A15}
$$

We obtain an expression for the demand function of an existing product in terms of a price index using the same procedure as before. Inverting Equation (A12), we have

$$
Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta} = \left((1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1} \left(P _ {i, n + 1} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {j, n + 1} ^ {\theta}\right) ^ {- 1}\right) ^ {\theta / (1 - \theta)}.\tag{A16}
$$

Summing Equation (A16) over all $i = 1 , . . . , n ,$ , we obtain

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} Q _ {i, n + 1} ^ {\theta} = \left[ (1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1} \right] ^ {\theta} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} P _ {i, n + 1} ^ {- \theta / (1 - \theta)}\right) ^ {1 - \theta}.
$$

Applying this to Equation (A16), we derive the demand function for an existing product:

$$
Q _ {i, n + 1} = \frac {(1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1}}{P _ {i , n + 1} ^ {1 / (1 - \theta)} \overline {{P}} _ {n + 1}},\tag{A17}
$$

where

$$
\overline {{P}} _ {n + 1} \equiv \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} P _ {j, n + 1} ^ {- \theta / (1 - \theta)}
$$

is the price index. Inverting Equation (A17), we obtain the inverse demand function for an existing product in terms of the price index:

$$
P _ {i, n + 1} = \left(\frac {(1 - \alpha) Y _ {n + 1}}{Q _ {i , n + 1} \overline {{P}} _ {n + 1}}\right) ^ {1 - \theta}.
$$

$$

The problem of the typical fringe firm in the typical market is

$$
