# The digital divide and t-government in the United States: using the technology acceptance model to understand usage

Janice C. Sipior<sup>1</sup>, Burke T. Ward<sup>1</sup> and Regina Connolly<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Business, Villanova University, Villanova, PA, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Dublin City University, Dublin 9, Ireland

## Abstract

This paper applies the technology acceptance model to explore the digital divide and transformational government (t-government) in the United States. Successful t-government is predicated on citizen adoption and usage of e-government services. The contribution of this research is to enhance our understanding of the factors associated with the usage of e-government services among members of a community on the unfortunate side of the divide. A questionnaire was administered to members, of a techno-disadvantaged public housing community and neighboring households, who partook in training or used the community computer lab. The results indicate that perceived access barriers and perceived ease of use (PEOU) are significantly associated with usage, while perceived usefulness (PU) is not. Among the demographic characteristics, educational level, employment status, and household income all have a significant impact on access barriers and employment is significantly associated with PEOU. Finally, PEOU is significantly related to PU. Overall, the results emphasize that t-government cannot cross the digital divide without accompanying employment programs and programs that enhance citizens’ ease in using such services. ## Introduction

Transformational government (t-government) emphasizes the critical role of information and communication technologies (ICT) to enhance the relationship between citizens and government services (King & Cotterill, 2007). T-government seeks to change the way government works by leveraging ICT to support service transformation. An ambitious vision for the delivery of government services, t-government embraces the use of new and existing technologies to change the way citizens interact and communicate with government. It can be seen as the future of public services, evolving from the preceding e-government era, to take advantage of technological advances. However, citizens who have most to gain from this transformation in government services may have the least access to it (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). As e-government evolves to t-government, the digital divide, among other challenges, must be addressed (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007; Rossel & Finger, 2007).

This paper explores the digital divide and t-government inclusion in the United States (U.S.) by examining the usage of the predecessor to t-government, e-government services, among members of a digitally disadvantaged public housing community struggling to come out of computer-illiteracy. The technology acceptance model (TAM) is applied to explain e-government usage among members of this underserved community. Thus, we extend the literature on the TAM by applying it to a techno-disadvantaged user community, by adding five key demographic characteristics as external variables, and by including perceived access barriers among the key beliefs about a technology influencing its use. By including access barriers to digital citizenship, we address a ‘gap in the literature’ identified by Shelley et al. (2006, p. 229). The contribution of this empirical study is to enhance our understanding of the factors encouraging e-citizenry among digitally disadvantaged citizens to promote greater inclusion of these citizens.

The paper first describes t-government and governments embracing the transformation of government services. T-government inclusion and the digital divide are discussed next. Previous research on acceptance factors for t-government and the digital divide is then reviewed. On the basis of the review of previous research, we present our research model, followed by our hypotheses, research methodology, and data analysis and results. We then discuss our findings, followed by a recognition of limitations of the study and directions for future research.

## Transformational government

T-government is ill-defined, but it is clear that t-government is multifaceted (Janssen & Shu, 2008), involving a combination of social, technological, and organizational issues at both governmental and individual citizen levels (Irani et al., 2007a). Transformational change is intended to bring about new customer-oriented government practices to meet the needs of citizens. ICTs are the enablers of a customer-oriented government realized through innovations in service delivery, in citizen engagement and interaction (Irani et al., 2007b), and for efficiency improvements (Irani et al., 2005). Among ICTs that offer the potential to improve government services are Internet access through in-home digital TVs (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007), social networking (King & Brown, 2009), mobile technology (Ostberg, 2003; Moon, 2004; Trimi & Sheng, 2008), as well as voice systems, biometric-based security, knowledge interfaces (navigation standards, search robots, semantic web configurations), sensor networks, and radio frequency identification (RFID)-based systems (Rossel & Finger, 2007). Mobile technology helps remove infrastructure constraints, offering a solution to overcome Internet connectivity issues contributing to the digital divide (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). We therefore focus on exemplifying mobile government applications.

## Mobile government applications

Innovations in mobile technologies, for example, such as mobile phones, personal digital assistants, laptops, or tablet PCs, can improve anytime-anywhere access and delivery of government information and services (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). In the U.S. for example, the government of the state of Virginia is a leader in mobile applications (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). ‘My Mobile Virginia’ was the first wireless state portal in the U.S. that made government information and services, including emergency weather conditions, legislative information, lobbyist lists, election notices, tax-related information, and tourism information, available from mobile devices (Moon, 2004). Text messages have also been used to communicate with citizens. For example, the London Police Department’s short messaging system (SMS) sends citizens information about security threats and emergency alerts (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). In Sweden, where over 95% of the population uses mobile phones, innovative SMS applications include city job postings in Stockholm, a mobile parking fee payment system, a government inspector service, tax services, mobile healthcare providers, and MapMate, a wireless map system (Ostberg, 2003). Further, a global positioning system is used in Seattle, WA, U.S.A., for the Mobile Traffic Map service. Instantaneous information on traffic congestion, traffic lights, and traffic flows is provided for 176 miles of major roadways (Trimi & Sheng, 2008).

Mobile technology enables location-based services, the delivery of services personalized to an individual mobile device user (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). What makes this possible is that a mobile device is usually used by one user who keeps the device with him, providing user ‘identity’. An interesting ‘identity’ application, provided by Finnish telecommunication company Sonera, turns a mobile phone into an official identity document by embedding personal identification information into a mobile phone’s unique Subscriber Identification Module card code (Trimi & Sheng, 2008). The Finnish government is upgrading the country’s electronic ID card to enable citizens to use it as a passport.

## Governments embracing transformation

As a result of those technological advances, the possibility of transforming government services has become a realizable objective and the benefits it offers to both citizens and stakeholders have become the focus of greater attention from government bodies and leaders. In 2007, the United Nations hosted the 7th Global Forum on Reinventing Government and examples abound of governments who are making concerted efforts to transform the delivery of services to their citizens through the use of technology (e.g., Heeks & Stanforth, 2007; Grimsley & Meehan, 2007; Connolly & Bannister, 2007; Irani et al., 2007a). For example, the U.K. government is currently engaged in a major exercise in t-government (HM Government, 2007) and in 2005, the then U.K. Prime Minister Tony Blair publicly embraced the transformation of government services:

The future of public services has to use technology to give citizens choice, with personalised services designed around their needs not the needs of the provider. (in the Foreword, HM Council, 2005)

Similarly, his successor, Gordon Brown, has praised transformation efforts to provide the public with e-government services, which, through innovative online delivery or IT implementation, have positively transformed the lives of citizens, local communities, business and stakeholder groups, stating that:

Today’s citizens are quite rightly demanding more and technology has the capacity to provide it, breaking new ground and helping to achieve new goals. (eGovernment National Awards, 2007)

The benefits accrued by the application of technology in the government sector are not limited merely to the delivery of new services. Many researchers claim that technological innovations also contain the ability to reform government resulting in an entirely transformed government culture. One example of this belief is that provided by Blackstone et al. (2005) cited in Rocheleau (2007, p. 586), who describe e-government as a:

Technological innovation and move from an inefficient and mainly unaccountable bureaucracy to a new entrepreneurial culture.

Despite the fact that in many quarters the terms t-government and e-government have become synonomous, there is a distinction as the realization of such transformation is predicated in the first instance on citizen adoption and usage of e-government services. Thus, while authors such as Bonham et al. (2001) define transformation as the highest stage of development of e-government, they emphasize that disparities in computer access must be addressed in order to enable the realization of that tranformation. Similarly, Irani et al. (2007b) have pointed to the need for greater acknowledgement of the salience of non-technical issues (including equity of access, designing for public value, and engendering trust in system use) that influence citizen adoption of eGovernment.

Within the U.S., the E-Government Act of 2002 was enacted to ‘create a law that will make it easier to get more government information and services online’ (Hasson, 2002). The U.S. government’s t-government plan focuses on the integration of ICT to create a more ‘ “citizen centered” government’ (Johnson, 2006). The realization of this plan therefore requires that the digital divide be addressed so that equity in citizen access is achieved. In a study of e-government use by citizens, the digital divide was found to be more pronounced among government website visitors than among Internet users in general (Thomas & Streib, 2003). Citizens who are the biggest users of government services are the least likely to be connected to the Internet (Dugdale et al., 2005). A major challenge is how to get such citizens more involved and encourage greater interaction with t-government (ICT Results, 2008). T-government risks increasing the disadvantage of already disadvantaged citizens unless inclusiveness of all citizens in t-government usage is encouraged (Dugdale et al., 2005; Shelley et al., 2006).

## T-government inclusion and the digital divide

The divide between those with access and skills to use the Internet and new ICT and those without, or in other words, the gap between the ‘technology haves’ and ‘havenots’ is referred to as the digital divide (Novak & Hoffman, 2000; Wilhelm & Thierer, 2000; OECD, 2001; Holmes, 2002). ‘The Digital Divide is arguably the single, largest, segregating force in today’s world. If it is not made a national priority, a generation of children and families will mature without these tools that are proving to be the key to the future’ (PR Newswire, 2000). New t-government plans create an even more pressing need for equity among all citizens in accessing the Internet and emerging ICT. The preferred channels for information and transactional services are expected to be the telephone, Internet, and mobile channels as well as the increasingly important channels with the digital home (Cabinet Office, United Kingdom Government, 2006).

The most common application of e-government within the U.S. has been providing citizens with access to information via the Internet, raising the issue of equal access (Marchionini et al., 2003). In a study addressing the adoption of e-governance, public Internet access was found to be the most important factor affecting the use of online government services (Prattipati, 2003). While the Internet has the potential to create well informed and empowered consumers, it will also help to change the passive relationship most people have with the government (Symonds, 2000). Exclusion from online voting, and other interactive opportunities, will weaken the voice of those who are techno-disadvantaged (Althoff, 2004). These individuals are among the citizens likely to benefit most from government services (Lamb, 2004). If the digital divide is not narrowed, the powerful communication tools meant to enrich lives will serve as a social divider.

## A community-based initiative

Empirical evidence suggests that access to ICT does not guarantee use of services available unless the concerns and abilities of users are addressed (Brookes, 2004). Narrowing the digital divide requires a comprehensive approach rather than a quick fix. It requires a process that is, for example, driven by a local community program and strategy to initiate and sustain members’ use of technology. Therefore, this study follows up on a community-based initiative designed to stimulate computer literacy and access to ICT for members of a techno-disadvantaged public housing community and neighboring households (Sipior et al., 2004). This initiative was undertaken by the William Penn (WP) Tenants’ Association of the WP Housing Development in Chester, Pennsylvania (PA) U.S.A. and Unity Center, Inc., a nonprofit corporation founded in 1987 to ‘bring people together who would normally not come together’ to work on a common concern or project. The Assets-based Community Development Model (Kretzmann & McKnight, 1993) was applied, leading to the formation of a community development plan based on a survey of community needs, securing resources to provide ICT access, and coordinating volunteers to provide computer training. For a detailed discussion of narrowing the divide within this community, please see Sipior et al. (2004).

This community thereby increased their capacity to access the Internet and be positioned to participate in and use e-government, but external threats to the community initiative, including isolation from mainstream society, exploitive dependency by those ostensibly assisting the community, and a culture of failure are still lurking (Sipior & Ward, 2005). The results of a study intended to provide insight into e-government inclusion among members of this community underscore the importance of a community organizing strategy to secure Internet access, coordinate education and training, and sustain Internet use and e-government participation among the techno-disadvantaged (Sipior & Ward, 2005). Furthermore, computer experience led to government website awareness and visitation. The first visit may be encouraged by group activity devoted to a specific or compelling reason. This insight is consistent with the finding that e-government be not only technically accessible, but usable and engaging (Donnelly & Merrick, 2003).

More must be done, however, to assure such communities will continue to receive benefit, especially from emergent t-government services. Members of this community continue to receive computer training. While the training program began at the WP Community Center and a local church, it continues today through the on-going efforts of Unity Center. Unity Center now provides community programs, including computer training and other educational and athletic programs, at its own facility. Community members served as survey respondents for this study.

## Previous research on acceptance factors for t-government and the digital divide

The information systems literature does not appear to have directly considered technology acceptance factors for t-government among the digitally disadvantaged. T-government has recently succeeded e-government (King & Cotterill, 2007), with researchers now viewing transformation as the final or highest stage of e-government (e.g., Bonham et al., 2001). E-government is the basis upon which t-government is built. T-government is the transformation of e-government services to ‘the highest level of maturity for e-government programmes’ (Weerakkody & Dhillon, 2009, p. 2), leading to integrated government services and information accessible from a single point (one-stop-shop) (Irani et al., 2006). Thus, understanding e-government use will provide insight into t-government use. Previous research has thus focused on e-government to understand the factors that influence citizen usage of this preceding era of Internet access to government services. In this section, we first discuss theories applied in previous e-government usage studies. We then focus our discussion on previous studies applying the TAM to e-government acceptance and the factors included in these studies. Finally, we highlight those studies that address the digital divide.

## Theories in e-government usage studies

Van Dijk et al. (2008) observe that ‘a theory of the acceptance and the use of government Internet services is lacking’ (p. 383). Ojha et al. (2009) note, in their review of citizen level e-government adoption studies, that the theoretical basis of such studies is generally the TAM, or the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991) upon which the TAM is based. The TPB is based upon the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). According to the TRA, an individual’s behavior is determined by his behavioral intention (BI), which in turn is determined by attitude (ATT) and subjective norm (SN). ATT refers to a person’s positive or negative feelings about performing a target behavior. SN is the person’s perception that salient others think he should or should not perform the behavior in question. The TPB extends the TRA by including perceived behavior control (PBC) as an additional variable. PBC is a person’s perception of how easy or difficult it would be to carry out a specific behavior, thus emphasizing the subjective degree of control over the behavior performance.

While TAM is the most widely used (King & He, 2006) and the most influential theory in technology acceptance (Ojha et al., 2009), the theoretical models upon which the TAM is based have themselves been applied in e-government acceptance studies, alone, decomposed, and in combination. Further, the TAM has also been extended, and such extensions have themselves been extended. For example, the TAM is the basis for the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al., 2003) and Van Dijk et al. (2008) built their own multidisciplinary model based upon UTAUT. We exemplify the application of these various theoretical models to e-government studies.

Exemplifying a decomposed TPB Hsu & Chiu (2004) used a decomposed TPB to examine factors influencing Taiwanese citizens’ intention to continue using electronic services (e-services). Specifically, their research model decomposes PBC into Internet self-efficacy and perceived controllability, SN into social influences and interpersonal influence, and ATT into perceived usefulness (PU), perceived playfulness, and perceived risk. The results suggest that citizens’ intention to continue is determined by Internet self-efficacy and satisfaction, while satisfaction, in turn, is jointly determined by interpersonal influence, PU, and perceived playfulness, validating the decomposed TPB model.

Exemplifying a combination Fu et al. (2006) is an example of the application of a combination of TPB, upon which the TAM is based, with the TAM itself. Specifically, Fu et al. (2006) integrate TPB and TAM to identify the determinants of Taiwanese taxpayers’ intention to adopt a particular tax-filing method, including manual, twodimensional barcode, or Internet filing. The results, which reveal that taxpayers tend to focus on the usefulness of a tax-filing method and may be fairly pragmatic in the development of general attitudes toward using the filing method, support the application of combined theoretical models.

Exemplifying an extension of the TAM Using an extension of the TAM, the UTAUT (Venkatesh et al., 2003), Van Dijk et al. (2008) built their own multidisciplinary model to explain the acceptance and use of e-government services. First, they assessed the applicability of theoretical models, including Diffusion of Innovations Theory (Rogers, 1995), Domestication Theory (Silverstone, 1991; Silverstone & Haddon, 1996), the Uses and Gratification Theory (Rosengren, 1974; Katz et al. 1974; Palmgreen & Rayburn, 1985), Social Cognitive Theory as applied to media use (Bandura, 1986; Larose et al., 2001; Larose & Eastin, 2004), the TAM, and UTAUT. UTAUT was selected as the basis for the multidisciplinary model developed. The results of the application of this model reveal that the availability of Internet services, the knowledge of this availability, the preference to use digital channels, and the ability and experience to do this are the primary factors in the acceptance and use of government Internet services in the Netherlands.

## E-government acceptance and the TAM

Davis et al.’s (1989) TAM is the most widely applied research paradigm to understand user acceptance of technology (Venkatesh, 2000) and one of the most widely used in the information systems field (King & He, 2006). This wide applicability of the TAM is attributable, in part, to its understandability and simplicity (King & He, 2006). The TAM is based on theories from social psychology, including the TRA (Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980) and the TPB (Ajzen, 1985). The theoretical basis of the TAM suggests the belief–attitude–intention–behavior causal relationship to explain and predict acceptance of new technology. TAM hypothesizes that technology acceptance is determined by a user’s voluntary intentions to use that technology (Al-adawi et al., 2005). Two beliefs about technology, perceived ease of use (PEOU) and PU, determine a user’s attitude toward use of that technology, which in turn determines his intention to use it.

The TAM is a valid and robust model of technology acceptance (King & He, 2006) across levels of user expertise (Gefen, 2002) and across various contexts including social networks (Hossain & de Silva, 2009), health IT applications (Anonymous, 2009), online trading (Lee, 2009), and software firewalls (Kumar et al., 2008) among many others. The TAM potentially has wider applicability (King & He, 2006). Thus, the TAM is regarded as ‘a preeminent theory of technology acceptance in IS research (Al-adawi et al., 2005, p. 5). However, the TAM is not perfect as not all relationships are significant in all studies (King & He, 2006), with inconsistent finding in the predicted effects across various contexts (Legris et al., 2003).

In exploring the research models used in previous studies examining e-government acceptance, the research model employed is primarily the TAM. Consistent with this previous research on e-government acceptance, we apply the TAM, as opposed to other theoretical models. To understand citizen acceptance of t-government, the TAM provides a conceptual framework to explore factors influencing citizen acceptance. We focus our review on previous research that has applied the TAM to e-government acceptance, the predecessor of t-government. Recently, several studies have been conducted to explore the acceptance of e-government services among both citizens and government employees through the application of the TAM, as shown in Table 1.

On the basis of this previous research, we construct a taxonomy, presented in Table 2, of the independent variables included in empirical studies of e-government acceptance applying the TAM. This taxonomy guided the development of our model. In the interest of parsimony, we include two primary constructs from TAM, PU and PEOU. In furtherance of achieving t-government inclusion among techno-disadvantaged citizens at greatest risk of exclusion, we include access barriers, consistent with Carter & Weerakkody (2008), who also considered the digital divide. The focus on digital inequality naturally extends to a focus on socio-economic inequality, given that income and education are major factors in explaining use and non-use of ICT (Jung et al., 2001; Lenhart, 2002; Hsieh et al., 2008). We therefore include demographic characteristics. The variables in our model are discussed in the following sections.

E-government acceptance and the TAM: PEOU and PU Consistent among the studies focusing on citizen use is the inclusion of constructs such as PEOU and PU. While PU was generally found to significantly influence e-government service use (Wang, 2003; Chang et al., 2005; Schaupp & Carter, 2005; Dimitrova & Chen, 2006; Fu et al., 2006; Phang et al., 2006; Carter, 2008; Lee & Rao, 2008; Tan et al., 2008; Wangpipatwong et al., 2008); the significance of PEOU (Wang, 2003; Carter & Be´langer, 2005; Chang et al., 2005; Phang et al., 2006; Carter, 2008; Tan et al., 2008; and Wangpipatwong et al., 2008) received somewhat less empirical support than did PU.

## E-government acceptance and the TAM: demographic characteristics

General demographic characteristics of citizens who use e-government services have been a focus of previous research on e-government. For example, Dimitrova & Chen (2006), who also address the digital divide, concluded that age, education, and income are significantly related to citizens’ engagement with e-government. Thomas & Streib (2003) found that e-government users tend to be white, with a higher income and higher educational level, than general Internet users. Further, e-citizens who were younger in age were more likely to visit e-government websites. The results of a study by Hart-Teeter (2003) were similar, concluding that e-citizens were more likely to be young, white, professional, and college graduates. In the studies addressing the demographic characteristics of e-government users, there is a consensus that e-citizens tend to have a higher educational level and higher income (Hart-Teeter, 2003; Thomas & Streib, 2003; Shelley et al., 2006). Phang et al. (2006) focused on the senior citizen demographic segment, motivated by the government’s political mandate requiring that all sections of the public be served.

## The digital divide and e-government use

Dugdale et al. (2005) directly assess the digital divide and e-government use. Consistent with previous research focusing on demographic characteristics, these researchers found that socioeconomic disadvantage alone influences Internet use. Low income, low educational level, and employment status all increase the risk that households will not be connected to the Internet, thereby contributing to their exclusion from e-citizenship. While these are the very households most likely to use government services, they are the least likely to have the capacity to use online services including e-government. Thus, as the evolution of t-government expands into new forms of access such as mobile devices and television sets, with higher levels of sophistication such as giving citizens personalized service and understanding and responding to citizen needs, those who are underserved are at risk of drifting further from the very services they need. In response to the need for e-government inclusion and equity among all citizens, we present our research model and hypotheses in the next section.

## Research model

On the basis of previous research, we apply the TAM to e-government usage among techno-disadvantaged citizens to explore demographic characteristics and technology acceptance factors. To our knowledge, no other study has applied the TAM to techno-disadvantaged users.

According to Davis et al.’s (1989) TAM, the fundamental determinants of technology usage are external variables that influence PU and PEOU, which influence attitude toward accessing government services using the Internet (Attitude toward Use), in turn influencing BI to use, which determines use (Usage). Attitude toward Use was included in the original formulation of our research model. However, linear regression analysis revealed that Attitude toward Use was not a significant factor influencing Usage, as shown in Table 3. Attitude toward Use was therefore removed from the model, which is consistent with previous research (Carter & Be´langer, 2005). Our research model is shown in Figure 1.

Drawing upon Porter & Donthu (2006), we include age, educational level, employment status, and household income as external variables antecedent to PU and PEOU. To these four, we add Internet experience, based upon Oh et al. (2003) and Venkatesh & Morris (2000), to capture the emergence of Internet usage among our community members. As Porter & Donthu (2006) observed, perceived access barriers could influence the use of personal technology. Thus, to the TAM’s two primary predictors, PU and PEOU, we add a third determinant, access barrier. We thereby extended the TAM by adding five key demographic characteristics as external variables, included perceived access barriers among the key beliefs about a technology influencing its use, and applied the model to a techno-disadvantaged user community.

## Hypotheses

T-government places citizens and communities in the center. The vision is for a future where ‘citizens are informed and engaged’, ‘services are tailored to local need’, and ‘organizations are fit for purpose’ (Cabinet Office, United Kingdom Government, 2006). To achieve this vision an understanding of the acceptance of e-government services by all types of citizens, including those at greatest risk of exclusion, is required. The following relationships among the research model are hypothesized to provide insight into current e-government use among the technodisadvantaged. ## Usage

Accessibility or cost of Internet access may be a factor associated with technology use (Mathieson, 1991; Venkatesh & Brown, 2001). Citizens may be excluded from e-government because they are unable to access the Internet and therefore e-government services (Lau, 2003). Carter & Weerakkody (2008) found access to be a barrier to e-government adoption. Porter & Donthu (2006) found no significant relationship between access barriers and Internet use, based on a survey administered to a convenience sample of real consumers. These two studies provide mixed results. We hypothesize that perceived access barriers, a belief that the Internet is expensive to use and difficult to access, is negatively associated with e-government service usage. According to the TAM (Davis et al., 1989), PU and PEOU predict an individual’s use of technology.

H1a: An individual’s perception of access barriers to using e-government services on the Internet is negatively associated with their usage of e-government services on the Internet.

H1b: An individual’s PEOU of e-government services on the Internet is positively associated with their usage of e-government services on the Internet.

H1c: An individual’s PU of e-government services on the Internet is positively associated with their usage of e-government services on the Internet.

## Access barriers

Consistent with Porter & Donthu (2006), we collectively include age, educational level, employment status, and household income. On the basis of Oh et al. (2003) and Venkatesh & Morris (2000), we add Internet experience. Porter & Donthu (2006) found older individuals perceive more access barriers associated with the Internet. Low levels of education are associated with access barriers (Ryder, 2007). Shelley et al. (2006) note that just 15% of individuals without a high school diploma use the Internet. On the basis of an analysis of previous research, education, followed by income and age are associated with access barriers (Ryder, 2007). Ryder (2007) further noted that low levels of employment are associated with access barriers. Porter & Donthu (2006) found lower-income individuals and Blacks/Hispanics perceive the Internet as more costly. Further, lower-income demographic segments have less access to the Internet than higher income segments (Lau, 2003).

H2a: Age is positively associated with an individual’s perception of access barriers to using e-government services on the Internet.

H2b: Level of education is negatively associated with an individual’s perception of access barriers to using e-government services on the Internet.

H2c: Employment status is negatively associated with an individual’s perception of access barriers to using e-government services on the Internet.

H2d: Household income is negatively associated with an individual’s perception of access barriers to using e-government services on the Internet.

## PEOU and PU

Porter & Donthu (2006) found older and less educated individuals have lower PEOU. Thomas & Streib (2003) found education, income, and age, in addition to race and metro status, to be important predictors of which Internet users will also visit government websites. According to a survey of Internet use, less educated individuals reported insufficient knowledge as one of the main reasons they chose not to use the Internet (NTIA, 2002). Given these findings, we believe it is reasonable to hypothesize that employment status and household income may also have a positive association with PEOU.

Consistent with Oh et al. (2003) and Venkatesh & Morris (2000), we hypothesize a positive association between Internet experience and PEOU.

H3a: Age is negatively associated with an individual’s perception of ease of use in using e-government services on the Internet.

H3b: Level of education is positively associated with an individual’s perception of ease of use in using e-government services on the Internet.

H3c: Employment status is positively associated with an individual’s perception of ease of use in using e-government services on the Internet.

H3d: Household income is positively associated with an individual’s perception of ease of use in using e-government services on the Internet.

H3e: Internet experience is positively associated with an individual’s perception of ease of use in using e-government services on the Internet.

Although a negative association between age and PU was hypothesized by Porter & Donthu (2006), it was not significant. Phang et al. (2006) and Schaupp & Carter (2005) focused on particular age segments in exploring PU. Household income was found to have a significant positive relationship with PU (Porter & Donthu, 2006). On the basis of Oh et al. (2003) and Venkatesh & Morris (2000), we hypothesize a positive association between Internet experience and PU.

H4a: Age is negatively associated with an individual’s perception of usefulness in using e-government services on the Internet.

H4b: Household income is positively associated with an individual’s perception of usefulness in using e-government services on the Internet.

H4c: Internet experience is positively associated with an individual’s perception of usefulness in using e-government services on the Internet.

According to the TAM (Davis et al., 1989), PEOU has a direct effect on PU:

H5: An individual’s perception that e-government services on the Internet are useful is positively associated with their PEOU of e-government services on the Internet.

## Methodology

A questionnaire administered to a representative sample of a technologically underserved community was utilized for this study because this methodology increases generalizability, facilitates replicability, and provides statistical power (Dooley, 2001).

## Sample

The questionnaire respondents are members of the WP Housing Development and neighboring households, a community representative of the disadvantaged side of the digital divide. The sample size of 37 represents a 100% response rate to the questionnaire by those community members who chose to partake in training or avail themselves of the community computer lab hours during spring 2008.

The selection of the WP Housing Development was based on the characteristics of the community members and their circumstances. Located in Chester, PA, U.S.A., this former industrial city was described as ‘one of the most distressed cities in the nation’ (Council of the City of Chester, 1994, p. 167). The low-income population of 39,000, which has the highest infant mortality rate in PA, is 65% African-American (Worsham, 2000). By contrast, the remainder of the county is 91% White (Brief of Amicus Curiae, 1998). Residents of the WP Housing Development, who are 100% African-American, are surrounded by deteriorating houses, vacant lots, and social ills such as low educational performance, teenage pregnancy, vandalism, graffiti, trash, drug use on the streets, violence, drive-by shootings, murders, etc. The community members represent the technologically disadvantaged as few possessed skills or training in computer technology and had virtually no access to computer technology (see Sipior et al., 2004). In response, a computer-training program was launched in the fall of 2000 and continues through the present (see Sipior et al., 2004).

Access to the community computer lab and the training program was initially promoted at their inception in fall 2000 by an emergent leader in the community. She demonstrated excitement and interest in the training program, through word of mouth communication, to announce the program to members of the community and neighborhood. After initial weekly planning meetings, she was observed going from person to person she encountered, on warm summer evenings, talking up the program and distributing fliers and sign-up forms. For years subsequent to fall 2000, word of mouth communication about the community computer-training program and computer lab was supplemented with postal mailings to the community.

## Measures

Constructs were measured using multiple-item scales, based on validated measures from previous research, adapted to the context of the study. Measurement items for PU and PEOU are pervasive in the e-government acceptance literature and can readily be adapted from existing empirical research (e.g., Davis et al., 1989; Carter & Be´langer, 2005; Carter, 2008; Carter & Weerakkody, 2008). Items representing the Attitude toward Use measure were adapted from Porter & Donthu (2006); items for Perceived Access Barriers and Usage measures were adapted from Porter & Donthu (2006). All items were measured using a five-point Likert-type scale, with anchors of ‘strongly agree’ and ‘strongly disagree’. Age, educational level, employment status, and household income were single-item measures, based on categorical responses. Internet experience was measured with an open-ended question asking for the number of years of experience using the Internet. The questionnaire was reviewed by three colleagues who have expertise in both methodology and in the subject area in order to identify any problems with framing and wording of questions. In addition, the questionnaire was pilot tested, resulting in minor modifications.

## Data analysis and results

Quantitative analysis is employed to provide a basic understanding of the relationships among the salient variables. All scales utilized were first validated using factor analysis and Cronbach’s alpha. For hypothesis testing, multiple regression analysis was used. Multiple regression was selected as the approach for hypothesis testing because it allows the researcher to control for specific variables and then identify the incremental variance explained by a given construct (Gallivan, 2004). An advantage of multiple regression is that an extremely large sample size of 100–200 respondents is not necessary; rather, a minimum sample size of 30 respondents is required (Gefen et al., 2000). In this field study, the sample size is small (n ¼ 37) because it is limited to the number of community members who chose to participate in computer training and use.

## Scale validation

Convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs, PU, PEOU, Perceived Access Barriers, Attitude toward Use, and Usage, were assessed. The results of a principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation are presented in Table 5. Five factors with eigenvalues above 1.0 resulted. These five factors explain over 95% of the variance. All of the items reflecting each construct load highly together on one factor and much lower on all the other factors. Internal consistency reliability analysis was performed using Cronbach’s alpha. Each factor showed high internal consistency with alpha coefficients of at least 0.92, above the 0.80 threshold (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994).

## Discussion

In contrast to e-government, t-government aspires to greater inclusivity in serving citizen needs, including those previously excluded (Pagani & Pasinetti, 2009). To attain greater inclusivity as e-government undertakes its journey, evolving toward transformation, an understanding of the factors associated with the usage of e-government services among the techno-disadvantaged is necessary. This study focused on e-government use by members of one technology-deprived community in the U.S. to provide insight into the factors that can increase inclusiveness in the move toward t-government.

Internet access alone does not mean that those who could benefit most from online government services will become users of online government services (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). ## Perceived usefulness

Consistent with Huang et al. (2002) who focused on e-government use in Australia, we found PU was not significantly related to usage. As Huang et al. (2002) noted, this ‘was an unexpected research finding’ that ‘was against the prediction of the TAM theory’. In response, the validity of applying the TAM to citizens and public organizations is called into question (Huang et al., 2002). We concur with Huang et al.’s cautionary approach, recognizing that other important factors may influence e-government use among citizens and public organizations, calling for future research to further explore PU and other factors.

An alternative interpretation of this finding is that the PU, that is the actual benefits of using online services, may not be clear. Pilling & Boeltzig (2007) recommend that government initiatives be undertaken to increase awareness of the benefits and to show citizens the relevance of using online services to meet their needs. In the move toward transformation, innovative applications could exemplify benefits and relevance through sensory-stimulating and engaging technologies such as pictures, graphics, audio, and video clips. For example, video clips could provide step-by-step guidance to complete an online form, or offer assistance in filling out a specific part of a form. This type of innovation has considerable potential for encouraging use of online government services (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007).

## Perceived ease of use

As hypothesized according to the TAM, PEOU was found to be significantly related to usage of e-government services. This is consistent with the findings of Carter (2008), who surveyed citizens at a community concert to investigate e-government use intentions, but PEOU had the weakest influence on intention to use. Carter & Be´langer (2005), however, who focused on the intention to use Virginia state e-government services in the U.S., found PEOU to be significantly related to utilization of e-government services based on a sample of citizens with a varied level of computer and Internet experience, but not among those very experienced. Thus, they suggested that governments attend to the digital divide to preclude the exclusion of certain citizens from benefiting from online services. Such a suggestion is of particular concern as the t-government era of advanced technological applications arrives.

Government websites continue to be less user-friendly than they should be (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). Online government services are not always designed to meet the needs of the digitally or socially excluded (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). Service provision should not only be designed to be easy to use and intuitive, but should also include educational materials and training (Carter & Be´langer, 2005), which are especially responsive to the particular needs of those individuals and communities in need of a reduction in the barriers that exclude them. For example, the Carpenters Connect project in the U.K. innovatively uses digital TV to present how-to video clips to using different aspects of online government services (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). This educational approach is coupled with free in-home Internet access, provided through digital TV via an easy access TV and set top box. The Carpenters Connect is a disadvantaged council estate, which is affordable housing provided by local government in East London, U.K. Such educational materials and training can contribute to leveling the digital playing field (Dugdale et al., 2005). Other research also supports the significance of the impact of PEOU on usage and the design implications for developers of e-government services (Wang, 2003; Chang et al., 2005; Phang et al., 2006; Tan et al., 2008; Wangpipatwong et al., 2008), and by extension, t-government services.

Online government websites are not always easy to use (Pilling & Boeltzig, 2007). The PEOU of e-government websites indirectly enhances usage through PU. The easier e-government services are to use, the more useful these services are perceived to be. This finding further supports the need for developers to design e-government websites that are easy to use, enhancing the usefulness of e-government services and indirectly increasing usage (Wangpipatwong et al., 2008).

## Access barriers

As expected, access barriers are a significant determinant of usage. Research by Pilling & Boeltzig (2007) found a slow increase in the growth of Internet access, with marginalized groups facing many hurdles in accessing and using the Internet, suggesting that more needs to be done to remove access barriers to encourage use of online government services. In response, they recommend that policymakers consider free computers and Internet access be provided to reduce barriers. They further recommend that policymakers should support local initiatives that provide not only access, but an environment conducive to learning, which encourages use of computers, the Internet, and online government services. Trimi & Sheng (2008) view mobile technology as potentially the best solution in overcoming inequitable access to online government services due to the digital divide. We agree that mobile devices remove infrastructure constraints to Internet access, making it more convenient than other venues to obtain government services. However, we would like to underscore the importance of education and training in the use of mobile devices as well as increasing awareness about the benefits and personal relevance of using online services.

Carter & Weerakkody (2008) observe that the digital divide is a major barrier to e-service adoption in the U.S., in both the public and private sectors. They further note that ethnicity is recognized as one of the major components of the digital divide in the U.S. As a result, we concur with Carter & Weerakkody (2008) and call for future research that considers Internet access more fully, especially for comparing adoption across cultures.

## Antecedents of access barriers

Among the proposed antecedents of access barriers, support was found for level of education, employment status, and household income. These three factors are the very three factors that increase the risk that households will be disconnected from the Internet (Dugdale et al., 2005). Socioeconomic disadvantage alone strongly influences Internet use (Dugdale et al., 2005) and thereby influences online use of government services. Our results show that low income, educational level, and employment status all characterize households who perceive that there are barriers to accessing the Internet and e-government services. These demographic characteristics help to define a ‘customer group’, to which government service needs to be responsive (Cabinet Office, United Kingdom Government, 2006). It is these very households who are most likely to be users of government services, but they are the least likely to use e-government services (Dugdale et al., 2005). Indeed, they are at risk of exclusion from technologically sophisticated t-government services, including the Internet and mobile channels as well as increasingly important channels within the digital home.

## Antecedents of PEOU

Apart from employment status, no other antecedents to PEOU were significant. This finding shows that employment status plays a part in explaining technodisadvantaged citizen usage of e-government websites. Holding an employment position may be related to PEOU for several reasons. As an employee, an individual may have access to the Internet at their place of employment. Demands of employment may increase the confidence of an individual in performing new tasks, resulting in an increase in PEOU of online government websites. Thus, those holding employment may be more likely to avail themselves of e-government services.

## Limitations and directions for future research

## Sample

As with any empirical field research, this study has limitations. This study focused on e-government use within one techno-disadvantaged community in the U.S. The empirical basis is quite narrow and comprised of only 37 respondents. The sample size is small because it includes those members, within the natural setting of a public housing community comprised of 158 households and neighboring households, who chose to partake in training or use the community computer lab hours during spring 2008. A questionnaire was administered to all 37 community members who availed themselves of the computer lab, resulting in a 100% response rate, based on signup sheets for use of the lab. This small sample limits the statistical power and generalizability of the results. The figures presented in the results table should be read with caution because they describe a sample of community members of one technologically disadvantaged housing development in one city within the U.S. Although this community and its members are quite typical of the technologically disadvantaged (see Sipior et al., 2004 for a relative assessment of Internet use among participants in this same community contrasted with previous findings characterizing the digital divide), the generalizability of the findings is limited.

Previous research has characterized the digital divide according to various demographic, ethnic, geographic, political, and cultural dimensions (NTIA, 1995, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2002, 2010; Jung et al., 2001; OECD, 2001; Lenhart, 2002; Hsieh et al., 2008; Howard et al., 2009. While income and education, which suggest socioeconomic status (Hsieh et al., 2008), are major factors in explaining use and non-use of ICT (Jung et al., 2001; Lenhart, 2002; Hsieh et al., 2008), we recognize that a complex combination of factors determines in which side of the divide an individual resides (Sipior et al., 2004). Future research could explore the formative measures of use and non-use of specific ICT among the techno-disadvantaged, such as internet TV, cell phones, and RFID-based systems, to assist governments in promoting the reach of t-government. Future research could also attempt to include a larger sample size and a more geographically diverse sample. Since this may not be possible in undertaking field research, future research could be designed to include qualitative approaches, allowing a richer exploration to broaden the research questions.

## Data

The data are based on self-reported practices and assessments, rather than observation or objective data. Further, this study was based on a cross-sectional design. Hence the measures were obtained at only one point in time. Future research could employ a longitudinal design to capture data over an extended time frame to measure changes in e-government website visitation over time. In addition, greater depth of insight could be obtained by using both qualitative and quantitative measures involving in-depth interviews, actual usage, and observations.

## Additional factors

This study utilized a very concise questionnaire in the interest of parsimony. Future research could attempt to capture a more comprehensive set of constructs to explore additional factors influencing usage. For example, social factors, interpreted by Thompson et al. (1991) based on Triandis (1977), are ‘the individual’s internalization of the reference group’s subjective culture, and specific interpersonal agreements that the individual has made with others, in specific social situations’, may be important influencers in e-government use. Factors pertinent to e-government adoption have been found to vary by culture (Carter & Weerakkody, 2008). Previous research indicates that members of the African-American minority segment are expected to have less favorable perceptions of the Internet and lower usage rates (Herbig & Dunphy, 1998). Members of this minority segment are often associated with lower than average incomes (Humphreys, 2004). Those in the lower social status segment tend to be late adopters of innovations (Rogers, 1995) and tend to use each other as sources of information about the usefulness of an innovation (Stanley, 2003), but African-Americans have a smaller base of existing Internet users (NTIA, 2002). Thus, research that identifies salient social factors influencing use may provide guidance in narrowing the gap between being left behind in the e-government era and moving into the t-government era.

## Synthesis with literature on e-government and TAM

Within the literature evaluating e-government acceptance through the application of the TAM. PEOU and PU have the most empirical support in affecting citizens’ acceptance of e-government in general. Overall, there is more statistically significant support for the influence of PU (Wang, 2003; Chang et al., 2005; Schaupp & Carter, 2005; Dimitrova & Chen, 2006; Fu et al., 2006; Hung et al., 2006; Phang et al., 2006; Carter, 2008; Lee & Rao, 2008; Tan et al., 2008; Wangpipatwong et al., 2008; Vathanophas et al., 2009), than for PEOU (Wang, 2003; Carter & Be´langer, 2005; Chang et al., 2005; Fu et al., 2006; Hung et al., 2006; Phang et al., 2006; Carter, 2008; Tan et al., 2008; Wangpipatwong et al., 2008; Vathanophas et al., 2009).

Our results are contrary with the finding that PU is a significant predictor of e-government usage, but consistent with the finding that PEOU is a significant predictor. While Carter (2008) observed that most studies addressing e-government conclude that PU is the most important determinant of use, our results are consistent with Huang et al. (2002) in finding that PU was not significant in influencing e-government usage among public citizens. However, our sample is comprised of citizens who are members of a public housing community, and neighboring households, and are among the digitally disadvantaged. Our finding may reflect this circumstance. We agree with Carter & Be´langer’s (2005) observation that if this is the case, ‘governments have to be careful with the potential for the digital divide, to prevent certain citizens from benefiting from online services’ (p. 18). In their commentary on the TAM, Benbasat & Barki (2007) call for future research to ‘reach more comprehensive understanding of what influences adoption and acceptance in different IT use contexts’ (p. 6) and to investigate ‘what actually makes a system useful’ (p. 2). We believe the need to address these objectives in future research is underscored by our findings that, among the digitally disadvantaged, PU is not a significant predictor of e-government usage while PEOU is. Further, PEOU was found to be an antecedent of PU. This finding is consistent with previous e-government research (Wang, 2003; Chang et al., 2005; Fu et al., 2006; Phang et al., 2006; Tan et al., 2008; Wangpipatwong et al., 2008; Vathanophas et al., 2009).

We further focused on PEOU by investigating demographic characteristics as antecedents. Within the literature on e-government evaluated by applying the TAM, we did not identify any previous studies that examined an association between demographic characteristic and PEOU. We found that members of the digitally disadvantaged community who are employed are likely to hold a perception that e-government is easy to use. Such findings, which encompass demographic characteristics, may be important in gaining a more comprehensive understanding of what influences e-government use, as called for by Benbasat & Barki (2007).

Perceived access barriers were found to be a significant determinant of e-government usage. This finding is consistent with the only empirical e-government study we identified, which considered this variable, undertaken by Carter & Weerakkody (2008). Internet accessibility was found to be a significant predictor of e-government adoption in the U.S., but not in the United Kingdom. Our sample specifically targeted the digital divide. Carter & Weerakkody’s (2008) sample was drawn from the general public, including neighbors, friends, and family of one of the researchers and those at an outdoor shopping area in North West London, which represents ‘one of London’s less affluent areas and is made up largely of ethnic minority (i.e., South Asian and African) citizens’ (p. 477). Carter & Weerakkody (2008) and Dimitrova & Chen (2006) undertook the only empirical studies we identified, which examine e-government use among the techno-disadvantaged. Since socio-economic inequality was empirically found to be associated with different behaviors (Hsieh et al., 2008), it is important that additional studies be undertaken in furtherance of building an understanding of the role of perceived access barriers in e-government use and adoption. We investigated demographic characteristics and found education, employment status, and household income to be significant predictors of perceived access barriers in e-government use.

## Conclusion

This research applied the TAM to e-government service usage among members of a public housing community on the disadvantaged side of the divide. The citizencentric approach of t-government, which is to have an impact on citizens’ daily lives, requires a consideration of convenient Internet access among such citizens. The removal of barriers that exclude individuals from participating in the digital age is a prerequisite to e-government inclusion, and could certainly preclude t-government inclusion. Creating and retaining the capacity and capability to utilize technology to make citizens feel more engaged with the process of democratic government (Cabinet Office, United Kingdom Government, 2006) must involve a consideration of all communities, including those technologically disadvantaged. This is especially important as t-government achieves seamless handovers across service delivery channels, such as from the telephone to the Internet, and exploits mobile technologies and new digital channels for service delivery (Cabinet Office, United Kingdom Government, 2006).

In addition to access barriers to e-government services on the Internet, our study found PEOU, level of education, employment status, and household income of community members to be significant determinants of e-government service usage. Overall, the results serve as a reminder that t-government will not reach technodisadvantaged segments of society unless access and ease in using such services can be enhanced for members with low educational levels, unemployed status, and low income.

While our study did not find PU to be a significant determinant of e-government usage, we nonetheless believe that as e-government evolves toward t-government, initiatives to improve accessibility to the Internet should be accompanied by efforts to increase PU in addition to ease of use for the digitally excluded. On the basis of mixed research results for the significance of PU in e-government studies, we concur with Carter & Be´langer (2005) by encouraging future examination of this and related constructs within e-government usage research. Carter & Be´langer (2005) combined PU and relative advantage as a construct labeled relative advantage, which was found not to be a statistically significant predictor of intent to use state e-government services. This result prompted them to encourage future investigation of PU and relative advantage, to which they added performance expectancy based on Venkatesh et al. (2003).
