# Longitudinal use of method rationale in method configuration: an exploratory study

## Abstract

Organizations that implement a company-wide method to standardize the way that systems development is carried out still have a need to adapt this method to specific projects. When adapting this method the end results should align with the basic philosophy of the original method. To this end, goal-driven situational method engineering has been proposed. However, there are no longitudinal studies on systems developers’ use of such approaches and their intentions to balance their need of adaptation with the basic philosophy of the original method. This paper explores how goal-driven method configuration has been used by two project teams in six successive systems development projects, with the intention to balance the goals and values of a specific method with the systems developers’ need for method adaptation. We do that through the use of method rationality resonance theory. Through content examples of method configurations, we report on (a) lessons learned from the project teams’ work on balancing the goals of the company-wide method with their needs and (b) theoretical development of the method rationality resonance theory.

## Introduction

Efficient and effective development processes are important for systems development organizations worldwide. Many organizations implement a company-wide method to standardize the way that systems development is carried out, either introducing an off-the-shelf product or developing an in-house method (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a). However, it is currently believed that a one-size-fits-all method for systems development does not exist (Henderson-Sellers & Serour, 2005; A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2006; Fitzgerald et al, 2006; Henderson-Sellers & Ralyte´, 2010). Existing research has shown that all projects require flexible method support to handle their unique characteristics (Russo et al, 1995; Tolvanen, 1998; Fitzgerald et al, 2003; Serour & Henderson-Sellers, 2004).

The search to find a solution to this challenge has been on the research agenda for quite some time (Basili & Rombach, 1987; Kumar & Welke, 1992; Cameron, 2002; Fitzgerald et al, 2003). Situational method engineering is often suggested as a way to achieve a flexible and situated development process. Existing research shows a wide range of situational method engineering approaches (Odell, 1996; Ralyte´ et al, 2003; Niknafs & Ramsin, 2008; Sunyaev et al, 2008; Henderson-Sellers & Ralyte´, 2010) and computerized environments (e.g., Plihon, 1996; Harmsen, 1997; Karlsson & Wistrand, 2004; Rossi et al, 2004) for achieving this end. It is possible to distinguish between at least two types of approaches (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a). The first type deals with this challenge through the construction of project-specific methods. Modular constructs, such as method fragments and method chunks (Brinkkemper, 1996; Rolland & Prakash, 1996), are used to assemble a method that fits the specific project characteristics. The second type, method configuration, takes its point of departure in one method, a base method, which is then adapted to suit the project at hand (e.g., Cameron, 2002; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2004; Bajec et al, 2007; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b).

A method is adapted to fit the situation at hand in order to reach rationality resonance (Stolterman & Russo, 1997). It means that systems developers attempt to harmonize their goals with the goals found in the method, or parts thereof, which they chose to use. But it should, at the same time, align with the basic philosophy of the original method. This means that it is necessary to preserve the goals and values of that method (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a). Otherwise, there is a risk of losing the method’s core idea. To achieve this balance, a goal-driven situational method engineering approach is often suggested (e.g., Rolland & Prakash, 1996; Gonzalez-Perez et al, 2007; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b). However, while goal-driven approaches seem adequate for addressing the need to promote the goals and values of a particular method, this process of balancing these two sides is not well understood (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a).

The aim of this paper is to explore the evolution of systems developers’ intentions to balance goals and values of the base method with their needs of method adaptation, when using goal-driven method configuration. We do that through the use of method rationality resonance theory (A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2006; Wistrand, 2009) in a longitudinal study. We follow the individual members of two project teams over three successive projects per team, with each project sharing several characteristics. This allows us to investigate how they work with method goals over a longer period of time. In doing so, we advance existing research on goal-driven method configuration showing that (a) systems developers strive to anchor their configuration decisions in goals of the base method and (b) the achieved results depend on the systems developers’ knowledge about the method, earlier experience and social interaction. In addition, we also contribute to the method rationality resonance theory in four parts: (a) introducing time as a concept, (b) providing a more nuanced view on the concept of collective rationality resonance, (c) identifying a function that influence method rationality resonance and (d) elaborating on how to measure method rationality resonance.

The paper is structured as follows. The following two sections introduce existing research on goal-driven method engineering and the method rationality resonance theory. Section “Research Method” presents the adopted research approach. In the “The MMC and MC Sandbox Key Concepts ” section, we introduce the key concepts of Method for Method Configuration (MMC)

and MC Sandbox to create a basic understanding of the goal-driven method configuration tools that are used in the longitudinal case study. We go on to discuss the goals and values behind eXtreme Programming (XP), the method that we followed in the six projects. In the “Emperical experiments” section, we report on the longitudinal experience on using goal-driven method configuration. Finally, we draw conclusions and point to potential future research.

## Related research

Existing research in the field of situational method engineering shows that there are different types of mechanisms for selecting method parts. One type of selection mechanism that is found in several different meta-methods is the use of method rationale (Nilsson, 1999; Rossi et al, 2000; Ralyte´ et al, 2003; Rossi et al, 2004; Gonzalez-Perez et al, 2007; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b). Basically it means that the method rationale of the modular method construct is used to determine if the method module is relevant for the situation at hand. The mechanism itself is termed and implemented slightly differently, depending on the approach used. For example, Rolland & Prakash (1996) and Ralyte´ et al (2003) used the concept of intention, while Brinkkemper (1996), Harmsen (1997) and Gonzalez-Perez et al (2007) used the goal concept. The goal concept was also used by Wistrand & Karlsson (2004) and Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2004, 2009b). However, they introduced a second complementary concept of value to describe an ideal that is accepted by an individual or a group.

As discussed in the ‘Introduction’, there are a limited number of studies discussing how goal-driven situational method engineering is used in practice. A number of studies on the development of conceptual method rationale frameworks exist (e.g., Tolvanen, 1998; A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk & A<sup>˚</sup> hlgren, 1999; A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk & Wistrand, 2003). However, there are some notable exceptions. Wistrand et al (2008) have explored how method rationale can be used in teaching methods. Within the area of Computer Assisted Methodology Engineering (CAME) tools, studies have been carried out on how to capture method rationale. For example, Oinas-Kukkonen (1996) studied the use of method rationale in communicating a situational method between method engineers and systems developers. Rossi et al (2004) presented empirical examples of method rationale captured during situational method engineering and method use.

Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2009a) reported on project members adapting XP using goal-driven method configuration. They found that systems developers strove to preserve the goals of the original method during their work. Team members used the goals of XP to make well-informed decisions concerning method configuration, even in cases when goal conflicts were introduced into the situated method. This study was invaluable to our own work, giving us snapshots on: (a) how systems developers worked with method goals during method configuration of isolated projects and (b) systems developers’ appreciation of different method constructs at a specific point in time.

We can conclude, then, that when it comes to goaldriven situational method engineering approaches no longitudinal studies have been carried out on the systems developers’ intentions to promote specific method values and goals. Existing research about goal-driven situational method engineering seems to focus on conceptual development and implementation as computerized tool support. Research that aims to provide a better understanding of the use of goal-driven situational method engineering, and how a method rationale evolves over time, is much less common.

## The method rationality resonance theory

Systems development methods have several functions in systems development. One of these is a means to communicate knowledge about the systems development process. These best practices are based on goals the method designer stresses are important (Jayaratna, 1994; Goldkuhl et al, 1997). However, a basic condition for a method to be accepted by a systems developer is that it is perceived as useful in practice (Riemenschneider et al, 2002). Stolterman & Russo (1997) discussed this perceived usefulness in terms of rationality resonance. Hence, this is similar to the notion of value congruence between a method and its users, which is discussed by Jayaratna (1994).

A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2006) has laid out a theory of method rationality resonance. The theory consists of six components: actor, value, goal and method fragment/method component (in-concept and in-action). He used the method fragment construct to describe method modules, while Wistrand (2009) in a later extension used the method component. The important aspect, however, is relating the method module to its rationale, that is, its goals and values. Consequently, it is possible to use other method module concepts, such as the method chunk (Rolland et al, 1998). We choose to use the method component concept in the theoretical discussion, as it fits the goaldriven method configuration approach we chose to study.

How these components are associated with each other is illustrated in Figure 1 as a unified modelling language (UML) class diagram. Wistrand (2009) has added collective method rationale to the theory and acknowledged that it is possible to achieve rationality resonance on a project team level. This theory is anchored in Weber’s (1978) notion of practical rationality, but also aligns with purposeful systems as described in general systems theory (Ackoff, 1971). It involves individuals who consider different ends when acting socially at a given time and, in some systematic way, choose the means to achieve these ends. These ends are in turn related to values, which means that individuals act in accordance with certain principles. In terms of the method rationality resonance theory, this means that:

1. Method components, which are self-contained parts of a method. can achieve a set of goals.

2. An actor chooses a method component to achieve certain goals. Wistrand (2009) termed this achieving rationality resonance.

3. Values guide an actor towards the goals considered worthy of achieving.

4. Principles guide an actor towards acceptable method components.

In Figure 1, ‘goal base’ corresponds to (1) in the list above, capturing the potential goals that can be achieved through the use of a method component. A method component can be used to achieve several goals. Furthermore, a given goal can be achieved by more than one method component. Note that these goals exist at different levels of granularity. For example, the goal of the ‘Vision Card’ method component is probably ‘to capture the purpose of the system’. This, however, is only a means to achieve a more high-level goal, such as ‘customer requirements understood’. Hence, goals exist in hierarchies or networks, as illustrated by the goal achievement association. The same principle also applies to values. This is in line with general systems theory, where the rationale can be understood as ‘a hierarchy of systems’ (Skyttner, 2005).

The ‘goal rationale/rationality resonance’ corresponds to (2). It shows the actor’s intention for choosing the method component. This intention may correspond to a direct goal of a method component or a more high-level goal. In addition, the actor does not need to take the complete set of method component’s goals into consideration when making that choice, or even be aware of the complete set. Depending on the systems developers choice they will alter the situational method (in-action) and the goals that are included. Hence, these choices affect how the situational method aligns with the basic philosophy of the original method (in-concept).

The ‘value rationale’ corresponds to (3) in our list above, illustrating the values that guide the selection of a method component. Finally, the ‘value base’ association represents the actor’s principles, which corresponds to (4). The framework captures the fact that not all values are operationalized as goals. Instead, they exist as guiding principles that the actor turns to as new development situations emerge. Moreover, the value base can be an empty set, as it represents only known explicit values. Depending on the stage of analysis, these values may still have to be elicited. Rationality resonance occurs (a) on the individual level when an actor agrees with the values and goals behind a method component and (b) on a group level when two or more actors share the same method rationale; that is, when they agree to a common set of values, goals and method components.

To summarize, the method rationality resonance theory is an analytical framework to understand why systems developers chose to use certain parts of a method. The theory can be used to explain how systems developers prioritize different parts of a method based on the goals and values that they hold. Consequently, the theory can be used, by both managers and researchers, to predict potential conflicts of interests in a project team and project members’ non-compliance with a decided method.

## Research method

This study can be classified as a longitudinal multi case study (Lee, 1989; Yin, 1994). We followed two project teams over three successive projects per team, between 2007 and 2011. These projects revealed the method configuration process in a software development company in Sweden, which builds industrial software for the global market. To a large extent, the company is a virtual organization with a philosophy of working with systems development projects on a network basis. Consequently, only certain development competences exist in-house and project teams are formed through external contacts.

The choice of a situational method engineering approach was based on the following premises: (1) the approach had to support method configuration, (2) the approach had to be based on a method rationale selection mechanism, (3) the approach had to have a computerized support (an industry requirement) and (4) the approach had to support different types of systems developments methods so as not to restrict the industrial collaboration to a specific method that would limit the range of industrial partners. MMC was selected, together with the computerized tool support MC Sandbox (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b).

The choice of industrial partner was based on the following premises: (1) they had to agree to provide access to systems development projects that shared characteristics, (2) they had to have project teams that could work together over several projects and (3) they had to agree to set aside resources to introduce MMC and MC Sandbox. The choice of XP as the base method was made by the industrial partner. It is not used as a true organization-wide method, but rather with specific clients. However, as one of the owners stated, this repeated use is similar to that found in an organizationwide method, as ‘we share the same challenge, to use a specific method on many occasions’.

The projects were selected from the software development company’s existing project portfolio. A compilation of the project characteristics is presented in Tables 1 and 2. The projects are labelled 1–3 and 4–6 in chronological order for the first project team and the second team, respectively. As we were interested in following the same project teams, the range of possible projects was limited. In addition, following larger projects would also have been difficult to combine with the longitudinal approach, as larger projects often require more time to complete. Hence, the choice of projects was to some extent a practical trade-off and, as a consequence, our focus on configuration work in smaller projects is a limitation of this study.

The author acted as project manager during Project 1, but was not responsible for the method configuration. As discussed in Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2009a), where Project 1 was published earlier, this was a necessary trade-off to gain access to the project. It was also an obvious limitation in terms of the research design, as it introduces the risk of researcher bias. As discussed in the ‘Data collection’ section, this is somewhat mitigated through the use of several different data sources. It was also important to replicate our findings in a second project group. The author played no active role in Projects 2–6. Earlier, the author had mentored two of the systems developers when introducing MMC in the company and been involved in building the initial version of the base method (XP) in MC Sandbox. The latter was necessary to enable method configuration using MMC.

The study’s results, which are based on six projects, cannot be statistically generalized. Indeed, some researchers have argued for the use of a nomothetic approach, as case studies are seen to be too context-specific to offer the possibility of generalization (Benbasat et al, 1987). On the other hand, other scholars have argued that case study research allows for generalization, not to other cases, but rather to theoretical constructs (Eisenhardt, 1989; Klein & Myers, 1999; Lee & Baskerville, 2003). In this case it means contributing to the further development of the method rationality resonance theory, which is found in the ‘Discussion’ section. Yin (1994) argued that a case study may reveal important information about the object under study through the rich details it provides. In order to ensure high quality in case descriptions, he prescribed the use of multiple data sources and respondent checking. Thus, we do not claim that our findings, which report on the project teams’ work to balance the overall goals of the base method with their needs for method adaptation, are valid beyond the projects investigated. Nevertheless, our findings are useful to refine the method rationality resonance theory.

Method configuration was distributed among the developers, with the majority of the method configuration decisions taken together during method-user-centred method configuration workshops (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2005). During such workshops, the objective is to strive for consensus decisions and to increase understanding for each other’s method needs. In order to facilitate such workshops, each project team received an introduction to MMC and MC Sandbox, which lasted for approximately 3 h. This introduction was delivered by the mentored systems developers.

## Data collection

Data sources from the six systems development projects included: configurations stored in MC Sandbox, logbooks from the method configuration workshops, interviews with the developers and questionnaires. This combination provided a multi-method approach to data triangulation. The method configuration workshops were carried out along the lines of MMC, MC Sandbox and methoduser-centred method configuration (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2005). As discussed above, all project members were involved in the configuration work. An overview of the configuration results is found in Table 4. The point of departure for the configuration work was XP, as implemented in MC Sandbox (e.g., see Figure 3), together with method components taken from complementary methods (mainly Rational Unified Process and SCRUM), and existing configuration packages and templates (details of these concepts can be found in the section ‘The MMC and MC Sandbox key concepts’). The project teams could gradually extend MC Sandbox’s content, based on their needs and earlier experience of other methods. During the configuration workshops, the systems developers used the goals in the external view of method components to discuss the implications of choosing different method components. The internal view was only used when the method component content was unknown to them. During Project 1, one project member was in charge of documenting the configuration decisions in MC Sandbox; however, during other projects, the responsibility rotated. In addition, the driver role shifted during the workshops depending on which method parts were in focus. MC Sandbox offers the possibility to store information about the project team’s configuration decisions, together with comments that explain why these decisions were made. The decisions are stored in a structured format, while the comments are stored as free text (e.g., see Figure 3). Thus, the latter made it possible to retrieve citations from the configuration workshops for research purposes.

The author wrote the logbooks during the configuration workshops. These logbooks contained observations about the process, and the project members’ arguments and decisions. Hence, when it came to arguments and configuration decisions, it was possible to compare them with the data in MC Sandbox to reduce researcher bias. In addition, when documenting the configuration process, data about problematic areas and viewpoints on these areas was captured. This data is not found in MC Sandbox.

The author interviewed each project member after they had participated in the configuration workshops. This made it possible to track their standpoints concerning the situational method for each project: (a) at a specific point in time and (b) through changes in project members preferences between the configuration workshops. The interviews were semi-structured using the logbooks and data from MC Sandbox as an interview guide (Patton, 1990). During the interviews we centred on the method configuration process and how, and to what extent, the configuration decisions fulfilled the project members’ needs; in other words, whether or not, rationality resonance was achieved.

In addition, the interviews were complemented with a questionnaire that used Likert survey questions to measure project members’ rationality resonance. This questionnaire was designed by the author and based on the selection of method components. For each method component we used the following statement together with a four-degree disagree–agree response scale: ‘I find the goals of method component X useful given project characteristic Y’. The response scale had the following structure: (1) strongly disagree, (2) disagree, (3) agree and (4) strongly agree. The four-degree scale was used to avoid people ‘sitting on the fence’.

## Analysis

The analysis was carried out using parts of the method rationale resonance theory. The parts used in this paper are the associations ‘goal base’, ‘goal rationale/rationality resonance’ and ‘collective rationality resonance’, and the components that these associations connect (actor, goal, method component). This choice made it possible to build on existing knowledge about systems developers’ intentions to preserve the goals of a base method during goal-driven method configuration (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a).

The analysis was carried out in three steps. First, we used the ‘goal base’ to identify which goals are associated with each of the method components in the base method (XP). Second, we used the ‘goal base’ to identify which goals are associated with each of the method components that the systems developers added to the configuration packages, and hence included in the situational method. Third, we used the ‘goal rationale/rationality resonance’ association to analyse how each systems developer viewed the usefulness of the included components. This was carried out using the Likert-scale data and interview data searching for their viewpoint on specific components. We carried out this analysis for each project team, on three different occasions, as presented in Tables 6, 8 and 10, in order to capture how the rationality resonance evolved. For the first project team we compared the ‘goal rationale/rationality resonance’ association results from Projects 1, 2 and 3, and for the second project team we compared the results from Projects 4, 5 and 6. This comparison is both based on the interviews and the survey data from individual systems developers. Finally, we compared the median, the first quartile and the third quartile of the survey data (these calculations have been carried out using the Percintil.INC function in Microsoft Excel) to compare the ‘collective rationality resonance’. Comparing median for a given component from project to project allowed for an assessment of how the average developer’s opinions changed over time, while comparing the span between the first quartile and the third quartile for a given component from project to project served to show convergence among developer opinions about that component. A cross analysis between the project teams was also carried out.

## The MMC and MC Sandbox key concepts

MMC and the computerized tool support MC Sandbox were developed by Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2004, 2009b) to support method configuration, the planned and systematic adaptation of a specific method through the use of reusable assets. The meta-method is built around three core concepts: method components (Karlsson &

Wistrand, 2006), configuration packages (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b) and configuration templates (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009b).

## The method component concept

Method configuration should be systematic. A modularization concept is therefore needed. Through the use of such a concept, specific method parts can be suppressed, added or exchanged in a coherent way. In addition, through the use of a modularization concept we can achieve information hiding during method configuration, focusing only on the necessary details. MMC is based on the method component concept – a selfcontained part of a method that expresses the transformation of one or several artefacts into a defined target artefact, and the rationale for such a transformation. This concept can be viewed as an aggregate of method fragments. Hence, the concept aligns with the method chunk construct where the connection between process and product parts of a method is ‘hard-wired’ (Henderson-Sellers et al, 2008). Henderson-Sellers & Ralyte´ (2010) argued that the advantage of this type of construction is ‘speed of usage insofar as there is often a smaller’ number of modules that need to be handled. However, unlike method chunks and method fragments, a method component is non-hierarchic and exists on a ‘fixed’ level of granularity. A method component is defined based on the artefacts (or deliverables) of the base method; hence the types of identifiable artefacts determine its granularity.

A method component consists of two parts: content and interface. The content describes the transformation process from input to output, and the method rationale. The interface enables information hiding, by presenting a selection of those parts. The basic idea is to avoid focusing on the method component’s inner working during the method configuration. Consequently, this idea is closely related to the traditional usage of the component concept and the principle of encapsulation in software engineering (McIlroy, 1968; Stevens & Pooley, 2006).

Method component content The method component’s transformation process is described using an aggregate of method elements. A method element is a part of a method that manifests a method component’s target state or facilitates the transformation from one defined state to another. Method elements are constituted by prescribed actions (e.g., split tasks), concepts (e.g., task card), notations (e.g., textual), artefacts (e.g., task) and actor roles (e.g., developer). A development activity is essentially a set of prescribed actions with associated sequence restrictions that guide project members’ actions in specific situations. In performing these actions, developers use a set of concepts that direct their attention towards specific phenomena in the problem domain. These concepts are used to understand both the problem domain and the method itself. The development results are documented in artefacts using the prescribed notation, which gives the concepts a concrete representation. Thus, artefacts are both deliverables from and input to development activities in the process. However, it should be noted that methods are viewed as heuristic procedures in MMC. Accordingly, specified inputs are viewed as recommended inputs. Finally, actor roles describe the functions that actors play in the method component. The selection of actor roles is determined by the prescribed actions that are needed for the transformation process. The method component’s rationale consists of two concepts: goals and values. Each method element has been included by the method creator for a reason, which is made explicit by associating method elements with goals. Furthermore, these goals are anchored in values, which are principles that make goals worthy of achieving. Together, these goals and values reflect the underlying perspective of the method, from which the method component originates.

When working with MMC, method rationale is more important than the deliverable itself. Through the method rationale it is possible to address the goals that are essential to fulfilling the overall aim of a specific project. The method component content is viewed as a means to achieve these goals. Consequently, method rationale can help systems developers to avoid losing sight of the ultimate result. Indeed, it can help them find alternative ways forward.

Figure 2 shows the internal view of the Task method component, as it is represented in MC Sandbox. The graphical user interface has three main parts. The leftmost part contains a tree view of all the method elements included. Here, for example, we find the task artefact. The middle section shows the rationale of the method component, where the goals of the individual method elements are modelled using goal graph technique. Finally, the rightmost part provides links to external sources that contain method element content. For example, here we find a link to an external HTML page that gives a description of the task concept. The use of these external sources depends on how method components are classified during configuration work. As MMC focuses on whether or not to suppress, exchange or introduce deliverables in the development process, no modifications are made to the modelling languages or the computerized tools required in the process.

The method component interface The purpose of the method component interface is to reduce the complexity during method configuration through information hiding. It draws on the fact that the primary interest during method configuration is in the results offered by the method component and the required inputs, although it is not completely possible to separate a method component’s results from how they are achieved. However, previous empirical findings (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a, b) have shown that at this stage systems developers are less interested in how a development activity is executed. The method interface represents an external view of a method component, containing references to a selection of method elements and rationale that are relevant to the configuration task at hand.

Concretely, this means that the primary focus is on the method component’s overall goals and the artefacts, designated as input and/or deliverable (output). The latter classification is necessary in order to deal with the four fundamental actions that can be performed on an artefact (Gonzalez-Perez et al, 2007): create, read, update and delete. In MMC, an artefact is classified as a deliverable when it is created only by the method component. In cases where the artefact can be updated by the same method component, it is also classified as an input. As per the definition, a component can take one or several input artefacts, but has only one type of deliverable. Finally, the interface expresses the method component’s overall goals, which represent the method rationale. ## The configuration package concept

Method configuration is about deciding whether or not method components shall be performed in a base method and, if they shall, to what extent. In MMC this is done through the use of method rationale, which is expressed in the method components’ interface as discussed above. Method components are classified with regard to how relevant their overall goals are for a specific project characteristic. A characteristic is viewed as a question about one isolated aspect of the development situation. Consequently, each characteristic addresses one or several method components and their purpose. Typically, there are several possible answers to a posed question, constituting the characteristic’s dimension. Each answer corresponds to a configuration of the base method suitable for a characteristic’s value. This answer is called a configuration package, which contains the classified method components from the base method. If a base method lacks a necessary method rationale, it is also possible to include method components from complementing methods.

The classification of method components is based on a two-dimensional classification schema, presented in Table 3. The horizontal dimension focuses on the potential for achieving rationality resonance between the base method’s content and the systems developers’ intensions, shown as ‘satisfactory’, ‘unsatisfactory’ and ‘missing’. That is to say, to what degree an overlap is found between the method component’s goals and the developers’ preferences. The four aspects of the vertical dimension, shown as ‘none’, ‘insignificant’, ‘normal’ or ‘significant’, complete the matrix. This dimension is referred to as the attention given to the method component, and focuses on how important the method component’s goal is in relation to the project’s overall goal. Together, these two dimensions provide different variants of the fundamental method configuration scenarios (Wistrand & Karlsson, 2004) that need to be supported: selection, exchange and addition.

Figure 3 illustrates the modelling of configuration packages in MC Sandbox. The graphical user interface has three main sections. The upper left part contains the interface and classification status of a selected method component. The upper right part contains available characteristics and configuration packages. Finally, the lower part contains the method modelling area. For example, in Figure 3, the Task component is selected in the modelling area. This area makes use of the external view of method components and each method component is presented as a rectangle. Different colours are used in MC Sandbox to illustrate the method component’s classification. Arrows connecting method component show the flow of artefacts between the components, either as one-way or two-way. For example, in Figure 3 the result from the Task component is recommended as an input to the Signed Task component. In addition, the Task component’s interface is presented in the upper left section of the screen. In this case, the interface contains the overall goal (To decompose User Stories into tasks), the recommended input (the User Story, the Supplementary Specification and the Iteration Plan) and the output (the Task). In the upper right corner of Figure 3, we find the complete set of existing configuration packages available for the base method in use. The tree structure is sorted by the characteristics that match the configuration packages.

## The configuration template concept

Configuration packages and characteristics are conceptual tools to simplify analyses of the base method. However, projects do have multiple characteristics and there is a need to handle these more complex situations. To that end, MMC includes configuration templates. A configuration template is a combined method configuration that covers the complete base method. It is based on one or more configuration packages, for a set of recurrent project characteristics. The purpose of the concept is to reuse combinations of configuration packages, which target development situation types that are common within the organization. The graphical user interface for defining a configuration template has a similar structure to that used for defining configuration packages. Hence, the screen is horizontally divided into two sections. The upper part contains information about the selected method component and existing configuration templates. The lower part is divided vertically, where the left part contains the functionality to select configuration packages and the right part contains a method modelling area. The selection is based on the set of available characteristics and configuration packages. One configuration package can be chosen per characteristic and, if a characteristic is irrelevant, it can be left out when selecting configuration packages.

The empirical work is based on method configurations from six projects. Owing to space limitations it is impossible to describe all method configurations in detail. ## Discussion

The research objective of this paper was to explore the evolution of systems developers’ intentions to balance goals and values of the base method with their needs of method adaptation, when using goal-driven method configuration. Hence, this study has the unusual characteristic of being a longitudinal study, which is not common in the area of method engineering. Among the empirical studies that do exist (e.g., Oinas-Kukkonen, 1996; Rossi et al, 2004; Wistrand et al, 2008), it is more common to follow a single case. With regards to this aspect and the configuration processes in the six cases, some notable lessons can be learned with regard to: (a) the goal-driven method configuration practice, (b) MMC and MC Sandbox and (c) the method rationality resonance theory.

## The goal-driven method configuration practice

It is possible to verify earlier findings (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a) that the systems developers strive to anchor their configuration decisions in the goals of the base method – although there is not always consensus on how to operationalize these goals in an individual project. One example is the exchange of User Stories for the benefit of Use Cases (see Table 8), which was controversial during Projects 1 and 4. Other examples of disagreements in both project teams concerned the rationality resonance of the Business Glossary component and the Metaphor component.

The longitudinal perspective of our study has shown that there was an additional complexity to goal-driven method configuration decisions. As presented in Tables 6 and 8, in both project teams the systems developers’ rationality resonance changed between the projects. Consequently, a systems developer’s view of the usefulness of a method or its parts in relation to a given characteristic was not constant over time. For example, the systems developers who did not appreciate the Use Case component, as already discussed, changed their minds between Projects 1 and 2, as well as between Projects 4 and 5. In Project 1, the Use Case component was initially classified ‘exchange as is’ (see Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a for more details), a classification that not all of the first team’s members agreed on. In Project 3, this component was classified as ‘exchange emphasized’, a classification that was reused by the second team in Projects 4 and 6. Hence, our findings have shown that all systems developers finally agreed on this classification in both project teams. One of the systems developers in the first project team who opposed the use of the Use Case component over the User Story component stated that: ‘I admit that I was against the exchange. But I have changed my mind, since I now realize that we deliver good results’ and ‘it has been shown that this component works for us. And the difference between these two ways of working shall not be exaggerated’. Similar opinions were expressed in the second team: ‘after using it I find it applicable’ and ‘it is not an issue any more’. Thus, goal-driven method configuration implemented through method user centred workshops seems to help project teams move towards consensus over time.

Our empirical findings also corroborated earlier findings that systems developers introduce goal contradictions into the situational method (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a) when using goal-driven method configuration. Moreover, we have been able to show that these configurations, which include these contradictions, were reused in later projects. These configuration decisions are carefully crafted; however, as we have shown, the method users prioritize between method goals based on their earlier experiences. For example, the studied project teams introduced business modelling components into the method, and reused this configuration package (Knowledge about business process ¼ Low) during six projects. Business modelling components are to some extent in conflict with the agile goal ‘Valuable software delivered early and continuously’. However, the project members seemed to prioritize the more high-level goal, ‘customer satisfied’, above this. They argued, based on their earlier experience, that ‘if you do not understand their business needs, you may not solve the real problems’. Hence, having a good understanding of the business is important for fulfilling this more high-level goal.

## MMC and MC Sandbox

With regard to MMC and MC Sandbox, two lessons can be learned. First, we can confirm Karlsson’s & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk’s (2009a, b) conclusions on the usefulness of implementing an interface/body model in modular method engineering constructs when method users are active participants in goal-driven method configuration. This is exemplified by the following statements: ‘you do not need those details here’ and ‘at this stage it is enough to know that these things are achieved. I think all of us know how they are to be done’. This is an important verification in the sense that the use of interfaces has been debated. For example, Henderson-Sellers et al (2008) questioned its usefulness, stating that ‘no information needs to be hidden’.

Second, as our investigation was a longitudinal study, we were able to show how MC Sandbox acted as an organizational memory for the project teams when they made changes in existing configurations. This is illustrated by the following voices: ‘the tool [MC Sandbox] helped us with why a component had been added’ and ‘we can start with why we made the decision earlier, and then discuss how we see it now’. Another systems developer expressed the view that ‘since tailoring is not a frequent activity, it is important to have the possibility to document why we have decided certain things’. These findings complement previous studies on MMC (Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2004; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a, b) that have not reported on changes to existing configurations, and how the documented configuration rationale has been used for that purpose. Consequently, MC Sandbox creates awareness of the background of past configuration decisions and supports what Rossi et al (2004) call ‘learning by doing’.

## The method rationality resonance theory

Our results show that time is an important component in the method rationality resonance theory. However, this component is missing in the work by A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2006) and Wistrand (2009). It means that values guide an actor towards the goals considered worth achieving at a given time, which is illustrated in Figure 6. Hence, we have, from a general systems theory perspective, created the possibility to capture different states (Ackoff, 1971) of rationality resonance, which was previously lacking.

This finding is in line with Fitzgerald et al (2002) who have earlier concluded that a systems developer’s appreciation of methods is not constant; rather it is U-shaped. They conclude that novice systems developers tend to follow the method by the letter. When they are more experienced, however, they relax their use of methods. Finally, with more experience they return to methods, but now to adapt them for use in a particular way. On a group level, this means that several U-shapes exist in parallel, something that was evident in our study. However, when the systems developers work together these shapes seem to converge over time. Consequently, the systems developers establish a shared view of what constitutes a useful method, that is, what goals are important to achieve.

The method rationality resonance theory laid out by A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk (2006) focuses on systems developers as single actors, while Wistrand (2009) introduced multi actors into the theory. In the latter version, rationality resonance can be found on the group level, but only captured as a consensus or non-consensus. Our results show that method rationality resonance on the group level is more complex than that. Through the use of a different measurement model compared with the one used by Wistrand (2009) we can show that some systems developers experience more resonance compared with others. It seems more reasonable to discuss method rationality resonance on the group level as a derived measurement based on actors’ rationality resonance when these actors belong to a project team. Consequently, the modified method rationality resonance theory, found in Figure 6, contains a collective rationality resonance association that is derived from the individual rationality resonance. Hence, the use of the Likert-scale measurement model also contributes to the operationalization of how to measure rationality resonance. Earlier studies (e.g., Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2005; Wistrand et al, 2008; Karlsson & A<sup>˚</sup> gerfalk, 2009a) have relied on qualitative measurements only, while we now offer a possibility to triangulate data using different measurement models.

In addition, one systems developer expressed the opinion that the workshop discussions affected the use of method components (in this case the Use Case component during Project 4): ‘They provided good arguments for this change’. Thus, a systems developer’s rationality resonance is not only a function of his or her understanding of the method component and past project experiences, but is also affected by social influence. This implies that the ability of the systems developers to reach a consensus, also, depends, at least in part, on each actor’s ability to communicate his/her understanding of the method rationale. Put another way, effective communication and argumentation seem to be prerequisites to achieve rationality resonance. These findings contribute to the method rationality resonance theory implying that rationality resonance, from a process perspective, also includes a negotiation component.

By extension, each systems developer’s understanding of a method, at a specific point in time, may be incomplete. The group discussion may therefore serve not only for consensus building, but also as a tool for learning through which systems developers can expand their individual knowledge about the method(s) being discussed. Hence, this means that information asymmetry among the developers can be reduced the more they interact.

## Conclusions

In this paper we have reported on systems developers’ intentions to balance goals and values of the base method with their need of method adaptation when using goaldriven method configuration. This has been done from a longitudinal perspective, where we followed two project teams during six systems development projects in which they adapted the agile method XP. This study was justified by the limited understanding of how goal-driven method configuration works in practice – especially with regards to maintaining coherency with the overall goals and values of the base method.

We have shown, through content examples of goaldriven method configuration, how the project teams adapted XP. On the basis of these examples the following conclusions were drawn. First, we can conclude that project members try to make well-informed decisions both when they promote goals in the base method, as well as when they degrade them or introduce goal contradictions. Moreover, our study shows that team members do not always agree on the importance of reaching certain goals or how they should be implemented in the method. However, based on our longitudinal study, we observed that the longer the project teams worked together, the fewer disagreements were noted. These findings contradict earlier research results that describe method configuration as involving non-consensus decisions. Such a view is valid when observing a single project, but becomes more nuanced in a longitudinal study that incorporates several projects. However, this also raises a future research question concerning collective rationality resonance: Is it something to strive for or is there a risk of a herding behaviour? For example, if over the course of several projects the systems developers achieve consensus on which method components to use, will they resist suggestions to try other (possibly superior) method component of achieving the same goal(s) when working on future projects?
