# CUSTOMER COMPLAINT AVOIDANCE: A RANDOMIZED FIELD EXPERIMENT OF PLATFORM GOVERNANCE BASED ON VALUE CO-CREATION AND APPROPRIATION<sup>1</sup>

Xia Zhao Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A. {xia.zhao@uga.edu}

Peijian Song Department of Marketing and Electronic Business, Business School, Nanjing University, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China {songpeijian@nju.edu.cn}

Lanfei Shi McIntire School of Commerce, University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA, U.S.A. {ls4tj@comm.virginia.edu}

Ling Xue Department of Management Information Systems, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, Athens, GA, U.S.A. {ling.xue@uga.edu }

Fan Feng Department of International Economics and Trade, Business School, Nanjing University, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China {fengfan@nju.edu.cn}

From the theoretical perspectives of value co-creation and value appropriation in platform governance, this study explores how a platform can design motivation mechanisms to induce the proactive efforts of providers to reduce customer complaints. We conducted a field experiment on a major Chinese peer-topeer real estate platform offering long-term rental properties. Specifically, when a renter (customer) complaint about a host (provider) occurs, we sent reminder messages to other unaffected hosts in the same neighborhoods (as the affected host) and urged them to proactively prevent similar complaints. The reminder messages varied in terms of their emphasis of the roles of different stakeholders in platformbased value co-creation (provider-emphasized vs. customer-emphasized) and how they explicated different value appropriation mechanisms (competition-based vs. cooperation-based). Results show that compared to the control message, customer-emphasized messages effectively motivated providers’ proactive efforts to reduce customer complaints. Contrastingly, provider-emphasized messages led to the undesirable outcome of increasing customer complaints, possibly due to providers’ shirking behavior. We also found that a competition-based value appropriation mechanism strengthened the motivating effect, whereas a cooperation-based mechanism undermined the motivating effect. The study provides important theoretical and practical implications for platforms on the design of effective governance mechanisms.

Keywords: Value co-creation, value appropriation, platform governance, complaint, digital platforms, randomized field experiment

## Introduction

Digital platforms provide a common place for third-party providers and customers to interact and transact with each other (Evans & Schmalensee, 2007; Guttentag & Smith, 2017; Zervas et al., 2017). A typical challenge on platforms is that information misrepresentation and miscommunication between third-party providers and customers can affect business quality and result in customer complaints (Boyce, 2016). Consider the empirical context of this study: a peer-to-peer (P2P) real estate platform offering rental properties, where third-party providers are hosts and customers are renters. The rental properties (e.g., singlefamily homes or apartments) listed by hosts on the platform are complex products with many features. Renters with varying tastes must assess different options based on information available on the rental platform and/or acquired from hosts. However, renters often complain about inaccurate listing information and the lack of timely responses from hosts. These issues can result in lower renter satisfaction and fill rates (Sittic, 2015).

To prevent renter complaints and mitigate dissatisfaction, hosts’ proactive efforts are crucial. Many key tasks in business interactions, such as updating listing information and direct communication between hosts and renters, are not entirely under the platform’s control and require voluntary and proactive efforts on the host side. Since third-party hosts are independent individuals or entities, the platform lacks formal control over their voluntary behaviors (Huber et al., 2017). It is therefore crucial for platforms to nudge thirdparty hosts through motivating mechanisms.<sup>2</sup>

In this study, we take a value perspective to examine how the platform motivates third-party providers to take proactive efforts to prevent customer complaints. In platform governance, a value perspective concerns how platform participants jointly create value (i.e., value co-creation) and how different participants capture their corresponding benefit shares (i.e., value appropriation) (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Oh et al., 2015). As the platform cannot solely rely on formal controls to regulate third-party providers, our interest is in exploring how the platform can motivate third-party providers by reinforcing the providers’ sense of value cocreation in the platform environment and whether such a potential motivating effect would be dependent on value appropriation mechanisms.

In the research context of a real estate rental platform, we focus on the case in which the platform sends reminder messages to hosts and encourages them to take proactive actions to prevent complaints. When a renter complains about a host, the platform sends messages notifying other hosts in the same neighborhood as the affected host about the complaint, even though these hosts have not themselves received complaints. These messages are for the purpose of reminding hosts to exert efforts to prevent similar complaints in the future. To the extent that these hosts are not encountering complaints themselves, their efforts are proactive and voluntary in nature. We consider how the platform can incorporate various informational statements about value co-creation and value appropriation in the reminder messages to effectively motivate the proactive efforts of hosts.

We first examine whether reminder messages can motivate hosts by reinforcing their sense of value co-creation. We consider two types of informational elements that can be incorporated into the reminder messages to stress value cocreation (which we generally refer to as value co-creation statements). First, the platform can emphasize the role that hosts play in value co-creation by reminding hosts about their roles and responsibilities in satisfying customers. We refer to this informational element as a provider-emphasized statement (or host-emphasized statement in our specific empirical context). Second, the platform can emphasize the importance of renters for value co-creation outcomes—in the hope hosts will recognize the imperative mission of satisfying renters. We refer to this informational element as a customeremphasized statement (or renter-emphasized statement in our specific empirical context). We distinguish between these two informational elements because, conceptually speaking, providers and customers are the two relevant parties in customer complaints. Emphasizing their importance in value co-creation could potentially strengthen providers’ sense of value co-creation and motivate their efforts. However, the extant research has offered limited insights about which side (the provider side or the customer side) providers are more sensitive to in terms of value co-creation. By distinguishing between these two informational elements, we aim to better understand the providers’ view of value co-creation and to thereby identify effective motivating mechanisms. Therefore, in our specific research setting, the first research question is: Can value co-creation statements with emphases on different parties (i.e., host-emphasized vs. renter-emphasized) motivate third-party hosts to proactively prevent renter complaints?

From a perspective of incentives, providers are not solely motivated by the ideological goals of value co-creation. They also need to appropriate their shares of co-created value as returns (Jacobides et al., 2006; Lavie, 2007; MacDonald & Ryall, 2004; Saloner et al., 2001). We therefore investigate how the platform can incorporate information about value appropriation mechanisms in reminder messages (which we refer to as value appropriation statements), and how value cocreation statements and value appropriation statements jointly influence providers’ efforts to prevent customer complaints. Reward-based mechanisms are commonly used in practice due to their ease of implementation and integration with other incentives. We thus consider different reward-based value appropriation mechanisms that the platform can implement and explicate in reminder messages. In a platform ecosystem, third-party providers are both cooperators in creating overall economic value and competitors in appropriating shares of value (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004). The competition and cooperation relationships form the base for the platform to design different value appropriation mechanisms to reward providers. In a competition-based value appropriation mechanism, the platform can reward providers based on their individual performance in competition. For example, the platform can reward individual providers who outperform others. In contrast, in a cooperation-based value appropriation mechanism, the platform can reward providers based on their collective group-level performance. With the possible uses of both value co-creation statements and value appropriation statements to motivate providers, our interest is in exploring their joint effects. Therefore, in our specific research setting, the second research question that we address is: What are the joint effects of value appropriation statements (i.e., competition-based vs. cooperation-based) and value cocreation statements in motivating providers to proactively prevent renter complaints?

In our empirical investigation, we conducted a randomized field experiment on a major Chinese peer-to-peer (P2P) real estate platform offering rental properties. The subjects of our experiment were over 11,000 rental property hosts. In our experiment, when a complaint about a property was made and verified by the platform,<sup>3</sup> the platform sent out messages to the hosts of other listed properties in the same neighborhood as the affected property. Although these hosts had not received recent complaints, the messages notified them that a verified complaint about a property in their neighborhood had been made and reminded them to take proactive efforts to prevent future complaints about their own listed properties. Through randomization, each host in our sample received one of nine messages, including a control message without any value cocreation or value appropriation statements, and eight treatment messages that incorporated different combinations of value co-creation statements (host-emphasized or renteremphasized) and value appropriation statements (competitionbased or cooperation-based). We tracked and compared the verified complaints in each group to assess the effectiveness of different reminder messages. In addition, we observed the specific activities of hosts, such as listing information updates and timely communication by phone, and corroborated that hosts indeed made proactive efforts to prevent renter complaints. We also used a detailed follow-up survey to verify the insights and the underlying mechanisms.

Our experiment yielded interesting findings with important theoretical and managerial implications. First, we found that compared to the control message, the messages with renteremphasized statements effectively motivated hosts to take actions that reduced renter complaints. In contrast, the messages with host-emphasized statements did not exhibit a significant motivating effect. More importantly, the hostemphasized statements even actually increase the number of subsequent complaints received by the affected hosts. The implication is that emphasizing value co-creation with renters resonates well with hosts and induces proactive efforts, whereas emphasizing the value co-creation role of the hosts themselves does not arouse great interest among hosts. These findings advance the understanding of the importance of the provider’s view of value co-creation in the platform environment.

Second, we found that with the competition-based value appropriation statement, the dampening effect of the hostemphasized value co-creation statement on host effort declined or even disappeared, whereas the motivating effect of the renter-emphasized value co-creation statement was not significantly affected. In contrast, with the cooperation-based statement, the motivating effect of the renter-emphasized value co-creation statement vanished, and the dampening effect of the host-emphasized value co-creation statement became stronger. These findings highlight the important interaction effects between value appropriation and value co-creation. Rewarding providers based on group performance induces the problem of free-riding and undermines the effectiveness of using the sense of value co-creation to motivate providers. In contrast, rewarding providers based on competition supports the desirable effect of motivating providers using their sense of value co-creation. These insights extend the theoretical understanding of the value perspective in platform governance.

Third, we also explored heterogeneous treatment effects and found that hosts with no recent complaint history were more sensitive to reminder messages, compared to hosts with a recent complaint history. This finding confirms that the host efforts were indeed preventative in nature. Also, competitionbased value appropriation statements exhibited a stronger effect in neighborhoods with more listings, suggesting that rewarding hosts based on competition is more effective in an environment with stronger externalities. Overall, these findings generate important theoretical and practical implications on how to better engage providers to proactively prevent customer complaints.

## Theoretical Framework And Hypothesis Development

## Value Co-Creation in the Platform Ecosystem

Value co-creation is a key mission in the ecosystem environment (Schreieck et al., 2016) involving various stakeholders, including the platform operators, third-party providers, and end customers. In value co-creation, the platform and third-party providers exchange and share resources to develop value propositions in the form of products or services to be consumed by customers (Gulati, 1998). Providers play an important role and share crucial responsibilities in co-creating value (Grover & Kohli, 2012; Sarker et al., 2012). Customers, although not necessarily directly involved in the production process, are always value co-producers because values are created only after the offered products/services are consumed. Customer experience and perception determine the co-created values and the third-party providers’ payoffs (Gronroos, 1994). Therefore, both providers and customers are integral parties of value cocreation on the platform. Customer dissatisfaction and complaints about providers would jeopardize the value cocreation outcomes and eventually undermine the benefits of platforms. In this regard, reinforcing the sense of value cocreation for providers is a plausible way to motivate their efforts to prevent customer complaints.

Using the sense of value co-creation to motivate providers is aligned with the spirit of value-based platform governance (Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2013). In value-based platform ecosystems, values refer to broad goals and desired spirits that serve as guiding principles for collaboration (Gulati et al., 2012; Tiwana et al., 2010; Wareham et al., 2014). Value-based platform governance focuses on leveraging informational statements to promote values (i.e., common goals and desired spirits) and develop shared norms in terms of orchestrating the platform ecosystem (Gulati, 1998). Without formal organizational hierarchies, such an informational approach is a flexible and practical way to provide ideological foundations to guide provider behaviors (Gulati et al., 2012). However, in the extant literature, there is limited understanding of the provider’s perspective of value co-creation and how the notion of value cocreation expressed in informational statements can motivate providers (McIntyre & Srinivasan, 2017). Also, as value cocreation involves multiple parties, different informational elements can be included in the statements to emphasize different key aspects of value co-creation, such as provideremphasized statements to highlight the shared responsibilities and duties of providers and customer-emphasized statements to stress the common goal of satisfactorily serving customers. It is worthwhile to examine which aspects of value co-creation effectively resonate with providers and motivate them to proactively prevent customer complaints.

## Value Appropriation

In the context of interorganizational collaboration, value appropriation refers to the distribution of generated value among involved parties (Gulatim & Wang, 2003; Hamel, 1991; Khanna et al., 1998). As economic entities, the platform and third-party providers need to capture their shares of cocreated value in order to thrive and succeed (Lavie, 2007; Saloner et al., 2001). Therefore, the share of value that a provider can obtain would shape the provider’s incentive to participate in the value co-creation process. In a platform ecosystem, how value co-creation and value appropriation jointly influence providers’ incentives to prevent customer complaints remains underinvestigated.

Value appropriation is typically implemented through pricing mechanisms or revenue-sharing contracts between economic entities (Lavie, 2007; Tiwana, 2010). In addition to explicit contracts that platforms develop with each provider, platforms can implement value appropriation mechanisms based on the relationships between providers. In a platform ecosystem, thirty-party providers are collaborators in the creation of economic value as well as competitors in the appropriation of co-created value (Prahalad & Ramaswamy, 2004). Using a competition-based value appropriation mechanism, the platform can induce competitive behaviors by rewarding providers who outperform other peers. Alternatively, using a cooperation-based value appropriation mechanism, the platform can reward a group of providers with better collective performance than other peer groups, hoping to inspire cooperative actions within groups. Given their close theoretical connections, value appropriation and value cocreation should jointly influence the incentives and efforts of providers. But such joint effect has received limited empirical investigation in the extant literature. Therefore, our study also examines the interactions between value co-creation and value appropriation statements in motivating providers to proactively prevent customer complaints.

In contrast to the use of “broad and highly abstract statements,” the implementation of value appropriation often requires the platform to specify explicit rules and agreements about how to reward providers to promote value co-creation (Gulati, 1998). However, the use of rules does not contradict value-based governance because they are not mutually exclusive, and in fact, they often complement each other (Huber et al., 2017; Poppo & Zenger, 2002). When actions and outcomes are specifiable and verifiable, the platform can use rules to define the rights and duties of providers (Tiwana et al., 2010; Wareham et al., 2014) in the forms of guidelines, contracts, and agreements (Evans & Schmalensee, 2007; Park & Ungson, 2001; Poppo & Zenger, 2002; Reuer & Ariño, 2007). When provider behaviors are largely voluntary in nature and driven by abstract norms and spirits that are not formally specifiable or verifiable using rules, informational statements about goals and spirits can play an important role in influencing and governing these behaviors (Huber et al., 2017). Therefore, our main focus is on the motivating effects of using the notions of value co-creation and value appropriation rather than on the specific implementation format of these notions.

## Hypothesis Development

In a platform ecosystem, third-party providers are independent entities and are coordinated mainly at arm’s length. Using value co-creation statements is a viable approach for the platform to advocate common goals and instill a sense of shared mission for providers (Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana et al., 2010). In these value co-creation statements, the platform can contextualize the general goals of value co-creation in the specific scenario of preventing customer complaints and emphasize the roles and responsibilities of providers in this scenario. To the extent that providers are involved in both value co-creation and customer complaints (due to their direct interactions with customers), the use of provider-emphasized statements can help reinforce providers’ awareness of how their proactive efforts in preventing customer complaints would contribute to value co-creation. For example, customer complaints are often attributable to the lack of diligence on the provider side (Moon et al., 2019). The platform can remind providers how their proactive efforts in improving information provision and timely communication are crucial for customer satisfaction and value co-creation outcomes. As value cocreation eventually benefits providers and is therefore aligned with their interests, we expect that providers would be motivated by statements emphasizing their value cocreation roles so that they can proactively exert efforts to prevent customer dissatisfaction. Therefore, in our specific research context, we hypothesize that:

H1: Compared to a control message, a message emphasizing the importance of hosts in co-creating value with the platform better motivates hosts to proactively prevent renter complaints.

While provider-emphasized statements seek to motivate providers based on their own responsibilities, customeremphasized statements seek to motivate providers from the perspective of customer satisfaction in value co-creation. The consumption experiences and satisfaction of customers eventually determine the value co-created by providers and the platform (Kohli & Jaworski, 1990; Narver & Slater, 1990; Payne et al., 2008). Therefore, focusing providers’ attention on the importance of the customer side in value co-creation can help to contextualize the general goals of value co-creation in the specific scenario of preventing customer complaints. In this case, by using customer-emphasized statements, the platform essentially seeks to motivate providers by highlighting the importance of customer satisfaction for value co-creation (in contrast to highlighting providers’ own responsibilities in value co-creation).

The use of customer-emphasized statements to motivate provider efforts is also aligned with the idea of the cross-side network effect (CNE), in which a thriving customer side is a compelling driver of providers’ participation and engagement (Tucker & Zhang, 2011). The attractiveness of the network effect is built not only on network size but also value proposition (Afuah, 2013). By explicating how providers’ proactive efforts, e.g., effective information provision and communication, can help prevent complaints, enhance customers’ satisfaction (Normann, 2001), and consequently nourish the customer side, the platform essentially justifies the value of these providers’ efforts, which can result in a motivating effect. Therefore, in our specific research context, we hypothesize that:

H2: Compared to a control message, a message emphasizing the importance of renters for value co-creation on the platform better motivates hosts to proactively prevent renter complaints.

In contrast to value co-creation statements that highlight the ideological goals of providers, value appropriation statements signify how co-created values are distributed among providers. Competition-based value appropriation mechanisms reward individual providers when they outperform peers (Becker & Huselid, 1992; Green & Stokey, 1983; Lazear & Rosen, 1981; Nalebuff & Stiglitz, 1983). For example, platforms can reward providers who receive fewer customer complaints. In the platform ecosystem, while providers collectively generate value in collaboration with the platform, they also compete with each other for their share of co-created benefits (Brandenburger & Nalebuff, 2011; Lavie,

2007). This sense of competition can serve as an impetus for individual providers to exert efforts and improve performance (Becker & Huselid, 1992; Beersma et al., 2003). Therefore, we expect that competition-based value appropriation would strengthen the motivating effect of value co-creation statements. With a competition-based mechanism, individual providers will anticipate that their own efforts will enhance their advantage over other peers in terms of value appropriation, which will consequently increase their share of the benefits. Such increased marginal returns of efforts can lead to a stronger motivating effect on the proactive efforts of providers (Lazear & Rosen, 1981; Tirole & Jean, 1988). Prior studies on interfirm collaboration have also shown that multilateral competition among partners can curb opportunistic partner behaviors (Lavie, 2007). In our specific research context, we thus hypothesize that:

H3: A reminder message stating that the platform is implementing a competition-based mechanism to reward third-party hosts will strengthen the motivating effect of a value co-creation statement.

Cooperation-based value appropriation mechanisms reward individual providers based on their collective performance (Katz, 1986). For example, a real estate rental platform could reward (e.g., through promotion) neighborhoods with a superior collective performance of hosts in preventing renter complaints, which would benefit all hosts in these neighborhoods. A cooperation-based value appropriation mechanism can internalize the externalities of host behaviors and therefore enhance the overall returns of hosts’ collective efforts (Katz, 1986). Also, cooperation-based value appropriation can instill a sense of cooperation between third-party providers, which is aligned with the general goals and spirit of value co-creation. Moreover, cooperation-based value appropriation can help establish psychological bonds between individual providers and can therefore unite them in the achievement of value co-creation objectives (Chen et al., 2012).

From the perspective of motivation, however, the downside of cooperation-based value appropriation is that it may lead to the problem of free-riding (Albanese & Van Fleet, 1985; Latané et al., 1979). Rewards based on aggregate performance allow individual providers to benefit from others’ efforts, which inevitably leads to shirking behaviors (Holmstrom, 1982; Lavie, 2007). In this regard, cooperation-based value appropriation may undermine the motivating effect of value co-creation statements. The free-riding issue is especially prominent in the platform environment because third-party providers are independent entities that participate in a loose organization (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Oh et al., 2015). Higher co-created value fostered through cooperation does not necessarily result in higher appropriated value for providers to sustain their efforts (Cennamo & Santaló, 2019; Veugelers &

Cassiman, 1999). Therefore, we expect that cooperationbased value appropriation statements will weaken the motivating effect of value co-creation statements. In our specific research context, we hypothesize that:

H4: A reminder message stating that the platform is implementing a cooperation-based mechanism to reward third-party hosts will weaken the motivating effect of a value co-creation statement.

## Methodology

## Research Context

We collaborated with a major Chinese P2P real estate platform offering rental properties to conduct a field experiment. The platform was established in 2015 and has become one of the major rental platforms in China. Its operation expanded from one city in 2015 to 10 major cities in 2019. The business model of this mobile-based platform is similar to that of Airbnb. It provides a marketplace for hosts (i.e., property owners) to list their rental properties and for prospective renters to learn about and rent properties. Unlike Airbnb and other platforms that facilitate short-term rental transactions, this platform focuses on relatively long-term rental periods that usually span at least three months. Therefore, it is more critical for the platform to implement effective governance mechanisms to better serve renters and thereby reduce the number of complaints.

The platform operates with an open business model using a simple screening process. Hosts provide the attributes (e.g., location, size, amenities, and rent), pictures, and descriptions of their property. The platform screens the information and approves the listing. Hosts then pay the platform a listing fee, which is based on the rental rate, and the platform lists the property. Renters are able to use the platform for free. Before a property can be successfully leased, there is a matching process involving several steps. The prospective renter browses a list of properties using the platform’s app. If the renter is interested in a particular listed property, the renter calls the host via the app to make further inquiries, negotiate with the host, and perform on-site inspections. Similar to other P2P platforms, communication is mediated by the platform to protect privacy and prevent harassment for both parties.

Due to the loose coupling between the platform and hosts, prior to our experiment, this platform mainly relied on renters to detect the undesirable behaviors of hosts. The platform allows renters to lodge official complaints about hosts. On each property’s page, there is a link that renters can use to submit complaints. The platform classifies complaints into seven types: (1) property has already been leased, (2) rental rate is incorrect, (3) picture is incorrect, (4) host does not answer phone calls, (5) host charges additional fees, (6) property type is incorrect, (7) other. If “other” is selected, renters are prompted to manually enter a reason. Similar to other rental platform contexts, the main reasons for complaints on this platform are information inaccuracy (1-3, 5, 6) and poor communication (4). The platform employs a standard procedure to investigate each complaint and identify the hosts and/or renters exhibiting undesirable behaviors. Once a complaint is submitted, a call center worker contacts the corresponding host to verify whether the complaint is valid. If there is a significant discrepancy between the reports of the involved parties, a manager will inspect the relevant property on-site. Usually, the renter receives feedback about the complaint from the platform within 24 hours. If the complaint is deemed to be valid, the platform will remove the listing of the involved property from the app for a month and record the incident as a verified complaint. If a host accumulates three verified complaints, the platform will suspend the host’s account for three months. If the complaint is deemed to be invalid, the platform with add an invalid complaint record to the renter’s account. If a renter accumulates more than three invalid complaints in a month, the renter will be banned from the platform and will no longer be able to contact hosts or rent properties.

## Field Experiment Design

Our field experiment was conducted in four major Chinese cities. The platform grouped the listed properties into residential neighborhoods based on the corresponding industry practices. The platform kept track of the verified complaints in each residential neighborhood. In the experiment, once a complaint was verified, the corresponding neighborhood became a target neighborhood for the experiment involving the motivating interventions described above. In this case, the platform sent text messages to the other hosts in the target neighborhood (who had not received complaints). The messages notified these hosts that a listed property in their neighborhood had received a verified complaint. The platform did not send a message to the affected host who received the verified complaint that triggered the experimental messages.<sup>4</sup> The messages served as a reminder for unaffected hosts to employ efforts to prevent similar complaints about their properties.

A target neighborhood was randomly assigned to one of the nine experiment groups, i.e., hosts in the same neighborhood received the same treatment or control message. The randomization was conducted at the neighborhood level (rather than the individual host level) for two reasons. First, on a practical level, hosts in the same neighborhood can communicate offline and learn about each other’s messages. Therefore, sending hosts in the same neighborhood the same message helps mitigate potential contamination between different messages.<sup>5</sup> Second, from a theoretical perspective, neighborhood-level randomization supports the examination of the cooperation-based value appropriation mechanism, which rewards hosts based on collective neighborhood performance. Therefore, it makes sense to send hosts in the same neighborhood the same message. Since our analysis examining the proactive efforts of individual hosts is at the host level, we further employ matching at the host level to ensure that individual hosts across the treatment and control groups are comparable so that randomization is approximated at the host level (as explained in more detail below). To prevent hosts with multiple listed properties in different neighborhoods from being assigned to multiple experimental groups simultaneously, we removed these hosts from the analysis.<sup>6</sup>

Our neighborhood-level randomization conforms to the design of the clustering randomization in which interventions are randomly assigned at the cluster level (in our study, the neighborhood level) rather than the individual level (Alferes, 2012; Athey & Imbens, 2017). Clustering randomization is widely used in economics and medical contexts for the same purpose—i.e., preventing complications due to local interactions at the unit level. Experiments that adopt clustering randomization can perform individual-level analyses to examine the treatment effects (Baird et al., 2012; Gertler, 2004; Kramer et al., 2008). Our study draws from existing studies and further performs CEM to better balance the covariates at the individual host level.

Based on our interest in the motivating effects of value cocreation and value appropriation statements in reminder messages, we considered two distinct statements pertaining to value co-creation, i.e., host-emphasized and renter-emphasized, and two distinct statements pertaining to value appropriation mechanisms, i.e., competition-based and cooperation-based. We have considered scenarios with only one value co-creation statement, scenarios with only one value appropriation statement, and scenarios with none of these statements in the reminder message (i.e., the control message). We therefore adopted a 3×3 design with nine groups―one control group and eight treatment groups (see Table 1). Following the practices in the literature (Fjeldsoe et al., 2009; Hurling et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2019), all groups, including the control group, received a message to control for the potential reminder effect of a message.

Each message consisted of two or more of the following four informational elements. The first element was a basic statement informing the host that a property in their neighborhood received a verified complaint. This statement was general and did not specify the complaint type or reason. The second element was a value co-creation statement, which emphasized either the important role of hosts in value co-creation (i.e., hostemphasized) or the importance of renter satisfaction in value cocreation (i.e., renter-emphasized). The third element was a value appropriation statement, which indicated whether the platform would promote properties based on the individual performance of the corresponding hosts (competition-based) or based on the aggregate neighborhood-level performance (i.e., cooperationbased). Performance was assessed based on the extent to which renter complaints were prevented. The fourth element was a statement to remind the host to take measures to ensure information accuracy and responsive communication, which are the proactive actions at their disposal to prevent renter complaints. The control message consisted of the first and fourth informational elements. The treatment messages augmented the control message by including varying combinations of the second and third elements, as explained below.

The message for Treatment Group 2 (renter-only group) included a renter-emphasized statement, i.e., “Our platform is keen on the business of renters. The success of value co-creation by the platform and third-party hosts hinges on the satisfaction of renters.” The message for Treatment Group 3 (host-only group) included a host-emphasized statement, i.e., “Our platform is keen on the contributions of third-party hosts. The success of value co-creation by the platform and third-party hosts hinges on the diligence of hosts.” The messages for Group 2 and Group 3 did not include any value appropriation statement.

The message for Treatment Group 4 (competition-only group) included a competition-based statement, i.e., “Our platform would recommend the properties with high service quality to renters. The property with complaints will be in a disadvantageous position relative to other properties.” The message for Treatment Group 7 (cooperation-only group) included a cooperation-based statement, i.e., “Our platform would recommend the residential neighborhood with high overall service quality. Residential neighborhoods with properties that receive complaints will not be prioritized in the recommendation.” The messages for Group 4 and Group 7 did not include any value co-creation statement.

The message for Treatment Group 5 (renter competition group) included both a renter-emphasized statement and a competitionbased statement. The message for Treatment Group 6 (host competition group) included both a host-emphasized statement and a competition-based statement. The message for Treatment Group 8 (renter cooperation group) included both a renteremphasized statement and a cooperation-based statement. The message for Treatment Group 9 (host cooperation group) included both a host-emphasized statement and a cooperationbased statement. ## Data

The field experiment was implemented between May and July 2019. Our dependent variable was the number of complaints that a host received, i.e., complaints. The complaints were filed at different times. Our data indicated that because the number of complaints about each host was small, aggregating the number of complaints at the daily level would not produce a sufficient variation of the variable value for analysis. Therefore, the observation window for each host was one week before and after the intervention.

We performed individual-level analysis to gain granular insights. Since the randomization was at the neighborhood level, we employed a matching approach to balance the characteristics of individual hosts across groups. We followed Blackwell et al. (2009) in using coarsened exact matching (CEM) to match the hosts for different groups. As a monotonic imbalance-reducing matching method, CEM requires fewer assumptions and offers more attractive statistical properties for many applications than other matching methods (Blackwell et al., 2009). The covariates that we used in CEM include host gender, host type (professional or not), host tenure, property size, rental rate, historical count of phone calls, number of past complaints, city in which the host lives, number of other listed properties in the same neighborhood, distance to the city center, average rental rate of listed properties in the neighborhood, average size (in square meters) of listed properties in the neighborhood, rental rate deviation of listed properties in the neighborhood, property size deviation of listed properties in the neighborhood, fill rate of listed properties in the neighborhood, and number of metro stations within two kilometers of the neighborhood. The sample size of Group 6 (host competition group) was the smallest. We used CEM to match each host in Group 6 with the closest host in other groups. After matching, each group had 1,325 hosts. The total number of hosts in our analysis was 11,925 (=1,325×9). Table 2 summarizes the descriptive statistics.

## Model Specification

To further study the effect of value co-creation and value appropriation statements, we employed a difference-indifferences (DID) model to examine the relationships between the number of complaints and various informational statements. DID analysis requires that the time trends of dependent variables for treatment and control groups are the same before treatment. We followed Angrist and Pischke (2008) and used a relative time model to examine the parallel trend premise. Ttests showed that the differences in the coefficients between treatment groups and the control group were insignificant before the treatment but statistically significant after the treatment. The analysis and results confirm that the parallel trend assumption holds and that the DID framework can thus be applied here to identify the treatment effects (Please see Appendix C).

The DID analysis compared the treatment groups and the control group before and after the intervention. The benefit of using a DID model is to control for unobservable sources of heterogeneity across groups and rule out alternative explanations (Goldfarb et al. 2014). Because complaint incidents are sparse and cause excessive zero values for the dependent variable complaints, we employed a zero-inflated negative binomial count model to examine the impact of statements on the number of complaints. Zero-inflated negative binomial regression corrects the rare event biases and standard error inconsistency and thereby generates more accurate estimates (Greene, 2018). Our first model specification is:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {Complaint} _ {i} = \\ \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} 0 & \text {with probability} \varphi_ {i} \\ \text {NegativeBionomial} (\text {Complaint} _ {i} | X _ {i}) & \text {with probability} 1 - \varphi_ {i}, \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where i is the host index and φ<sub>i</sub> is the probability determined by the zero-inflated link function, which is specified as a logistic regression (Sheu et al., 2004). X<sub>i</sub> represents the key independent variables and interaction terms, including After<sub>t</sub>, Renter<sub>i</sub>, Host<sub>i</sub>, Competition<sub>i</sub>, Cooperation<sub>i</sub>, After<sub>t</sub>×Renter<sub>i</sub>, After<sub>t</sub>×Host<sub>i</sub>, After<sub>t</sub>×Competition<sub>i</sub>, After<sub>t</sub>×Cooperation<sub>i</sub>, and control variables. Renter is a dummy indicator with a value of 1 if the host received a message with a renter-emphasized statement, and a value of 0 otherwise. Host is a dummy indicator with a value of 1 if the host received a message with a host-emphasized statement, and a value of 0 otherwise. Competition is a dummy indicator with a value of 1 if the hosts received a message with a competition-based statement, and a value of 0 otherwise. Cooperation is a dummy indicator with a value of 1 if the host received a message with a cooperation-based statement, and a value of 0 otherwise. The coefficients of After<sub>t</sub>×Host<sub>i</sub> and After<sub>t</sub>×Renter<sub>i</sub> are the coefficients of interest. They reflect the effects of two distinct value co-creation statements on the treatment groups relative to the control group.

Figure 1. Average Complaint Numbers by Group after Treatment

The control variables capture host characteristics, property characteristics, and neighborhood characteristics (as defined in Table 2), including city dummies, host gender, host type (professional or not), host tenure, property size, rental rate, historical count of phone calls, number of past complaints, number of other listed properties in the neighborhood, distance to the city center, the rental rate of listed properties in the neighborhood, size of listed properties in the neighborhood, rental rate deviation of listed properties in the neighborhood, property size deviation of listed properties in the neighborhood, the fill rate of listed properties in the neighborhood, and the number of metro stations serving the neighborhood.

To examine the moderating effect of the value appropriation statements, we set the control group as the baseline group and used eight dummy indicators to indicate treatment groups. The model specification is:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {Complaint} _ {i} = \\ \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} 0 & \text {with probability} \phi_ {i} \\ \text {NegativeBinomial} (\text {Complaint} _ {i} | Z _ {i}) & \text {with probability} 1 - \phi_ {i} \end{array} \right. \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

Similar to φ<sub>i</sub> in Model (1), ϕ<sub>i</sub> is a probability determined by a zero-inflated link function. Z<sub>i</sub> includes eight treatment dummies: Renter\_Only<sub>i</sub>, Host\_Only<sub>i</sub>, Competition\_Only<sub>i</sub>, Renter\_Competition<sub>i</sub>, Host\_Competition<sub>i</sub>, Cooperation\_Only<sub>i</sub>, Renter\_Cooperation<sub>i</sub>, Host\_Cooperation<sub>i</sub>, and their interactions with After<sub>t</sub>. The control variables are the same as those in Equation (1). The coefficients of the interactions between treatment dummies and After<sub>t</sub> are the coefficients of interest.

The finding that the provider-emphasized value co-creation statement led to more complaints suggests that the emphasis on the provider’s role in value co-creation does not resonate well with providers. Moreover, provider-emphasized value co-creation statements could aggravate shirking behavior (Shapiro, 2005), probably because these statements remind providers more about their responsibility costs than about the collaborative benefits gained on the platform. The result is aligned with the implication of agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Shapiro, 2005) which suggests that providers are independent and self-interested entities and the platform, as a loosely coupled organization, lacks effective mechanisms to monitor and regulate providers (Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2013). This unexpected finding further confirms that the platform should carefully craft value co-creation statements to prevent undesirable outcomes when providers are loosely connected to the platform.

The coefficient of After×Renter is negative and significant ( = -0.393, p < 0.05, Column 1 in Table 3), suggesting that compared to hosts receiving the control message, hosts encountered fewer complaints after receiving a message with a renter-emphasized statement. Specifically, the inclusion of a renter-emphasized statement reduced the number of complaints by 32.5%, with other variables held constant. In contrast to the host-emphasized statement, the renteremphasized statement motivated hosts to proactively prevent complaints. Therefore, H2 is supported. In summary, we find that emphasizing the importance of renter satisfaction rather than the role of hosts in value co-creation is effective in motivating the proactive efforts of hosts to prevent renter complaints.

We further examine how the impact of the value co-creation statement is influenced by the value appropriation statements. The coefficient of After×Host\_Competition is negative and significant $( \beta = - 0 . 6 9 8 , \mathrm { p } < 0 . 1 0 )$ , in contrast to the positive but statistically insignificant coefficient for After×Host\_Only ( = 0.319, n.s.). A t-test (t = -1.969, p < 0.05) further revealed that the difference is significant. Combining a hostemphasized statement with a competition-based statement reduced the renter complaints by 50.2%. The implication is that incorporating the notions of both value co-creation and value appropriation can help form a more complete and effective value perspective, which then motivates hosts.

The coefficient of After×Renter\_Competition is negative and significant ( = -0.998, p<0.01). Although it is smaller than that of After×Renter\_Only ( = -0.797, p < 0.05), a t-test (t = -0.352, n.s.) showed that the difference is not significant. Therefore, using a competition-based statement did not significantly influence the effect of the renter-emphasized statement. The use of a renter-emphasized statement alone decreased renter complaints by 54.9%. The further benefit of adding a competition-based statement, however, was limited. Hence, H3 is partially supported. A possible reason is that as a renter-emphasized statement already had a strong motivating effect on hosts, further motivating hosts using a competition-based value appropriation mechanism was not necessary. When aware of the importance of renter satisfaction to value co-creation, hosts appear to be motivated to improve renter satisfaction.

## Robustness Checks

We conducted several additional analyses to verify the robustness of our results. First, our main analysis used a zeroinflated negative binomial count model to examine the factors influencing the number of complaints. In the robustness checks, we used a negative binomial count model and a Poisson count model as alternative model specifications. The results were largely qualitatively consistent with the main results concerning the reminder messages (see Tables D1 and D2, Columns 1 and 2, Appendix D).<sup>8</sup>

Second, we also used an alternative matching method to verify the robustness of our results using CEM. In particular, we followed McCaffrey et al. (2013) and used propensity weighting and generalized boosted models (GBM) to resolve the possible issue of covariate imbalance in the case of multiple treatments. This method uses GBM to estimate propensity scores for each treatment, assigns weights based on the propensity scores, and uses a doubly robust procedure to estimate the treatment effects (McCaffrey et al., 2013). The estimation results are summarized in Tables D1 and D2, Column 3, in Appendix D. We found that the results qualitatively held with this alternative matching method.

Third, in the main analysis, we tracked the incidence of complaints within one week after the hosts received the messages. If several hosts in a neighborhood received complaints successively within this window, other hosts in the same neighborhood could have received multiple interventions.<sup>9</sup> This might have confounded the treatment effect itself. To address this issue, we conducted a robustness analysis by adding a binary indicator variable (overlap). This variable has a value of 1 if the host had already received one or more interventions within one week before receiving a focal intervention and a value of 0 otherwise. The results are qualitatively consistent with the main results (Please see Column 4 in Tables D1 and D2 in Appendix D). The coefficient of overlap is statistically insignificant, suggesting that the effects of multiple interventions did not accumulate.

Fourth, we conducted a falsification test to ensure that our results were driven by receiving reminder messages, and not by coincidence. Specifically, we created a fake shock on pretreatment observations (Pamuru et al., 2021)—i.e., we used the week right before the actual treatment as the false posttreatment period and the week before the false post-treatment period as the false pre-treatment period. Using the same matched treatment groups and control group, we performed a DID analysis. The results suggest no significant differences in the complaint numbers between the treatment groups and the control group (see Column 5 in Tables D1 and D2 in Appendix D). The robustness test provides additional supporting evidence that the significant results of reminder messages are not caused by coincidence.

Finally, we also performed a randomized treatment test (Pamuru et al., 2021) to verify the treatment effect. In this test, we randomly reassigned the hosts to new treatment and control groups and reestimated the model. We repeated these steps 1,000 times and obtained the probability distributions of the estimated coefficients for After×Host and After×Renter. The distributions are centered around zeros. T-tests showed that the means of the two distributions were not statistically different from zero $( t = 1 . 1 3 3 , p = 0 . 1 9 5 ; t = 0 . 8 6 0 , p = 0 . 3 9 0 )$ , which suggests that if the groups had been randomly assigned, the main effect of host-emphasized and renter-emphasized on the complaint numbers would not exist. Assuming that the coefficients follow a normal distribution, the probabilities of obtaining at least the estimated coefficients (i.e., a coefficient for After×Host larger than $\beta = 0 . 3 9 4$ , and a coefficient for After×Renter smaller than $\beta = - 0 . 3 9 3 )$ in the main analysis are very small (0.005, 0.004). The t-tests and probabilities both support that the main effects in our analyses are valid.

## Underlying Mechanisms: Proactive Efforts of Hosts

Our main analyses illustrate that value co-creation and appropriation statements used by the platform to remind thirdparty hosts can effectively lead to a reduction in renter complaints. A follow-up question is whether the observed decreases in complaints are attributable to hosts’ proactive efforts. We further explore the underlying mechanisms that drove the observed reduction of complaints. As aforementioned, renter complaints are mainly caused by inaccurate listing information posted by hosts and poor communication with renters. If hosts indeed made proactive efforts to prevent renter complaints, we should have observed improvements in these activities—e.g., more frequent property information updates and longer durations of phone calls with renters. To dig deeper into these potential mechanisms, we acquired additional data on host operations from the platform and performed additional analyses.

For each listed property, the platform’s operational database records the history of voice calls (e.g., the starting time and ending time) initiated by prospective renters with the corresponding host, and the history of information updates made by the host before the property is leased. We used the average length of voice calls and the number of information updates as the objective measures of hosts’ proactive efforts and examined whether they channeled the effects of the treatment messages.

The results are summarized in Table 4. Panel A shows a negative and significant impact of both the average length of voice calls $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ and the number of information updates (β $= - 0 . 0 0 5 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ on the number of complaints. Thus, more frequent information updates and longer phone calls with renters effectively helped hosts reduce complaints. Table 4, Panel B shows that the renter-emphasized statement had a positive and significant impact on the average length of voice calls $( \beta = 4 . 5 9 1$ $p < 0 . 0 5$ , Column 1) and on the number of information updates $( \beta = 2 . 2 6 9 , p < 0 . 0 5$ , Column 3). These results suggest that hosts updated their listings more frequently and extended the length of their phone calls with renters after receiving a message emphasizing the importance of renter satisfaction. The hostemphasized statement had a negative and significant impact on the number of information updates $( \beta = - 2 . 5 0 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 ,$ Column 3), suggesting that emphasizing the important role of hosts in value co-creation leads hosts to update their listings less frequently. These results help explain why the use of a renteremphasized (host-emphasized) statement eventually leads to fewer (more) renter complaints.

The coefficient of After×Host\_Competition is positive and significant for the number of information updates $( \beta = 3 . 2 0 1 , p$ < 0.01, Column 4), in contrast to the insignificant coefficient of After×Host\_Only $( \beta = - 0 . 2 6 0 , \mathrm { n . s . }$ , Column 4). The results reveal how combining a competition-based statement with a hostemphasized statement motivates hosts to more frequently update their listings, which eventually leads to fewer complaints. The coefficient of After×Renter\_Cooperation for the average length of voice calls is insignificant $( \beta = - 2 . 2 1 7 , \mathrm { n . s . }$ , column 2) but the coefficient of After×Renter\_Only is positive and significant $( \beta =$ 6.933, p < 0.05, Column 2). ## Manipulation Checks

We conducted a follow-up survey on the hosts on the platform to perform manipulation checks. We used a separate sample from the experiment subjects for manipulation checks to prevent the potential contamination effect on the treatments in the experiment (Hauser et al., 2018) and we also confirmed that the survey sample was identical to the experiment sample in terms of the distribution of participants’ background attributes. The survey participants were randomly assigned to only one of the nine groups. We invited the participants to read the reminder messages and fill out a questionnaire, which included questions on manipulation checks, perceptions, and behavioral intentions (Please see Appendix E for the questionnaire). We received 488 valid questionnaires that passed our attention check questions.

We conducted independent sample t-tests to confirm the manipulation of the treatments. First, we validated that the manipulation of (1) renter-emphasized value co-creation was effective—on average, the respondents receiving a renteremphasized statement (Groups 2, 5, and 8; Mean = 4.267, SE = 0.043) perceived a higher level of renter-emphasized value co-creation than those not receiving this statement (Groups 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 9; Mean = 4.086, SE = 0.035). The t-test showed that the difference was significant (t = 3.243, p < 0.01). Similarly, we also confirmed that the manipulations of (2) host-emphasized value co-creation messages (Groups 3, 6, and 9 vs. Groups 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, and 8), (3) competition-based value appropriation messages (Groups 4-6 vs. Groups 1-3, 7- 9), and (4) cooperation-based value appropriation messages (Groups 7-9 vs. Groups 1-6) were all effective.<sup>10</sup>

## Free-Riding

We conjecture that free-riding was a driving factor of the observed undesirable effect of the cooperation-based value appropriation mechanism. To verify this potential mechanism, we also invited the respondents to evaluate their free-riding intentions in the follow-up survey. Table 5 reports the average levels of free-riding intentions of the nine groups. The results show that the respondents receiving a cooperation-based value appropriation statement (Groups 7-9; Mean = 3.742, SE = 0.060) had stronger free-riding intentions than those not receiving a cooperation-based value appropriation statement (Groups 1-6; Mean = 3.519, SE = 0.042). A significant difference in the means between these two categories exists (t = 3.019, p < 0.01). The results suggest that the cooperationbased mechanism (compared to the competition-based mechanism) caused more severe free-riding among hosts. In particular, the level of free-riding intention was highest in the host cooperation group (Group 9; Mean = 3.820, SE = 0.095), which is consistent with our main result indicating that this group was associated with an increase in renter complaints. To summarize, the survey results provide further evidence that the free-riding problem was the underlying mechanism driving the dampening effect of cooperation-based value appropriation.

## Heterogeneous Treatment Effects

We conducted two additional analyses to explore the heterogeneous treatment effects. First, we examined whether the observed effects were heterogenous between different types of hosts based on whether they had recently encountered complaints (within six months) prior to our experiment. Specifically, we calculated the number of complaints encountered by each host in the last six months<sup>11</sup> and used it to split the sample. Based on the history of past complaints, we split the sample into a group consisting of hosts without any complaints in the past six months and a group consisting of hosts with at least one complaint in the six months prior to the experiment. We estimated the models separately. The results are summarized in Columns 6 and 7 in Tables D1 and D2 in Appendix D. For hosts without any complaints in the past six months, the results are largely consistent with the main results in Table 3. For hosts with complaints within the six months prior to the experiment, the effects of the value-cocreation statements became nonsignificant, while the dampening effect of the cooperation-based statements on the value-co-creation statements remained the same. This suggests that hosts with no complaints were more responsive to our designed messages compared to hosts with recent complaints, which is aligned with our intention to study the preventative efforts of hosts in this study.

Second, we considered possible heterogeneous treatment effects depending on the number of listed properties in the same neighborhood. The number of listed properties is a proxy of the degree of within-neighborhood externalities. Listings in the same neighborhood share many common features, such as location, safety, surrounding environment, and amenities that renters care about. The listings in the same neighborhood are likely to be alternative options for renters. Therefore, competition between hosts in neighborhoods with more listed properties should be more intensive, and the efforts of individual hosts would generate greater externalities. We used the average number of listed properties in the same neighborhood to split the sample. Based on the mean value, we divided the sample into a small-neighborhood sample and a large-neighborhood sample.

## Discussion

This study takes a value perspective of platform governance to examine how to address complaint issues on digital platforms. We specifically focus on how platforms can use the notions of value co-creation and value appropriation to motivate third-party providers to proactively take actions to increase customer satisfaction and reduce complaints. Using a randomized field experiment on a peer-to-peer real estate platform offering long-term rental properties, we examined messages consisting of different conceptual elements of value co-creation and value appropriation. The findings generate both important theoretical contributions to the existing platform research and practical guidance for platform operators.

## Theoretical Implications

The theoretical implications of our study are threefold. First, our results provide indicative evidence of the motivating effect of value co-creation and value appropriation statements on third-party providers. In our context of a rental property platform, a major finding is that by using reminder messages that emphasize the value of renters (i.e., the customers in this context) and the host-side competition, the platform can motivate hosts (i.e., the providers in this context) to exert proactive efforts to reduce potential complaints from renters. This finding adds to the theoretical perspective of platform governance (Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2010). We show that messages based on the notion of value co-creation and value appropriation effectively resonate with providers and can motivate them to improve overall customer satisfaction. In this regard, the value perspective of platform governance is not only sensible but also executable. This finding contributes to the understanding of the platform ecosystem by incorporating perspectives from the provider side, which have been relatively overlooked in governance (McIntyre & Srinivasan, 2017). A general implication is that platform operators do not have to solely assume the role of platform regulator and conflict arbitrator. When incentivized properly, providers can also play an active role in addressing potential conflicts and improving the overall platform ecosystem for value co-creation. In addition, our study provides empirical evidence for the effectiveness of a message-based mechanism in a multiplayer environment. Properly designed reminder messages can successfully coordinate independent third-party providers and reduce the platform’s operating expenditures. However, platform providers incur minimal costs in implementing the reminder message-based mechanism.

The second contribution of our study is to consider different designs of reminder messages used by the platform for governance purposes and their differential effectiveness in motivating providers. This consideration is informed by the view that value co-creation involves multiple stakeholders who may play distinct roles (Gulati 1998) and the notion that defining values is critical for designing platform governance practices (Huber et al., 2017). It is worthwhile to examine which aspects of value co-creation in a platform ecosystem could generate more desirable motivating effects on providers. A key finding is that providers respond proactively when they are reminded about the importance of their customers, which is in stark contrast to their lack of response when reminded about their own role in value co-creation. A key implication is that value messages should be designed in accordance with the features of two-sided or multisided platform ecosystems that involve end customers. Providers are likely to care more about and be more sensitive to outcomes on the customer side in value co-creation, i.e., customer satisfaction, than their own responsibilities. A narrow sense of value co-creation that misses the importance of the customer side may fail to resonate with providers and make them underestimate their responsibilities in value co-creation.

Third, our study contributes by integrating the value cocreation perspective and the value appropriation perspective in consideration of platform governance. While providers can potentially benefit by engaging in value co-creation with the platform (Constantinides et al., 2018), capturing value is a challenging issue (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Oh et al., 2015). Our findings show that value appropriation mechanisms based on provider-side competition rather than provider-side cooperation can effectively motivate providers to attend to the issue of customer complaints. The identified inefficacy of cooperation-based value appropriation mechanisms aligns with the theoretical awareness of the free-riding problem in the platform ecosystem (Cennamo & Santaló, 2019), suggesting that individual providers may lose their contribution incentives if the platform rewards their contributions collectively. Our unique contribution is to illustrate the relevance of this issue to platform governance and the related nuance that platform operators need to be careful about in advocating for value co-creation.

## Practical Implications

Our findings have important practical implications. Our study provides useful platform governance guidelines for platform operators on how to engage providers in reducing complaints. An effective approach is to use reminder messages to induce proactive efforts from providers. In this process, platform operators should carefully design their message content to stress customer-related value aspects in order to better motivate providers. Also, platform operators can benefit from incentivizing more virtuous competition between providers. They need to educate providers about how they can be rewarded individually for fewer complaints and improved customer satisfaction. Platform operators should be cautious and ensure that the free-riding problem does not undermine the collective effort of value co-creation.

Our study proposes a preventative approach to mitigating consumer complaints proactively, as this would greatly lessen the burden of the reactive approach. Handling customer complaints is costly and time-consuming for platform operators. For example, the platform in our context aims to respond to and resolve complaints within 24 hours. Once a complaint is filed, a customer representative manually reviews the complaint, calls the host for verification, and implements the resolution (e.g., updating the listed information, delisting the property, suspending the host’s account, etc.). If necessary, a customer representative or local employee may Inspect the property on-site for verification. The customer representative continues communications with the complaining renter. Our proposed message-based mechanism can generate significant financial benefits at a minimal cost. According to our interview with the platform operator, on average, handling one complaint takes about four hours and costs approximately \$40. In the four cities covered in our study, the platform receives an average of 1,200 complaints per month. Using reminder messages that emphasize renters (and the competition-based mechanism), the number of complaints dropped by 55.5% - 63.4% in our study. Our back-of-theenvelope calculation suggests that using reminder messages could save platforms approximately \$28,800 (\$40×1,200×60%) per month. Since this platform company operates in more than 20 cities, the annual savings are economically significant. More importantly, a reduction in complaints would suggest enhanced renter satisfaction, which would be expected to result in long-term intangible benefits such as high renter retention and reputation improvements.

Our study illustrates a design of using reminder messages to improve platform governance and provides empirical evidence of its effectiveness. In this design, the platform sends reminder messages after a complaint is verified for two reasons. First, in practice, platforms typically do not want to overburden their customers with unnecessary communication. Therefore, a verified complaint in the same neighborhood constitutes a legitimate reason for a platform to initiate a reminder communication. Such messages would be more relevant to regular hosts than random communication without a triggering condition. Second, and more importantly, from the perspective of communication effectiveness, a reminder message following a verified complaint in the same neighborhood can develop a sense of urgency<sup>12</sup> and therefore should be more effective than other routine messages in motivating the target hosts. Hosts would likely be more responsive to these substantiated reminder messages. In this regard, it is reasonable to expect that the effectiveness of these targeted reminder messages is dependent on the specific context of constructing these messages based on verified complaints. If the platform uses other routine or random communication approaches to reminding hosts, the effect may not be as salient as that found with the proposed messages.

Our study can also help providers better understand the imperativeness of collectively improving the governance of the ecosystem environment in which they operate. They need to proactively engage in preventative activities, such as enhancing their information presentation and improving communication with customers, to increase customers satisfaction and reduce complaints. Achieving customer satisfaction will ultimately help them to better realize the value that they are co-creating with the platform. From a value appropriation perspective, providers should also recognize that making an effort to prevent potential customer complaints is also key to their success in a competitive environment because peer providers in the same environment are likely to seek to establish their own competitive advantage by proactively preventing customer complaints.

## Future Research

Our study also has some limitations and future research can extend this line of investigation in several promising directions. First, while we believe that our main insights are generalizable, some potential constraints of our setting are worth mentioning. For example, our study focuses on the short-term efforts of providers that can be evoked by reminder messages. Whether such efforts are persistent over time is worthy of further exploration. Future research could also extend this investigation to other platform contexts where interactions between customers and providers and the platform are more frequent.

Second, given our main theoretical objective, our experimental design serves as a starting point to explore effective motivating strategies in platform governance. It would be interesting to explore hosts’ subsequent behaviors if the reminder messages were specifically targeted to certain types of complaints. Another possible extension would be to examine the role of cultural differences in the intervention effect.

Third, we expect the findings and implications hold on other two-sided platforms (e.g., crowdsourcing platforms, carsharing platforms, C2C e-commerce markets, and short-term property rental platforms), as their business models and crosssided interactions are similar to those of the long-term rental platform in our study. In the meantime, future research could examine the design of effective motivating mechanisms on twosided platforms such as online review sites (e.g., Yelp and Foursquare) and content-sharing websites (e.g., YouTube, and Twitter) where content providers voluntarily contribute content (e.g., comments, ratings, videos) without direct pecuniary payoffs.

Finally, this study focuses on motivating the proactive efforts of providers to prevent complaints. The reactive efforts of the affected hosts also warrant investigation. Our robustness test showed that hosts with no recent complaint history were more responsive to the treatment messages, compared to hosts with one or more recent complaints. Future research might consider designing other types of value-based statements to motivate reactive efforts among affected hosts.

## Parallel Trend Analysis

We adopted the relative time model (Angrist & Pischke, 2008) to check the parallel tread assumption. The relative time model is specified as:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & C o m p l a i n t s _ {i t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} H o s t _ {i} + \beta_ {2} R e n t e r _ {i} + \beta_ {3} C o m p e t i t i o n _ {i} + \beta_ {4} C o o p e r a t i o n _ {i} + \sum_ {\tau = - T} ^ {T} (\lambda_ {1 \tau} H o s t _ {i} + \lambda_ {2 \tau} R e n t e r _ {i} + \\ & \lambda_ {3 \tau} C o m p e t i t i o n _ {i} + \lambda_ {4 \tau} C o o p e r a t i o n _ {i}) \times D _ {i \tau} + C o n t r o l V a r s _ {i t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}, \end{array}
$$

where $D _ { i \tau }$ is a set of dummy variables indicating the periods. We included the same control variables as those in Equations (1) and (2). The set of coefficients, $\{ \lambda _ { 1 \tau } , \lambda _ { 2 \tau } , \lambda _ { 3 \tau } , \tau = - 3 , - 2 , 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ , identify whether a pre-treatment trend exists and how the effect changes after the treatment. Note that since we used the $\tau = - 1$ as the baseline, its coefficient was dropped due to collinearity. Table C1 summarizes the estimation results for $\{ \lambda _ { 1 \tau } , \lambda _ { 2 \tau } , \lambda _ { 3 \tau } , \lambda _ { 4 \tau } \}$ . T-tests showed that the coefficients for the weeks prior to the treatments, $\{ \lambda _ { 1 \tau } , \lambda _ { 2 \tau } , \lambda _ { 3 \tau } , \lambda _ { 4 \tau } , \tau = - 3 , - 2 \}$ , were all insignificant. But after the treatments, the majority of coefficients, $\{ \lambda _ { 1 \tau } , \lambda _ { 2 \tau } , \bar { \lambda } _ { 3 \tau } , \lambda _ { 4 \tau } , \tau = 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ , were different from zero and the differences were statistically significant. The analysis and results confirm that the parallel-trends assumption holds and indicate that the DID framework can be applied to identify the treatment effects.

## Follow-Up Survey Questions

All items are assessed using 5-point Likert scales (1-Strongly disagree, 2-Disagree, 3-Neutral, 4-Agree, 5-Strongly agree)

Host-emphasized value co-creation Source: Grover and Kohli (2012)

(1) The success of value co-creation (i.e., jointly providing valuable services) by the platform and hosts hinges on the contributions of hosts.

(2) Third-party providers play an important role in co-creating value (i.e., jointly providing valuable services) with the platform.

(3) Hosts are important and valuable to the platform.

Renter-emphasized value co-creation Source: Payne et al. (2008)

(1) The success of value co-creation (i.e., jointly providing valuable services) by the platform and hosts hinges on the satisfaction of renters.

(2) The satisfaction of renters plays an important role in determining the value co-created by the platform and third-party hosts.

(3) Renters are important and valuable to the platform.

Competition-based value appropriation Source: Beersma et al. (2003); Becker and Huselid (1992)

(1) I can obtain more benefits by outperforming other peer hosts in competition.

(2) To obtain more benefits on the platform, I need to establish competitive advantages over other peer hosts.

(3) The hosts that provide a low service quality will be in a disadvantageous position relative to other hosts in the competition.

(4) I have intense competitive relationships with other peer hosts in the same residential neighborhoods.

Cooperation-based value appropriation Source: Beersma et al. (2003)

(1) Other peer hosts in the same residential neighborhoods and I collectively create values and benefits on the platform.

(2) My benefits on the platform are linked with the residential neighborhood performance.

(3) If other peer hosts and I collectively improve the service quality, I will obtain more rewards.

(4) The platform rewards individual hosts based on their collective residential neighborhood performance.

Free-riding Source: Albanese and Van Fleet (1985); Latané et al. (1979)

(1) It is beneficial to me if my neighbor hosts collectively improve their service quality.

(2) If my neighbor hosts collectively improve their service quality, I can free ride on their effort.

(3) If my neighbor hosts collectively improve their service quality, I will slack off.
