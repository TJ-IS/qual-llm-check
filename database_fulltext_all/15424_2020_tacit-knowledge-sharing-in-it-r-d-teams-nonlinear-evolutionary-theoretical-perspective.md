# Tacit knowledge sharing in IT R&D teams: Nonlinear evolutionary theoretical perspective

Guoyin Jiang<sup>a,1,⁎</sup>, Yan Xu<sup>b,1,⁎⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Public Administration, University of Electronic Science and Technology of China, Chengdu, 611731, China <sup>b</sup> Business School, Qingdao University of Technology, Shandong, Qingdao, 266520, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Structural changes Tacit knowledge sharing Tacit knowledge hiding IT R&D team Payo<sup>f</sup> structures

## A B S T R A C T

Tacit knowledge sharing in<sup>fl</sup>uences the performance of IT R&D teams. However, information regarding the mechanism of tacit knowledge sharing is limited. Accordingly, we develop a nonlinear dynamic model to explore the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of structural changes on tacit knowledge sharing behavior in an IT R&D team, particularly in response to gradual changes in payo<sup>f</sup> structures. The following results were observed：(1) Certain situations and a traditional correlation analysis con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the current contradictions in the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of reward systems on knowledge sharing behavior are uni<sup>fi</sup>ed. (2) Under special conditions, the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of several critica factors (e.g., reciprocity, penalty of social reputation, and supervising mechanism) lose e<sup>fi</sup>cacy. As an alternative, managers can only enhance the reward system or reduce sharing cost. The e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of knowledge sharing substantially improves only once it reaches certain critical thresholds. (3) During a catastrophe, a team’s behavior may become immune to the deterioration of payo<sup>f</sup> structures. However, sudden jumps (i.e., from sharing to hiding knowledge) may occur when another threshold is reached. In summary, once team members experience a sudden jump, retrieving the previous cooperative status becomes di<sup>fi</sup>cult.

## 1. Introduction

The complex and dynamic commercial environment has forced IT enterprises to face uncertain challenges and prompted top leaders to address the crisis emphatically by means of technological and managerial innovations. In general, knowledge signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacts a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s performance [1] and market share [2], and knowledge sharing plays a mediating role in a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s decisions [3]. Teamwork is a critical factor in knowledge creation [4]. R&D teams are the major forces by which IT enterprises can e<sup>f</sup>ectively launch self-dependent innovations and develop core competitiveness. R&D teams are typical knowledge-based teams comprising knowledge workers from disparate professional <sup>fi</sup>elds who possess core irreplaceable knowledge.

The innovation performance of R&D teams critically depends on e<sup>f</sup>ective cooperation among the members. However, cooperation in the context of a knowledge-based team is based on (or directly described primarily by) the interpersonal sharing of intellectual capital, particu larly tacit knowledge. Knowledge transfer is positively related to team performance [5], and tacit knowledge sharing is the foundation of a sustainable competitive advantage [6]. For example, the powerful R&D teams of Tencent.com invented competitive IT products, namely, QQ and WeChat, which are two of the most famous instant messaging services. These teams engaged and cooperated with skilled accountants to gain knowledge. Such interactions can stimulate tacit knowledge transfer, improve team e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and promote the success rate of IT projects. However, in certain occasions, more than 70% of employees hide their knowledge from colleagues [7]. Therefore, promoting and sustaining tacit knowledge sharing remain core problems in knowledge management.

Social exchange theory indicates that tacit knowledge sharing is essentially and initially a social exchange behavior between a knowl edge provider and a knowledge receiver [8,9]. In return, people who share proprietary tacit knowledge may wish to obtain intrinsic and extrinsic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [10]. During social exchange, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs impact knowledge contribution. Therefore, we use the social exchange theory as the primary framework to describe the decision-making process in tacit knowledge sharing. In addition, game theory indicates that tacit knowledge sharing is considered a social interaction process between sharers and recipients in organizations [11,12]. Although a team aims to speci<sup>fi</sup>cally improve competitive strengths, each team member acts strategically. Consequently, the decision of members to share their knowledge during an exchange process can be formulated as a game model. In such a situation, the game payo<sup>f</sup>s essentially in<sup>fl</sup>u ence the evolution and preservation of cooperation. Accordingly, thi cooperative relationship is a function of the overall payo<sup>f</sup> structure. In the game context, knowledge sharing poses a cooperation dilemma in the absence of e<sup>f</sup>ective external interventions. Therefore, managers should focus on restructuring payo<sup>f</sup> functions to overcome the knowledge sharing dilemma.

In particular, a dynamic decision-making environment should be considered in knowledge sharing. In view of an R&D team’s actions in a dynamic scenario with a variety of variables, the behavior of the team evolves over time and exhibits strong nonlinear characteristics [13]. Shifts in preferences through either thoughtful strategies or exogenous actions can convert a state from one class of game to another and considerably change the character of a relationship.

Dynamic changes in the internal and external environments of <sup>fi</sup>rms alter the initial payo<sup>f</sup> conditions and stimulate the contribution or breakdown of cooperative relationships. Therefore, assuming that changes in the environment and incentive characteristics in<sup>fl</sup>uence a team’s dynamic behavior is reasonable.

Given the importance of changes in payo<sup>f</sup> conditions, we explore how changes in payo<sup>f</sup> structure elements in<sup>fl</sup>uence tacit knowledge sharing. A few traditional studies concerning certain elements have been determined to be inconclusive and lack unity. Other studies have reported a positive relationship between knowledge sharing and reward mechanisms [14], while other studies showed that the reward system exerts a negative [9] or insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant [11,15] e<sup>f</sup>ect on performance. The supervising mechanism [16–18] and social reputation penalty [16,19] are two positive motivators that prompt cooperative knowledge sharing. The occurrence of knowledge sharing cannot be forced (i.e., increasing the social reputation penalty and supervision intensity) but can only be encouraged (i.e., improving the reward system) and facilitated (i.e., reducing sharing cost and potential loss) [7,12,20–22].

In indeterminate situations, researchers are motivated by the critical inspiration provided by the intrinsic nonlinearity in knowledge sharing dynamics [16,23,24]. We examine how the payo<sup>f</sup> structure impacts tacit knowledge sharing from the perspective of a nonlinear coevolu tionary process. Moreover, we address the following questions:

1) What is the nonlinear property exhibited in the coevolutionary process of tacit knowledge sharing?

2) How does the reward system in<sup>fl</sup>uence tacit knowledge sharing behavior?

3) Do the social reputation penalty and supervising mechanism lose e<sup>fi</sup>cacy?

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the literature review. In section 3, we describe the model setting of tacit knowledge sharing behavior. Section 4 describes the analysis of the gradual and catastrophic changes in tacit knowledge sharing. A discussion and implications are provided in section 5. Section 6 highlights the conclusions and directions for future research.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Social exchange theory in knowledge sharing

Social exchange theory is used to understand individual behavior involved in the resource exchange process characterized by bilateral interaction and reciprocity [25]. According to this theory, self-interested parties conduct exchanges to realize accomplishments that they cannot achieve on their own. During the exchange process, each human behavior involves bene<sup>fi</sup>t maximization and cost minimization. If a party no longer recognizes reciprocity, the exchange may cease [26].

Social exchange theory is frequently used in knowledge management to investigate the perceived individual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of knowledge sharing [8,10,27–29]. Atreyi [10] used the social exchange theory to identify the bene<sup>fi</sup>t and cost elements that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the usage of electronic knowledge repositories. The results showed that self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy and enjoyment signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impact the usage of electronic knowledge repositories. Liang et al. [30] conducted a meta-analysis to observe how di<sup>f</sup>erent elements in social exchange theory impact knowledge-sharing behavior. In addition to considering the e<sup>f</sup>ects of intrinsic and extrinsic elements, a manager’s reward power and expert power directly impact knowledge sharing [31]. In knowledge sharing, individuals contribute knowledge only if the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts out weigh the costs. Consequently, managers introduce various interventions to drive knowledge sharing by restructuring bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs and ensuring that the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., rewards or appreciation by colleagues) are perceived to be higher than the cost. Empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that reputation feedback in a knowledge management system is critical for successful knowledge sharing [27]. Scholars are concerned about the e<sup>f</sup>ects of intrinsic and extrinsic factors on contributions in various knowledge types. Considering general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge in online sharing communities, Yan et al. [8] found that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts sti mulate, whereas costs hinder, knowledge sharing. These in<sup>fl</sup>uences vary between general and speci<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge sharing.

## 2.2. Tacit knowledge sharing

As a social exchange behavior, tacit knowledge sharing has been analyzed from various theoretical perspectives in di<sup>f</sup>erent types of studies, including empirical analyses, mathematical modeling, and si mulations. To develop appropriate incentive policies that can solve the cooperative dilemma, we tentatively propose four basic aspects that should be addressed.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst aspect involves the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a certain knowledge sharing scenario. The complexity of a subject induces researchers to explore it in varying organizational contexts (e.g., inter- and intra-organizations, diversi<sup>fi</sup>ed teams, and virtual communities) in which different behaviors and elements that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing may occur during the exchange process [32,33]. Therefore, the current study proposes the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the model scenario.

The second aspect is related to the development of the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t framework based on all alternative exchange relationships, particularly given the knowledge sharing scenario. In the game context, this aspect involves investigating payo<sup>f</sup> functions. Social exchange theory indicates that the cost dimension is mostly related to the agents’ waste of time and e<sup>f</sup>ort required to codify tacit knowledge for sharing [27] and the potential loss of the players’ core competitiveness or social position in the social network due to the loss of proprietary knowledge. Meanwhile, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t dimension is related to the agents’ demands (i.e., reasons for sharing knowledge). People who share knowledge may desire to gain certain intrinsic and extrinsic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in return [8,10]. Intrinsic demands (e.g., enjoyment of performing the task or helping others) do not rely on any external pressure or reward [28]. Therefore, the agents considered in the current study are sel<sup>fi</sup>sh rather than al truistic. Hence, intrinsic demands are excluded, enabling us to focus on extrinsic demands, which refer to the performance of an action that results in a desirable outcome. An economic reward, reciprocity, and reputation feedback are three typical extrinsic demands [27]. Agents naturally consider economic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which are derived from top managers. Reciprocity (i.e., acquisition of new knowledge and synergy productive/innovative value) is derived directly from opposite sides [23,34]. Reputation feedback, which is a form of social demand, is mostly rewarded indirectly by other coworkers [8,27,34–38]. Theoretical and empirical studies have shown that people with a good reputation are likely to receive assistance from others [39,40]. Therefore, this research integrates the three demands into the payo<sup>f</sup> structure (Section 3).

The third aspect involves the meaning of the exchange result, particularly given the current payo<sup>f</sup> structure or the conditions under which tacit knowledge sharing is likely to occur. Furthermore, researchers may investigate the following question: “how do changes in payo<sup>f</sup> structure elements in<sup>fl</sup>uence tacit knowledge sharing?”

However, the investigated empirical studies merely rely on a linea paradigm (i.e., absolutely positive or negative correlations with knowledge sharing). A few studies concerning certain elements (e.g., reward system) may be inconclusive. Various studies have shown that reward systems exert positive [14], negative [9], or insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant e<sup>f</sup>ects on knowledge sharing [11,15]. In addition, several studies have determined that the supervising mechanism [16–18] and penalty of social reputation [16,19] are two positive motivators that prompt knowledge sharing behavior. However, other researchers have argued that knowledge sharing cannot be forced (i.e., by increasing the social reputation penalty and supervision intensity) and can only be encouraged (i.e., by improving the reward system) and facilitated (i.e., by reducing the sharing cost and potential loss) [7,12,20–22]. These facts motivated us to use a nonlinear analysis framework to reexplore how payo<sup>f</sup> structure elements indeterminately in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing.

Structural solutions, such as altering the structure of the payo<sup>f</sup> matrix, can e<sup>f</sup>ectively solve the dilemma. The fourth aspect is related to the deep exploration of motivational solutions that focus on the impact of varying team members’ perceptions of the social environment (e.g., expectations of other members’ behavior and beliefs regarding group identity, trust, and self-e<sup>fi</sup>cacy) on cooperation. Through knowledge sharing, managers can conduct various incentive interventions by positively or negatively optimizing factors. These factors may arise from the psychological, social, organizational, and knowledge levels. Although these factors may not explicitly exist in the cost–bene<sup>fi</sup>t framework, they indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence willingness in tacit knowl edge sharing. We conducted a brief review of the current literature and tentatively classi<sup>fi</sup>ed these factors into <sup>fi</sup>ve aspects (Table 1). The rationale for such classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is that knowledge sharing is regarded as a critical social interaction process within organizations [11,12,41]. In addition, agents may interact with knowledge, IT, the self, other agents, and other organizational elements, including managers. Although our focus is not on exploration in this stage, we present a description of the fourth aspect to ensure integrity.

## 2.3. Game theory in tacit knowledge sharing

Game theory studies choice situations in which the outcome de pends on the decision of each participant. Game theory is typically used to model knowledge sharing between knowledge providers and receivers. Bandyopadhyay and Pathak [43] built a two-stage dynamic game model to describe the interaction between two players. These authors determined that when complementary knowledge among players is su<sup>fi</sup>cient, more payo<sup>f</sup>s can be gained if the top administration enforces cooperation between the players. Li and Jhang-Li [59] constructed a multi-person game framework to examine the motivations of knowledge sharing actions in various types of communities of practice. This framework is represented by individual pro<sup>fi</sup>les and decision structures. Wang and Shao [42] adopted a principal–agent method to examine the incentive mechanism in special knowledge sharing by considering knowledge-inherent characters. Yang and Wu [23], Wang et al. [16], and Jiang et al. [58] used an agent-based modeling method to explore the evolution of knowledge sharing behavior. Moreover, these studies view tacit knowledge as a general in tegral concept, even though this knowledge has inherent properties (e.g., tacitness and subjectivity); however, these properties are disregarded in the traditional literature. Therefore, the incentive mechanism of tacit knowledge sharing should be considered in tacit knowledge-inherent properties. In this research, we build a game theory model to analyze the evolution of tacit knowledge sharing behavior.

## 3. Model setting

Qualitative research provides an extensive and in-depth analysis of the organizational context in which tacit knowledge sharing occurs. This section and the subsequent sections develop and discuss the model setting of tacit knowledge sharing.

## 3.1. Identifying a tacit knowledge sharing game scenario

The study of knowledge sharing in team settings has become important because teams generally represent the basic social entity of organizations’ knowledge contribution [27]. The current study focuses on individual-level tacit knowledge sharing within a knowledge-intensive team.

To cooperate e<sup>f</sup>ectively in accomplishing an R&D project (e.g., conceptualizing an idea and researching and developing a software system) by a deadline, team members constantly engage in random communication with one another. In a knowledge-based team, such communication is based on (or directly described by) the interpersonal exchange of intellectual capital, particularly tacit knowledge, i.e., the members randomly send a request for knowledge requirement to one another and determine whether they should share/exchange core knowledge. This paradigm is consistent with that of traditional research, which argues that knowledge sharing is a dynamic rather than a one-o<sup>f</sup> process [16,23,24].

Without losing generality, we assume that the players consider only one unit of core tacit knowledge for exchanging subject matters in each interaction and that each piece of tacit knowledge has equal value or utility [23]. The following observations are applicable to any pairwise interaction:

(1) Any player plays two roles (i.e., provider and demander of knowledge), and each player possesses certain irreplaceable tacit knowledge that others lack. Thus, the team members are homogenous. The tacit knowledge level is considered the basis of the willingness to share among the members [60].

(2) Any player can request knowledge from another player. After receiving a request, a player has the same optional action set as follows: {sharing knowledge; hiding knowledge}. As a limited resource, knowledge is typically hidden [61], and knowledge hiding decreases the performance of an assignment [62]. Connelly et al. [7] demon strated that knowledge hiding comprises the following three related factors: evasive hiding, rationalized hiding, and playing dumb. In addition, knowledge hiding is not simply the opposite of knowledge sharing but is a uniformly negative behavior. This study regards knowledge hiding as a deceptive and negative action, i.e., the provider intrinsically has the ability to share knowledge but emphasizes evasive hiding and playing dumb.

(3) In relation to deciding whether to share tacit knowledge in each unit of time, we argue that the players have the same strategy set as follows: {sharing knowledge (however, if a member <sup>fi</sup>nds that an opponent does not share his/her knowledge, the member ceases to share his/her remaining knowledge); hiding knowledge}. Rationality and reality arise from the intrinsic properties of tacit knowledge (e.g., tacitness and subjectivity). We explain the rationale as follows: tacit knowledge exists in a person’s brain and is di<sup>fi</sup>cult to express to and formulate for sharers. The shared knowledge should be learned and absorbed by the receivers. Therefore, e<sup>f</sup>ective tacit knowledge sharing is an exchange process in which time and e<sup>f</sup>ort are used by sharers and receivers, i.e., decision-making is a simple action, but executing a decision is a waste of time. During the process, if a sharer determines that bidirectional reciprocity is lacking, he/she has the power to not share his/her remaining knowledge.

## 3.2. Identifying the cost–benefit framework

As a critical antecedent, motivation impacts knowledge sharing behavior. Although current game theory studies have cited various aspects [16,23,24,43], they can be substantially divided into the following two acquisitions: economic and intellectual demands. However, the acquisition of social demand is an important motivation that cannot be disregarded by knowledge workers. Therefore, in contrast to the traditional game theory literature, our study introduces the motivation of social demand acquisition. In the current research, this form of social demand is primarily referred to as “reputation feedback.”

The theoretical rationale of introducing reputation can be described as follows: knowledge sharing is a typical social exchange behavior. In addition, knowledge sharing has comparatively long-term relationships of interest compared with a one-time economic exchange. In such exchanges, a speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanism underlying the evolution of cooperation, namely, indirect reciprocity, plays a role [39]. Compared with indirect reciprocity, direct reciprocity relies on repeated encounters between the same two individuals. However, interactions among members are frequently asymmetric and instantaneous, and an individual may not gain returns when he/she helps another individual. Direct reciprocity obtains <sup>fi</sup>rsthand return similar to that in a barter economy; in contrast, indirect reciprocity resembles the invention of money. Reputation is a result of indirect reciprocity [39].

We observed the following four possible situations in any pairwise interaction (we designated the two players Players 1 and 2 in the random match).

## Case 3. Player 1 sharing versus Player 2 hiding

Compared with explicit knowledge, the action of “free-riding” is considerably di<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify. Therefore, introducing punishment and reward mechanisms is a typical intervention in traditional studies [16,19].

However, such interventions may induce other problems. For example, organizations should be able to identify members who share knowledge for reward incentives to be e<sup>f</sup>ective. Nevertheless, managers occasionally fail to accomplish this task because of the stealthiness of tacit knowledge. “Free-riding” generally occurs because of thi reason. Accordingly, we introduce a parameter denoted by $q ( q \in [ 0 , 1 ] )$ to describe the intensity in which managers can supervise or identify “free-riding” behavior if it would occur in reality. On the one hand, Player 2 may be successfully identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a free rider with a probability of q. On the other hand, Player 2 may be abortively identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a cooperator with a probability of 1 − q. This phenomenon can be used to account for the asymmetric information between managers (principals) and employees (agents).

In contrast, if Player 2 is mistakenly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a cooperator, he/ she will naturally acquire award J . If Player 2 is also identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as a free rider, he/she will receive a social reputation penalty, which can be executed by his/her colleagues by spreading information. The interaction between the players in knowledge sharing can be observed by a subset of populations who may inform others [39]. R ( 0)≥ denotes the social penalty utility, which arises from reputation feedback (or a team’s cultural orientation of emphasizing public praise). Thus, helping/betraying someone who establishes a good/bad reputation is rewarded/penalized by others. Moreover, once the sharer determines that bidirectional reciprocity is lacking in this situation, he/she has the power to not share his/her remaining knowledge. Player 2′s capability to acquire new knowledge and synergy productive value and Player 1′s cost are relatively lower than those in Case 2 due to the lack of an e<sup>f</sup>ective interchange of ideas. Thus, we reduce the two variables by half for simplicity.

## 3.3. Evolution of group behavior

The complex evolution of collective behavior, which is based on the aforementioned individual-level tacit knowledge sharing decisionmaking, cannot be easily formulated using mathematical functions. To achieve the objective of this research, we focus on a scenario in which the evolution of group decision-making can be determined using the replicator dynamic equation, i.e., Eq. (1). This equation is used to describe the net growth rate of a team’s behavior over time as follows:

$$
\frac {\mathrm{dx}}{\mathrm{dt}} = \mathrm{x} \cdot (1 - \mathrm{x}) \cdot (\mathrm{M} \cdot \mathrm{x} - \mathrm{N})\tag{1}
$$

where

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c} M = \frac {(K + P - C)}{2} + R - (1 - q) \cdot J \\ N = \frac {C}{2} - J \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

and $x ( \in [ 0 , 1 ] )$ denotes the proportion of members who share knowledge in the group in stage t. This equation can help outline knowledge sharing mechanisms in a macro environment in which the members randomly match and interact during each period regardless of the network structure and internal micro personal strategy decision mechanisms [9]. The underlying time scale of the model is assumed to be linked to the decision-making process, and thus, the model is realistic under the time framework [13].

## 4. Gradual and catastrophic changes in tacit knowledge sharing

We focus on analyzing how changes in payo<sup>f</sup> structure elements in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing behavior. The basic method is used to provide responses to the observable equilibrium behavior of a team in relation to changes, particularly mild and gradual changes. In reality, these changes are easily disregarded by incentive policy designers in exchange for chronical stupefaction or blind con<sup>fi</sup>dence based on previous e<sup>f</sup>ective incentive management in relation to previously dis cussed key variables. The major <sup>fi</sup>nding is that the response to knowledge sharing behavior may undergo structural changes as the parameters gradually change, i.e., the response pattern in a few situations is simply “gradual changes result in gradual changes.” In contrast, “gradual changes result in catastrophic changes” may occur under special conditions. In particular, we have the following patterns.

## 4.1. Pattern 1: Gradual changes result in gradual changes

Theorem 1. The observed behavior of a team only su<sup>f</sup>ers from smooth changes that correspond to gradual changes in the six elements if and only if $M < 0$ holds.

Proof: To render the research more realistic, we assume that these parameters satisfy $M \neq 0$ and $\frac { N } { M } \in [ 0 , 1 ] .$ . In such a situation, $\frac { N } { M }$ is an interior equilibrium point. If $\stackrel { \triangledown } { M } < 0$ holds, only one equilibrium scenario exists for the team, i.e., $\begin{array} { r } { x = \frac { N } { M } . } \end{array}$ . Logically, x is a continuous function of the six elements. Q.E.D.

## 5. Discussion and implications

Intrinsic and extrinsic factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing in IT R& D teams. Drawing upon social exchange theory and game theory, we build a nonlinear game theory model of tacit knowledge sharing. This model incorporates factors associated with the reward system (J ), supervising mechanism (q), value of reputation feedback (R), acquisition of new knowledge (K ), sharing cost (C), and synergy productive/ innovative value (P). Using this model, we explore how these factors work and determine the equilibrium under gradual and catastrophic changes in the game structure. This research makes three contributions to the <sup>fi</sup>eld of information systems.

First, this study presents a new perspective regarding the analysis of the behavior of tacit knowledge sharing in IT R&D teams by observing the nonlinear evolution of sharing numbers. Traditional empirical research concerning knowledge sharing examines how factors linearly impact contribution behavior, and only a few studies examined the nonlinear relationship between the behavior and independent vari ables. Our model can represent a relationship knowledge strategy (sharing and hiding) and independent variables, including a reward system, a supervising mechanism, value of reputation feedback, acquisition of new knowledge, sharing cost, and synergy productive/innovative value. This model can support the examination of a nonlinea relationship in tacit knowledge sharing in IT R&D teams.

Second, this study provides novel knowledge regarding the relationship between factors and knowledge sharing behavior. An empirical study was performed to examine the correlation between factors and behavior at the overall level. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be summarized as follows: positive correlation, negative correlation, and no correlation. Our model not only reexamines the overall correlation but also explores the dynamic and combined e<sup>f</sup>ects of factors. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show different equilibriums across various combinations of variables. Smooth and catastrophic changes in the equilibrium can be addressed with certain settings in game payo<sup>f</sup>. This condition indicates that a nonlinear change exists in knowledge sharing behavior, and the related results enrich our understanding of knowledge management in information systems.

Third, this research contributes to managerial strategies in tacit knowledge sharing by examining the role boundary of reward and punishment mechanisms. In general, rewards can stimulate knowledge sharing, whereas the supervising mechanism can suppress the intention to share knowledge. However, these results are not consolidated in any scenario. Furthermore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings indicate that a team’s behavior will su<sup>f</sup>er from a catastrophic improvement when the reward value in creases or sharing cost decreases gradually and reaches a critical threshold. The penalty and supervising mechanisms fail to work in certain situations. In particular, in certain cases, increasing the penalty and supervision intensity can lose e<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and thus, the blind implementation of this strategy is meaningless. A lower level of reward value increase or sharing cost decrease can gradually stimulate sharing behavior. In contrast, a higher level of the strategy can achieve catastrophic improvement.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have three signi<sup>fi</sup>cant IT practical implications. First, building knowledge management infrastructure based on the proposed model provides a foundation to explore and utilize the mechanism of tacit knowledge sharing in IT teams. Predicting and addressing gradual and sudden changes in performance are challenging. The infrastructure can support managers in capturing optimal intensity combinations and making optimal decisions, allowing them to eliminate negative changes from knowledge sharing to overall knowledge hiding. Second, to motivate the occurrence of knowledge sharing behavior, managers should focus on modifying the reward system and sharing cost rather than on reciprocity, reputation feedback, and supervising mechanisms. These inferences are consistent with those reported in traditional studies [6,10,61]. Thus, these inferences prove that the occurrence of knowl edge sharing cannot be forced (i.e., by increasing the social reputation penalty and supervision intensity) and can only be encouraged (i.e., by improving the reward system) and facilitated (i.e., by reducing the sharing cost and potential loss). Third, each factor (R, K, P, C, J, and q) may result in a sudden change from knowledge sharing to knowledge hiding, but retrieving the cooperative status is di<sup>fi</sup>cult. Thus, managers should avoid such a change in status in practice; otherwise, a larger sharing reward and a lower sharing cost should be implemented as soon as possible. The extent of communication is positively associated with the e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of knowledge transfer among team members [67]. Hence, appropriate isolation and temporary information hindrance can avoid negative (or positive) catastrophic changes in collective behavior.

## 6. Conclusions and recommendations for future research

Change is not only a state but a process of constant ongoing and never-ending instances in organizational life. Such an environment forces managers to understand the dynamics and mechanisms of knowledge sharing. How administrative change elements in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing and the ideal managerial incentive policies aiming to stimulate knowledge contribution are issues that should be addressed. To address these issues, we developed a nonlinear evolutionary theoretical framework for tacit knowledge sharing. This framework describes knowledge sharing as dynamic and nonlinear rather than one o<sup>f</sup> and linear. Our results con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the contradictions in the in<sup>fl</sup>u ence of reward systems on knowledge sharing behavior are uni<sup>fi</sup>ed. However, several critical factors (e.g., reciprocity, penalty of social reputation, and supervising mechanism) can lose their e<sup>fi</sup>cacy in special scenarios. Enhancing the reward system or reducing the sharing cost is an alternative strategy for stimulating knowledge sharing. The e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness of knowledge sharing can be substantially improved when certain critical thresholds are met. Once a sudden jump occurs, retrieving the previous cooperative status is extremely di<sup>fi</sup>cult.

Our model delves into the process of tacit knowledge sharing; however, this research has several limitations that should be addressed or overcome in the future. First, although this study concentrates on employees’ three demands as antecedents to their knowledge sharing decision in a team, several factors may be involved (e.g., fairness and risk). The justice–knowledge sharing relationship has received minimal research attention, although the role of justice can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the quality of social exchange relationships between knowledge seekers and providers [20,37,68]. Risk is an important factor in project success [69–72] given that its role in knowledge sharing is relevant. Thus, future re search should aim to introduce justice and risk perception utility into the payo<sup>f</sup> function to explore their roles in adjusting strategies. Second, our model is only a typi<sup>fi</sup>cation under certain assumptions (i.e., replicator dynamic evolutionary process to focus on investigating the catastrophe mechanism). In this scenario, we examine context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c details. Although this particular model is not exhaustive in identifying the dynamics of tacit knowledge sharing, our theoretical analysis marginally o<sup>f</sup>ers the directions of the tested contents (i.e., existence of nonlinear characteristics of sudden jumps and hysteresis). Scholars have argued that the catastrophe model, which is a part of the dynamic system approach and has been used to explain and describe sudden jumps in dynamic systems [73–75], is unique because it provides an explanation for hysteresis [75]. Therefore, one line of testing research could focus on techniques that apply the statistical formulations of catastrophe models to future studies.
