# The Impact of Multi-channel and Multi-Product Strategies on Firms’ Risk-Return Performance

Abstract: This paper investigates the impact of a firm’s multi-channel and multi-product strategies on its risk-return performance. We argue that channel diversity is positively associated with a firm’s profitability and negatively associated with its profit volatility, and that channel diversity and product diversity are complementary in driving these profit-enhancing and volatility-suppressing effects. We test our hypotheses in the U.S. credit union industry, in which credit unions offer a variety of loan and deposit products through a variety of online and offline channels, including physical branches, ATMs, telephone banking, online banking, and mobile banking. By analyzing a longitudinal dataset of 7,577 credit unions in 2009-2016, the study provides empirical evidence that operating a full spectrum of online and offline channels leads to higher profitability and lower profit volatility for credit unions. The study also reveals differential moderating effects of loan product diversity and deposit product diversity, in that loan product diversity enhances the profit-enhancing effect of channel diversity while deposit product diversity enhances the volatility-suppressing effect of channel diversity. This paper contributes to the studies of channel-based strategic differentiation as a competitive strategy and the studies of firms’ risk-return performance.

Keywords: Channel Diversity; Product Diversity; Online Banking; Mobile Banking; Risk-return; Credit Union

## 1. Introduction

Information technology (IT) is continuously revolutionizing how firms deliver their products and services to customers. Developing new web-based and mobile channels is becoming the “arms race” for companies in retailing, finance, entertainment, transportation, and other customer-facing industries. In the banking industry, for example, one practitioner’s survey reports that 89% of the banks were increasing their investments in “channel innovations” [1]. Many advantages have been attributed to the new webbased and mobile channels [2], and these new channels complement traditional channels such as physical stores, call centers, and catalogs to provide product information and fulfill transactions [3, 4]. We have accumulated abundant academic and anecdotal evidence that multi-channel customers are buying more, feeling more satisfied, more loyal, and ultimately more profitable [for a review, see 5]. Although we still lack firm-level empirical evidence on the strategic value of operating multiple channels [5], it appears that leveraging multi-channels as a source of competitive advantage, as Porter speculated in his seminal strategy works [6], is plausible. This study aims to, first of all, validate this speculation.

Moreover, most of the research attention on the performance impact of operating multiple online and offline channels so far focuses on the return dimension of firm performance such as revenue, cost, or profit [7-9] but not on the risk dimension. Both management and IT studies of firm performance, as a contrast, have paid increasing attention to examine risk-return performance as a whole [e.g., 10, 11, 12]. We know from previous studies that IT investment often increases both risk and return for a firm [13, 14], but it is also plausible that some firms leverage IT initiatives as a managerial intervention to optimize their risk-return performance, sometimes even simultaneously increasing return and reducing risk [11]. We may reasonably doubt that, even if operating multi-channels can contribute to a firm’s profitability, such benefit could be gained at the expense of increased risk. In fact, we know little about the impact of multi-channels on firms’ risk from the extant literature. Thus, this study also aims to fill in the gap by examining the performance impact of operating a combination of online and offline channels from a holistic risk-return perspective.

Lastly, this paper also investigates the interaction between channel strategies and product strategies.

According to Porter’s view of strategy [6], firms can differentiate themselves from their competitors based on either product or channel differentiation, which, if effectively implemented, should both be able to confer sustainable competitive advantage. However, we have little discussion, let alone evidence, about the relationships between the benefits of an effective channel strategy and those of an effective product strategy. Are they merely additive, in that the benefits of one strategy just add to the benefits of the other and the two strategies work in parallel without interacting? Or are they substituting each o her, in that the two types of strategies cancel each other’s performance effects? Or could they compl ment each other? As its third research objective, this paper aims to reveal the nature of the possibly existing interactions between channel and product strategies on both the risk and return dimensions of firm performance.

The empirical context of this paper is the U.S. credit union industry. Like retail banks, credit unions provide services mostly in two product categories, including loans and deposits; and credit unions deliver their services through a variety of online and offline channels, including physical branches, ATMs, telephone banking, online banking, and mobile banking. There has been increasing enthusiasm, in both academia and practice, about the wide use of online banking and the recent surge of mobile banking [e.g., 1, 15, 16]. Like in many other industry sectors, online channels, including both web-based and mobile device-based ones, are widely believed as disruptive innovations in the credit union industry. Because of the practical salience of operating multiple channels in this industry, and also because credit unions are particularly subject to high cross-channel integration demands when they choose to operate multiple channels, the credit union industry constitutes an ideal empirical context for us to investigate the performance effects of multi-channel and multi-product strategies.

We organize the rest of this paper as follows: We start with reviewing relevant literature in Section 2, followed by our hypothesis development in Section 3. We then describe our research context in Section 4 and our research method in Section 5. The results of our analyses are reported subsequently in Section 6. We then conclude the paper with discussing its contributions and limitations in Section 7.

## 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Firms’ Risk-Return Performance

## ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

The risk-return performance of firms has long been a central research topic in the strategic management literature [e.g., 10, 17, 18]. While financial economists often study a firm’s risk and return as an absolute trade-off, management scholars notice that some firms appear to be able to achieve high return and low risk consistently, a phenomenon referred to by Bowman as the “risk-return paradox” for strategic management [17]. Since the publication of Bowman’s paradox, many studies follow up and diversification into multiple product markets offers one of the most powerful explanations on how firms manage to simultaneously reduce risk and increase return [e.g., 10, 19-22]. According to this stream of research, firms can improve their overall risk-return performance (i.e., increasing return with reduced risk) through diversifying into multiple product markets, as long as they carefully choose their market positioning and organizational structures during diversification [10, 22]. In terms of market positioning, firms diversified into related, rather than unrelated, product markets may particularly have better overall risk-return performance [10, 21]. In terms of organizational structures, diversified firms that implement a multi-divisional organizational structure appear to have better overall risk-return performance, although the benefit of such structure appears dependent on the specific diversification modes [22]. Overall, the strategic management studies of risk-return performance highlights the importance of product market diversification.

In the information systems literature, studies of IT business value have been historically focused more on the influence of IT investment on firms’ return rather than on risk [23]. In the recent decade, however, scholars started to pay increasing attention to the impact of IT on firms’ overall risk-return performance [e.g., 11, 12, 14]. In particular, there appear to be two groups of related studies along this research direction.

The first group of studies focus on the risk and return of IT capital investment. While IT investment may potentially lead to positive organizational outcomes, it also implies great technical, financial, and strategic risks at the organizational level [24-26]. Dewan and his colleagues report, in a series of studies, that IT capital investment contributes more to firms’ risks than other non-IT capital investments [14], and the positive return on IT capital investment often comes at the expense of increased IT risks [12-14, 27]. However, scholars also identified some business strategies that can improve the overall risk-return performance of IT capital investment: particularly related to our study, Dewan and Ren [12] find that product diversification can simultaneously increase the return on IT investment and decrease the risk of it.

The second group of studies focus on the use of specific IT systems as a strategic weapon to help firms improve their overall risk-return performance. Taking a perspective of resource complementarity, Tanrverdi and Ruefli [11] discuss conceptually that IT initiatives can be used as managerial interventions to shift the risk-return tradeoff and allow firms to achieve favorable overall risk-return performance. Empirical studies along this line of inquiry often report supportive evidence. Among them, Otim and his colleagues find that IT investment that is transformational or informational in nature can significantly reduce firms’ overall risk [28]; Tian and Xu report that the implementation of ERP systems reduces firms’ risk [29]; Mithas and his colleagues similarly report that the implementation of CRM systems improves customer satisfaction [30], which in turn simultaneously increases return and reduces risk [31]; and, in the context of supply chain operation, Xue and his colleagues report that modularized information systems can reduce the perceived operational risk [32]. Although rather disparate, these studies collectively suggest a promising direction that firms can actively leverage IT systems and IT initiatives to manage their overall risk-return performance.

With only a few exceptions [e.g., 13], the literature of IT and firms’ risk-return performance so far focuses mostly on IT capital investment or specific IT systems, but it does not adequately examine the risk-return performance of IT-enabled business initiatives. While the capital investment in IT or a specific IT system is certainly important, IT-enabled business initiatives are arguably more strategic, and they have more direct business impacts and can be more effective in conferring sustainable competitive advantage [33]. Launching new online distribution channels, for example, represents a strategic, ITenabled business initiative. Yet, we know very little about the overall risk-return implications of such initiatives from the literature.

## 2.2 Multi-Channel Strategies and Their Performance Impacts

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

Unlike multi-product strategies that have been widely studied in the business diversification literature [for a review, see 34], multi-channel strategies received relatively less attention from strategy scholars. This neglect is somewhat puzzling, because back to 1980s, Michael Porter, in his seminar works of strategy, discussed strategies as positioning, including both variety-based positioning by choosing to offer only some products and services selectively and access-based positioning by choosing to deliver products and services to customers in a selected set of channels [6, 35]. Subsequent strategy scholars have intensively studied the former type of positioning and its implications on firms’ risk-return performance, particularly in the literature of business diversification [10, 19], but they have rarely studied the latter one. Marketing scholars, on the other hand, have a long tradition of studying distribution channels, but they rarely linked multi-channel management back to firms’ overall risk-return performance.

The situation starts to change after the inception of online distribution channels. The salience of online businesses finally triggered the surge of multi-channel management studies [4, 36]. These studies typically focus on the relationship between online and offline channels. Many theoretical and empirical insights have been accumulated [for reviews, see 5, 37]. Collectively, we now have abundant evidence that online and offline channels mostly complement rather than cannibalize each other [5, 38, 39], and such complementarity depends at least can be enhanced by, firms’ cross-channel integration activities [3, 7, 40-42].

However, the empirical evidence on the performance impact of multi-channel management so far is mostly on consumer side. Studies have intensively examined consumer behaviors in face of different channels [e.g., 43, 44-46], and collectively, these studies confirm that multi-channel consumers tend to more profitable than single-channel consumers [5]. While customers’ choices and behaviors will certainly inform firms’ strategic decisions, the question of choosing and configuring multiple channels from a firm’s perspective, despite its direct connection to Porter’s [6] original conceptualization of strategy, has received little research attention. Neslin and Shankar [5] note in their review article the following observation: “Is it [multi-channel marketing] an approach to develop enduring competitive advantage? Very little research has investigated this issue.” (pp.73)

Considering risk-return as a holistic view of firm performance, we have even less theories and empirical evidence about the performance impact of multi-channels. On the return dimension, a few studies have revealed that the performance effects of channel choices are not linear but instead depend on a firm’s previous experiences [7] and its competitive environments [9, 40]. On the risk dimension, we have some evidence that multi-channel customers tend to be more loyal and satisfied [5], and customer firm-level empirical evidence on the performance implications of operating multiple channels, especially from a holistic risk-return view.

## 3. Hypotheses Development

## 3.1 Channel Diversity and Profitability

As mentioned, at the consumer level, we have accumulated certain evidence that multi-channel consumers on average yield more revenue to a firm. In fact, Neslin and Shankar [5] summarize related studies and then conclude that “the empirical evidence that the average multi-channel customer buys more and is more valuable than the single-channel customer is reaching the point of an empirical generalization” (pp. 72). In this paper, we start by arguing that this consumer-level performance effect of operating multiple channels also holds at the firm level, which serve as our baseline hypothesis.

We define channel diversity as the extent to which a firm delivers products and services to its customers through a variety of online and offline channels. By allowing the co-existence of online and offline channels and unleashing their potential synergies, channel diversity can, as we elaborate below, contributes to a firm’s profitability through both revenue generation and cost reduction.

The combination of online and offline channels can increase the revenue of a firm in several ways. The first and straightforward explanation is that channel diversity increases the availability of a product to customers [37]. Because of the existence of multiple distribution channels, customers simply have a higher chance to notice the product or have an easier access to it, and consequently they purchase more. For example, online channels are particularly convenient for consumers to collect information about a large assortment of products [2], and physical channels can offer deeper, richer, and more customized

information that might be needed by customers to make a specific purchase decision. Second, channel diversity also increases the potential market size by allowing a firm to cover more customer segments and geographic markets. Because online and offline channels often attract different types of customers [5], operating multiple channels can increase a firm’s customer base. Moreover, online channels can also expand a firm’s geographic reach by allowing the firm to serve geographically distant customers, thus complementing offline channels. As a result, the increased market size is likely to lead to increased revenue. Lastly, channel diversity generates opportunities of cross-selling and other cross-channel synergies [7, 47]. With multiple channels in place, a firm can purposefully introduce its customers from one channel to other channels and consequently have multiple points of contact with the same customer. For example, grocery retailers often encourage their online shoppers to pick up their orders in physical stores, with the hope that these shoppers will make extra purchases during their pick-up trips.

The combination of online and offline channels can also reduce cost and enhance a firm’s operational efficiency. IT-based self-service channels are particularly known for their cost efficiency. In the banking industry, for example, it was estimated that a transaction with personal services in a branch would cost a bank \$1.15, while an ATM-based transaction would cost \$0.36, and an online transaction only \$0.02 [48]. IT-based self-services are available around the clock and have few scalability constraint, thus becoming an effective source of economies of scale. However, pure play firms with only self-service channels can face many other challenges, because not all types of transactions or customers are suitable for self-services. When a firm operates a variety of channels, it can optimize its cost structure by directing simple, routinized transactions to IT-based self-service channels and focus on other transactions that need high human-touch in some of its offline channels [49]. In line with these arguments, scholars have repeatedly found that it is the mix of human- and IT-based services that is optimal for a firm’s performance [49, 50]. Lastly, channel diversity can also lead to economies of channel scope. Different channels often share at least some common back-end IT systems and business support functions, so the marginal cost of adding one more channel is often much lower than operating this channel alone. Further, because different channels can share marketing campaigns, for a given level of effectiveness, marketing costs can also decrease when a firm operates multiple channels and organizes cross-channel promotions [5].

Summarizing the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Channel diversity is positively associated with a firm’s profitability.

## 3.2 Channel Diversity and Profit Volatility

Firms face greater financial and operational risks when their performance fluctuates abruptly and constantly over time. Profit volatility, defined as the variance of a firm’s profitability across different time periods, thus has been widely used in the literature as an indicator of a firm’s overall risk [e.g., 51, 52, 53]. In this paper, we further argue that, in addition to increasing profitability, channel diversity can also suppress a firm’s profit volatility through two mechanisms, including reducing customer churn and defending the firm from competition.

First, the combination of online and offline channels can reduce customer churns through building trust, increasing customer loyalty, and creating customer lock-in. Online channels have the advantages of convenience and availability [2], but firms with only online channels particularly face the challenge of gaining customers’ trust, as customers often prefer a physical location for product returns, complains, or personal conversations [54]. Physical channels are geographically proximate to customers and can be better situated in a customer’s social networks and communities, so they can help a firm gain the customer’s trust through direct and indirect social exchange [55]. Similarly, mobile channels are more effective when the mobile channels can direct customers to activities in nearby physical stores [56]. Studies show that retailers operating multiple channels can offer a richer shopping experiences to customers, thus increasing their satisfaction and ultimately gaining customer loyalty [57]. Moreover, operating a combination of online and offline channels sometimes allows a firm to collect a comprehensive set of data about a customer and, through mining the data, the firm can personalize its services to the customer, especially in the form of personalized recommendation, targeted promotion, and certain customer loyalty programs. When a customer has accumulated a long history of browsing and purchasing data with one retailer and gets used to the retailer’s services, the customer will be locked in and less likely to switch to other retailers. As evidence, in a study of the roadside assistance services of automotive manufacturers, Scherer, Wünderlich and Wangenheim [50] find that customer retention is maximized when a service provider combines intermediate levels of personal services and self-services instead of relying primarily on one of the two. Because customer churn leads to fluctuations in firms’ performance, and customer satisfaction and loyalty can instead reduce such fluctuations [31], overall we argue that channel diversity can suppress a firm’s profit volatility through reducing customer churn.

In addition, implementing emerging online channels could be a defensive move of a firm in order to protect itself from competition. In the recent decades, online channels diffuse quickly in many customerfacing industries, and those firms lagging behind in adopting these channels face the increasing risk of losing their customers. Consequently, sometimes firms expand into online channels as a defensive launch web-based service channels as a strategic necessity to avoid competitive disadvantages relative to their competitors. Du [59] also find that financial institutions launch their mobile services partly as a response to similar launching activities of their geographic peers. As the diffusion of online channels accelerates, adopting these channels can protect a firm by keeping it on parity with other adopters and thus buffer its profitability from increasing market competition, which in turn suppresses profit volatility.

Summarizing the above arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Channel diversity is negatively associated with a firm’s profit volatility.

## 3.3 Product Diversity and Channel Diversity

From the perspective of a customer, channels, after all, are just means through which a product or service is delivered, and thus channels are certainly no substitute for the product or service itself. Studies have revealed that the value of channels depends largely on various characteristics of the products and services delivered on the channels [45, 46]. However, although scholars have repeatedly examined the direct performance effects of multi-product strategies in both marketing [e.g., 60] and strategy fields [e.g., 34, 61], the joint performance effects of multi-channel and multi-product strategies at the firm level are yet to be explored to our knowledge.

We define product diversity as the extent to which a firm delivers products and services in a broad spectrum of categories. We then further hypothesize that product diversity will enhance the positive influence of channel diversity on firm performance, including both profitability enhancing and profit volatility suppressing effects for the reasons below.

As we argued, channel diversity could increase profitability by expanding a firm’s reach in terms of both customer segments and geographic markets. When a firm reaches more customer segments through different channels, product diversity will generate more sales opportunities, including both simply selling the same products to a broader customer base and also more cross-selling opportunities. In the banking industry, studies have found that multi-channel banking customers tend to purchase a larger variety of financial products [62]. Banks, for example, can first attract customers by offering favorable interest rates or incentives for their standard deposit or credit card products, which are typically managed in automated self-service channels, and then cross-sell other financial products, such as real estate mortgages or longterm financial investment products, which usually involves services in a physical branch. In this banking example, product diversity enhances the performance effect of channel diversity by creating more crosschannel selling opportunities. Similarly, when online channels expand a firm’s geographic market reach, offering more products will allow the firm to generate more revenue from its newly reached markets. In summary, we propose the following hypothesis on the complementarity between channel diversity and product diversity:

H3: Product diversity enhances the effect of channel diversity on a firm’s profitability.

We have argued that channel diversity could reduce profit volatility through reducing customer churn and protecting firms from competition. We further argue below that both mechanisms can be more effective when the firm offers a broader spectrum of products.

First, channel diversity can better create customer loyalty and lock-ins when a firm offers diverse products to the same customers and gains a competitive advantage by becoming customers’ “one-stop shop” [63]. The logic behind the “one-stop shop” arguments is rooted in the studies of information asymmetry as a source of competitive advantage for sellers [64, 65]. Because customers typically has

# ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

informational disadvantage with regards to the quality of products or services, they have to take certain risk in dealing with a seller and bear certain transaction cost to investigate the seller. Because of such transaction risk and cost, a rational customer would prefer dealing with a minimum number of sellers and building trust with them through repeated transactions. Thus, firms that can meet more needs of a customer have a particular type of advantage over specialized firms, referred to as the advantage of being a “one-stop shop” [64, 65]. By selling a variety of products through a variety of channels, a seller can potentially meet more demands from the same customer, such as buying standard products online and seeking advices about complicated products from sales specialists in person, thus increasing customers loyalty [50].

Second, channel diversity as defensive strategy can be more effective when a firm offers a broader spectrum of products. Competition is costly. When a firm meets its competitors in more product markets, as the theory of multi-market contact [e.g., 66] suggests, the competing firms start to restrain themselves from aggressive competition moves in order to avoid having a total war in all of their overlapping product markets. For example, when a competitor expands into new online channels, the focal firm can match this move by similarly expanding into online channels, and the subsequent competition among them on the new online channels could be intense and costly. When the focal firm offers a broader spectrum of products, however, it can pose a more creditable threat to its competitors and force them to consider a state of “tacit collusion” [66], in which the two competitors may still keep contact in multiple channels and product markets but do not compete with each other aggressively. Thus, product diversity in this case can make channel diversity as a defensive strategy more effective.

In summary, as product diversity enhances the effects of channel diversity to increase customer loyalty and defend firms from competition, it will enhance the overall effect of channel diversity on suppressing profit volatility. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H4: Product diversity enhances the effect of channel diversity on a firm’s profit volatility.

## 4. Research Context

The empirical context of this study is the credit union industry in the U.S. The businesses of credit unions and retail banks are much alike, including primarily acquiring deposits from and selling loans to individuals, households, and businesses. Credit unions differ from retail banks mostly in their ownership structure, in that credit unions in the U.S. are mutually owned by their members and established as cooperative financial institutions.<sup>1</sup> The U.S. credit union industry has been continuously consolidating in the past decade. As of 2016, there are about 7,500 credit unions in operation. In comparison with retail nd the median number of customers as 3,344 as of the second quarter of 2016. They hold about 10% of U.S. household deposits and 9% customer loans, and were considered filling a niche of the overall finance sector [67].

Like retail banks, credit unions often provide banking services through multiple channels, including face-to-face services in a physical branch, self-services through ATM machines, remote services through telephone banking, and also remote self-services including online banking and mobile banking. Each credit union can adopt some or all of the five different channels above, and we refer to a specific credit union’s choice of channels as its channel configuration. For the convenience of discussion, we denote a specific channel configuration as a combination of up to five letters, including B (branch), A (ATM), T (Telephone banking), O (online banking), and M (mobile banking). Thus, for example, a credit union with a BATOM configuration operates all the five channels; a credit union with a BTO configuration operates branches, telephone banking, and online banking only; and a credit union with a type B configuration operates only branches; etc. Because there are five different channels potentially available, the total number of possible channel configurations, in principle, is 31.<sup>2</sup>

Our conceptualization of credit unions’ channel structures as configurations has its conceptual base rooted in the configurational theories [68, 69]. Configurational theories take a systematic and holistic view on organizations by studying the patterns or profiles of organizations’ choices among a large number of organizational attributes [69], and, as information systems scholars have argued [68], they are particularly suitable to study firms’ IT decisions and activities. In our research context, a credit union faces an organizational design question as how many distribution channels it needs to implement out of the totally five possible ones. Each channel configuration thus represents a unique design option for its channel structure. Some of these configurations could lead to stronger risk-return performance than others. count variable or a set of five binary variables, we can more effectively investigate the potential causal relationship between channel configurations and performance.

We collected the channel information of all the U.S. credit unions from NCUA (National Credit Union Administration) between 2009 and 2016 to generate a descriptive report on the status of service channels in this industry. Previous academic studies have extensively investigated the organizational impact of several service channels, such as ATMs [70], online banking in the context of both credit unions [71, 72] and retail banks [15, 16, 58, 73], and, to a limited degree, mobile banking [74]. Collectively, these studies provide empirical evidence that banks and credit unions on average can benefit from these channels separately. The studies of multiple banking channels, however, have been limited and almost exclusively focused on the perspective of banking customers [e.g., 44, 62, 75, 76]. ## 5. Methods

## 5.1 Data and Sample

As introduced above, we chose to use the credit union industry in the U.S. as the empirical context to test our hypotheses. In the U.S., credit unions need to submit a quarterly report, named “5300 Call Report,” to NCUA (National Credit Union Administration), which in turn makes these firm-level Call Report data publicly available. The Call Report data consist of detailed demographic, financial, and operational data of credit unions, including the financial products they offer and the service channels they operate. We collected the quarterly Call Report data from NCUA for all the credit unions from the first quarter of 2009 (2009Q1) to the second quarter of 2016 (2016Q2), the latest reporting period as of this study. The initial dataset consists of an unbalanced panel with 209,501 quarterly observations from 7,903 unique credit unions. The sample size subsequently decreases due to missing data for various study variables and because we introduced time lag structures in our estimation models, as we will explain below.

## 5.2 Dependent Variables

Profitability. Following previous finance studies in similar empirical contexts [e.g., 16, 53, 71, 73, 77], we used both return on assets (ROA) and return on equity (ROE) to measure the profitability of credit unions. Specifically, ROA was computed as the net income of a credit union in a given quarter divided by the average of its beginning- and ending- balances of total assets in the same quarter; ROE was computed as net income divided by the average net worth of a credit union in a given quarter [53]. The net worth of a credit union is self-reported by the credit union in its quarterly Call Report and consists of undivided earnings, reserves, and other capital items.

Profit Volatility. We followed previous studies [e.g., 53] to measure a credit union’s profit volatility as the standard deviation of the credit union’s profitability in six consecutive quarters. Similar to the measurement of profitability, we computed two versions of volatility measures, including the standard deviation of ROA or ROE respectively. We chose to use six quarters as the length of the computation window because there have been evidence that it takes at least 1.5 years for the performance impact of newly introduced online banking channels to show up significantly [16, 73].

Because our initial dataset covers the entire population of credit unions, there were sometimes extreme cases in which credit unions were extremely small and unstable, underwent major restructuring activities, or simply made reporting mistakes. In those cases, their ROA or ROE, as well as the volatility of their ROA or ROE, would appear to be far outliers in our dataset. We thus trimmed these four variables at their 1<sup>st</sup> and 99<sup>th</sup> percentiles to eliminate the influence of extreme cases.

## 5.3 Independent Variables

Channel Diversity. As introduced earlier, while there is a great number of possible channel configurations for a credit union to choose, the most commonly chosen ones are types B (physical branch only), BATO (branch, ATM, telephone banking, and online banking only), and BATOM (branch, ATM, telephone, online, and mobile banking). We used the type B as the baseline configuration for comparison, and then included in our analyses three binary variables to indicate whether a credit union has a channel configuration of BATO, BATOM, or others respectively. BATOM represents the most diverse channel configuration in our empirical context.

Product Diversity. Loans and deposits are the two most common and important types of products a credit union offers. Loans generate revenue for credit unions, while deposits provide the critical financial resources for credit unions’ loan businesses. We thus measured a credit union’s loan diversity and deposit diversity separately, as two different types of product diversity. Specifically, we adapted an entropy-based measure for both of them. Entropy has been widely used in various academic disciplines to measure the degree to which a system is made up of diverse parts. Palepu was among the first to introduce it to business studies by developing an entropy-based formula to measure the diversity of a firm’s revenue sources [78]. Since then, entropy-based diversity measures have been widely adopted in multiple business

disciplines, including information systems [e.g., 79, 80].

From credit unions’ Call Report templates, we first obtained two lists of loan or deposit products commonly offered in the credit union industry, with each list consisting of seven categories of loan or deposit products.<sup>4</sup> Credit unions are mandated to report the ending balance of their loan or deposit products in each and every category of these two lists in their quarterly reports, should they offer products in that specific category. Then, assuming that $P _ { i }$ is the share of the loans (deposits) in category i to a credit union’s total amount of loans (deposits) across all the N different categories of loan (deposit) products offered by this credit union (N<=7), we computed loan (deposit) diversity as $\begin{array} { r } { \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } P _ { i } \mathrm { l n } ( \frac { 1 } { P _ { i } } ) } \end{array}$ . With this measure, a credit union’s loan (deposit) diversity is determined by both the number of different categories of loan (deposit) products it offers and the degree to which its loan (deposit) balance is evenly or unevenly distributed across the product categories. For example, if a credit union offers loan products only in one product category, then its loan diversity is 1×Ln(1) = 0, the minimum possible value, because there is no diversity in this credit union’s loan product offerings. Then, if a credit union offers two loan products, each accounting for 50% of its total loan balance, then its loan diversity is 0.5×Ln(1/0.5) + 0.5×Ln(1/0.5) = 0.693. It is an increase from the first example because this credit union offers products in more categories. For another example, if another credit union also offers two loan products, one accounting for 90% of its total loan balance while the other 10%, then its loan diversity is 0.9×Ln(1/0.9) + 0.1×Ln(1/0.1) = 0.325. It is a decrease from the second example because, despite that both credit unions offer products in two categories, in the third example the credit union has one dominant product category, and thus less diversity in its product offerings.

Because loan diversity and product diversity serve as moderating variables in our study, we meancentered the two variables for the convenience of result interpretation.

## 5.5 Model Specification

As our dataset consists of cross-sectional, time-series panel data, we used a fixed-effect panel data model as our primary statistical technique. With each of the dependent variables (ROA, ROE; ROA volatility, ROE volatility), we estimated the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \text {Profitability} _ {i, t} (\text {or Volatility} _ {i, t}) & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {Channels} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {2} \text {LoanDiversity} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {3} \text {DepositDiversity} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \beta_ {4} \text {BATOM} \times \text {LoanDiversity} _ {i, t - 1} + \beta_ {5} \text {BATOM} \times \text {DepositDiversity} _ {i, t - 1} \\ & + \text {Controls} _ {i, t - 1} + \alpha_ {i} + \varepsilon_ {i, t} \end{array}
$$

where subscript i denotes a given credit union and t denotes a given quarter; $C h a n n e l s _ { i , t - l }$ is a set of three binary variables indicating different channel configurations (BATO, BATOM, and Others, with B as the omitted category for comparison); $\mathbf { \mathcal { C } o n t r o l } _ { i , t - 1 }$ is the set of control variables as discussed in Section 5.4; ${ \mathfrak { a } } _ { \mathrm { i } }$ is the fixed effect specific to a given credit union i; and $\varepsilon _ { i , t }$ is the residual.

We ran Hausman specification tests on all our estimation models in order to differentiate between fixed- and random-effect panel models. For all the models, the Hausman specification tests rejected the null hypotheses that, if not rejected, would support the use of random-effect models [82]. Thus, we specified all of our panel data models as fixed-effect models.

The differential effects of loan and deposit diversity may reflect the unique business models in the retail banking and credit union industries. Retail banks and credit unions generate revenues mostly from loan products, but yet they have to rely on deposit products to acquire financial resources to sustain their loan businesses. From the perspective of profitability, loans contribute positively, but deposits contribute negatively by incurring interest payments. From the perspective of profit volatility, however, having a stable inflow of capital is critical for retail banks and credit unions’ businesses. When a credit union has a variety of customer service channels, they can better segment loan customers and provide differentiated services by, for example, automating credit card or vehicle loan services online but keeping real estate loan services with high human touch. The diversity of loan products thus enable the credit union to fully realize the profitability potential of its diverse service channels. Customers of deposit products, in contrast, are often sensitive to interest rates and demand less customized services. They tend to add more volatility to the operations of credit unions, especially when they move their deposits frequently to other financial institutions that offer a higher interest rate. One way to gain the loyalty of deposit customers could be to serve them with multiple deposit products through multiple channels, thus increasing their potential switching cost. Thus, we observe from our analyses that the BATOM configuration interacts with deposit diversity to suppress profit volatility further.

We consider that the differential performance effects of loan and deposit diversity enriches our original theoretical arguments. In developing the hypotheses H3 and H4, we argue in general that product diversity can enhance the effects of channel diversity on profitability and profit volatility. The results appear to suggest that the types of products also play an important role in moderating the relationship between channel strategies and firms’ risk-return performance: the diversity of revenue-generating products primarily moderates the relationship between channel strategies and the return dimension of performance while the diversity of resource-acquiring products primarily moderates the risk dimension.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

Using U.S. credit union industry as the empirical context, this paper studies the performance impact of channel diversity and product diversity. The results provide evidence that channel diversity increases profitability and suppresses profit volatility, and both effects are enhanced by product diversity. More specifically, in our empirical context, credit unions who operate a full spectrum of channels including branches, ATMs, telephone banking, online banking, and mobile banking will simultaneously have higher profitability and lower profit volatility. Moreover, loan diversity enhances the profit-enhancing effect and deposit diversity enhances the volatility-suppressing effect. These findings can make the following contributions to the literature.

First, the paper makes an empirical contribution to the literature of multi-channel management by validating the association between channel diversity and profitability at the firm level. Although there is abundant evidence on the value of multi-channels at the customer level [5], firm-level empirical evidence so far remains rare. Due to the absence of firm-level evidence, scholars have been uncertain about whether multi-channels can confer competitive advantage [5]. While our paper does not probe into the underlying mechanisms at resource and activity levels, its results nevertheless provide initial evidence on the strategic value of operating a diverse set of online and offline channels.

Second, by studying the volatility-suppressing effect of multi-channels, the paper adds new insights to the academic inquiries about firms’ risk-return performance in both strategy and information systems disciplines. Strategy scholars have long been interested in the risk-diversifying effects of managing a portfolio of businesses and products [e.g., 19, 53, 60]. Channel diversity as a risk-diversifying tool, however, has not received similar research attention. According to this study, business risks, indicated by a firm’s profit volatility, can also be reduced by operating a wide spectrum of online and offline distribution channels. The findings thus extend the literature of diversification and risk-return performance by evidently suggesting channel diversity as a new tool for a firm to manage its risks. In the field of information systems, scholars of IT business value have been interested in the risk-return implications of IT investment. IT investment itself may increase firms’ performance at the expense of distribution channels, may simultaneously increase return and reduce risk according to our results. Thus, this study adds to a few other studies [11, 28, 31] to reveal the possibility of leveraging IT initiatives as managerial interventions to optimize firms’ overall risk-return performance.

Third, by revealing that the performance impact of multi-channel strategies is enhanced by multiproduct strategies, this paper validates Porter’s speculation [5] that product-based and channel-based strategies should be interconnected. According to our results, firms simultaneously pursuing multiproduct and multi-channel strategies can achieve better risk-return performance than those pursuing the two strategies separately. This newly revealed complementarity could have great implications not only on business research but also business pedagogy. Current business education tends to teach students different strategies separately in different modules or sessions of a class, with an implicit assumption that these strategies are independent to each other. In practice, these strategies are most likely interconnected. Some of them may be complementary to each other and others substituting. By revealing the positive interaction between multi-product and multi-channel strategies, this paper calls for more research attention on the interconnections of different strategies.

Lastly, the paper also contributes to the studies of online and mobile banking. IT has been significantly reshaping the competition in the finance industry [58], and among its many influences, IT has particularly changed the means through which customers receive financial services. We have accumulated evidence on the positive effects of online banking on a bank’s profitability [16, 73], however, we still lack evidence to inform practitioners on how to optimize their product-channel mix. By showing the differential effects of loan and deposit product diversity on profitability and profit volatility, this paper offers new insights for practitioners to consider the risk-return trade-offs when managing multiple products and channels: while channel diversity contributes to both increasing returns and decreasing risks, a firm that prioritizes high returns may focus on innovating in loan products, while a firm that prioritize mitigating risks may focus on innovating in deposit products.

One limitation of this study is that we could not directly observe the actual operation of different channels. While the configuration of channels can also be revealing, future studies that directly investigate the activities within and across channels may generate more theoretical insights. Another limitation of this study is its lack of explicit consideration in cross-channel integration. The existence of multiple channels does not necessarily imply coordination and integration across them. In fact, crosschannel integration has been argued as a more important mechanism t ve firm performance than the -channel integration, however, might be more salient in retailing and other industries but less so in the banking industry. It is because in the banking industry, multi-channel customers naturally expect the visibility to their transactions that happened in all the channels despite which channel they are using in the moment. This expectation renders cross-channel integration as a prerequisite to launch multiple channels in the banking industry, thus assuring a minimum level of integration already in place when multiple channels co-exist. Our hypothesis development arguments are independent on industry contexts, and we believe that these hypotheses can hold in many other industries, but, as discussed above, in other industries, researchers probably need to pay closer attention to cross-channel integration. We speculate that cross-channel integration might be a necessary condition for our proposed relationships to hold, but in many other industries such integration remains a major challenge to accomplish because firms can not easily track a customer’s identity across channels. Nevertheless, future studies can build on our findings to specifically investigate the effects of channel and product strategies on risk-return performance in other industry contexts.
