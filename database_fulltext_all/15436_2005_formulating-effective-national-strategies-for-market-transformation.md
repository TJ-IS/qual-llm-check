## Abstract

National initiatives for the development of information infrastructure have become critical to moving nations into the information age. In this paper, we are concerned with the features of information infrastructure development strategy, the social determinants for a country to choose a strategy and the principles of its design. We investigate China’s telecommunications market transformation over the last two decades. China’s experience is interesting as it is a case par excellence of a large transitory economy. We observe that China’s nationa strategy was based on multiple social elements, not on technological prerogatives alone. This suggests that a national strategy for infrastructure construction should be derived from carefully considering both the technological options available as well as the socia and political environment and the situation in the market structure.

## Introduction

he role of governments in information infrastructure development has recently received increased attention in information systems research (Sawyer et al., 2003). Several studies have attempted to explore the impact of governmental strategies on technology diffusion and examine how social factors influence the choices of strategies (e.g., special issue 20(3) on social determinants for public policy in The Information Society). This paper expands this literature by studying China’s strategies in transforming its telecommunications market. We will, in particular, examine how the deep social transition in Chinese society has shaped the formulation and choice of strategy in a set of telecommunications reforms. In their efforts to reform their respective telecommunications sectors, different countries have adopted alternative strategies, thus leading to confusing results (OECD, 1995; Petrazzini, 1995). The Chinese case helps to illustrate some of the reasons why specific strategies do or do not work.

There are three reasons that China’s telecommunications industry offers an interesting research case to study the problem of national strategy. First, the development and deployment of information infrastructure has become one of the top priorities in managing organizations’ information resources (Brancheau et al., 1996). However, this task does not depend solely on organizations’ own initiatives, but rather is also conditioned by the success of the national strategies for building telecommunications infrastructure that are deployed on a global scale (De Boer and Walbeek, 1999). Second, most current studies on policies have focused on developed countries. There is a paucity of research on developing countries, in particular on transitory economies that are currently influencing the global economy (Hempel and Kwong, 2001). China’s telecommunications reform has taken place in an environment characterized by a deep transition of its economical and political system. From China’s experience, we can gain insight into how the social contexts influence the choice of a national strategy for infrastructure creation. Third, the size, scope and success of China’s telecommunications industry collectively represent a rich and interesting case for soliciting principles of the design of national strategies for effective market development. These principles can address questions such as how to promote the diffusion of e-business or how to speed up the shift towards thirdgeneration wireless services.

Chinese success in telecommunications reform speaks for itself. According to Communications Development Statistic Communique´s, published by China’s telecommunications authority (available at http://www.mii.gov.cn/mii/ hyzw/tjxx.html), China’s telecommunications reform has been a success when measured by typical indicators for infrastructure development. In 1978, the Chinese mainland had 4.12 million telephones and the telephone penetration rate was merely about 0.4% per capita. In contrast, Hong Kong had at the same time 2.8 million subscribers out of 5 million residents. As a result of a series of reforms, by 1998 the number of subscribers had grown to 112 million and the penetration rate had reached 10.6%. At that point China had the second largest telecommunications network in the world. In 2002, China announced that it operated the largest telephone network in the world with 421 million telephone users and a penetration rate of 33.7%. The reforms have also promoted significant advances in technology. China’s present telecommunications network is highly advanced, providing a broad range of services from broadband access to multimedia services. The public has also seen substantial improvement in service quality. In 1998, the average waiting time for telephone installation was about 2 weeks. In 2002, even in the most remote areas, telephone installation could be provided within 1 week.

Current studies on China’s telecommunications industry have basically ignored the issue of national strategy. Besides, new dynamics in the telecommunications market have not been explained (Gao and Lyytinen, 2000; Zhang, 2002). This paper considers China’s telecommunications market transformation in the last two decades. Our goal is to analyze China’s national strategy that resulted in significant telecommunications developments. We explore its gestation as determined by multiple social factors. We do so by examining the strategy within the context of specific political and market factors that shaped its formulation. We show that the design and choice of this strategy was not based on technological opportunism, but rather was derived from a plethora of economic and political contingencies that lead to a specific mix of market transformation measures at different stages of the market evolution.

The reminder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we define the concept of telecommunications transformation strategy and develop an analytical framework. In the third section, we discuss the research design, data collection and analysis. Next, we develop a contextbased, process-oriented account of China’s telecommunications market change. In the subsequent section, we compare the Chinese model with major international experiences. Finally, we draw conclusions and suggest future lines of research.

## Framework

We define a telecommunications market transformation as a movement enacted by a regulatory body such as a state or other institution that seeks to change the market structure, governing principles and competitive environment in which specific telecommunications services are offered and transacted. In principle, this transformation may mean adding regulatory control and monopolizing the service, as was the case in the late part of the 19th century (see, e.g, Bekkers, 2001); it may also imply market making, which denotes the measures by which states decrease such controls by permitting free or more open entry to service markets and by changing the industrial structure of the markets. During the last three decades, the trend has been toward higher levels of market transformation and making through a set of measures as depicted in Table 1.

Overall, we can distinguish between three market transformation approaches that can be observed in both monopoly creation and market opening. These include (1) liberalization, (2) restructuring (which includes devolution and consolidation) and (3) re-regulation. These transformation approaches are rarely used alone; to adapt to a set of different technological and social situations, a government normally adopts a specific mix of these approaches at different stages of a market transformation (Noam and Kramer, 1994). Accordingly, we define a national telecommunications transformation strategy as the dynamic configuration of these three approaches that is appropriate for country for a specific period of time (Table 1).

Liberalization denotes the purposeful introduction of new entrants into a monopolized market in order to promote competition. Devolution denotes an act that splits the current (monopoly) structure into separate units that will compete with one another. Consolidation forms the opposite approach to devolution: it seeks to create larger units in the market that can compete with incumbents (Noam and Kramer, 1994). Thus, both devolution and consolidation identify the incumbents as targets of change, thereby triggering a market restructuring process and creating a category of restructuring. This is different from liberalization, which introduces entirely new elements into the market.

The above approaches are measures of adjusting market structure, but are not adequate measures of market change. In most cases, simultaneous support of regulatory reforms is needed to define how markets will be allowed to operate. Deregulation is a primary component of regulatory reform that connotes a general tendency to reduce a government’s intervention in licensing, pricing schemes, etc. (Collins and Murroni, 1997). Yet, because competitors can wield unequal power in the market and there can be other powerful institutional mechanisms to protect incumbents, such as standards, economies of scale and social networks, regulation is very critical to protecting the market from monopolies in most cases during market transformation. Moreover, organizational arrangements for regulation will need to change in response to new variations in markets. In keeping with these changes, governments may need to engage in re-regulation. Re-regulation refers to changes in the content and patterns of regulation using methods like deregulation and asymmetric regulation, and organizational reforms to set up new forms of regulatory bodies (Lucio et al., 2001).

## Telecommunications market transformation strategy

Throughout most of the 20th century, nearly all telecommunications markets were organized as monopolies. In each country, Posts, Telegraph and Telephone (PTT) acted as the state network operator and monopolized the telecommunications market (Director, 1992). During the 1970 s, this regime began to crumble as multiple national initiatives were launched to promote competition. These initiatives embedded a mix of approaches pertaining to the telecommunications market discussed above.

Traditional telecommunications markets were a natural monopoly. The justifying rationale was that economies of scale made it more efficient for one firm to provide telecommunications services; in addition, these monopolistic practices were justified by military and industrial reasons. As technology advanced throughout the 1970s and 1980s, this concept was increasingly challenged. On the one hand, the improved telecommunications network enabled the development of new kinds of services and created new markets, while the monopoly’s operating logics turned out to be inefficient in responding to these new opportunities and creating new wealth. On the other hand, as switching and transmission costs fell, economies of scale grew smaller and a nation’s ability to minimize the cost of telecommunications became a precursor to remaining competitive (Smith and Staple, 1994; Gong and Srinagesh, 1996). As a result, the basis and legitimacy of the natural monopoly concept collapsed in economic and business terms.

In each country, the specific shape of telecommunications reform has been different; each government has used a specific mix of approaches over time to transform the markets. The experiences of the US and UK provide typical examples. In the US, AT&T held the de facto monopoly on services and infrastructure since the 1930s, when national security and universal service commitments became national priorities. The first step in the transformation of the US telecommunications market occurred when the courts decided against AT&T in liberalizing long distance services in 1978 by allowing MCI’s dedicated network to offer long-distance services. Soon, other big names like Sprint and Qwest followed suit. Then, in the early 80 s, restructuring began. To promote fair competition, the federal government devolved AT&T in 1984, with the Modification of Final Judgment that mandated AT&T to divest itself of its local service sector. The devolution was subsequently carried out through the formation of seven regional holding companies now known as the Baby Bells (Snow, 1995; Baliga and Santalainen, 1999).

The opening of the UK market began with re-regulation: a new Telecommunications Act abolished the exclusive rights of British Telecom in public service in 1984. The government next used liberalization measures to further open the market by allowing a private operator, Mercury, to provide long-distance services. Finally, in 1991, the UK was the first country in the world to enact a full liberalization through a full re-regulation (Thatcher, 1999).

Determinants of national strategy choice: an analytical framework As noted above, rapid technological change has largely undermined the legitimacy of national monopolies over telecommunications networks. However, in addressing this challenge, governments must balance the costs of transition to a national economy and a society, the incapability of the incumbent monopoly to respond to new market demands and the potential short-term loss of economies of scale due to increased competition. This balancing act represents the challenge of formulating the national strategy for telecommunications market transformation (Smith and Staple, 1994).

In the literature, a broad range of social factors influence governments when they choose reform strategies. These factors include a nation’s economic environment, their general national policies concerning competition and the status of their telecommunications infrastructure and industry. For example, in China, the national desire to join the World Trade Organization (WTO) pushed the government to set timetables for liberalizing the telecommunications market to foreigners (Mueller and Lovelock, 2000). In the UK, the telecommunications reform was originally part of the government’s policy to improve state-owned industries through privatization (Spiller and Vogelsang, 1996). In some developing countries, where the national economy is normally weak and lacks capital, liberalization to foreign investment has been preferred as it may help to obtain a stronger financial position for telecommunications development. For example, Argentina, Jamaica and Mexico followed this strategy in the early 1990s; in the mid-1990s, Malaysia and the Philippines followed suit (Petrazzini, 1995). In the EU, most countries have cautiously liberalized their markets as they are wary of large foreign operators entering their markets before domestic operators are ready. They have normally been more satisfied with the high universal service levels within the countries, although some, such as Sweden and Finland, have very effective and low cost services and advanced infrastructures that have been created through monopolistic practices (Collins and Murroni, 1997).

## Research goals, setting and methods

Our goal in studying China’s telecommunications reform was to determine why certain outcomes at the level of policy emerged as a function of strategy choices and its environment. This is clearly a complex, emergent phenomenon. Hence, we adopted a contextual, process-oriented research methodology (Holmes and Poole, 1991; Orlikowski, 1993). Using the strategy concepts defined in Table 1 as the unifying theoretical lens, we followed the model depicted in Figure 1 to chronicle a process account of the dynamics of social contexts and the changes that occurred in the telecommunications market in China.

Our main research question was to find connections between specific policy-making contexts and chosen strategies. Accordingly, in data collection and interpretation we sought to discover reports and accounts from key stakeholders concerning the content and motivations behind telecommunications reforms at different stages. To improve the data reliability, we used multiple data sources to triangulate the evidence (Klein and Myers, 1999).

We collected data through extensive field work that covered most areas of China’s telecommunications industry. The first author worked as a consultant for the Ministry of Posts & Telecommunications (MPT) of China for 5 years, until the end of 1997. He personally experienced the early phases of China’s telecommunications transformation and participated in several projects concerning the formulation of the national strategy. His personal experience helped us understand the history of China’s telecommunications transformation in terms of key events and its evolution in specific social environments. Since 1998, when the first author left China and entered into an academic career, the authors have together explored China’s telecommunications industry. For the purpose of data collection, we established close connections with key actors in the Chinese telecommunications market. These connections enabled us to obtain data in multiple ways. As a second method of data collection, the first author tracked the dynamics of the Chinese telecommunications market by regular email and telephone communications with his partners working in China’s telecommunications industry. Third, during 1998– 2001, we conducted a number of substantive interviews among leaders and leading experts in major manufacturers, research institutions, regulatory authorities and telecommunications operators in China. The purpose of these interviews was to find first-hand reports of what the Chinese telecommunications market dynamics had been and why specific market transformation strategies had been chosen and what their impacts had been. As the topics were familiar to both the interviewer and the interviewees, we carried out most interviews in an unstructured manner. To encourage free dialogue, interviews were conducted many times in informal occasions, sometimes over dinners in which we kept notes. We do not have transcripts of all of these discussions. In this way, we could also more easily obtain confidential information.

To identify critical events that changed the direction of the telecommunications reform we used archival news reports in official newspapers, speeches of the ministers and national leaders and consulting reports prepared by the ministries’ research centers. We also used other secondhand literature as the data source for international telecommunications reform.

Data were mostly analyzed by finding facts about specific policy-making outcomes and then exploring interviews and other data to find reasons for these changes. These helped us to understand the main contours of the changes and the specific reasons at the level of national policy in order to promote those changes. In conducting this analysis, we engaged in two types of dialogical reasoning while interpreting the collected data: during the discussion between the authors, we triangulated the data from one perspective that had a deep background in China’s telecommunications industry and from another perspective, analyzed it from the view point of an outsider generally entrenched in regulation and national policies. Another dialogical perspective allowed us to engage in discussion with the practitioners who acted in the Chinese context (Monterio and Hepso, 2002).

## The Chinese telecommunications reform

Telecommunications form an important component in modern society and its infrastructure. Hence, its reform cannot be carried out independently of other macro policies. China is no exception here. Between 1949 and 1978, China adhered strictly to a socialist regime in which the state controlled and ran an economy that excluded competition. With limited public-owned elements to supplement the economy, China’s economy was controlled by state-owned enterprises. Private and foreign capital was restricted. At the same time, the socialist regime did not recognize the rule of law in regulating the markets. Instead, resources were allocated and the market was regulated using administrative measures (Ma and Ling, 1998).

In 1978, in its third meeting of the 11th session, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China (CCCPC) announced China entered an era called the ‘system reform and market opening.’ The traditional socialist regime began to undergo a series of reforms toward a ‘socialist market economy.’ The spirit of the reform has been to adopt increasingly market-based mechanisms while maintaining the political regime of socialism. The main reform target has been the role of the state as a significant economic agent. This has significantly changed over the last 20 years. To avoid major political and economic risks, China has employed an approach that could be called ‘act after trials,’ in that major policy measures are used only when they have been tested on a smaller scale. Thus, instead of initializing an overall reform by issuing new laws and completely adjusting its administrative regime, the Chinese government has introduced competition to the market gradually, first to less-important sectors and later to infrastructures. The small-sized state-owned firms were first made to compete, while the tight control of the state on large-sized companies was gradually decreased (Ma and Ling, 1998).

Within this background, the Chinese telecommunications sector began to undergo transformations from the early 1980s. In terms of critical events, the telecommunications reform can be divided into four main stages. We have observed the pace and direction of change in each phase was driven by the general progress in the economic and political systems and the dynamics of key national policies. In addition, the status of China’s telecommunications infrastructure was an important consideration in deciding the next steps of their strategy. The dynamics of these social factors have determined how China has transformed its telecommunications market by applying a varying mix of strategy approaches (Figure 2).

## Road to 1994: liberalization on equipment and VAS

In the late 1970s, most telecommunications networks were still operated as a monopoly (Noam and Kramer, 1994; Noll, 2000). In China, MPT acted as the incumbent monopoly on public telecommunications. As a department of MPT, the Directorate General of Telecommunications (DGT) was responsible for network operations. Reforms to the traditional socialist economic system had just begun. Ideologically, competition was not regarded as a symbol of capitalism but as a means of improving the national economy. Yet, in the beginning it was adopted only in less important sectors of the national economy, such as the light manufacturing industry. The telecommunications network was viewed as part of the national infrastructure and a sovereign body and thus it had to be maintained under the absolute control of the state (Ma and Ling, 1998).

Moreover, the state-owned telecommunications network was too weak to withstand competition. The telecommunications sector was a bottleneck in the effort to build a strong national economy (MII, 1999). To quickly improve the telecommunications network’s poor infrastructure, the government granted preferential policies to its telecommunications sector. For example, the State Council approved MPT to exact an installation fee from every subscriber that equaled the construction cost of one line. In addition, MPT had tax privileges (MPT, 1992). Until 1994, more than half of the entire investment capital of MPT was derived from these preferential policies (MII, 1999).

New value-added services (VAS) emerged during the middle 1980s (e.g., paging services). These services had no significant dependencies on public service telecommunications networks, which implied that competition in VAS could be easily adopted without affecting current markets and basic services in telecommunications networks (Gong and Srinagesh, 1996). Moreover, MPT alone could not meet the new market demand for VAS. In viewing it not as a part of national information infrastructure, the Chinese government in the late 1980s liberalized the VAS market to all domestic non-private institutions (MPT, 1996). As a result, it emerged as a highly competitive market with thousands of operators, most of which were small in size and operated in local areas (MII, 1999).

Composed of multiple small-sized firms owned by the state, China’s telecommunications manufacturing sector was outdated. At the same time, MPT lacked the foreign currency required to import equipment from abroad. To ensure the growth of telecommunications networks through advanced technologies, in 1992 China fully liberalized the equipment sector to domestic and foreign competition. From the macroperspective, this was possible as the state had decided to allow competition in the manufacturing industry and lifted its control on small-sized companies (Ma and Ling, 1998). China preferred to introduce domestic production lines through joint ventures or independent foreign investments. Foreign manufacturers were also actively investing in advanced technologies in China due to the significant growth potential of the Chinese market. Consequently, compared with foreign equipment, the domestic-made switching and terminal products were on par with technology quality. The prices were also much lower, based on low manufacturing costs. As a result, the procurement of domestic equipment has dominated major parts of China’s network market (Chismar et al., 1996; MII, 1999).

From 1994 to 1997: liberalization and re-regulation on PTT regime Owing to preferential policies, by 1994, China’s telecommunications growth level was equivalent to the general growth level of the national economy. The telecommunications market shifted from a buyer’s pull to a seller’s push (Yang, 1997). Unlike during earlier times when the overriding objective had been the development of a telecommunications infrastructure, service quality and price reductions became the new focus. These could be best achieved through enforcing competitive mechanisms, as demonstrated by the international experience (OECD, 1995). At the same time, the preferential policies of the state brought significant profits for the telecommunications monopoly. As the telecommunications sector’s bottleneck position in the national economy had been removed, there was no reason to retain such preferential policies. Hence, beginning in the middle 1990 s, the government began to purge preferential policies (MPT, 1996).

In 1992, Deng Xiaoping had his historical inspection tour to Southern China, which was the ‘pilot’ area of economic reform. He made a series of speeches during this tour and called for ‘further opening of the market and enhancing reform.’ He set the stage for reform by defining socialism as the collective wealth of the people as opposed to the public ownership and state’s control on economy. He also affirmed the principle of ‘act after trials,’ and urged the government to ‘deepen reform on the national economy after collecting experience through trials.’ As a result, competition began to be introduced to the infrastructure, the previously ‘restricted area’ of market access (Ma and Ling, 1998).

Accordingly, in 1994, China reformed the system of economic administration so that its organization and governance better suited the demands of competition. Part of this macro change involved defining the re-regulative measures through which the PTT system was restructured. Registered as China Telecom, DGT as the operating sector of MPT was changed from a ‘functional’ department of MPT to a separate Public Telecommunications Operator (PTO, the PTT after its regulatory functions had been split up). At the same time, the market was liberalized. The State Council allowed the Ministry of Electronic Industry (MEI), along with other state institutions, to form Unicom, which competed with China Telecom in all telecommunications services and Jitong, which was dedicated to competition in Internet services. Some ministries jointly set up Sino Satellite Communications and China Orient to compete with ChinaSat, which had a monopoly over the satellite communications (MII, 1999). Yet, all these operators were owned by the state.

## From 1998 to 2000: liberalization, re-regulation and PTO restructuring

The market changes in the second round of reforms were mostly cosmetic. The two comprehensive operators, China Telecom and Unicom, demonstrated great disparities in market strength and capital assets. Unicom’s income was less than 1% of China Telecom’s (MII, 1999). This was an unbalanced market situation in view of fair competition, especially when China at the same time lacked the rule of law to regulate the market. More importantly, China Telecom was directly under MPT’s control without independent oversight of finance, investment and personnel. Hence, on the one hand, MPT could not fairly regulate the market as it might introduce a bias toward China Telecom due to its close affiliation with MPT. On the other hand, MPT lacked enough power to intervene in the domains of other ministries, such as MEI, which now participated in the telecommunications market (Zhang, 2002). The situation was the same for the whole national reform. By the principle of ‘act after trial,’ as the first attempt of macro reform it was not intended to fully change the current system, but to conduct a ‘pilot’ test for future moves as the ministries retained a tight control over key enterprises (Ma and Ling, 1998).

Profound changes in the telecommunications market started from 1998. These changes raised the attention of public media. According to official news reports of People’s P&T published by the telecommunications authority and People’s Daily by CCCPC, we can recall the process of the third stage of telecommunications market transformation as follows.

In 1998 a new round of governmental reform was launched. This reform resulted in deeper changes in the governmental system. As a part of this effort, the Ministry of Information Industry (MII) was established based on the regulatory function of both MPT and MEI. MII became a sole coherent regulator mandated with exclusive power in administrating the whole information industry.

Consequently, the telecommunications regulative system was significantly improved. Promoted by MII, the unbalanced telecommunications market was transformed by devolution and consolidation. The aim was to strengthen Unicom and enforce fairer competition. During this stage, the government applied both the consolidation and devolution approaches. The paging sector of China Telecom (whose turnover was five times that of Unicom) and the mobile network of Great Wall (an army-run wireless operator!) were appropriated to Unicom.

In autumn 1999, CCCPC as the supreme decision-making organ decided to modify its policy concerning large-sized, state-owned enterprises. While their dominant position in the national economy was maintained, some measures such as absorbing private and foreign capital were encouraged.

Accordingly, China set a goal of joining the WTO in the few coming years. The telecommunications market formed one of the key points in negotiations between China and WTO members. China needed to convince the organization that it welcomed competition and the market was ready for it (Mueller and Lovelock, 2000). Meanwhile, in the late 1990 s, China’s telecommunications development entered a new phase as it now had advanced, cross-country networks that provided a solid basis for intensifying market based competition (MII, 1999). Against this background, in April 2000, a devolution approach was deployed to separate the mobile sector of China Telecom into an independent body called China Mobile. Owing to liberalization, China Netcom was formed in August 1999 by some state institutions. Moreover, the state granted the telecommunications networks of national railways to enter into the public market and China Railcom was established in December 2000. Thus, the market became more competitive as the dominant position of China Telecom was decreased and more operators were included in the market.

## After 2000: foreign liberalization and market restructuring

The fourth stage of reform commenced when China was officially accepted as a member of the WTO at the beginning of 2001. According to the ideological requirement that the state dominates the socialist economy (Ma and Ling, 1998), China agreed to permit 49% foreign ownership in mobile communications within 5 years, 49% in international and domestic services within 6 years, and 50% in VAS within 2 years (Zhang, 2002).

After three rounds of reforms, fair competition in telecommunications markets was not yet a reality. Both Unicom and China Netcom were too weak to effectively compete with China Telecom. As foreign companies planned to enter China within a few years, it was necessary to restructure the market in preparation for their entrance. Therefore, in May 2002, China Telecom was restructured into two parts by areas. The northern part was built from 11 northern provincial networks, China Netcom and Jitong, and it adopted the name of China Netcom. Keeping the name of China Telecom, the southern part retained networks of other 19 provinces. Moreover, as early as December 2001, ChinaSat merged with Sino Satellite Communications and China Orient (according to official news reports of People’s Daily).

## Discussions: China in the international context

In this section, we will discuss China’s telecommunications reform strategy in the context of the international experience. Specifically, we want to demonstrate that particular social contexts of China’s telecommunications market have determined that its transformation strategy is distinctive compared with those of other countries.

Believing that competition in the manufacturing industry would not challenge socialism, China began its economic reform from this sector. The telecommunications equipment market formed part of this industry. Besides, China’s backward manufacturing industry could not meet the huge demands of fast network extension. Hence, China liberalized the equipment market more quickly and intensively than had many developed countries. Owing to significant national manufacturing capabilities, many countries did not liberalize their markets easily. For example, it was only in the late 1980s when most European Union members opened terminal equipment sales to countries that had agreements with them of equal market access (European Commission, 1988; Collins and Murroni, 1997).

In most countries, the telecommunications market change has been enacted by promulgating laws. For example, in the UK, the 1984 and 1991 Telecommunications Acts have provided the basis for its market transformation (Thatcher, 1999). In the US, the 1996 Telecommunications Act has led to a full liberalization (Baliga and Santalainen, 1999). In contrast, in China the telecommunications reform has been carried out by simple governmental orders without the supervision of laws. This is the result of adhering to the national policy of reforming the economic system through an ‘act after trial’ process.

Generally, re-regulation should be carried out simultaneously with market changes, if not prior to them (Director, 1992; Melody, 1999). Concerning regulatory institutions, a reform toward forming an independent, exclusive regulatory body is necessary when a market is liberalized. For example, in the UK, the regulatory power was transferred to the Department of Industry when the market reform began in 1981; later, the independent regulatory body Office of Telecommunications (Oftel) was formed in 1994 as market liberalization went deeper (Spiller and Vogelsang, 1996). In India, the Telecom Regulatory Authority (TRA) was formed as the regulatory agency that organized the market change (Petrazzini and Krishnaswamy, 1998). But in China, the change in regulatory institutions has been carried out by following the national reforms of the governmental system. A neutral regulatory body was brought about in 1998, 4 years after the PTT system had been changed (MII, 1999).

In the international sphere, telecommunications competition has largely been introduced through liberalization and by introducing new competitors. The devolution approach has been used rarely, except for the AT&T case in the US (Noam and Kramer, 1994; Snow, 1995). In China, devolution was adopted as a major measure and applied to the PTO, which was split first by services and function and later by areas. This was carried out because, without necessary laws such as competition and anti-trust laws, the existence of a strong incumbent might influence the institutional environment and jeopardize regulation enforcement and fair competition (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983). Meanwhile, China’s state competition facilitated its government to adopt a strong restructuring measure on its telecommunications sector (MII, 1999).

Although China has increasingly introduced competition to the national economy, it has not given up the principles of socialism while pursing economic reform. The ‘socialist market economy’ favors that the state retains control over the telecommunications infrastructure (Ma and Ling, 1998). Hence, the telecommunications competition has been created between state-owned operators and limitations on foreign shares have been set up to enable state-owned Chinese companies to assume a dominant position in the future market place. In this sense, China’s strategy has been different from that of many other countries. In other developing countries, the basic aim of opening the telecommunications market was to attract private – especially foreign – investments (Petrazzini, 1995; Noll,

2000). In developed economies such as EU countries (see e.g. Noam and Kramer, 1994; OECD, 1995), the market liberalization was achieved through introducing private and foreign competitors.

## Conclusion and future work

In literature, a practical definition and systematic discussion on national strategy for telecommunications market transformation is largely missing. In this paper, we clarified this concept being a sequence of liberalization, restructuring and re-regulation. This definition allowed us to comprehensively explore the social determinants of China’s strategy for telecommunications transformation, and could be drawn by future researches. We identified two sets of social factors that exercised significant influence on the choice of the reform strategy. First, the status of the telecommunications market (size and penetration) and its infrastructure (quality and scope) were important consideration when the government presided over the market change. Countries with advanced infrastructure and liberal national policies, such as the US and UK, successfully pioneered the market transformation. Yet, due to its weak starting position, it was imperative that China first support PTT to construct a strong national infrastructure and thereafter gradually introduce competition when the market and infrastructure had developed sufficiently. Second, the telecommunications transformation formed part of a macrotransition toward a ‘socialist market economy’ through learning by trials. Therefore, progress in the telecommunications sector has been significantly influenced by national policies and priorities such as joining the WTO, reforming the governmental system and adapting to changing dominant ideologies that define the official modus operandi of the Chinese socialism.

Our study enriches knowledge on the formulating mechanism of national strategy and fills a gap in understanding the effects of national strategies for changing the telecommunications market in transitory economies. The findings have several practical implications. For policymakers, our analysis suggests that the transformation of a specific industry should be viewed as part of a macrotransition. This implies that developing countries should prefer a strategy of stepwise change. Like China, usually these countries have a poor economic basis and inefficient public administration. These systems need to be changed first so that a specific telecommunications market can emerge (Petrazzini, 1995). This result can be generalized beyond a transitory economy. We suggest that the formulation of national policies concerning technology diffusion and market development, for example strategies for the mobile network transformation from the second generation to the thirdgeneration, should be based not only on technology imperatives and opportunities, but also take into account a nation’s specific social context including economic status, the political environment and the status of market development efforts. This important lesson was forgotten by many European governments when they auctioned the third-generation licenses in 2000 for prices that have stalled service uptake and derailed telecommunications industries into deep difficulties while the Far East has taken the lead in this sector.

For researchers who study national strategies for technological innovation, our research results imply that they should apply a technology strategy formulation within a specific social context. Thus they may be able to explain, for example, why some countries have adopted a ‘beauty contest’ method while others have used a bidding system in issuing licenses to operate the third-generation mobile telecommunications systems.

There are limitations to this study. The paper directly connects social context to strategy. It only takes into account how the strategy developer, that is, the central government, designs the policy according to contextual factors. But the design of a national strategy is a complex, distributed process involving the interests of incumbent operators, potential competitors, the public and society, and the state. We should disclose the social interactions of these market stakeholders that have influenced the decision process (Orlikowski, 1993). For example, we need to reveal their struggle through negotiations and trials of power to have their interests represented in the national strategy for telecommunications transformation. Such in-depth research would help identify multiple connections between the context and the strategy.
