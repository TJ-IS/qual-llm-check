# MULTIFARIOUS ROLES AND CONFLICTS ON AN INTERORGANIZATIONAL GREEN IS<sup>1</sup>

Dorothy E. Leidner Hankamer School of Business, Baylor University, 1632 S. 3<sup>rd</sup> St., Waco, TX 76706 U.S.A. {Dorothy\_Leidner@baylor.edu}

Juliana Sutanto Management Science Department, Lancaster University Management School, Lancaster University, Bailrigg, Lancaster LA1 4YX UNITED KINGDOM {j.sutanto@lancaster.ac.uk}

Lazaros Goutas Center for Digital Business Transformation, IMD Business School, Chem. de Bellerive 23, 1003 Lausanne SWITZERLAND {lazaros.goutas@imd.org}

Under increasing pressure to demonstrate environmental responsibility, organizations realize that they cannot claim to be environmentally sustainable if their supply chains are not. This research seeks to understand how an interorganizational green IS influences environmental sustainability (ES) initiatives within organizations in a supply chain. We examine a green IS taking the form of an interorganizational ES platform. Our analysis sheds insights into how role conflicts arising from the various roles played by the platform users compromise the nature of actions associated with platform beliefs. In particular, cooptition conflict arising from participants’ roles as supplier to the platform owner and competitor to other platform participants explains the symbolic organizational content contribution, whereas the professional conflict resulting from participants roles as employee of an organization and knowledge peer to participants from other organizations explains the substantive personal content contribution. The lack of organizational substantive content creates content paucity, which platform users respond to by developing off-platform relationships with content contributors. The personal ES knowledge acquired through platform content consumption and the relationships with content contributors help individuals advocate for ES initiatives within their organizations. Our research is among the first to consider green IS at an interorganizational level and the corresponding multilevel perspective of the green IS users as they are at once organizational and individual actors.

Keywords: Green IS, interorganizational green IS, role theory, role conflict, belief–action–outcome framework, environmental sustainability

## Introduction

Organizations are under increasing pressure from governments, industry associations, nongovernment groups, and ethically conscious consumers to demonstrate environmental responsibility. Green IS—information systems (IS) that play an informational, supporting role in environmental sustainability (ES) initiatives (Corbett 2010; Loeser et al. 2017)— can support organizational ES initiatives by providing information to support green decision making (Corbett 2010), producing reports on environmental performance (Bengtsson and Ågerfalk 2011), tracking environmental compliance (Butler 2011), or analyzing work practices against the relevant environmental performance indicators to create awareness that induces action (Seidel et al. 2013). Research on green IS has made great strides in conceptualizing green IS (see, for example, Elliot 2011; Melville 2010) and in understanding the drivers of (Hanelt et al. 2017; Hu et al. 2016; Loeser et al. 2017) and performance results from various forms of green IS used by organizations (Benitez-Amado and Walczuch 2013; Nishant et al. 2017).

It has recently become clear that organizations cannot claim to be environmentally sustainable simply by adopting ES initiatives at the intra-organizational level. Supply chains are integral to most commercial activities, and often involve global interactions with unknown and potentially adverse impacts (Reefke and Sundaram 2018). The scrutiny that multinational clothing companies received for working with suppliers who were dumping toxins into rivers is an example in this regard (Villena and Gioia 2020). To avoid the reputational and financial damage associated with working with suppliers that follow unsustainable practices, organizations need to examine the adoption of ES initiatives across their supply chain (O’Rourke and Strand 2017; Villena and Gioia 2020). Toward this end, an interorganizational green IS may be employed to encourage organizations in a supply chain to undertake ES initiatives.

An interorganizational IS is a system deployed to exchange information between firms, and is known to play a key role in enabling knowledge sharing between firms across the supply chain (Dong et al. 2017). An interorganizational green IS is different from a traditional interorganizational IS in several ways. First, in terms of participating organizations, whereas a traditional interorganizational IS involves bringing together organizations with existing client–supplier relationships, an interorganizational green IS may also bring together direct competitors and/or independent suppliers who are not partners or clients of one another. Second, in terms of the benefits to participating organizations, in a traditional interorganizational IS, the benefits such as just-in-time production and reduced inventory are directly observable. In an interorganizational green IS, the benefits may be less direct, such as building a reputation as a green organization. Finally, in terms of the purpose of the IS, whereas traditional interorganizational IS are focused on sharing operational information to promote mutually beneficial efficiencies in the supply chain (Dong et al. 2017), an interorganizational green IS is focused on sharing information about ES initiatives in order to encourage ES initiatives within participating organizations that do not notably benefit other organizations. These differences create a unique set of motivational and contextual factors that may have important repercussions for green IS use and effectiveness.

Previous green IS research lays an excellent foundation for the study of interorganizational green IS (for reviews, see

Gholami et al. 2016; Malhotra et al. 2013), yet important gaps in our understanding remain. First, the literature to date is primarily single level and intra-organizational (Cooper and Molla 2017; Hanelt et al. 2017; Loeser et al. 2017; Seidel et al. 2013). At the individual level, the research has examined issues such as the antecedents of green IS adoption and use by individuals (Loeser et al. 2017; Wunderlich et al. 2019) and the influence of environmental awareness and beliefs on individuals’ behaviors (Corbett 2013). At the organizational level, the research has focused on green IS strategy (Bansal and Roth 2000), the acquisition of sustainability knowledge from stakeholders, customers, and/or suppliers (Cooper and Molla 2017), and the importance of environmental awareness (Hu et al. 2016) and individual champions (Hedman and Henningsson 2016). This leaves a gap in our understanding of interorganizational green IS. This gap is important because of the contextual differences that distinguish interorganizational green IS from the typical intra-organizational green IS that have been studied so far. The users of an interorganizational system are simultaneously embedded in the social and organizational environments within which they are employed (Zhao et al. 2018). Therefore, a multilevel analysis is needed to explain the dynamics of the interorganizational green IS users as they are at once organizational actors (acting on behalf of their organization) and individual actors (acting as individuals interested in environmental sustainability). The dual roles and potential role conflict that may arise combined with the multitude of ways that a green IS can be used create an interesting dynamic. The organizationally oriented uses of an interorganizational green IS may become more personal when the green IS users are acting as individual actors. Understanding the interplay of this dynamic toward organizational ES initiatives makes this study unique and important.

Second, the organizational green IS literature has largely considered cases of organizations that already had particular environmental transformation objectives or specific ES initiatives. In these instances, the green IS supports the environmental transformation effort or supports the specific ES initiative by offering sensemaking and sustaining practicing affordances that alloworganizations to seek information about their current work practices and reconsider such practices based on ES considerations (Seidel et al. 2013). However, the literature is relatively quiet on how organizations that do not yet have sustainability initiatives in place develop awareness about potential initiatives to undertake. Research is needed that explores the case of organizations in a supply chain that have not necessarily embarked on ES transformation and the spread of ES knowledge across organizations in a supply chain.

Third, previous green IS research has noted the importance of industry alliances as a vehicle for ES innovation and has advocated for alliances to share the burden of ES awareness building through the sharing of information and knowledge (Elliot 2011). Organizations often learn from and mimic other organizations within an industry (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Yet to mimic, one must first be apprised. Behind the scenes, initiatives such as water saving technologies are not readily imitated because they are not readily visible. There is a need for research that considers how an interorganizational green IS may provide a platform in which to raise awareness within an industry of potential ES initiatives.

These gaps in our understanding of interorganizational green IS and the corresponding multilevel perspectives of the green IS users as they are at once organizational and individual actors lead us to pose the following research question: How does the use of an interorganizational green IS in a supply chain contribute to organizational ES initiatives within organizations in the supply chain? To address this question, we examined the use of an interorganizational green IS in a food supply chain, which was developed with the purpose of supporting members of the food supply chain in their ES transformation journey. We adopted a grounded theory approach in order to unfold the ways in which supply chain members used the green IS. Grounded theory is particularly appropriate for investigating new organizational phenomena and studying sociotechnical behavior in an emerging research domain (Wiesche et al. 2017), which well describes our domain of interorganizational green IS. Our aim is to develop theory (descriptions of variables, their relationships, justifications of the relationships, and boundary conditions) from empirical data, which is consistent with the espoused goal and highest impact form of grounded theory (Wiesche et al. 2017).

The paper is organized as follows. We next present our research method where we explain the analytical lens, which emerged through the various iterations of our analysis. Then, we present the case description, findings, and propositions. Finally, we present the study’s contributions and implications for research and practice, limitations, and conclusions.

## Research Methodology

Subscribing to the grounded theory method, our case adheres to the principles of emergence by which systematic generation and conceptualization of data provides meaning and relevance to the phenomenon under consideration (Corbin and Strauss 2015). We examined an interorganizational green IS platform that was built on the premise of facilitating exchange about ES best practices among the suppliers of a client in the food industry.

## The Interorganizational Green IS Platform

The Preserve and Protect the Environment (PPE) platform (a pseudonym) is owned by one of Europe’s largest supermarket chains, referred to in this paper by the pseudonym SUPER-MART. As part of a comprehensive sustainability initiative, SUPERMART envisions sustainable sourcing. Toward this end, SUPERMART targets its suppliers since, in the words of the Head of Corporate Sustainability at SUPERMART, “90% of our environmental impact is through our products and supply chain rather than in our operations.” SUPERMART teamed with 6SENSES (a pseudonym) to develop the PPE, an interorganizational green IS platform designed for the purpose of sharing environmentally sustainable business practices among its suppliers. As a supporting third party, 6SENSES hosts the platform and facilitates knowledge exchange. SUPERMART’s suppliers form the membership of the PPE. SUPERMART funds the PPE but does not actively participate in it. However, SUPERMART does receive reports from 6SENSES on the engagement levels of its suppliers in the PPE: how often each supplier posts information to the community. Members can upload case studies and post questions or answers. Suppliers are free to assign the employee(s) of their choice to be member(s) of the PPE. The real identities of the participants and the organizations they represent are disclosed in the platform.

## Data Collection and Data Analysis

The main method of data collection was semi-structured interviews. We also acquired information through archival documents, four conference calls, and two email conversations during the inception of the PPE platform. All interviews were recorded and then transcribed. We interviewed representatives from the three main parties involved in the PPE: 6SENSES, SUPERMART, and a number of suppliers participating in the PPE. Several interviewees were interviewed on more than one occasion in order to clarify their responses and ask follow-up questions. In total, we conducted 31 semistructured interviews, including 23 with representatives from 19 different supplier organizations, 7 with 3 representatives of 6SENSES, and 1 with a representative from SUPERMART (Appendix A). One of the 6SENSES representatives apprised us of the engagement levels of the suppliers in our sample, allowing us to conduct more detailed and directed probing during the interviews (Bansal and Roth 2000), to further contextualize the data and increase the reliability of our findings.

The initial interview questions included open-ended questions intended to provide insights into the specific ways in which the PPE affected organizational ES initiatives. In the first interview round, we purposefully chose the three interviewees because of their important role in the program: they were PPE members from the very beginning, they were active contributors and acquirers of information, and they were on the platform’s Advisory Board (a committee of stakeholders to help guide the strategic direction of the program). We also interviewed the 6SENSES Community Manager, Account Manager, and Technical Product Director to gain complementary perspectives.

In the second interview round, the platform engagement statistics and resource efficiency performance ranking served as a basis for the interviewees’ selection. The platform activity of the individual representatives of seven suppliers that we interviewed ranged from “weak” to “leading star” according to 6SENSES statistics, and the resource efficiency performance ranking ranged from top to bottom based on 6SENSES’ benchmarking service. The interview questions became more focused. As in the first round, we conducted a complementary interview with stakeholders from 6SENSES. Overall, the data from the second interview round enabled us to identify additional beliefs about the PPE platform that affected actions on the PPE and eventually led to organizational ES initiatives.

In the third interview round, we interviewed 13 supplier representatives. Four supplier representatives from the first two interview rounds and nine new supplier representatives with varying levels of platform engagement were interviewed. Stakeholders from 6SENSES and SUPERMART were also interviewed again to gain complementary views.

Data analysis was conducted by the first two authors. Our coding process adhered to the guidelines prescribed by Birks et al. (2013), Corbin and Strauss (2015), and Urquhart et al. (2010) in developing grounded theory. We began by constantly comparing instances of data to derive initial codes. We noted every platform-related behavior mentioned by the informants and then abstracted the behaviors into two types: symbolic and substantive. The two authors agreed that symbolic behavior only made sense in the context of platform actions that were visible to others. Content contribution is highly visible not only to other suppliers but also to SUPER-MART through the engagement reports provided by 6SENSES. We labeled content contribution that was described by the individuals themselves as aimed toward influencing the opinion of others as symbolic. We labeled as substantive content contribution the content that the individuals described as being done with the aim of helping others. By contrast, content consumption is neither visible to others nor does it count in the engagement metric; as such, it has no symbolic value and is, therefore, considered a substantive platform behavior.

Next, through several iterations of coding, the codes were interrelated to each other. It was through the iterations that we began to see platform beliefs emerging as an important issue. This motivated us to seek theory that helped explain beliefs in the context of green IS, leading us to Melville’s (2010) belief–action–outcome (BAO) theory. Additional iterations led us to understand that the link from beliefs to action was not thoroughly explained by the BAO theory. We were then drawn to role theory to help explain the link between beliefs and actions. This iterative coding, focusing particularly on the relationships between the codes, is the essence of theory building (Birks et al. 2013; Urquhart et al. 2010). Appendix B shows the resulting codes and examples from the data. Finally, we developed propositions by abstracting the codes and their interrelationships. We next provide a brief overview of BAO and role theory. We then discuss our findings before presenting our propositions.

## Analytical Lens

Melville (2010) developed the BAO framework for IS and sustainability to explain the interaction of macro- and microlevel processes in the formation of ES beliefs, the formation of ES actions, and the outcomes of ES actions. The BAO framework suggests that the macro-level constructs of societal structure and organizational structure influence micro-level psychic states (beliefs about the natural environment). The micro-level psychic states influence the micro-level environmental actions taken. The latter then influence macro-level outcomes in the social system and organizations. The BAO framework highlights the mediating role of individuals in linking macro-level constructs. Beliefs and actions are considered to occur at the micro/individual level whereas outcomes take place at the macro/organizational level.

Although IS does not itself feature prominently in the BAO framework, Melville recognizes the potential of IS in belief and action formation as well as outcome assessment, stating that,

Information systems are an important but inadequately understood weapon in the arsenal of organizations in their quest for environmental sustainability by enabling new practices and processes in support of belief formation, action formation, and outcome assessment (p. 14).

However, while green IS might enable ES belief formation, ES actions, and ES outcomes as claimed by Melville, the green IS might also itself be subject to its own set of beliefs (e.g., beliefs about the platform) that subsequently influence platform actions and platform outcomes. Our study will thus apply BAO to an interorganizational green IS in the form of interorganizational ES platform.

BAO is a multilevel framework, which is suitable for situations where the objective is to identify interrelationships among individuals, organizations and societal structures (Sarker and Valacich 2010; Zhao et al. 2018). As roles are the building blocks of social and organizational structures (Biddle 1979; Galletta and Heckman 1990; Merton 1957), we turn to role theory to understand the belief-action link. Role theory concerns the fact that human beings behave differently yet predictably in a given setting (Biddle 1979). A role is defined as a “bundle of norms that defines the rights, obligations, and privileges of a person who occupies a particular status” (Biesanz and Biesanz 1978 p. 145). Role theorists focus their attention on the behaviors of a person as an individual or a representative of a formal structure (Biddle 1979). Biesanz and Biesanz consider roles as evolving and being directed toward another actor; hence, compliance to norms is internalized (Biddle 1979). Biddle conceives of roles as patterns of behavior that are typical of persons whose structural positions are similar, ties roles to functions, and views compliance to norms as instrumental. This dual perspective of roles guides our analytical thinking of the users of an interorganizational green IS who are both individual and organizational actors and whose beliefs are influenced by society and organizations.

As individuals engage in multiple relationships and interactions with members of their role set, they enact multiple roles. Werbel and Walter (2002) apply the analogy of symbiotic relationships to concurrent roles (i.e., they may have a mutualistic relationship—the roles are complementary and the behavioral expectations are compatible and have cumulative benefits), or a parasitic relationship (the behavioral expectations are incompatible and conflicting). The latter is commonly referred to as role conflict (van Sell et al. 1981). We consider the role conflict emerging from the conflicting dynamics that might develop in an interorganizational system environment (Kumar and van Dissel 1996; Wasko and Faraj 2005).

## Findings

The PPE platform participants embodied four important roles in their platform participation: supplier to the PPE sponsor, competitor to the other suppliers, employee of a supplier firm, and peer to other participants. These roles are depicted in grey bar of Figure 1. These various roles spanning levels are of particular importance in an interorganizational setting. In the case of the PPE platform, the four roles occupied by the platform participants produce two notable role conflicts where the behavioral expectations are incompatible (van Sell et al. 1981; Werbel and Walter 2002) depicted as RC1 and RC2 in Figure 1. The first role conflict is an organizational (macro)- level conflict that arises between the roles of supplier to the platform provider and competitor to other suppliers. This conflict presents itself as conflicting desires to participate in order to cooperate with the platform sponsor (SUPERMART) and to limit participation in order to not benefit competitors. We refer to this role conflict as cooptition conflict (RC1 in Figure 1) because it entails cooperation constrained by competition. The respondents frequently referred to the importance of their role as a supplier with such comments as “there is quite a bit at stake” and “[SUPERMART] is a VERY important customer to us .... So, this program obviously is something that we take very seriously,” while at the same time referencing their role as competitor to the other suppliers as in “you have to be careful of what you say … under the nose of your biggest competitors.” The second notable role conflict is a personal (micro)-level conflict that arises between the roles of employee of a supplier firm and peer to other participants. This role conflict, which we label professional conflict (RC2 in Figure 1), pits the desire to personally help peers against the need to avoid creating potential backlash with their employer. Participants described this professional conflict: “Sometimes I would prefer to kind of tell a little more anonymous so it’s more my personal opinion not a company opinion,” “I think some people might be looking in case they say something wrong. I speak personally. I think people should be cautious of what they say.”

The participants frequently referred to these roles in describing their perspective of the platform, suggesting that in their interaction with the PPE platform, they were not merely “platform participants” or “green IS users,” but suppliers, competitors, employees, and peers. These manifold roles, and the resulting role conflicts, were complicit in the platform behaviors that emerged, as displayed in Figure 1 and described next.

## PB1: Platform as Manifestation of Client Power

The first belief that appears to have been formed when the supplier organizations were initially asked to join the PPE, is the belief that the platform is a manifestation of client power (PB1 in Figure 1). Because this belief concerns the supplier organizations’ relationship with the client, we label it an organizational belief. The client–supplier power imbalance is part of the social structure comprising the macro environment. As noted in Melville (2010), the social structure influences belief formation, in this case, the belief that the platform is a manifestation of client power. This belief reveals itself in the informants’ claims that to not engage on the platform by sharing their organizational ES practices would damage the relationship with the client. For example, Informant N stated, “SUPERMART has been our customer and their need is to be met; so their need is for an efficient supply chain and,therefore, we are able to supply their need.” Most of the individuals in the platform are the employees in the supplier companies who are responsible for maintaining the relationship with SUPERMART; therefore, the desire to not upset SUPERMART coupled with the belief that non-use might damage the relationship is a major driver. Informant Q said, “The nervousness from the supplier point is that it will be used eventually as a stick .... And it won’t just be us who’s got this nervousness. It would be everybody.” Many suppliers saw the system as one-way: they were forced to help SUPER-MART in order to maintain the relationship but were not receiving anything in return. For example, Informant D notes, “[SUPERMART] are asking us to do something. But what do [we] get back?” In this sense, the belief is negative—if one does not share their ES practices in the platform, one will be punished—as opposed to positive—if one shares, one will be rewarded.

Following Meville, one expects beliefs to influence actions and, therefore, one might expect the belief that the PPE is a manifestation of client power to trigger platform content contribution. Indeed, the suppliers did contribute content to the PPE. However, the nature of content contributed to the PPE cannot be predicted by beliefs alone and BAO theory remains silent on the specific nature of the actions that follow beliefs. Informants used terms such as “general information,” “old data,” “low hanging fruit,” “easy stuff,” “commercially available information,” “basics,” “public domain information,” and “only basic information” to describe the content that they contributed to the PPE. Equally revealing is what they openly admitted to not disclosing, which was information about “new technology that saves you money,” “cost savings,” “process information,” “things that are not yet a proven success,” “things that didn’t work,” and knowledge that “is not yet public domain.” We refer to this platform action as symbolic organizational content contribution (PA1 in Figure 1) because participants are deliberately contributing content that they know to be incomplete, outdated, or common for the purpose of appearing to be both cooperative and ES engaged. They are only sharing so that SUPERMART will think that their firm is cooperative. It is an organizational action because the participants are acting on behalf of their organization and are contributing organizational content. The symbolic organizational content contribution can be explained by the cooptition conflict: as suppliers, participants know that they must engage yet as competitors, they are reluctant. While Informant B said, “we’ll try our best,” he was “a little bit worried about engaging, very much, in an open forum.” This initial action of contributing organizational ES practices, or what most informants referred to as ES case studies, generates data for platform engagement reports (PO1 in Figure 1) as well as creates platform content (PO2 in Figure 1).

PPE engagement is measured by 6SENSES and communicated to SUPERMART each month in a report that summarizes engagement levels (measured as the number of posts per supplier). After the first month, the individuals also begin receiving participation metrics for their organization, reinforcing their belief that the platform is a manifestation of client power and, consequently, their organization’s symbolic contribution action. For example, informant Q mentioned,

I feel pressured to go on the site and engage. We were in the upper quarter, but I just knew that we will start to slip down that, because for 3 days we’ve got this external company [event] which is gonna take all our energy.

With no ambiguity in the link between the most active participants and their respective companies, the PPE engagement reports put the most active participants in the limelight. Informant E explained,

We had three people registered [on the platform]. We now have two because we actually get rid of one. [One person] did nothing … and that might be dragging our score down. I need to get someone else because it is pointless being registered if you are not going to do anything.

In addition to reinforcing the belief that the platform is a manifestation of client power (arrow from PO1 to PB1 in Figure 1), the PPE engagement reports also trigger the emergence of the second belief (arrow from PO1 to PB2 in Figure 1), which is the belief that the platform is an organizational showcase (PB2 in Figure 1).

## PB2: Platform as Organizational Showcase

With the PPE engagement reports (PO1 in Figure 1) being provided monthly to SUPERMART, the belief that the platform serves as an organizational showcase (PB2 in Figure 1) began to emerge. This is an organizational belief in that it concerns the suppliers’ relationship to the client as well as the suppliers’ ES reputation: the platform showcases the supplier organizations’ cooperative attitude to the client as well as the importance they attached to ES. Said Informant N,

It is a bit of a promotion for the company that we are actively doing our bit in part for our customer and so that others can see that we are taking the environmental sustainability quite seriously.

At the same time, the realization that other participants would also be using the platform to showcase themselves arouses the cooptition conflict: it is not sufficient to engage in ES, one must demonstrate this in PPE (in order to appear cooperative to the client and reap the reputational advantages) while avoiding revealing ES initiatives that might help competitors.

This belief that the platform serves as an organizational showcase combined with the cooptition conflict reinforces the organizational symbolic action (PA1 in Figure 1) of sharing “old” ES business cases on the platform in order that others who were only now undertaking the same initiatives, could not add the content to the system and receive reputational credit. Informant J described it as follows:

You then come to a point where you see lots of people making a name for their business on things that we have done before, a long, long time before. People will read that and say, ‘Oh, look XYZ has just done,’ when in fact, they have not really just done it, they did it a long, long time ago, and they’re getting the kudos from [SUPERMART] for saying so.

Participants themselves referred to this as “managed presence.” Although there is an urge to share first before other suppliers share a similar case study and claim the “glory,” the actual usefulness of the “managed” case study content to other suppliers is limited given the old and incomplete nature of the content.

In spite of the fact that the content shared was outdated and incomplete, the act of contributing nevertheless creates platform content (PO2 in Figure 1) in addition to generating data for the platform engagement score (PO1 in Figure 1). The existence of platform content forms the basis of the belief that the platform could potentially be a knowledge hub (PB3 in Figure 1), for even if the content supplied was old from the perspective of the contributor, it might be of value to someone else. Said informant H about the content shared, “even though it’s out there, it’s understood, but still probably lots of companies maybe more SMEs are going [to the platform] for it.”

## PB3: Platform as Knowledge Hub

The belief that the platform could serve as a knowledge hub (PB3 in Figure 1) is both an organizational and personal belief. The platform content can be both useful to the participants as competitors to other suppliers (knowing what initiatives competitors have undertaken) and, once personal content is contributed to the platform (as will be discussed in the next section), useful to participants as employees in providing them knowledge that they can use to develop internal justifications for ES initiatives. The belief that the platform is a knowledge hub further reinforces the organizational symbolic content contribution (PA1 in Figure 1) while also triggering a new platform action, that of content consumption (PA2 in Figure 1). In terms of reinforcing organizational symbolic content contribution, the cooptition conflict (RC1 in Figure 1) again comes into play: participants feel the obligation to contribute (in their role as supplier) and the urge to limit the value of what they contribute (in their role as competitor). Informants feared that if others were to adopt the ES practices that they had already adopted, then everyone’s prices would decrease. In fact, more ES advanced firms currently enjoy financial benefits from engaging in ES initiatives: they are able to save money while keeping their prices the same. Should other firms also undertake similar ES initiatives, then the price advantage would dissipate. One informant of an advanced ES supplier said, “why would I want to help them [less advanced ES firms] get to the level that I have without the hard work that I’ve put in.” Moreover, if SUPERMART is aware of the savings, they might negotiate a lower price. In this case, it would be SUPERMART rather than the suppliers who would ultimately benefit. One infor mant from a large supplier, who tended to be the more ES advanced, suggested that the PPE is designed “to drag the small and medium companies all to this equal level.” The participants from the larger, more advanced ES firms recognized the knowledge utility of the platform but were unconvinced that the knowledge benefitted them as much as it did the smaller firms. Informant Q stated that “if I was a small company, I would welcome this with open arms.” The belief that the platform was a useful source of knowledge to smaller firms reinforced the contribution of managed case studies.

A new platform action also emerged from the belief that the platform is a knowledge hub: the consumption of the content (PA2 in Figure 1). Content was consumed by participants from suppliers with highly advanced as well as less advanced ES practices. Informant A, an employee of a supplier with less advanced ES practices, explained,

From my perspective, when we started to talk about lights and changing to T5, you know, I sat with the technical director for food safety and so on; I am not an expert in every field but what I can do, is go to the [PPE] about lighting … and within half an hour I know what other companies have done, and now I know what the hell a T5 light is .... So in a nutshell that’s how I use the PPE, more than anything else to become not an expert but become informed.

And Informant K, who is an employee of a supplier with more advanced ES practices, commented,

From my perspective, there’s going to be information on there, that’s … not ground breaking, but that I call my ‘light bulb moments.’ Somebody has come up with a fantastic idea that really and truly can benefit a site like this.

The consumption of content appeared most closely tied to the roles of the participants as competitors and employees. As competitors, participants consume content as a way of keeping track of what other suppliers were posting, as revealed in such statements as “we look to see what they’re trying to do and what we’re trying to achieve” and “it encourages us as a business to see ok this is what other businesses are doing.” As employees, participants consume content to make sure that they are apprised of potential ES knowledge that might help them in their internal organizational role, as evidenced in these statements:

I have actually been using [the PPE] to gain a greater understanding because although I have taken the role of green one, I didn’t know about a lot of things.

Having the knowledge to be able to go into my site, analyze the process and work out what needs to be done .... I hope in the next two years I should be an expert in sustainability.

The role of employee in a supplier company and the role of competitor of the other supplier organizations did not appear to create conflict when individuals were consuming the content on the PPE. Instead, the two roles seemed complementary (the behavioral expectations are compatible and have cumulative benefits (Werbel and Walter 2002)): both roles encourage browsing content to keep abreast of what others were posting and, in turn, the acquisition of ES knowledge (NPO1 in Figure 1).

Despite this role complementarity, the deriving of value from the content proved difficult because although there is ample content, the lack of detail creates a paucity of usable content. Informant G said,

If, for example, I want to look at water recapture, I can find a case study which shows that is already done somewhere .... the main issue … is with understanding exactly what is required to deliver some of these things.

Ironically, it is the paucity of usable content that leads to an important non-platform action (NPA1 in Figure 1), that of seeking (and supplying) additional information offline. This is possible because the platform reveals the contact information of the content contributors. Informant I said,

There are details … on the PPE to say look ‘If you want to discuss this further, please contact me’ and [people] can contact [each other] via mobile phone or via email, and have direct conversations offline.

Informants report that “sitting down face-to-face” and “access” to other people has been “the most beneficial” aspect of the platform. Informant N commented,

I have made some pretty good contacts … and almost develop into friendship with one or two of them .... We exchange information … well, initially on the exchange and then it is phone call and email directly; so that is quite good.

Seeking and supplying offline content (NPA1), and the relationships formed by that activity (NPO2 in Figure 1), enhances the ES knowledge (NPO1) and triggers the final platform belief (i.e., the belief that the platform is an individual resource) (PB4 in Figure 1).

## PB4: Platform as Individual Resource

Once the participants begin to see the platform as potentially useful on an individual level—for advancement within their organization (if they can obtain valuable knowledge), for career opportunities at other organizations—their platform actions become influenced by the conflict between their roles as an employee of a single supplier and a knowledge peer with the other participants. The conflict in these roles, referred to as professional conflict, is that individuals see the potential to portray themselves in the best light possible for future opportunities by contributing their own personal knowledge to their peers, while at the same time fear that revealing too much about their organizational initiatives might endanger their own standing within their organization. Said Informant L,

Sometimes I don’t want to share because … there are some projects [his organization’s ES projects]

I’m doing that I’ve not heard of anyone else doing. The majority of stuff is stuff I can help other members, I’ll absolutely share it if it’s going to benefit. But as a whole, let’s see some projects I want to keep under wrap.

As a result of this professional role conflict (RC2 in Figure 1), the participants engage in the platform action of substantive personal content contribution: rather than provide content about their organization’s ES initiatives, they provide their own ideas, opinions, and expertise. This shifts the focus of content from organizational content (the original purpose of the platform) to personal content and enables them to play the role of knowledge peer while not impeding their role as employee. We refer to this platform action as substantive personal content contribution (PA3 in Figure 1). It is substantive because it is provided with the intent of helping others and is intended to be useful information. Such information, even if not the original purpose of the platform, proved valuable. According to 6SENSES,

Opinion based insight pieces, tips from people … can be just as valuable [as business cases] in terms of the content within them. They can be more qualitative and it is often easier for people to share them because if I was to approach you and you are working in a big company and asked you to share information on something your company’s done, you can easily imagine that this is commercially sensitive, so [you] are not going to be able to give me that. Whereas if I asked you for your own experiences of compressed air use or onsite renewable energy… you have worked through those projects and you know what you are talking about and be able to share some really good insights, but the fact that you’re giving it from your personal perspective means it probably does not need the same amount of sign-off.

## Informant H echoed this:

It is not necessarily just writing a case study. It is participating [in] discussion … so you can give some information … most people would do contribution in terms of discussions, the more ideas stimulate more discussions, more people get involved.

## Informant I added,

As each week goes by, we are getting more comfortable sharing information on the [platform] .... People are becoming more confident in asking open questions and also sharing ideas of things they are looking at.

The outcome of the substantive personal content contribution is the enhancement of the platform content (PO2 in Figure 1).

## Enhanced Platform Content: ES Outcome

The consumption of the enhanced content combined with the additional information obtained by offline relationships enhance individuals’ ES knowledge (NPO1 in Figure 1), helping them to champion ES initiatives in their firms (NPA2 in Figure 1) by raising their own ES awareness and providing them information to develop ES business cases. Informant B, who is from a large firm explains that knowing what other suppliers are doing enables him [emphasis added] to talk to each of his site’s management teams and develop “a plan for corrective action, even if the corrective action might be doing nothing.” Informant T who is also from a large firm said, “if we would look for a new [ES] project, the first thing that I [emphasis added] do is to jump on the platform.” ES awareness spurs dialogue, allowing the PPE participant to open internal communication channels with engineers who otherwise always seemed “to have an excuse” to not talk. Informant F, who is from a smaller firm, said “if it was just me [emphasis added] trying to engage all of the members and staff in something, it wouldn’t have as much impact.” Informant T claimed that, “[the PPE] helps me [emphasis added] to raise awareness of the sustainability strategy with my business.” Thus, the individual belief of the knowledge utility of the platform influences them to consume the content shared in the platform, which they then use to champion the ES initiatives within their respective organizations.

The platform content consumption with the additional information obtained in the offline relationships also helped solidify ES business cases. The 6SENSES Community Manager stated,

Someone is working on a project and faces a barrier, a hurdle, maybe they need help making a business case. They want trusted contacts to go to … [through] the PPE [platform, they] maybe find five trusted contacts that other companies have used to do similar projects, or they use case studies to build a much stronger business case to sell internally. You know, A doing this, B doing this … I think we should do it. Or there is a specific problem they have come across, [for example,] we are hoping to put up a wind turbine, but we are having problems with regulatory issues. Can anyone help us out?

[The] ideas and feedback help them solve the problem.

Ultimately, the championing of ES initiatives (NPA2 in Figure 1) by the participants within their organizations in some cases influenced specific ES initiatives (NPO3 in Figure 1), leading to concrete ES savings. For example, one informant reported changing the choice of a new compressor based upon platform content:

We actually went for a slightly more expensive compressor in the end, because the longer term payback would be better and will use less electricity. We use less electricity and that means more electricity for the grid.

## And the 6SENSES Community Manager noted,

We have seen people post about waste paper towels: their current waste contractor was not taking waste paper towels, they got all sorts of answers from people with ideas of things to do with them. And they gave [them] confidence to actually go back and say to the waste contractor: everyone else is getting money for these and you are saying you cannot even take them, that is rubbish. And the waste contractor ended up giving them money each week now, so that is something that has been solved within about five days.

## Discussion

While BAO theorizes a relationship between beliefs and actions, our study extends this to show that the nature of the actions undertaken in response to a platform belief in an interorganizational green IS are influenced by the different roles that participants occupy, and consequently by the interrole conflicts that they experience. The nature of green IS actions are explained by the participants’ roles and the inherent conflict between the roles that we identified. The desired nature of action in an interorganizational green IS is substantive organizational action. Only when the roles were complementary (Werbel and Walter 2002)—the roles of competitor to other suppliers employee in a supplier firm—was the platform action (content consumption) consistent with the desired action of substantive organizational action (see Figure 2).

By contrast, the roles as a supplier of SUPERMART and a competitor to the other suppliers have a parasitic relationship—the behavioral expectations are incompatible and conflicting (van Sell et al. 1981; Werbel and Walter 2002)— and the roles are highly integrated with the ambiguous boundary of each role creating conflict (Ashforth et al. 2000; Sundaramurthy and Kreiner 2008). In role conflict, when possible, individuals will choose among the incompatible norms (based on the degree they consider others powerful and their norms legitimate); if it is not possible to choose, they will make a compromise among the conflicting norms (van de Vliert 1981). We found that role conflict between the roles of supplier to the client and competitor to other suppliers led to cooptition conflict, which in turn resulted in the contribution of symbolic content about organizational ES initiatives, compromising the intent (see Figure 2).

Although the sharing of information about organizational ES initiatives on the platform was an intent behind the platform’s development, it was not intended that the platform content provided be symbolic, which took the form of dated and/or incomplete information. Additionally, the roles of employee of a supplier organization and knowledge peer to other participants produced professional conflict. This role conflict prompted the participants to shift their contribution to a personal level from an organizational one, compromising the domain (see Figure 2). In both cases of role conflict, the platform behaviors deviated from the original purpose of the platform (high quality information about organizational ES initiatives). Our first proposition is thus:

P1: The nature of platform actions taken in response to platform beliefs in an interorganizational green IS are influenced by the role conflict experienced by the platform users such that:

(a) cooptition conflict leads to organizational symbolic content contribution, a compromise of intent

(b) professional conflict leads to personal substantive content contribution, a compromise of domain

The distinction between symbolic and substantive content contribution is important. Contrary to research showing that organizations often engage in symbolic actions to avoid implementing meaningful change (Scott 2008; Tilcsik 2010) particularly in the area of environmental sustainability (Jenkin 2011), we discovered that contributing symbolic organizational content is a worthwhile endeavor in interorganizational green IS. The symbolic content prompts personal interactions outside the green IS to learn more about the content shared, which enhances personal ES knowledge and in turn creates personal relationships that give rise to a new role as knowledge peer. Another benefit of the symbolic organizational content in interorganizational green IS is that when it is shared by reputable large organizations, the users may draw upon the sharer’s reputation to justify the push toward specific ES initiatives within their own organizations even if the content itself is lacking.

Moreover, our study showcases that the mere use of interorganizational green IS does not directly lead to organizational ES initiatives. The actions taken on the platform enable a non-platform outcome (ES knowledge). But it was only when the initial patterns of symbolic organizational content contribution (intent compromised platform action) and content consumption (desired platform action) were coupled with offline personal interactions around ES information that substantive personal content contribution emerged. This domain compromised platform action ultimately enhances the value of the platform content and enable ES knowledge that supported the individuals’ championing ES initiatives within their respective organizations. This finding differs from the existing (individual- and organizational-level) green IS works that generally assume a direct link between green IS use and ES initiatives (e.g., Hanelt et al. 2017; Klör, et al. 2017; Seidel et al. 2017). Our second proposition is the following:

P2: Personal non-platform actions enhance personal outcomes of platform actions and enable organizational ES initiatives, specifically:

(a) the personal non-platform action of seeking additional information from content contributors enhances ES knowledge resulting from platform content consumption

(b) the ES knowledge spurs personal non-platform action of championing ES initiatives, creating organizational ES initiatives

It is important to stress our key finding that green IS is not just an individual action that is driven by ES beliefs or an organizational outcome resulting from individual action as treated in BAO. Green IS beliefs influence actions and ultimately ES outcomes, among which are the ES initiatives in cases where individuals apply the knowledge they acquire from the green IS to their organizations. For an interorganizational green IS to influence organizational ES, the actions and outcomes must move outside the platform realm. In our study, this occurred when the desired platform action of content consumption led to a non-platform personal outcome (ES knowledge) that spurred non-platform personal actions (contacting content contributors for more information and championing ES initiatives). These non-platform personal actions, which began to emerge after new platform beliefs had formed, made the organizational outcome (organizational ES initiatives) possible. An interorganizational green IS can thus be understood as a dynamic ecosystem of platform beliefs: platform actions that result in platform and non-platform outcomes, which in turn reinforce an existing platform belief or trigger a new platform belief. Perhaps because Melville’s primary focus is explaining how sustainability outcomes develop, BAO does not go further to consider the consequences of outcomes. In this sense, BAO is a static framework with outcomes representing the achievement of actions stemming from beliefs. Our results extend BAO to account for the impact of outcomes on beliefs, resulting in a dynamic framework in which beliefs evolve as outcomes are experienced. Our final proposition is:

P3: Platform beliefs are triggered by and/or reinforced by platform and non-platform outcomes.

## Contributions and Implications

Our study contributes to the growing body of research on green IS by considering an interorganizational green IS within a supply chain. Past green IS research has shown that environmental beliefs can be shared through an intraorganizational system that promotes the democratization of information and reflective disclosure (Seidel et al. 2013). Our results show that across competitor organizations, an interorganizational green IS that provides limited information about ES initiatives can nevertheless support the spreading of ES knowledge within organizations when it is buttressed by the development of individual relationships across the competitor organizations. It is not the same as competing organizations forming a relationship; it was peer-to-peer relationship in their role as knowledge peer outside the context of their respective organizations that enabled the content in the system to become valuable to the individuals and their organizations.

The extant green IS research describes several roles for green IS: an informating role such as IS that provide information to support green decision making (Corbett 2010), or track and report on environmental performance (Bengtsson and Ågerfalk 2011; Butler 2011); a transformational role such as IS that provide collaboration technology to reduce organizational dependence on air travel (Corbett 2010) or improve process efficiency to reduce energy consumption (Corbett 2010); or a direct role in the form of green IT assets and infrastructure (Nishant et al. 2017). In interorganizational context, our research suggests a cooptive role for green IS such that competitors may share information about ES in such a way as to not threaten their own competitiveness while still positively influencing ES activities in other organizations.

In addition to these contributions to the green IS literature, we also contribute to our analytical lens, namely the “native” BAO framework and its application in green IS research (Melville 2010) and the concept of role conflict in role theory (van Sell et al. 1981). First, we contextualize BAO to the context of green IS. Contextualizing theory enables researchers to develop richer theories and generate deeper insights into the associated phenomena (Hong et al. 2014). Although BAO is intended as a theory to promote IS for environmental sustainability, IS does not actually feature in BAO. Loeser et al. (2017) suggest that IS can be viewed as an action step in the BAO process and Melville provides examples of IS as both individual action and organizational outcome. Yet, viewing IS as an action in response to environmental beliefs or an organizational outcome resulting from individual environmental actions assumes a fairly straightforward relationship between IS and ES. This view ignores the complex dynamics at play in IS use itself such as contextual factors, beliefs, motivations, and behavioral factors that determine patterns of use. Contextualizing BAO to the context of an interorganizational green IS allows us to view the green IS as subject to beliefs, actions, and outcomes. Our findings point to the emergence of several important interorganizational green IS beliefs across levels: platform as a manifestation of client power and an organizational showcase arena (organizational-level beliefs), platform as a knowledge hub (organizational and individual-level belief), and platform as an individual resource (individual-level belief). Furthermore, our work extends BAO by showing how outcomes trigger the formation of new beliefs, some of which reinforce existing actions and others result in new actions. In this way, BAO when applied to a green IS is a dynamic framework.

Finally, we show that the link from beliefs to actions is not a direct link as suggested by BAO and assumed in previous studies (e.g., Hanelt et al. 2017; Klör, et al. 2017; Seidel et al. 2017), but rather one that is filtered through the roles that the participants play and the role conflicts that emerge. As the nature of actions in an interorganizational green IS (symbolic versus substantive, organization versus individual) is influenced by roles and role conflicts, the actions may deviate from the original purpose of the interorganizational green IS. Nevertheless, compromised actions may still contribute to the path toward the ultimate goal of the interorganizational green IS, which is to drive organizational ES initiatives. Hence, notwithstanding the importance of the green movements that put pressure on organizations to demonstrate environmental responsibility, we are optimistic that even without strong organizational ES belief, there is a potential for interorganizational green IS to drive ES initiatives within the participating organizations.

## Limitations and Conclusions

A limitation of this study is the lack of platform data files such as the timestamp of content contributions, the discussion threads, etc. The availability of such data could enable us to uncover additional findings. Another limitation of the study is that we only interviewed the individuals registered on the platform and individuals from the platform company; we did not interview senior managers from suppliers not using the platform. Hence, we are unable to delve into externalities and how such externalities might influence platform use. For example, when individuals act to improve their personal network and ES reputation, there is the potential for negative outcomes for their organizations. This is an interesting area for future research.

Environmental sustainability is one of the most pressing issues of our times (Watson et al. 2010). The green IS literature has to date focused either on the individual/employee (Corbett 2013; Loock et al. 2013) or on the organizational level (Seidel et al. 2013). In contrast, our study is multilevel and interorganizational, and explores the organizations (some of whom are direct competitors) that have not necessarily embarked on a specific green IS or on ES transformation. Our study demonstrates the possibility of a client-company to promote environmentally sustainable practices by providing an interorganizational green IS for its suppliers to share ES information. Using the belief–action–outcome framework complemented by role theory, our study reveals that the nature of platform actions taken in response to platform beliefs depends upon role conflict experienced by the platform participants. Because individuals in an interorganizational context embody multifarious roles (in this case, supplier, competitor, employee, and peer), each participant in an interorganizational green IS is more than a single “user.” Thus, the multiple participant roles and role conflicts must be understood in order to explain platform behaviors on, and results from, an interorganizational green IS.
