# An examination of the concern for information privacy in the New Zealand regulatory context

Ellen A. Rose

Institute of Information and Mathematical Sciences, Massey University, Auckland 1310, New Zealand Received 5 March 2004; received in revised form 23 April 2005; accepted 22 August 2005 Available online 5 October 2005

## Abstract

This study used confirmatory factor analysis on responses from a random sample of 459 New Zealanders to examine the theoretical basis of the concern for information privacy. The results were interpreted using two competing theories to explain differences found in the structural model of the construct when testing it with a non-US sample. Differences between US and New Zealand privacy protection approaches reflected cross-national differences in privacy concerns. The findings should contribute to our understanding of cross-national information privacy concerns and have implications for policy instrument choices. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Concern for information privacy; Data protection models; Information privacy theory; Structural equation modeling

## 1. Introduction

Increased consumer concerns about organizational practices in the collection and management of personal data have resulted from several. Firstly, advances in information technology (IT) have produced efficiencies that have increased the value of personal information as a commodity for exchange [22]. Secondly, globalization has increased the need for cross-border data flows leading to concerns about different levels of protection and safeguards. Thirdly, legislation to improve privacy protection has often been enacted in reaction to surveys of consumer concern but has later been followed by legislation to decrease privacy in reaction to catastrophic events, such as the 9/11 terrorist attack. Finally, an increase in the exchange of information between public and private organizations [17] and the availability of new surveillance capabilities has contributed to consumer fears of misuse. The ease of collecting and accessing information over global networks has made information privacy concern an international issue, complicated by the variability in the way it is defined and protected by laws and policies across countries [5,15,23,39].

The purpose of my study was to examine the CFIP construct using a non-US sample to determine whether empirical evidence could be found to support Westin’s control theory of privacy, upon which the construct is based. If a lack of individual control over one’s information is not the only plausible explanation underlying privacy concerns, individual procedural controls for fair information practice (FIP), such as choice and consent, may be necessary but insufficient ways to justify and manage privacy. Different policy instruments (such as law, privacy-enhancing technologies, international agreements, administrative regulation, and organizational policies) are used in different countries. Different conceptualizations of the dynamic associations between individuals, public and private organizations, and IT are reflected in the current variety of approaches [2]. Cross-national and cultural differences in the way that privacy is defined may also reflect different underlying explanations for information privacy concern.

## 2. Related work, theoretical framework, and hypotheses

## 2.1. CFIP as a multi-dimensional concept

A 15-item instrument, hereafter referred to as the CFIP instrument was developed by Smith et al. in Ref. [37] as a way of measuring the multi-dimensional nature of consumer concern for information privacy (CFIP). The CFIP instrument was based on an extensive review of the privacy literature and the use of rigorous procedures to test its validity and reliability using samples of US graduate students. The CFIP instrument was designed to measure levels of consumer concern about organizational information privacy practices with respect to the four first-order factors or dimensions of CFIP shown in Table 1. Concerns about organizational practice, such as collection, errors, secondary use, and unauthorized access to personal information, represent aspects of consumer concern in terms of losing the ability to control the collection, handling, and use of personal information. The fair information practices (FIPS) displayed here are the five principles adopted by the US Federal Trade Commission: notice, choice, security, access, and enforcement. They are common to all major international codes.

Despite the recognition of FIPS in US laws and organizational policies, consumer privacy concerns remain high, as evidenced by recent Harris Interactive polls [19–21] and the Federal Trade Commission studies [13,14]. Similarly, in NZ complaints to the Privacy Commissioner rose 10% in 2001 from the previous year. The largest categories of complaints were in gaining access to personal information held by others (29.6%) and concerns over unauthorized disclosure (29.3%) [36]. Privacy concerns reported in NZ surveys were higher when the Internet was involved (e.g. 86% concerned or very concerned) but lower (e.g. 47% concerned or very concerned) when other issues, such as health care, the environment, and crime, were simultaneously considered. The 47% represented a drop from 68% concerned or very concerned in the 1994 Brian Steel Survey (cited in Ref. [42]).

Two recent international studies [26,27] using the CFIP instrument found that consumers in countries with moderate regulatory models (e.g. US and New Zealand) had greater privacy concerns than consumers in countries with either no regulation or very high regulation. Levels of concern were not significantly different between countries with moderate regulatory models. It was assumed that high regulation resulted in fewer privacy violations, leading to fewer incidents reported in the media and lower concern or else that in countries with low concern, there was little pressure by the public or advocacy groups. Both studies used relatively small per country samples (n = 706 spread over 30 countries; n = 595 spread over 20 countries). All participants belonged to the IS Audit and Control Association (ISACA), resulting in a sample biased towards employed males with higher levels of education.

CFIP was represented as a reflective second-order factor, underlying the four CFIP dimensions in the findings of a recent study of US consumers (biased towards the more educated and affluent) [38]. This factor was interpreted as the common aspect of an individual’s concern about losing control over his or her private information underlying concerns specific to each dimension. This interpretation was based on the ‘‘control theory’’ of privacy attributed to Westin [44], whose 1967 definition stated: ‘‘Privacy is the claim of individuals, groups, or institutions to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others’’ (as opposed to the statement of Brandeis and Warren quoting Judge Cooley in 1890 that privacy was the right ‘‘to be let alone’’). This definition reflects the individualist cultural model that prevails in North America [24]. Control is not separated from privacy in Westin’s definition. The privacy as a control viewpoint has been widely used in recent studies, including [10,31]. Based on the control theory of privacy, I expected support for the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis H1a. Consumer concern for information privacy can be separated into concerns about unauthorized access, errors, secondary use, and collection where a common concern about loss of control underlies the distinct concerns.

This control theory has been criticized as confusing privacy with autonomy. Practically, it is impossible in today’s world to have total control over personal information once it has been collected and dispersed [40]. A more recent, alternative view [28] is Moor’s ‘‘control/restricted access’’ theory. This separates the concept of privacy from the concept of control, stating that it is possible to have privacy without control and control without privacy; thus, concept of privacy, justification for it, and its management are distinct but interrelated concepts. The normative concept of privacy is defined as protection from intrusion and information gathering. The concept of control plays a role in the justification and management of information privacy. It justifies framing policies (external controls) to protect privacy and the management of privacy using the internal controls of choice, consent, and correction. An underlying assumption is that in a highly computerized world, we may not be able to expect privacy in its current form to continue if individual control is required. Therefore, access to sensitive information should be restricted even if its owner is not able to control its circulation through global networks and between databases.

Moor’s theory distinguished between the condition of having privacy and a normative right to privacy as well as between a loss of privacy and a violation of privacy. Collection with consent entailed a loss while collection without consent entailed a violation. Information privacy is thus an expression of the need for security. Core values cut across cultures but may be expressed to different degrees in different societies. For example, giving personal information to the government for tax purposes entails a loss of control but not a loss of a normative right to privacy. If information privacy is defined only in terms of individual control, this would also constitute a loss of a right to privacy. This is a normatively private situation where an individual can expect his or her right to privacy to be protected through restricted access measures and a right to retain this even if the information is improperly disclosed. Thus, private situations or zones can be defined in terms of ‘‘who has access to what under which circumstances’’ [41]. Based on the control/ restricted access theory, concern about the adequacy of restrictions on access may underlie concerns about errors, secondary use and collection. Under this theory, concern about unauthorized access underlies concern about the other three dimensions: collection, errors, and secondary use.

In a world of global networks, managing privacy may require external controls, such as confidentiality agreements and international policy, based on restricted access. This goes beyond but complements fair information practices that specify individual controls of choice, consent, and correction. National and international privacy codes, policy and enforceable laws may be needed to protect a normative right to information privacy as distinct from our ability to control personal information. Based on the ‘‘control/ restricted access’’ theory of privacy, I expected support for the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis H1b. Consumer concern for information privacy can be separated into concerns about unauthorized access, errors, secondary use, and collection where a common concern about inadequate access restrictions underlies the other three distinct concerns.

Given that H1a and H1b are competing hypotheses, I expected to find support for one or the other, but not both.

## 2.2. Data protection: theories and policies

Cross-national differences in how information privacy is perceived have influenced the formation of different protection models. These may have implications in the use the CFIP instrument in some non-US contexts. Three definitions of privacy along with three approaches to resolving the problem were proposed in Ref. [4] and have been summarized in Table 2. Each approach is based on a different underlying theory about the relationships between individuals, personal information, organizational practices (of both public and private organizations), and IT. The variance in assumptions has resulted in a range of data protection models. US data protection involves both Self-Help and Voluntary Control. The NZ model, originally classified as Data Commissioner, was later re-classified as Coregulation in Ref. [8].

Fig. 1 represents the data protection models as a continuum, ranging from those with no central authority to those based on a central authority.

US data protection is based on corporate selfregulation combined with legal protection based on the privacy torts of Prosser [33] and sector-specific laws. Privacy legislation in the US has been characterized by conflict and compromise influenced by rapid technological growth, public concerns, and advocacy groups on both sides of the issue. There is a history of seeing litigation as preferable to administrative regulation as a way to protect privacy but not discourage technological and business innovation. In this centrist view, an individual’s right to protect the privacy of their personal information may be limited by legitimate requirements of society, to further scientific research, and to facilitate global commerce [6,9]. The US Privacy Act of 1974 dealt only with electronic records held by the federal government, gave individuals a right of access for making corrections, and required consent to disclose the information for purposes not already publicized.

Federal agencies were required to publicize a list of the systems they maintained. Under the Computer Security Act of 1987, federal agencies were required to conform to security standards to restrict access and to identify systems containing personal information [39].

In the US, the privacy problem was seen as the inability of individuals to control personal information and it was primarily addressed by giving individuals procedural rights based on fair information practices. Unlike the European Union (EU), the implementation of FIPS in the US did not require giving consumers access to all data (credit reports were an exception) nor did it require that they be informed of all secondary uses though some organizations did this via their own policies [11]. The focus was historically on restricting access to government records and more recently, with the advent of the Internet, to restricting government access to records held by other public or private organizations by requiring a court order for access in order to protect civil liberties.

While fair information principles underlie all privacy laws and many international agreements, nations disagree on how they should be implemented and enforced. The EU sees the information privacy problem as coinciding with advances in IT that began in the 1960s. Therefore, unlike US policy which focused on procedural rights for individuals to be dealt with in the courts, EU data protection policy focused on implementing and enforcing safeguards for proper organizational handling of personal information. The problem in the EU is seen to be misuse of IT and is addressed via laws and centrally administered regulation to provide oversight of the processing and sharing of personal information, public, and private. Two supra-nationa policies guide EU countries. The Convention on Data Protection recognizes privacy as a human right. The EU Data Directive seeks to standardize protections across the EU. Cross-border data flows are allowed only when all parties have adequate policies for protection.

New Zealand is a member of the EU and has enacted a co-regulatory policy that has been influenced by both the EU and the US. Under co-regulation, legislation and a small number of sector-specific codes of practice have been utilized to implement and enforce the protection of personal information. Co-regulation aims to prevent intrusion rather than react to them and is characterized by the following features [43]:

 statutory requirement of compliance with NZ Privacy Act of 1993 principles;

 collaborative development of the principles with data user representatives and specification of exceptions and exemptions;

 sector-specific codes of practice with complaint handling and enforcement mechanisms which may vary the principles;

 default complaint handling and enforcement mechanisms for sectors which do not develop their own codes of practice or do not specify compliance mechanisms;

 provision for appeals from code compliance decisions to provide quality control on interpretation of the principles;

 avoidance of generic registration or licensing requirements.

Under co-regulation, privacy is seen as a means to control the power of public and private bureaucracies. It places an intermediary (privacy office) between individuals and organizations and provides a legislative framework. The major legislation is the NZ Privacy Act. It deals only with the legal rights of individuals with respect to availability and protection of personal information against both public and private violations. Similar to US policy, the goal is to strike a balance between the rights of individual’s and the interests of the community but the means of realizing the goal differ. Privacy complaints must initially be brought to the Privacy Commissioner and may pass to a tribunal after the commissioner has made a decision. New Zealand law also includes a tort of invasion of privacy, which allows one to sue for damages if harm can be proven, but this has generally been too costly for most to pursue [7,35].

Public and legislative opinions are political resources that can be used to address power imbalances that might result in privacy violation. In sum, the problem is defined in terms of a lack of privacy in US policy whereas it is seen as part of the larger problem of bureaucratic accountability in NZ policy. Co-regulation could be seen as a viable alternative to the extremes of US and EU policy. It could address the historical distrust for government in the US and still allow for selfregulation but in a more cooperative fashion backed by a legislative framework to provide incentives to comply. Co-regulation provides a normative right to privacy and separates this right from the means to manage it in the form of both external and internal controls.

## 3. Research design

A survey was mailed to 2000 randomly selected individuals from the New Zealand electoral roll of voters. The electoral roll represents 94.7% of New Zealanders aged 18 years and over. Eight-five surveys were undeliverable, resulting in 1915 delivered. A response rate of 25% was achieved as 476 surveys were returned. Of those returned, 459 were complete and usable. All 15 questions from the rigorously developed CFIP survey instrument, used in the studies discussed in section two, were included in the survey instrument used for this study. A pre-test with a convenience sample of 12 participants was conducted. Participants included academic staff familiar with survey research and/or the subject matter as well as general staff and students. Content and construct validity were qualitatively tested via an interview with each pre-test participant which asked them to comment on their understanding of each question and to indicate what they thought the question was measuring. The results of the pre-test indicated that the 15 question items could be used as is with one minor change, replacing the word ‘‘companies’’ with ‘‘organizations’’.

Survey response data were analyzed with SPSS 11.5 and the AMOS 5.0 Graphics structural equation modeling (SEM) software. Fit indices were examined for both the structural model (paths between the four CFIP dimension factors) and the measurement model (paths between survey items and factors). Model specification and setup used maximum likelihood estimation, maximum likelihood bias for the covariance matrix and setting the regression weight of the path with the highest loading to 1.0 for each factor. All path weights from error terms to survey items were set to 1.0. All path weights from residual errors to factors were set to 1.0 to establish a scale for computing standardized weights. First-order factors were allowed to co-vary with all other first-order factors.

## 4. Results

## 4.1. Sample and descriptive statistics

Sample demographics are shown in Table 3. Census data from 2001 were used to compare the general population with the sample. Individuals with college degrees were slightly over-represented in the sample. All other demographics were representative of the population.

The rank order of the CFIP dimensions was comparable with previous findings as shown in Table 4. Concerns about unauthorized access and secondary use continued to be greater than concerns about errors or the initial collection of personal data. In my sample, the top two and bottom two concerns in the ranking were slightly different from previous findings, indicating that the ranking may vary across countries and over time.

Only age and Internet use resulted in significant differences in terms of average CFIP, as shown in Table 5. Respondents between 18 and 27 years of age (10% of respondents) had significantly lower concern (mean = 3.83, p = 0.003) than older respondents (mean = 4.24). Respondents who had used the Internet for 4 or more years (34% of respondents) also had significantly lower concerns (mean = 4.07, p = 0.005) than those who had either never used the Internet or only used it for 1–3 years (mean = 4.27). These results may imply the potential for downward bias in studies that use student samples.

Descriptive statistics for CFIP and its four dimensions are shown in Table 6. On average, concerns about unauthorized access (mean 4.49) and secondary use (mean 4.40) were significantly higher than concerns those about errors (mean 4.13) and collection (mean 3.79).

The model for H1a, with CFIP as a secondorder factor, resulted in a standardized factor loading of Access on CFIP in excess of 0.95 implying that CFIP and Access may not be distinct constructs. This provides evidence in support of hypothesis H1b, that concerns about unauthorized access rather than control may underlie concerns about errors, secondary use and collection. ## 5. Conclusions and implications

The purpose of my study was to determine whether empirical support existed for an alternative theoretical basis for concern for information privacy by examining the CFIP construct in a non-US context. A comparison of information privacy policy in the US and New Zealand indicated that the way they defined the privacy problem differed. Therefore, given that a democratic country’s policies reflect the beliefs of its citizens, we might reasonably have expected an alternative explanation for the nature of consumer privacy concern. Empirical evidence from our New Zealand sample found support for the measurement model. In contrast to previous findings, however, our data support Moor’s control/restricted access theory of privacy over Westin’s privacy as control theory. Greatest concern was shown in New Zealand for unauthorized access, followed by secondary use, in a similar way to US findings.

Finding support for Moor’s theory of control/ restricted access has a number of policy implications. First, separating the concept of control from the concept of information privacy has implications for the choice of policy instruments used to implement and enforce privacy protection. Under Moor’s theory, individual controls, such as choice, consent, and correction, are necessary but not sufficient to ensure a normative right to privacy. Policy instruments that represent external controls, such as a privacy law to assure normative rights, are also needed. These requirements appear to be met under the current legal framework and co-regulatory modelin New Zealand.Secondly, there mayalso bea need to do more in terms of educating the public, a function of the Privacy Commissioner in the NZ Privacy Act.

This may be why concerns are still high despite the existence of both internal and external controls. Consumers may not be aware of the protection that exists or of the procedures for seeking resolution to problems. A recent study in New Zealand found that 74% of consumers were unaware of the current privacy protection model but preferred the current model or even stronger policies [34]. Furthermore, the coregulatory model of protection was based on viewing the privacy problem as part of the larger problem of bureaucratic accountability. This implied that a general level of distrust of bureaucratic power and potential use of IT as a tool to strengthen that power may also account for continued high levels of concern.

The use of sector-specific laws in both the US and New Zealand is a means of establishing normatively private situations. Fair information practices establish a set of individual controls but there are differences in how they are implemented in the US and New Zealand. Opt-in policies provide greater choice and transparency for individuals and encourage data users to publicize the rules and conditions of information use so that consumers can make informed choices. Opt-out policies implicitly assume consent, putting the burden on consumers to discover the rules and conditions of information collection and use. New Zealand policy is pro-active and cooperative with an opt-in flavor. In contrast, self-regulation in the US is primarily reactive and tends to have an opt-out flavor.

Excessive costs are often cited as the reason for favoring opt-out in the US. This is consistent with the underlying theory of balancing individual civil rights with the needs of society. For example, the annual costs to US businesses and individuals, due to incomplete or insufficient privacy protections under the current model of self-regulation, have been estimated to be in the tens of billions of US\$ [16]; the costs of complying with proposed Internet privacy regulation has been estimated at US\$ 30 billion [18]. In contrast, actual total expenses for the NZ Office of the Privacy Commissioner amounted to NZ\$ 2.2 million (US\$ 1.5 million) in 2002, down from NZ\$ 2.3 million in 2001 [29]. Normalizing for population differences at the time of data collection and using the per-capita cost of the Office of the Privacy Commissioner (US\$ 0.38), would amount to a cost of US\$ 110 million. This can be compared with recent studies, such as [30], which found US consumers would spend an additional US\$ 6 billion online if privacy issues were less of a concern. In 2001, 96.5% of all privacy cases were handled by the Privacy Commissioner without a need to seek the expensive option of legal recourse [29,35]. So from a utilitarian viewpoint, the New Zealand co-regulatory model appears to be relatively efficient.

While support was found for the measurement model, one of the question items for secondary use appeared to need reconsideration. In addition not finding support for the structural model of H1a based on Westin’s control theory provided evidence that investigating alternative theories of the privacy problem that disaggregate rights should be considered as an alternative. A limitation of this study, or any other survey study, is using public opinion to guide policy. Critics feel that public opinion is often shallow and misinformed and warn against its use [32]. In contrast, advocates claim that policy should be responsive to public opinion in order to meet the ideals of a democratic society [25]. However, when defining the problem of privacy as a political problem of bureaucratic accountability, public opinion becomes a relevant resource for policy making and a factor for consideration.
