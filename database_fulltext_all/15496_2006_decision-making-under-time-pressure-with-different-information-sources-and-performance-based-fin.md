# Decision making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives: part 3

## Abstract

In prior reports (J.R. Marsden, R. Pakath, K. Wibowo, Decision making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives: Part 1, Decision Support Systems, 34 (2002) 75–97, J.R. Marsden, R. Pakath, K. Wibowo, Decision making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives: Part 2, Decision Support Systems, 34 (2002) 99–124), we describe the design and implementation of a software platform and a laboratory experiment aimed at a preliminary investigation of the efficacy of an alternative communication language for a time-pressured, incentive-driven decision-making setting. Our experimental findings suggest that, despite its relative novelty, the alternative language is at least as effective as the more traditional communication modes of written and spoken English in the specific setting examined, while succinctly conveying what must be conveyed. That work embodies three central underlying constructs—information communication mode, communication speed, and complexity level. The analysis in (J.R. Marsden, R. Pakath, K. Wibowo, Decision making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives: Part 2, Decision Support Systems, 34 (2002) 99– 124) focuses solely on mode. In this concluding portion of the research, we analyze the two remaining constructs—speed and complexity level. Taken together with that in (J.R. Marsden, R. Pakath, K. Wibowo, Decision making under time pressure with different information sources and performance-based financial incentives: Part 2, Decision Support Systems, 34 (2002) 99–124), our analysis enables a fuller understanding of the interplay between communication mode, speed, and complexity. We note two particular findings of our research: (1) whereas real-world systems are often designed based on prescriptive tradition, including <sup>b</sup>best practice<sup>Q</sup> criteria widely disseminated and adopted by practitioners without demonstrated benefits to users, in our study, we found that performance at any level of a particular construct was typically sensitive to the levels set for the remaining two attributes, and (2) despite the fact that many experimental studies (e.g., (D.M. Miranda, R. P. Bostrom, The impact of group support systems on group conflict and conflict management, Journal of Management Information Systems, 10 (3), (1993), 63–95, H. Nysveen, P.E. Pedersen, An exploratory study of customers’ perception of company web sites offering various interactive applications: moderating effects of customers’ Internet experience, Decision Support Systems, 37 (1) (2004), 137–150)) continue to emphasize subject perception over more objective performance measures, in our study, we found that subject preexperimental perceptions did not always coincide with actual experimental outcomes. Our work, like most controlled experimental analyses, is subject to caveats related to limited sample size and specific orderings examined. © 2004 Elsevier B V.A11 rights

## 1. Introduction

This paper is a continuation of the work described in Marsden, Pakath, and Wibowo [1,2]. In Ref. [1], we: (a) describe the motivation underlying our work in terms of prevailing technology trends and a review of the literature related to decision making under time pressure, induced value theory, ex-ante DSS evaluation, and ex-ante media evaluation; (b) describe the design and construction of a prototype experimental software platform (and an associated subject training application) to illustrate our ideas; and (c) describe how we assessed and refined these products to yield their final versions. We then parameterized the resultant final platform for use in the experiments reported in Ref. [2] where we also: (i) describe how we operationalize the concepts of explicit and implicit time pressure; (ii) describe and rationalize our experimental goal and design; and (iii) describe the construction of a performance-based reward/ penalty mechanism.

The focus of the analysis in Ref. [2] is to explain trends observed in the outcome measures pertaining to a single construct, the information presentation mode, both in an aggregate sense and in the context of the remaining two constructs—information presentation speed and information complexity level. This paper furthers the analysis initiated in Ref. [2] by a parallel examination of each of the latter two constructs in detail. The present analysis taken together with that in Ref. [2] enables a fuller understanding of the sometimes complex interactions between mode, speed, and complexity level.

The analysis offers partial insights into questions such as:

(a) for a given level of information complexity, which of the three communication speeds is the most effective?

(b) at which information complexity level does a given mode perform worst? and,

(c) which communication speed is the best overall?

As extensively discussed in Ref. [1], few prior studies simultaneously address the issues of ex-ante evaluation of support systems deployed in a timepressured, incentive-driven, multimedia setting. Often, technocrats design systems based on routinely used design practices (sometimes touted as <sup>b</sup>best practices<sup>Q</sup>), without adequate research into the true relative merits and demerits of design choices for the particular support task at hand. Likewise, much MIS research today (e.g., Refs. [3,4]) focuses exclusively on user perceptions of support system design and utility at the expense of other, less subjective insights. Our study points to the value of both objective measures of system utility (perhaps in addition to perceptive measures) and careful choice of design features rather than through de facto design.

The remainder of this paper is organized into six sections, generally paralleling those in Ref. [2] to facilitate cross-referencing. Further, Sections 2, 3, 4, 4.1 and 4.2 are summarized renderings of those in Ref. [2], intended as a quick recap to provide the necessary background for the material that follows. Section 2 summarizes the process used to determine three critical experimental time parameters. Section 3 summarizes the experimental goal and design. Section 4 presents the reward/penalty mechanism used in this study. In Section 5, we redefine the eight measures used in the study (Section 4.1), summarize the procedure used to aggregate experimental data (Section 4.2), and adapt the trends-based inferential procedure used as the primary analysis vehicle throughout this study for our present purposes (Section 4.3). We then apply this procedure to comprehend the roles played by speed and complexity level, as well as their interactions with one another and with mode, the construct previously extensively studied (Sections 4.4 and 4.5, respectively). Section 5 concludes the paper.

## 2. Establishing critical time parameters

Central to this work is the notion of induced time pressure and its operationalization. We created a timepressured decision-making setting through careful determination of three critical time bounds called the standard rule-learning time (SRLT), standard help time (SHT), and standard decision-making time (SDMT) and their use in conjunction with a suitably designed reward/penalty structure (described in Section 4). The time bounds were determined through three separate pretests using the same software platform and laboratory as used in the subsequent formal experimentation. Based on our analysis of the pretest data, we chose 3 min as the SRLT as this choice included approximately 95% of all rule-learning times recorded. We set SHT to 39 s as a very large proportion (82%) of observed help times fell within this time bound. With this choice of SHT, we set SDMT (with system help) at 39 s+3 s=42 s as 48% of the correct decisions made without system help during our pretests were made within 3 s.

## 3. The goal and design of the exploratory study

Our broad research goal addresses the following question:In our experimental setting, is there any evidence that the proposed symbolic language (Image) is at least as good as the conventional Audio and Text alternatives?

Three separate groups of subjects were each exposed to all three modes but in the following different sequences—Text (followed by), Image (followed by), Audio (i.e., TIA); Audio, Image, Text (AIT); and Image, Text, Audio (ITA). For each mode, subjects proceeded from a set of Level 1 problems to Level 2 problems to Level 3 problems. At each complexity level, subjects proceeded from a set of Normal speed problems to Slower speed problems to Faster speed problems. A subject assigned to a given mode sequence participated in three 2-h experimental sessions, at approximately the same time on 3 consecutive days. Within the 2-h time frame, we allowed three rule set displays in a particular mode, each followed by the presentation of two sample objects for classification. Thus, we have six experiments at each complexity level–speed combination for a given mode, for a total of 54 experiments in each session.

Our 27 member subject pool (nine subjects randomly assigned to each mode sequence) was comprised entirely of members drawn from the undergraduate population of the University of Kentucky. Each subject was first asked to appear for a training session to insure that all subjects were familiar with the environment, decision problem setting and experimental procedures. Subjects participated in the first session of their sequence on the same day, following a 15-min break. Prior to beginning the first session and after training, each subject was administered a short questionnaire that attempts to elicit preexperimental perceptions about the various modes, speeds, and complexities. We use the data gathered to conduct a comparative analysis of actual versus expected performance.

## 4.1. Measures used

We collected experimental data on eight specific measures for all subjects within each of several predefined categories of experiments (see Section 4.2 for a complete listing of categories). The measures used were:

(1) average earnings (AE): average of the monetary rewards earned (cents);

(2) decision accuracy rate (DAR): percentage of correct decisions;

(3) average decision time (ADT): average of the decision-making times (s);

(4) average rule-learning time (ART): average time (s) spent (presumably) learning the rules in a rule set;

(5) average number of rule reviews (ANR): average number of times a rule set was reviewed during rule learning;

(6) average number of sample reviews (ANS): average number of times that one or more of a sample object’s views were reviewed by a subject before confirming a final decision;

(7) average number of cancels (ANC): average number of times that a subject changed his/her mind before confirming a final decision; and

(8) average number of help uses (ANH): average number of times the help system was accessed before confirming a final decision.

The eight measures may be grouped as follows: AE, DAR, ADT, and ART may be viewed as <sup>b</sup>timeand-money<sup>Q</sup> measures that are primary measures of subject performance, whereas ANR, ANS, ANC, and ANH may be viewed as measures of <sup>b</sup>activity<sup>Q</sup> during the learning and decision-making phases (i.e., are secondary measures). Higher values for ANS, ANH, and/or ANC would tend to increase ADT. The resultant impact on earnings (AE) is dependent on the improvement, if any, in decision accuracy (DAR) relative to the increased decision time (ADT).

## 4.2. Aggregating experimental data

We used the Jonckheere–Terpstra procedure for comparing k independent samples to see whether we could collapse the independent TIA, AIT, and ITA observations, based on tests involving the four primary measures. A total of 108 separate hypotheses (corresponding to the 108 possible combinations of the 3 modes, 3 speeds, 3 complexity levels, and 4 primary measures) were tested. The tests suggest that highly localized ordering impacts are more likely present with Text. However, at a significance level of a=0.05, we fail to reject $H _ { 0 }$ in a majority of the cases—i.e., 58% of the Text cases, 86% for Audio, and 94% for Image. We therefore decided to collapse the data from the three sequences to create one large data set made up of 27 observations for each of the four primary measures, DAR, ADT, AE, and ART. Because the values corresponding to each of the 27 subjects are dependent observations, we analyzed these data using a trends analysis procedure that we devised.

## 4.3. Detecting trends in the observations

As in Ref. [2], our findings focus on a set of treatment breakdowns that allow us to focus on a single construct, say speed, in an aggregate sense and also in the context of one of the remaining two constructs, mode and complexity level. These treatment breakdowns are defined as follows:

We then created three intervals, described below, based on the average and standard deviation of a measure value (such as AE) for treatment breakdowns taken three at a time (such as (All-N, All-S, All-F) or (All-L1-N, All-L1-S, All-L1-F) and so on):

(i) Average measure value minus three standard deviations to average measure value minus one standard deviation;

(ii) Average measure value minus one standard deviation to average measure value plus one standard deviation; and

(iii) Average measure value plus one standard deviation to average measure value plus three standard deviations.

These intervals form the basis of our rankings. If two treatment breakdowns, say All-N and All-S, fall in the same category, then we rank them as equivalent. If not, we assign rankings in order of better performance. Our motivation for using the above approach derives from our lack of knowledge of the distribution of the measures.

## 4.4.2. Discussion

Prior to these findings, it was our conjecture that subject performance would be best at the Normal speed followed by the Slower and Faster speeds, in that order. This was based on the fact that the Audio–Normal combination was the base combination for generating all other mode–speed combinations. Very surprisingly, the Normal speed is the only speed that did not emerge as dominant in any one of these measures and, overall, is the least preferred speed.

The fact that the Slower class did consume more rule-learning time than the Faster class is to be expected. Even if a subject wished to move at a Faster rate, the system enforces limitations. Likewise, we find the fact that subjects performed fewer rule set reviews in this larger time span also intuitively appealing. Again, the speed imposes limitations that a subject may not be able to overcome even if he/she so desired. What is intriguing, however, is that neither of these factors appears to have really had any negative impacts on accuracy, speed, and earnings, suggesting that subjects were comfortable with these <sup>b</sup>constraints.<sup>Q</sup> This viewpoint is also supported by the fact that subjects were not excessively reliant on system help in making decisions, preferring instead to deliberate (considering the sample review and cancel-related orderings). A possible explanation for the Slower class doing as well as it did, overall, follows.

Unlike with everyday speech, in the case where the communicator is a computer, communication difficulties can be compounded unless special care is taken. For instance, in our experiments, a subject was unable to ask (or prevented from asking) for further clarifications, flow rate alterations, to <sup>b</sup>lip read<sup>Q</sup> (no visual support was made available), or make inferences based on <sup>b</sup>body language,<sup>Q</sup> voice intonation, and so on. Consequently, in our controlled setting, it appears that what we viewed as the more taxing Faster class was indeed so. However, it also appears that what we labeled as Normal was as taxing (and perhaps more so, for reasons described below) to our subjects as the class we deemed Faster.

## 4.4.3. Mode-based speed preferences

(Table 3 summarizes the experimental outcomes for the three modes.)

Text mode: In terms of the primary measures, the Slower class resulted in appreciably worse-than-Faster rule-learning times, better-than-Faster decision times, and the best accuracy rates and earnings. While Normal and Faster were commensurate in terms of the accuracy levels and earnings posted, rule-learning times were appreciably larger, and decision times were appreciably smaller with Normal.

The Slower class maintained its position in terms of the secondary measures as well. Subjects spent appreciably less time with Slower on rule set reviews. Both sample reviews and subject indecisivenessrelated measure values were appreciably better than with Faster, whereas help usage was appreciably greater. The Normal flow rate was as expensive as Faster in terms of preparation, yet as reliant as Slower on system help. Despite this reliance, earnings and accuracy rates, as mentioned earlier, were appreciably worse than with Slower. While subjects were surprisingly more self-reliant with the Faster flow rate, rule set reviews, sample reviews, and indecisiveness were all on the high side with this rate. Even so, decision times were longer, with no appreciable increase in returns in the forms of better earnings and better accuracy.

Thus, for Text, Slower resulted in appreciably better performance than Normal and Faster; Normal yielded better secondary measure performance than Faster.

Image mode: In the Image mode, the Slower speed class posted primary measure values that were identical to those for Text. The Faster class showed considerable improvement. While rule-learning time was still appreciably lower than Normal and Slower, it posted accuracy levels and earnings that were commensurate with that for Slower. Subjects tended to be more deliberate with the Faster flow rate than with Slower by taking more decision-making time. The Normal speed class turned out to be appreciably worse than Slower and Faster in regard to all four primary measures.

Turning to the activity measures, again, the Slower and Faster speeds appear to be better than the Normal class. The extent of rule set reviews, help usage, and subject indecisiveness were the least with Slower, while the number of sample reviews was the highest. The Faster rate required/permitted more rule set revisits but also enabled the smallest values for sample reviews, help usage, and subject indecisiveness. At Normal speed, subjects made as many rule set revisits as with Faster but were also considerably more indecisive than either of the other two modes.

To summarize, with Image, performance for both measures is appreciably worse for Normal than for Slower and Faster. In addition, while performance was best at Level 1 in terms of both measures, Level 3 resulted in primary measures commensurate with Level 2 with appreciably smaller secondary measure values.

Audio mode: With respect to the time-and-money measures, as with Text and Image, the Faster speed posted appreciably smaller rule-learning time than the other speeds. However, it was also appreciably worse than either one or both of the other two speeds in terms of decision times, decision accuracy rates, and earnings. The Normal speed class had its best showing here. While rule-learning time was appreciably larger than and decision times commensurate with Faster, accuracy and earnings were appreciably better. The Slower class is compatible with Normal in regards to rule-learning time but is appreciably better than Normal and Faster in regards to decision time. Consequently, even though the accuracy rate is not in the same class as with Normal speed, overall earnings were commensurate with that for Normal.

In regard to the secondary measures, the Slower class dominated with the least amounts of rule set revisits, help usage, and sample reviews. Subject indecisiveness with this speed, though, was appreciably greater than with the other two speeds. The Faster class painted just the opposite picture; whereas subject indecisiveness was appreciably less than for Slower, sample reviews, help usage, and rule set revisits were appreciably larger. The Normal speed class proved to be a via media; it was commensurate with Slower in terms of help use but as poor as Faster in the remaining measures.

Thus, for Audio, Normal and Slower are commensurate in primary terms, and Slower outshines Normal in secondary terms. Faster was not a contender from either perspective.

## 4.4.4. Discussion

The overall trends analysis in Table 2 indicated that Slower was the most preferred speed and Normal the least. In the preceding section, however, we see that Slower was not the sole best performer at all three modes and neither was Normal consistently the worst.

In aggregate terms of the primary measures, the Slower flow rate facilitates appreciably better performance than Normal and Faster for the Text mode. In the case of the Image mode, equally good performances are obtained at both the Slower and

Faster speed classes, while the same is true of both Slower and Normal for the Audio mode.

For all three modes, however, the Slower flow rate offers near-identical performance on each of the primary measures; it results in appreciably large preparatory times, relatively quick decision-making times, and relatively highest earnings. Accuracy rates are also the best in two of the three modes, Image and Text. Performances at the Faster and Normal rates are less robust as one moves from mode to mode. Thus, the Slower class is never dominated by the other two classes in any of the three modes insofar as the time-and-money measures are concerned. The same overall sense prevails when one considers the activity measures. The Slower class is at least as good as the best speed class in all three modes. Indeed, it is the overall best in both Text and Audio.

Observe that the Normal flow rate attains its best time-and-money standing in the Audio mode. Recall that we used the Normal speed of the Audio mode (i.e., what the pretest subjects regarded as Normal speech) as the basis for determining: (a) the Normal speeds for the Text and Image modes and (b) the Slower and Faster speeds for all three modes. It appears that the subjects preferred this <sup>b</sup>base case<sup>Q</sup> in the context of the Audio mode where it originated. However, using the speed of spoken words in Normal speech as the basis for determining the Normal speed rate for written Text and visual images appears to have resulted in speeds that were viewed as being undesirable by the subjects in the two modes.

One possible explanation that supports the above viewpoint is that, unlike the Audio mode, where the Normal speed class has real-world correspondence to what the subjects perceive as <sup>b</sup>Normal<sup>Q</sup> speech rate, the Normal class (as defined by us) in the Image and Text modes presumably had no real-world counterpart that the subjects could identify with. They were possibly encountering these Normal-Text and Normal-Image speeds for the first time in these experiments—and in these modes, the Normal class is not so Normal.

The negative impact of this unfamiliarity appears to be particularly pronounced in the case of the Image mode where Normal achieved its worst rankings. This, again, has a plausible explanation. The Image mode is a symbolic language with no counterpart, to our knowledge, in any other forms of everyday communication. Both Text and Audio used the familiar English language. Note that after the teething stage that occurs at Normal speed, performance with Image shows vast improvement at both Slower and Faster speeds.

## 4.4.5. Complexity level-based speed preferences

(Table 4 summarizes the experimental results for the three complexity levels.)

Complexity Level 1: In terms of both the primary and the secondary measures, subjects preferred the Faster speed class over Normal and Slower at the least complex level. This class resulted in the smallest rule learning and decision-making times but also offered the highest levels of accuracy and earnings. During the relatively smaller preparatory duration, subjects recorded an appreciably larger amount of rule set revisits but were also appreciably more conservative in regard to sample reviews, help usage, and cancellation. Thus, it appears that the speed enabled more reviews of preparatory material during a shorter preparatory duration partly due to the speed of this class and partly due to the relatively simpler nature of the material being studied. It appears that subjects mastered the rule set quickly and did not have to rely as much on deliberation in terms of sample reviews and cancellations nor have to rely on system help.

The Normal class was generally the opposite of Faster in most respects. Decision times were appreciably larger at this speed, while accuracy rates and earnings were appreciably worse. While rule-learning time was commensurate with Faster, the number of rule set reviews during this time was appreciably smaller. Subject indecision, sample reviews, and help usage were all appreciably larger at this speed. Normal was the least preferred speed at Level 1 complexity.

The Slower class emerged as the second most desirable in terms of both categories of measures at Level 1 complexity. While rule-learning time was appreciably larger with this speed, it was commensurate with Faster in decision-making time, with Normal in accuracy and with Faster, again, in terms of earnings. Thus, quicker, less accurate decisions resulted in this speed’s earnings being on par with that for the Faster class. While Slower takes appreciably more rule-learning time than either of the other speeds, the number of rule set reviews during the rule-learning time was commensurate with that for Normal speed and appreciably less than for Faster. Like Faster, subjects performed appreciably fewer sample reviews, but like Normal, the extent of indecisiveness and help usage were appreciably greater.

Overall, at Level 1, Faster yielded the best primary and secondary measures followed by Slower and then Normal.

Complexity Level 2: When subjects proceeded to the next complexity level, the Faster class plummeted in standing to become the least desirable. While subjects were highly active in terms of virtually all of the activity-related measures, performance in regard to the primary measures saw steep declines for decision times, accuracy levels, and earnings. This class continued to require the least amount of rule-learning time.

At this complexity level, the Slower speed class began to display measure values that were nearly identical to those for Faster at the previous level. While all four activity levels were comparatively smaller than for either one or both of the other two speeds, subjects took the least decision-making time and posted the highest accuracy levels and earnings with this speed. Rule-learning time, though, was appreciably larger than for the Faster class.

While the Normal class displayed activity levels commensurate with Faster at Level 1, this unfortunately resulted in critical primary measure values that were closer the Faster class at Level 2. While rulelearning and decision-making times were commensurate with those for the Slower class, earnings and accuracy levels were as poor as with Faster.

Overall, Slower yielded the best outcomes at Level 2.

Complexity Level 3: Slower continued to retain its dominant position when subjects proceeded to the Level 3 experiments. However, to maintain this position, subjects became appreciably more active than at Level 2. Rule-learning times were still appreciably larger than for Faster, and the number of rule set reviews were appreciably smaller than for Normal and Faster. However, sample reviews, cancellations, and help usage were appreciably larger.

While the Faster class continued to do as poorly as it did at Level 2 for the primary measures, a shift in activity levels is discernible. Subjects seem to have to have continued to struggle with rule set reviews but essentially appear to have given up on any other form of activity. The number of sample reviews, cancellations, and help usage are the smallest for the Faster class.

Likewise, while the Normal class continued its poor showing for the primary measures, there is also an appreciable increase in terms of the activity levels. Thus, subjects appear to have tried quite hard but were unable to do well.

Slower produced the best outcomes in primary terms at Level 3, while, for the activity measures, Faster yielded better outcomes than Slower and Slower better than Normal.

## 4.4.6. Discussion

Recall that all subjects proceeded from Level 1 to Level 2 experiments and then to Level 3 experiments. Within each level, they encounter the Normal speed experiments first, followed by the Slower, and then the Faster classes. At Level 1, activity levels are high with Normal speed, and these levels taper off as we proceed to Slower and Faster. At the same time, however, performance on the primary measures improves as subjects move from speed to speed. It appears that either the two relatively Slower speeds are an impediment to performance at this level or that subjects have grown more proficient with increased experience with Level 1 problems.

At Level 2, while speed drops back to Normal again, subjects, fresh from their increasingly positive experience at Level 1, appear to have put in less effort than necessary given the greater complexity of the material. They appear to have continued this behavior at the Slower speed but with appreciably greater success. At the Faster class, one discerns a spurt in subject activity levels as they grapple with the added complexity induced by increased speed. However, they do as nearly as poorly as they did with the Normal class, despite the increased activity.

Upon encountering Level 3 experiments, again at the relatively reduced Normal speed, subjects continued to remain highly active. Yet, the added complexity and their unfamiliarity with this complexity seem to have prevailed. The primary measure values are relatively at the same levels that they were at Level 1 and Level 2 with Normal speed. Upon moving to the Slower speed experiments, next, the same high levels of activity are maintained with positive rewards. Earnings, accuracy levels, and decision times are the best amongst all Level 3 experiments. It appears that the sustained high levels of activity over the prior three level-speed combinations had left the subjects exhausted. They seem to have quit completely upon encountering the Faster speed experiments. In particular, help usage, which had been at relatively high levels over the last three sessions, now drops to an appreciably lower level. Performance, in terms of the primary measures, also stays as low as it was at Level 2.

These findings support the intuitively appealing idea that more complex material ought not to be conveyed at higher flow rates. The Normal rate, the rate that subjects first encounter at all three complexity levels, shows sustained lukewarm performance throughout. The Faster speed class quickly fizzles as complexity levels increase. The Slower class appears to be the most appealing as complexity increases.

## 4.5.3. Mode-based complexity level preferences

(Table 6 summarizes the experimental outcomes for the three modes.)

Text mode: Performances in terms of the primary measures were the best at Level 1 complexity and progressively deteriorated as subjects proceeded to Levels 2 and 3. The same is true of activity levels. All four activity measures were at their relative minimums at Level 1. As subjects proceeded to Level 2, rule-learning time and rule set revisits during this time showed appreciable increases. While sample review and help usage remained commensurate with Level 1, subjects displayed increased levels of indecision during a decision-making time period that was also commensurate with that for Level 1.

Despite the added preparation time and effort, both accuracy and earnings levels dropped appreciably below those for Level 1. Activity levels were even higher at Level 3. Subjects tended to increase their level of sample information reviews and increased their dependence on system help. They tended to be less indecisive than at Level 2. Yet, performance in regard to the primary measures dropped to its lowest level.

Overall, Level 1 yields better outcomes than Level 2, which in turn yields better outcomes than Level 3 in both categories of measures.

Image mode: Performances in terms of the primary measures were the best at Level 1 complexity and progressively deteriorated as subjects proceeded to Levels 2 and 3. The same is true of activity levels. All four activity measures were at their relative minimums at Level 1. As subjects proceeded to Level 2, rulelearning time and rule set revisits during this time showed appreciable increases. While sample review and help usage remained commensurate with Level 1, subjects displayed increased levels of indecision during a decision-making time period that was also commensurate with that for Level 1. Despite the added preparation time and effort, both accuracy and earnings levels dropped appreciably below those for Level 1. Activity levels were even higher at Level 3. Subjects tended to increase their level of sample information reviews and increased their dependence on system help. They tended to be less indecisive than at Level 2. Yet, performance in regard to the primary measures dropped to its lowest level.

Overall, Level 1 yields the best outcomes in both measure categories while Level 3Vs outcomes dominate Level 2’s in activity terms.

Audio mode: Performances with the Audio mode in terms of the four primary measures are the same as with the Text mode at each of the three complexity levels. Thus, Level 1 performance is the best, followed by Level 2 and Level 3, in that order. The activity related findings are also the same as for Text at Level 1 complexity. At Level 2, subjects are more active than they were with Text; their activity pattern is similar to that with Image at Level 2. At Level 3, subjects tended to become relatively more indecisive than at Level 2, while being commensurate with Level 2 in terms of the remaining activity measures.

Overall, as with Text, for both categories of measures, Level 1 yields better results than Level 2, which yields better results than Level 3.

## 4.5.4. Discussion

Upon examining the above pictures pertaining to the three modes, one intriguing fact emerges. While primary measure-related performance is identical for Text and Audio, where Level 1 is the most preferred and Level 3 the least, that for Image is different. Here, Level 3 performance while commensurate with that for Level 2 is actually marginally better in decision time, earnings, and accuracy levels. At the same time, Level 3 is no worse than Level 2 in terms of effort; indeed, it is marginally better. This lends credence to our earlier observation that, perhaps, Image is better suited with increasingly complex material, provided compact, holistic symbols could replace information that must otherwise be gathered through sequentially processed words. This is especially intriguing when one also considers that our subjects are least fluent in Image and our three mode sequence arrangements (TIA, ITA, and AIT) collectively offer comparatively less experience with the other modes before a subject encounters Image.

## 4.5.5. Speed-based complexity level preferences

(Table 7 summarizes the experimental results for the three complexity levels.)

Normal: The best performance in terms of both primary and secondary measures was realized at Level 1. As subjects proceeded to Levels 2 and 3, there was a decline in both types of measures. While subjects displayed higher levels of indecision and relatively less reliance on system help at Level 1, subjects made heavier use of system help and appeared less uncertain at Level 2. They also spent appreciably larger amounts of time on rule learning and made more rule set revisits at Level 2. Yet, the relatively higher activity level did not prevent performance loss in primary measure terms. Barring decision-making time, Level 2 was appreciably worse than Level 1 in both earnings and accuracy. Level 3 was by far the most undesirable scenario. It resulted in the least preferred rankings for all four primary measures and in two of the secondary measures—rule set revisits and subject indecision were both marginally better than at Level 2 and Level 1, respectively.

Overall, in both primary and secondary measure terms, Level 1 resulted in better performance at Normal speed than Level 2, which in turn yielded somewhat better performance than Level 3.

Slower: At the Slower speed, both the primary and secondary measure rankings at Level 1 were identical to those at Normal speed, and the best performance occurred at this level. At Level 2, the activity-related measure rankings were identical to those for Level 2 at Normal speed. However, the time-and-money measure rankings reflect a further decline due to the appreciably larger decision-making time at Level 2 compared to Level 1. The primary measure rankings at Level 3 were identical to those for the Normal speed. The secondary rankings, however, reflect a smaller, more desirable rank for the number of rule set revisits (which is intuitively appealing given the Slower throughput at Slower speed).

Overall, Level 1 yielded better outcomes than Level 2 and Level 3 in terms of both primary and secondary measures.

Faster: At the Faster speed, primary measure rankings for Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 were all identical to those at the Slower speed. Activity rankings for Level 1 were better than those at the Normal and Slower speeds as subjects proved to be appreciably less indecisive at this level with Faster speed. At Level 2, activity rankings were worse than at the Slower and Normal speeds as the number of sample reviews and cancellations both reflect an increase over Level 1 at Faster speed. At Level 3, the rankings for the activity measures are somewhat better than at Level 2 as due to reduced levels of indecision.

Again, Level 1 yielded better outcomes than Level 2 and Level 3 for both measure categories. For secondary measures, Level 2 yielded the lowest measurements.

## 4.5.6. Discussion

At all three speeds, Normal, Slower, and Faster, the best time-and-money performances were recorded with the Level 1 class problems followed by the Level 2 and the Level 3 classes. While Level 2 was marginally preferable to Level 3 at Normal speed, no appreciable differences between these two levels were detected at the Slower and Normal speeds. Activity measures also reflected a general preference for Level 1 over Level 2 and Level 2 over Level 3, except at the Faster speed. Overall, these preferences are very similar to what we detected for the immediately preceding mode-based analysis.

In our experimental design, at all three complexity levels, a subject has relatively the least amount of cumulative prior experience when beginning a Normal class experiment and the most when beginning a Faster class experiment. Despite the fact that there are differences in experience levels between the three speed classes, in all three cases, performance at the Level 1 class is superior to that at the Level 2 class, which is marginally superior to that at the Level 3 class. Experience differences with various speed levels do not appear to have caused any differences in performance while tackling problems belonging to the different complexity levels.

## 5. Concluding remarks

This paper extends the analysis in Ref. [2] to the two constructs that were left not fully addressed in that study—information communication speed and information complexity level. We summarize key findings here. Our analysis reveals the following aggregate picture. Overall, the primary measures indicated a preference for Slower over Faster and Faster over Normal with no discernible trend for the secondary measures. We also noted an increase in activity levels (i.e., the secondary measures) with increasing complexity and a decline in the time-andmoney measures beyond Level 1. From the perspective of individual modes, we perceived the following trends. For the Text mode, the Slower flow rate resulted in appreciably better performance for both types of measures with Normal outdoing Faster in secondary terms as well. Subjects performed at their best with Level 1 problems and their worst with Level 3 in both time-and-money and activity terms. With Image, performance for both measures is appreciably worse for the Normal flow rate. Whereas Level 1 Image mode problems elicited the best results for both measure types, Level 3 activity levels were appreciably smaller than those for Level 2. In the case of Audio, subjects performed equally well at both the Slower and Normal speeds for the primary measures but Slower overshadowed Normal in terms of activity levels. As with Text, subjects preferred the less complex settings to the more complex ones in both primary and secondary terms. Our findings for Speed and Complexity Level in the context of each other are as follows. At Level 1 complexity, the Faster class was the most preferred, and Slower dominates Normal in terms of both types of measures. The Slower class was the most preferred at Level 2 for both measure types. While this was also true of the primary measures at Level 3, the activity measures favored Faster over Slower and Slower over Normal. At Normal speed, the less complex levels were preferred over the more complex ones for both measure categories. While this preference is also true for Slower in secondary measure terms, subjects showed no preference for Level 2 over Level 3 in primary terms. This indifference in primary terms also manifested itself at the Faster speed. We next contrast subject preexperimental expectations with actual experimental outcomes in terms of decision accuracy (i.e., DAR) and decision-making speed (i.e., ADT). Subjects stated before the experiment that in terms of both of these primary measures, they would do best with Slower speed and worst with Faster problems. Also, they felt that their best performance would occur with Level 1 problems and their worst with Level 3.

As may be gleaned from the above synopsis of our findings, two interesting overall insights emerge. First, whereas real-world systems are often designed based on prescriptive tradition, including <sup>b</sup>best practice<sup>Q</sup> criteria widely disseminated and adopted by practitioners without demonstrated benefits to users, in our study, we see that performance at any level of a particular construct was typically sensitive to the levels set for the remaining two attributes. Thus, our findings suggest that adopting de facto system design practices as a matter of course may not be in the best interests of the consumer. Second, despite the fact that many experimental studies (e.g., 3, 4) continue to emphasize subject perception over more objective performance measures, in our study, we found that subject pre-experimental perceptions did not always coincide with actual experimental outcomes. Thus, it appears important for MIS researchers to emphasize non-perceptive measures of system utility along with the perceptive.

Finally, we must note that our findings may be sensitive to the fixed order in which subjects were exposed to the three speeds and the three complexity levels. Controlled laboratory experiments provide an important tool for analyzing the issues of interest, but the experiments are a costly tool. Though we feel the experimental results reported here are worthy of note, they are only a beginning in a thorough investigation of important issues related to the performance of alternative visual languages. We concluded [1] by addressing several possible research extensions to this preliminary effort. Given our current experimental findings, we are fine tuning follow-up rounds of experimentation to investigate the possible ordering sensitivity and to investigate further refinements and comparisons of speed levels, image types, and complexity levels. As we move forward, our research is being directed toward two critical questions: (i) will initial encouraging results continue across task domains and (ii) is it possible to structure alternative visual languages that are functionally effective in today’s miniature display environments?

Ram’s research articles have appeared in such refereed forums as Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, Information and Management, and Information Systems Research. He is author of the book Business Support Systems: An Introduction published by Copley. Ram has also contributed refereed material to a number of well-known books including Handbook of Industrial Engineering, Multimedia Technology and Applications, and Operations Research and Artificial Intelligence. Ram served as Director of the MIS Research Laboratory of the College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, from 1993 to1997. He is an Associate Editor for Decision Support Systems and an Editorial Board Member of Journal of End User Computing and Management.

Kustim Wibowo is an Associate Professor in the MIS and Decision Sciences Department of the Eberly College of Business and Information Technology, Indiana University of Pennsylvania. He received his PhD in MIS from the University of Kentucky. Dr. Wibowo also holds an MS in Computer Science from Baylor University. His current research interests include e-commerce and web security. information systems for educational technology, human resource information systems, and OLAP (online Analytical Processing). He is a co-founder of ABIT (Academic of Business and Information Technology), and currently, he is the editor of JBIT (Journal of Business and Information Technology.).
