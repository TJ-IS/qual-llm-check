## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online knowledge communities Knowledge contribution Usefulness voting Commenting

## A B S T R A C T

Participants are increasingly using online knowledge communities to access and share information and to col: laboratively solve problems. However, in online communities that address technical questions (i.e., utilitarian, rather than hedonic or supportive, in nature), a key problem is encouraging suficient, ongoing knowledge contribution. While many may use the community to post and obtain information periodically, fewer take the time to consistently contribute knowledge to the community. Interestingly, research has yet to comprehensively consider the impact of motivational afordances, in the forms of voting and commenting features, to address this challenge of under-contribution in online knowledge communities. To fill this research gap, this study proposes a research framework that integrates consideration of usefulness voting and the collaboration tool of commenting to explain variation in individuals' online knowledge contribution behaviors. The research model is estimated with a fixed-efects Poisson regression applied to a longitudinal panel dataset collected from a technical online question and answer community. We find that positive votes motivate participants' knowledge contributions, while received negative votes significantly decrease their knowledge contributions. Thus, while maintaining content quality is important, negative motivational afordances (i.e., down voting) can reduce sustained contributions. We also find commenting to be an important motivator for online knowledge contribution and, interestingly, find that comments moderate the relationships between voting and knowledge contribution. Overall, this research not only extends our current understandings of knowledge contribution in online com munities, but also sheds light on improving online community knowledge exchange.

## 1. Introduction

As a social media platform for crowdsourcing and collaborative problem solving, online knowledge communities can aggregate dis tributed information and knowledge, thus empowering participants via access to a vast amount of readily available knowledge and expertise. By participating in online communities, people can communicate, learn, and collaborate eficiently, as knowledge search and acquisition costs are reduced [1–4]. The sustainability of such benefits is condi tional, though, on the continued voluntary contribution of participants. When not all participants contribute suficiently to online communities, particularly in those focused primarily on utilitarian knowledge building and sharing in the technical context (versus more hedonically or emotionally supportive communities), the sustainability of such communities may be at risk. A well-known dilemma of the “tragedy of commons” [5] applies to online knowledge communities in that many participants tend to free ride or under-contribute rather than continually invest in the online knowledge base that is publicly and freely accessible by everyone. For instance, participants who rarely post make up a large majority of online community participants [6,7]. A key challenge then is that if knowledge contributions atrophy, the usefulness of the online community may be questioned, potentially resulting in a destructive cycle of less participation and subsequent membership decline. Consequently, incentivizing users to contribute is one of the biggest challenges for the sustainability of such online communities [8].

To address the research and design challenge of under-contribution in online communities, we evaluate incentives, in the form of motivational afordances, to contribute to an online community that impact the intrinsic motivations (self-determination) of the participants. Motivational afordances, which are properties of an object that moti vate human behaviors, have attracted the attention of both practi tioners and researchers as a means of motivating user engagement in contexts such as training and education [9], marketing service [10], group collaboration [11], and online social media [12,13]. Online communities in particular can benefit from using features of motivational afordances to attract and maintain active contributors. Further, consideration of motivational afordances designed into online communities allows a more contextualized and comprehensive understanding of the antecedents that stimulate continued use.

In this study, we incorporate the perspectives of motivational affordances and self-determination theory to investigate the motivational efects of two important online community design features – usefulness voting and commenting – on knowledge contribution. To start with, usefulness of a knowledge contribution refers to the extent to which the contribution is perceived by individuals to solve the problem. Usefulness voting allows online peers to assess the efort and contribution by a participant, thus rewards or penalties may be granted to the contributor. The purpose of such usefulness votes is not only to assess the quality of knowledge contribution, but to attract and maintain the active engagement of participants. We argue that usefulness voting can signal the competence of the contributor, thus being an important motivator for the participant's continued voluntary knowledge contribution. Although motivational afordances are generally believed to be beneficial in encouraging user engagement, there is a lack of research using theoretical frameworks to explain the mechan isms that can afect user participation [14,15].

Commenting features ofered in online knowledge communities are also an important motivational afordance for incentivizing collaborative problem solving. Specifically, a knowledge contributor can receive feedback in the form of textual comments from peers regarding the problems, issues, or suggestions on her/his knowledge contribution. Those comments can help contributors to further refine and improve their contributions. Commenting as a form of online feedback has been studied by previous research in various contexts such as online marketplaces [16], corporate social media platforms [17], and online news communities [18]. However, to the best of our knowledge, research has yet to integrate both voting and commenting mechanisms to comprehensively understand knowledge contribution behavior in utilitarian online communities. Therefore, we ask:

RQ: How do motivational afordances, in the forms of voting and commenting features, impact participants' contribution to online knowledge communities?

To address this question, we first review the extant literature and set forth the theoretical background of the study. Then, the proposed research model and hypotheses are presented. We then test the proposed model empirically by applying econometric models to a rich dataset collected from a community-based technical question and answer site. Lastly, we discuss how our study contributes to theory development and seeks to improve our understanding of continued user contribution in online knowledge communities.

To address this gap, we develop a research model based on motivational afordances and self-determination theory.

## 2.1. Motivational afordances

An afordance describes the general actionable properties of an object that can satisfy individuals' particular needs [26]. An afordance is a combination of actual and perceived properties of a thing that determines how it would be used in a particular context [27]. The general principle of motivational afordances in information systems (IS) design is that motivational afordances can invoke intrinsic moti vations (i.e., self-determination), such as interest and enjoyment, that further promote behavioral outcomes such as continued engagement and contribution [28]. The design and management of an IS with high motivational afordance has motivated several IS studies in contexts such as corporate wikis [29], gamified app design [30], digital health communities [31], and group collaboration systems [11]. In addition, Zhang [32] suggests a set of IS design principles that are based on five motivational sources and needs including: (1) psychological: autonomy and self; (2) cognitive: competence and achievement; (3) social and psychological: relatedness; (4) social and psychological: leadership and followership; and (5) emotional: afect and emotion.

Interestingly, according to afordance theory, the same technological capability (i.e., actual or intended afordance) could be used very diferently by diferent users as they perceive the capability (i.e., perceived afordance) in diferent ways [33]. For example, reputation scores may be viewed by some users as a kind of achievement, while other users could regard them as a sense of recognition of their ex pertise. Therefore, to understand the motivational mechanisms under lying an IS design requires that researchers apply an afordance perspective, rather than merely focusing on particular technological features [34]. In this study, we adopt the perspective of afordances to explore the roles of usefulness voting and commenting mechanisms in motivating participant's contribution behavior in the context of online knowledge communities.

## 2.2. Self-determination theory and knowledge contribution

As a motivation-based theoretical framework, self-determination theory helps us to understand how online knowledge community participants perceive the motivational afordances provided by the usefulness voting and commenting features and how such perceptions would lead to behavioral outcomes that benefit the participants as well as the community as a whole. Self-determination theory focuses on social-contextual conditions that promote versus undermine the process of self-motivation, social functioning, and positive human potentials [35,36]. Deci and Ryan [37] identify three basic psychological needs – the needs for competence, relatedness, and autonomy – that drive ongoing motivations for individual growth and integration, social development, and personal well-being [36]. Self-determination theory diferentiates two most basic types of motivations: (1) intrinsic motivation pertaining to principal sources of inherent satisfaction and enjoyment, and (2) extrinsic motivation describing the inclination to do something for separate consequence. Intrinsic motivation is most applicable to explain why some participants are proactive and engaged in online communities where there are seldom tangible economic benefits or compensation for the time and efort spent by participants. As maintaining and sustaining intrinsic motivation requires supportive conditions, the theory of intrinsic motivation concerns various conditions that facilitate intrinsic motivation, rather than what causes thi inclined propensity [36].

Previous studies have utilized self-determination theory to explain why people contribute knowledge in various contexts. For example, Yoon and Rolland [25] find that perceived competence and perceived relatedness are positively associated with knowledge sharing behavior in virtual communities. Suh and Wagner [34] find that gamified motivational afordance implemented in an enterprise collaboration system adds hedonic value, which in turn motivates knowledge contribution quantity and quality by the users. Therefore, we view moti vation afordances (i.e., usefulness voting and comment, in this study) as motivators that provide conditions under which intrinsic motivation develops.

## 3. Research model and hypotheses

Based on the theoretical background discussed above, we put forth a research model to guide our intellectual exploration of the relationship between motivational afordances within online communities and knowledge contributions by participants. As shown in Fig. 1, the proposed research framework integrates usefulness voting and commenting mechanisms to explain variation in individual online knowledge contribution behavior. In summary, the motivational afordance ofered by positive voting is hypothesized to be positively associated with online contribution behavior, while negative voting is hypothesized to be negatively related to knowledge contribution. Peer feedback in the form of online comments is hypothesized to promote individual knowledge contribution. In addition, received online comments are hypothesized to moderate the efects of voting on individuals' online contribution behavior. The rationale for the proposed research model is explained further below.

## 3.1. Usefulness voting as motivational afordances

Motivational afordances in the context of online knowledge communities that depend on participants' voluntary contribution by themselves are intrinsic as they usually have no tangible economic benefits. The signaling function of voting mechanism in online communities helps participants to evaluate the trustworthiness of their peers and the reliability of their solutions/advices. The irredeemable usefulness votes gained from online contribution can be internalized into positive psychological outcomes such as enjoyment, feeling of competence, and recognition of abilities, thus driving participants' continued engagement and contribution in the online community. The number of positive usefulness votes received from peers indicates the original contributor's eforts and expertise in related problem domains, thus enhancing the contributor's perceived competence, self-eficacy [38], sense of community, and obligation, and subsequently promoting more contributions to the online community. Deci et al. [39] argue that positive feedback or rewards that strengthen these feelings increase intrinsic motivation, whereas punishment or negative feedback that weakens these feelings reduces intrinsic motivation. Both the signaling and intrinsic motivational roles of usefulness voting suggest that positive usefulness votes obtained from online communities work as catalysts for participants' continuance of knowledge contribution, while critical negative usefulness votes undermine the sense of perceived competence and hence intrinsic motivation [40]. ## 3.2. Efects of commenting

Social interaction in the form of commenting among online peers is another important factor that leads to the success of an online community. The diference between usefulness voting and commenting is that the former is mediated by the reward/reputation algorithm embedded in the online community whereas the latter is a channel for communication and collaborative problem solving. In general, com munication, particularly in work environments, has been considered by psychologists as positively or negatively reinforcing of one's own views about their self-worth, ability to successfully contribute to a group, and an individual's ability to succeed (e.g., [41]). Thus, in the online context we would also expect that communication about the veracity of claims (i.e., whether or not a recommended solution is viewed as helpful or viable) to impact the focal participant's view of their own contributions and, ultimately, their motivation for continuing to contribute. While extant research generally suggests that social interaction is one im portant motivation for participants to contribute in online communities [42–44], studies have not yet fully explored the motivational nature of comments in reinforcing contribution in the online context. This study specifically focuses on the commenting mechanism of social interac tions in the context of online knowledge communities.

We start by considering the quantity of comments received, as this may be viewed as a proxy for the relative worth of the post (i.e., more comments mean that more people are interested in what one has to say). The number of comments received can indicate the degree of connectedness and the extent of social interaction of the focal participant in the online communities. Individual participants receiving more comments from peers feel a higher level of connectivity with peers and a greater sense of belonging to the online community [45]. The importance of online feedback in the form of textual comments to guide and motivate efective behavior has been addressed by previous research. Pavlou and Dimoka [16] suggest that textual comment feedback helps build a buyer's trust in a seller's credibility and benevolence in an online marketplace setting. Brzozowski et al. [17] find that feedback in posted comments is highly associated with participants' subsequent engagement in a company-wide social media platform. Burke et al. [18] find that receiving feedback from peers promotes newly joined participants of an online news community to involve more in ongoing participation. Consequently, we expect that:

H3. Participants receiving more comments from online peers are more likely to contribute in online knowledge communities.

## 3.3. Moderating efects of commenting

We next consider how motivational afordances – usefulness voting and commenting – interact. Although the usefulness voting mechanism is a strong motivational afordance ofered by the online knowledge community, usefulness votes that participants receive from peers would not be as meaningful without comments describing why the posters feel a certain way, especially for those supplying negative votes. Although comments on answers submitted to the online knowledge community are mainly provided for the contributors to further improve and refine their answers, receiving a large number of comments on a participant's answers often indicates the insuficient quality of her/his answers. For instance, a few comments received may help provide additional context or nuance. But, significant debates, with many comments, are often indicative of topics where opinions are polarized and the statement or question originally posted either being intentionally vague, so as to spark debate, or ill-informed, which results in significant discussions. In sum, such comments often signal the mismatch of the participant's knowledge level (or degree of focus and specificity) and that required by the online knowledge community. Since knowledge contribution is strongly determined by an individual's willingness to engage in online interactions [24], this mismatch can invoke the feeling of being detached from the community and decrease the individual's willingness to further engage in the knowledge community, thereby weakening the process of internalizing positive usefulness votes received into intrinsic motivations such as self-eficacy and recognition of personal expertise that promote continued knowledge contribution. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H4. Participants' received comments moderate the efect of positive votes on knowledge contribution in such a way that the efect is less positive when the participant receives a larger number of comments.

On the other hand, comments provide meaningful reasons why the participant receives the negative votes. By reading through the com ments on answers submitted to the community, the original contributor can better understand the detailed problems or issues in the original answers. With more comments provided by online peers, social interactions become more meaningful. What matters is not only the result of the knowledge exchange (i.e., problems solved), but also the collaborative and educational process. Such comments, even though accompanied with negative usefulness votes, not only help the con tributors to learn new knowledge and improve their knowledge level, but also increase their feeling of the usefulness of online interactions, collaborating spirits embedded in the community, friendliness of peers, and belonging to the community. Thus, received comments, particularly when they are constructive, can mitigate the destructive efect of negative votes on knowledge contribution. Consequently, we expect that:

H5. Participants' received comments moderate the efect of negative votes on knowledge contribution in such a way that the efect is less negative when the participant receives a larger number of comments.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Research setting and data collection

A rich dataset was collected from a community-based question and answer (Q&A) site for user experience designers and IT specialists. Unlike other online communities that are focusing on topics such as social support $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ online health communities), microblogging (e.g., Twitter, Tumblr), social networking (e.g., Facebook), and customer review (e.g., Amazon), the Q&A site is a dedicated online community for knowledge exchange. Participants participate in the online interaction in the Q&A site with a purpose to solve their own problems or to share knowledge and help other peers. Thus, this Q&A site is a suitable setting for testing our proposed research model of contribution in online knowledge communities. Previous research has empirically in vestigated similar knowledge contribution behaviors in the context of Q &A sites (e.g., [21,46,47]).

The website requires participants to register to ask questions, provide answers, comments, or votes on user experience topics. The website applies afordance elements including usefulness voting and commenting to incentivize participants to ask and answer questions [4,12]. A typical online interaction on the site begins with a participant sub mitting a question on a particular topic, followed by comments, answers, and votes posted by other participants. Fig. 2 shows a typical scenario of knowledge exchange in the website. Specifically, participants in the community can vote on the usefulness of answers. A positive vote or negative vote indicates whether the posted answer is considered as useful by the voter. This online community uses a Wikilike crowdsourcing approach to encourage virtual collaboration and evaluation among peers. For any answers or questions posted by a participant, other participants can make comments for the purpose of improving knowledge contribution or refining questions asked. Participants also earn badges and reputation points based on certain criteria related to their activities and contributions.

This dataset contains 22,637 questions and 61,554 answers posted from September 2008 to March 2017. This allows the current study to measure theoretically interesting variables and extract meaningful insights from the detailed and contextualized user-generated content. Fig. 3 shows total monthly activities in the online community.

We excluded data before August 2010 to avoid the potential effect of the initiation of the online community. After eliminating the last period (March 2017) which contains incomplete data, we obtained 79 months of data from August 2010 to February 2017. In this study, we tested our proposed research model by using fixed-efects Poisson regression model that requires individuals to have at least two observations and post at least one answer in the online knowledge community.

After removing participants with only one observation or no answer posted, we obtained an unbalanced longitudinal panel dataset containing 2813 individuals and 46,470 observations.

## 4.3. Model specification and estimation

Our explanation of the main efects is based on Poisson fixed-efects Model 4, which includes all independent variables and controls for time efects. As shown in column 4 of Table 4, positive votes are positively associated with knowledge contribution (p-value < 0.01), whereas negative votes undermine an individual's knowledge contribution (pvalue < 0.01). We also found that the destructive efect of negative usefulness votes is more detrimental than the motivating efect of positive usefulness votes (p-value < 0.01). This result shows the existence of a general psychological principle of negativity bias [59], which contends that people tend to give greater weight to negative entities. The more comments received by the individual from other community members on her/his answers, the more likely the individual contributes to the community (p-value < 0.01). Interestingly, in regard to the control variables, we found that the number of bronze badges granted to the participant is positively associated with the individual's knowledge contribution (p-value < 0.01), whereas the earning of relatively high level silver badges has an un expected negative efect on knowledge contribution (p-value < 0.01). And, granting gold badges to the participant does not have a significant efect on the user's online knowledge contribution. One possibility is that the dificult-to-obtain gold and silver badges lead the recipients to over-evaluate their self-eficiency and competences. The strong feeling of satisfaction and achievement may undermine an individual's intention to contribute. This finding conforms to an empirical study by Schuckert et al. [60] that users with high-level badges tend to contribute less to an online hotel review site. Additionally, reputation score is found to be non-significantly associated with the knowledge contribution, keeping other factors constant. This finding is in contrast to those of previous studies in electronic networks of practice [61] and virtual communities [42]. Bock and Kim [62] argue that rewards, like punishment, may actually undermine intrinsic motivation under specific situations. It would be an interesting topic for future study to investigate why such non-significant or negative efects exist for badges and reputation scores in online knowledge communities.

The results also indicate that the number of comments posted by the participant is positively and significantly associated with her/his behavior of knowledge contribution, suggesting that active participation in the online community in the form of commenting is an important predictor of online contribution.

## 5.2. Robustness checks

We assessed the robustness and qualitative consistency of our findings by using diferent estimation methods and an alternative de finition of knowledge contribution, as summarized in Table 6. First, we applied random-efects Poisson model to estimate the relationships. As shown in columns 1 and 2 of Table 6, the results are consistent with our main analysis as shown in Table 4. Then we applied a random-efect negative binomial model and the results of robustness check shown in Table 6 columns 3 and 4 are highly consistent with our main analysis, except that the moderating efect of received comments on the efect of negative votes on knowledge contribution is found insignificant. We also assessed the robustness by defining knowledge contribution as the focal participant's posted answers that have been accepted by the question poster. In other words, this definition assumes simply posting answers may not be true knowledge contribution, but rather that knowledge contribution occurs when answers are accepted by the participant who asked the question. Knowledge contribution quality is a multi-dimensional construct. Previous empirical studies have measured knowledge quality from aspects such as relevance, accuracy, completeness, easiness of use, reliability, and timeliness [21,63]. This alternative definition of knowledge contribution ensures that the knowledge contributed by the focal participant meets the quality standards such as relevance, accuracy, and completeness [21,63]. Thus, the alternative measure considers not only the quantity of knowledge contribution, but also the quality or helpfulness of that knowledge [61]. Table 6 columns 5 and 6 show that all coeficient estimates retain the same direction and significance as those of models 4 and 5 shown in Table 4, suggesting that our findings are robust to this alternative, and more conservative, knowledge contribution definition.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Theoretical implications

The proposed research model is among the first to explain how online community features including usefulness voting and commenting impact knowledge contribution in utilitarian online knowledge communities. This study extends the current literature on knowledge contribution in online communities by providing evidence that online community design features are important afordances to motivate knowledge contribution behavior. The empirical results reveal that received positive and negative votes are significantly associated with participants' contribution behavior. This confirms the value of widely applied positive voting in promoting the reinforcing feeling of interest and enjoyment in online communities, thus motivating participants continued engagement and contribution. The usefulness votes that an individual receives from community peers are indicative of her/his expertise, competence, and the extent of past contributions. We argue that received positive votes work as a signaling function that helps the participant to build and enhance self-eficacy, sense of community, and obligation or responsibility, subsequently promoting more contribu tions to the online community, whereas negative usefulness votes are largely considered to be destructive to perceived self-eficacy and subsequently demotivating intention to contribute. Our data analysis provides evidence that negative usefulness votes undermine online participants' knowledge contribution. This finding empirically confirms the general IS design principle suggested by Zhang [32] that timely and positive feedback needs to be provided to satisfy user's need for com petence. Such IS design principle, although not equally applicable to all types of IS artifacts, has not been suficiently tested in online communities. In addition, this study provides evidence for the existence of negativity bias [59] in the setting of online knowledge communities where the destructive efect of negative usefulness voting on knowledge contribution behavior is more detrimental than the motivating efect of positive usefulness voting. Thus, this study also bridges and extends the current literature on online communities and IS design by showing the imperativeness of applying general IS design principles to guide the design of online community features.

This study has also investigated the impact of feedback in the form of textual comments provided by online peers on the recipients' contribution behavior. The results demonstrate that the commenting channel provided by online knowledge communities actually changes participants' contribution behavior. This research contributes by showing that motivational afordance in the form of textual comments is an important motivator in the setting of online communities that focus on knowledge building. Specifically, our empirical analysis provides evidence that online participants who receive more comments on their answers submitted to the online knowledge community are more likely to make further contributions. We argue that participants are sensitive to comments from their online peers in the setting of online communities. This finding conforms to previous studies in other online settings such as company-wide social media platform [17] and online news community [18] where receiving feedback from peers promotes the focal user's continued engagement in the online community.

Further, we explored the interacting role of voting and commenting mechanisms in online knowledge contribution. Our analysis supports the Nahapiet and Ghoshal's view [64] that knowledge contribution results from complicated and interrelated processes. We found that receiving a larger number of comments from online peers weakens the positive efect of positive usefulness voting on knowledge contribution, while the destructive efect of negative usefulness voting on knowledge contribution is mitigated if participants receive more comments from other peers in the online community. We posit that commenting is an important mechanism for collaborative online problem solving that provides meanings for voting especially those negative ones. Although commenting generally helps the contributors to learn new knowledge and further develop their knowledge contributions, too many comments can also signal a mismatch of the participant's knowledge level and that required by the community. Thus, it can diminish the internalization of positive usefulness votes into intrinsic motivations that drive the individual's continuance of contribution behavior.

On the other hand, commenting provides detailed reasons for those negative usefulness votes so that the participant may not feel personally attacked/insulted by peers and rather view the comments as con structive feedback. Comments with obiective assessment and recommendations for improvement can help the focal participant to realize the benefits of online interaction and the positive collaborating spirits embedded in the online community, thus mitigating the destructive efect of negative usefulness voting. Future research may consider the sentiment of the comments and how the degree of positive and negative comments impacts future contribution behaviors for the focal participant.

## 6.2. Practical implications

Ling et al. [65] argue that the failure to map behavioral theories to design options for online communities may result from the inability of most experimental behavioral studies to take into account multiple features of an online community that should be determined simulta neously. Our econometric analysis of a rich panel dataset collected from an online knowledge community has controlled for various factors of the community features as well as individual heterogeneity, thus having a higher level of external validity than the experimental research designs. Applying our findings to guide the design and management of online communities could make a diference for the success of online communities.

An interesting practical issue for online community designers and managers is how the engagement and contribution of participants can be fully motivated, given that the success and sustainability of such communities largely depend on participants' ongoing contributions. This research suggests a comprehensive view of which factors lead to participants contributing to online communities. This comprehensive view ofers insights regarding the design and management of such online communities.

Our analysis based on a 79-month panel dataset confirms the benefits of applying voting and commenting principles to incentivize participants' stickiness to online knowledge communities. Online knowledge community designers and managers should re-consider or redesign the features provided by their current online communities based on our findings to maximally encourage participant engagement and contribution. Our results show that positive usefulness votes positively associate with participants' contribution behavior, whereas negative usefulness votes lead to less contribution to the community. An essential consideration for the design and management of such online com munities is the tradeof between the two basic roles of negative usefulness voting: (1) quality control of online contribution; and (2) destructive impact on the participant's continuance of knowledge contribution. Some online communities may only allow positive voting on the answers submitted to the community, while the critical negative voting is not allowed in order to create a friendlier atmosphere in the community and promote a higher level of engagement of all partici pants.

Our results reveal that enhancing social interaction in the form of textual comments is an important motivator for knowledge contribution in online communities. Particularly, our analysis shows that the more comments online participants receive from their peers, the more likely they will contribute to the community. The commenting mechanism of online communities is not only a website feature that assists or mediates communication and collaboration in problem solving, but is also an important function that influences participant contribution behavior. We also found that the commenting mechanism provided by the online community can moderate the efects of voting on knowledge con tribution. Such findings yield insights into how the collaboration features of online communities can be designed and used in innovative ways to better promote continued contribution of community members. For example, commenting feature should be designed to be convenient and easy to use, and be encouraged especially when the participant submits a negative usefulness vote.

## 6.3. Limitations

We note several limitations of the present study. First, our data were collected from a community-based question and answer site which fo cuses on user experience and IT topics. The findings may not apply to other online communities that have diferent user population, IT artifacts, topic characteristics, and organizational structures such as online customer review sites [66–68], online health communities [31,69], and social commerce sites [70]. Future research can extend the current study by applying it to online communities focusing on other specific topics and representing diferent organizational structures and participant backgrounds. Particularly, we operationalized commenting as the number of comments received by the focal participant, given that the number of comments received indicates the frequency that the focal user can be influenced by the comments. Future research can explore diferent aspects of commenting such as sentiment expressed in online comments in other research settings such as online health communities and online customer review sites where subjective opinions are pervasive in user commenting. Our study focuses on individual-level characteristics in a specific online community to explain the variation in individuals' contribution behaviors. Other analyses could consider community-specific characteristics that might address more contextual factors such as variant IT artifacts, topic characteristics, and community organizational structures.

The second limitation pertains to the design of this study. Our analysis of a rich panel dataset shows that motivational afordances as suggested in our research model can explain the variation in individuals' online knowledge contribution behaviors. Such an analysis, though providing informative evidence on the theoretical relationships among variables of interest, may not be suficient to establish the underlying causality of our theory bases. Future research can use alternative research design such as experiments to further test and confirm the suggested relationships.

## 7. Conclusion

This study draws on the perspective of motivational afordances and self-determination theory to investigate knowledge contribution behaviors in online knowledge communities. We aimed to answer the question of how motivational affordances, in the forms of usefulness voting and commenting, lead to participants' continued contribution to online knowledge communities. We proposed a comprehensive framework to better understand the contribution behavior of participants in online knowledge communities. We then empirically tested the proposed research model through fixed-efects Poisson models applied to a rich dataset collected from an online question and answer community.

We found that motivational afordances designed into an online community, in the form of usefulness voting and comments, independently and jointly impact continued contribution on the part of the focal participant. Thus, this research contributes to the rapidly growing literature on user behaviors in online communities by empirically de monstrating that online communication in a utilitarian context is reinforcing, much as such communication is in the face-to-face context, and requires consideration by the community designer, particularly in regard to providing a medium for constructive communication and beneficially reinforcing activities.
