# TECHNOLOGICAL ENTITLEMENT: IT’S MY TECHNOLOGY AND I’LL (AB)USE IT HOW I WANT TO<sup>1</sup>

Laura C. Amo Management Science and Systems, School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY, U.S.A. {lccasey@buffalo.edu}

Emily Grijalva Organization and Human Resources, School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY, U.S.A. {ejgrijal@buffalo.edu}

Tejaswini Herath Information Systems, Goodman School of Business, Brock University, St. Catherines, ON, CANADA {therath@brocku.ca}

G. James Lemoine Organization and Human Resources, School of Management, State University of New York at Buffalo, Buffalo, NY, U.S.A. {jlemoine@buffalo.edu}

H. Raghav Rao Information Systems and Cybersecurity, Carlos Alvarez College of Business, University of Texas at San Antonio, San Antonio, TX, U.S.A. {hr.rao@utsa.edu}

Entitlement has been identified as a potentially valuable employee characteristic in the prediction of computer abuse but has not been studied systematically in the IS domain. We introduce the construct of technological entitlement as the persistent sense of being more deserving of technological resources, uses, and privileges compared to other employees. Adapting a model of general entitlement to the work technology context, we theorize that technological entitlement predicts computer abuse and that this relationship is amplified by perceptions of technology restriction. After developing and validating a scale to measure technological entitlement, we conduct three studies with working adult samples to test our hypotheses. In Study 1 (n = 187), using a behavioral design, we find that technological entitlement predicts computer abuse behavior (beyond general entitlement) and that this relationship is stronger when employees perceive organizational restrictions on technology usage. We replicate these findings in Study 2 (n = 339) with an experiment. In Study 3 (n = 156), we manipulate the context of restrictiveness within our experimental vignette to establish the generalizability of our moderator. We discuss how technological entitlement helps explain existing inconsistencies in the effectiveness of deterrence measures as well as other theoretical and practical implications of our work.

Keywords: Individual behaviors, psychological, behavioral, computer abuse, construct development, technological entitlement, technology restriction, multi-method

## Introduction

Employees who engage in the malicious misuse of organizational technology (i.e., computer abuse) pose considerable financial and reputational threats to companies—threats that continue to grow in frequency and cost (Ponemon, 2016, 2020). Between 2016 and 2020, the number of malicious attacks from inside employees has grown by 80% and the costs associated with a single event have increased by 118% (Ponemon, 2016, 2020). The growing prevalence of malicious insider events is reflected in the volume of attacks recently reported by a diverse set of high-profile companies such as Tesla, Facebook, General Electric, and Coca-Cola (Petters, 2021). For example, from 2014 to 2015, Facebook discovered that over 50 employees had abused access to user data for a variety of self-interested purposes (Jackson, 2021), and in 2019 General Electric reported two separate cases in which employees stole thousands of commercially sensitive data files (United States Department of Justice, 2019a, 2019b).

Given the costs of malicious insider threat management (average \$4.1 million annually; Ponemon, 2020), the identification of factors predicting computer abuse has become a priority for organizations and IS scholars alike (Willison & Warkentin, 2013). However, much of this existing work has focused on identifying organizationallevel predictors, such as information security policy and sanctions (e.g., D’Arcy & Hovav, 2007; D’Arcy et al., 2012; Hovav, 2017), with relatively less attention directed toward individual differences that characterize employees who engage in computer abuse. In addition, the limited research involving individual differences tends to focus on demographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, job type; e.g., Hovav & Darcy, 2012) with only a few studies emphasizing psychological attributes (e.g. Lowry et al., 2015; Maasberg et al., 2020). Consequently, the psychological makeup of the organizational insider remains somewhat of a “black box” requiring additional attention to ascertain the key characteristics of employees who are likely to engage in devious behavior and consequently, pose a security threat.

Entitlement is one such attribute that has yet to receive substantial attention in IS despite its well-established link to general aggression (Campbell et al., 2004; Grubbs & Exline, 2016) and to specific forms of deviant behavior in a number of non-IS contexts (e.g., counterproductive work behavior in Fisk, 2010). Entitlement is the pervasive sense of inflated self-worth and heightened expectations of special privileges (Campbell et al., 2004). Individuals with high levels of entitlement become frustrated when the environment does not confirm their exaggerated self-beliefs about exceptionality and distinction, making them prone to aggression and antisocial behavior (Grubbs & Exline, 2016).

It is not surprising that entitlement, although not yet directly modeled as a predictor of computer abuse, has been highlighted as a defining characteristic of malicious insiders in case analyses of computer abusers (e.g., Band et al., 2006; Moore et al., 2009; Nurse et al., 2014). In their recommendations for expanding computer abuse research, Willison and Warkentin (2013) call for more focus on entitlement (see Table 1, p. 7) and entitlement has been identified as a risk factor for malicious insiders by U.S. government agencies (e.g., National Cybersecurity and Communications Integrity Center, 2014).

Building on this emergent evidence, in the current paper we develop a contextualized theoretical model to better understand the connection between entitlement and computer abuse. We begin by drawing on general entitlement theory to provide a foundation for our assertions (Campbell et al., 2004; Grubbs & Exline, 2016) and then go on to adapt this theory to account for the embedded system of technology that characterizes modern organizations (Kling & Scacchi, 1982; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). The work technology context affects how variables relate and manifest; in this regard, we follow expert recommendations on how to account for the IS context in our theory building (Hong et al., 2014; Johns, 2006; Whetten, 2009). In particular, we develop theory for a type of entitlement specific to this context, technological entitlement, which is defined as the persistent belief that one is more deserving of technological resources than other individuals and tends to manifest in expectations of special technology access and privileges.

Technologically entitled employees feel that they deserve superior (i.e., better, newer, faster) technology compared to other employees and they expect unrestrained access to technology. When these exaggerated expectations of special status inevitably go unmet, technologically entitled employees often lash out in aggressive acts of misuse or abuse (i.e., computer abuse). Their strong sense of superiority and expectations for special treatment lead technologically entitled employees to have fewer qualms about breaking organization-related rules because these employees consider themselves “above” organizational restrictions. As such, we argue that technologically entitled employees pose significant security risks to organizations and, further, that these risks are exacerbated when these employees’ beliefs are challenged by restrictions placed on their work technology use.

Overall, the current paper has two primary objectives: (1) to develop the theory and measurement of technological entitlement, and (2) to determine how technological entitlement relates to computer abuse under different organizational conditions. To address our objectives, we conduct three separate studies with working adult samples and adapt key theoretical constructs from entitlement theory to reflect the work technology context. In each of the studies, we conceptualize versions of the predictor (i.e., entitlement), moderator (i.e., violation of expectation) and outcome (i.e., deviant behavior) that are specific to the work technology domain (Hong et al., 2014; Whetten, 2009). In doing so, we go beyond existing studies focusing on individual differences and computer abuse, which tend to look at general as opposed to contextualized models of computer abuse (e.g., Darcy et al., 2009; Kim et al., 2016; Maasberg et al., 2020).

In the following sections, we outline existing guidelines for theory contextualization in IS (Hong et al., 2014), and adhering to these guidelines, we review the general theory on which we build our model of technological entitlement. Next, drawing on this general entitlement theory, we elaborate upon our definition of technological entitlement and introduce our hypotheses. We then describe our three studies, present our results, and conclude by discussing theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and potential areas for future research.

## Theory

## Theory Contextualization in IS

Contextualized approaches to theory development emphasize that theories should be adapted with sensitivity to the context in which they are applied to maximize contributions to research and practice—making findings more accurate, robust, and powerful (Hong et al., 2014; see also Johns, 2006, 2017; Whetten, 2009). Although existing work suggests that entitlement is likely an important individual factor to include in empirical models predicting computer abuse, contextualized approaches to theory development would argue that simply applying this theory by examining how general entitlement is related to a variety of IS variables is not as useful as adapting it to focus on the unique manifestation of entitlement in the work technology setting (Johns, 2006,, 2017; Whetten, 2009). For example, Hong and colleagues (2014, p. 3) argue that “theory borrowing without accounting for contextual differences may lead to a misapplication of the theory, hollowing the theory of its original explanatory power.” Drawing on guidelines from Hong et al. (2014), we next describe our approach to contextualizing entitlement theory to the IS domain.

To begin, we adhere to the first guideline by identifying general entitlement as our foundational theory (Campbell et al., 2004). As per the second guideline, we next distill general entitlement theory to identify a set of core constructs that are relevant to the work technology context (see Figure 1). In particular, we refine the general model to introduce technological entitlement as a core construct relevant to computer abuse behavior. We also incorporate technologyrelevant variables, as discussed in the third guideline. Specifically, we determine that (1) computer abuse is a context-specific type of aggression, and (2) technology restriction is a context-specific manifestation of unmet expectations. Aligned with the fourth guideline to decompose the core tenets into context-specific factors, we frame technological entitlement as a decomposition of general entitlement and examine more nuanced policy manifestations of technology restriction (different types of technology use policies). In accordance with the fifth guideline, our model includes potential moderators within the work technology context, and to address the sixth and final guideline to explore a variety of models, we consider alternative models in our discussion of future research. We expand upon these steps below as we develop our theory of technological entitlement.

## General Entitlement Theory

The introduction of technological entitlement is rooted in general entitlement theory (Campbell et al., 2004). General entitlement, also referred to as psychological entitlement or trait entitlement, is a personality trait characterized by pervasive feelings of deservingness and exaggerated expectations of special treatment (Campbell et al., 2004). According to general entitlement theory (Grubbs & Exline, 2016), entitled individuals are more likely to experience unmet expectations because they possess unrealistically positive self-views, which are often not confirmed by other people or life experiences. These unmet expectations foster disappointment, ego threat, and/or a sense of injustice because entitled individuals sincerely believe that they deserve special treatment (Grubbs & Exline, 2016; Twenge & Campbell, 2003; Zeelenberg et al., 2000).

Upon experiencing disappointment (e.g., being denied a technology upgrade) or failing to receive special treatment (e.g., being subjected to the same rules as everyone else at work), entitled people tend to experience exaggerated psychological distress, resulting in volatile emotional responses and aggression (Burt et al., 2012; Calvete, 2008; Grubbs et al., 2013; Reidy et al., 2008; Ruiz et al., 2001). These emotional responses subsequently contribute to deviant outbursts, including violent criminal behavior (Fisher & Hall, 2011) as well as unethical and counterproductive work behavior (Grijalva & Newman, 2015; Lee et al., 2017; Miller, 2015). Moreover, the relationship between entitlement and aggression is strengthened by perceived provocation or injustice (Archer & Thanzami, 2009).

## Technological Entitlement

Building on this work, we position technological entitlement as a contextualized form of general entitlement. Whereas the dual cornerstones of general entitlement consist of having inflated self-views and an exaggerated sense of deservingness (Campbell et al., 2004), technological entitlement reflects an inflated sense of deservingness and exaggerated expectations, specifically with regard to work technology resources, uses, and privileges. In terms of how this manifests in the workplace, it is theorized that technologically entitled employees make unnecessary technological demands— believing that they deserve the best resources (i.e. newest, most expensive, and fastest) as well as unrestrained access to technology. For example, employees who are high in technological entitlement would believe it is important to have better technological resources than their peers, fewer restrictions, as well as elevated access to IT support staff.

Technological entitlement is conceptualized as a unidimensional personality trait, a relatively enduring pattern of thinking, feeling, and behaving (Funder, 2001). This unidimensional trait approach is consistent with how general entitlement (Campbell et al., 2004) and other contextualized types of entitlement have been defined and measured (e.g., Boyd & Helms, 2005; Miller & Konopaske, 2014). Although technological entitlement is likely positively related to general entitlement, the two variables are not synonymous. General entitlement captures a global propensity to feel entitled across situations and contexts, whereas technological entitlement measures the tendency to feel entitled to technology in particular. Viewed hierarchically, specific types of trait entitlement (e.g., technological entitlement, consumer entitlement, Boyd & Helms, 2005; and academic entitlement, Chowning & Campbell, 2009) are nested under general entitlement. Building on the basic principles of measurement theory (Kline, 2011) and research on general factors in psychology (e.g., Cattell, 1957; Eysenck, 1967), general entitlement consists of the shared, but not unique variance captured by specific forms of entitlement. This breadth means that general entitlement is good at predicting broad outcomes but is less precise at capturing entitlement related to specific contexts (i.e., the classic “bandwidth versus fidelity” dilemma; Cronbach & Gleser, 1965). Thus, general entitlement potentially overlooks meaningful variance that would help to predict technology-centric outcomes, which is captured by technological entitlement.

Because general and technological entitlement tap different sources of variance associated with their distinct foci and levels of aggregation, people can have differing levels of the two constructs (Grubbs & Exline, 2016). In other words, someone high in general entitlement is not necessarily also high in technological entitlement and vice versa. For example, an individual could have higher general entitlement than technological entitlement if they typically feel they are more deserving than other people, but this sense of privilege is less relevant to the work technology context. Technology could be less relevant if, for instance, one does not feel that technology plays an important role in completing one’s job. In addition, it is possible that technology is more central to some individuals’ identities than others’ (e.g., they believe they are particularly skillful with and knowledgeable about technology), which makes these employees feel more entitled in this domain than in other domains with which they identify less strongly. In sum, technological entitlement has a more specific emphasis than general entitlement, capturing unique variance related to expectations of technology resources, uses, and privileges at work. Thus, we argue that the study of technological entitlement is critical to predicting technologyrelated outcomes.

## Hypotheses

## Technological Entitlement and Computer Abuse

In particular, technological entitlement is useful to the IS domain because of its potential to predict computer abuse. As mentioned above, general entitlement is linked to a host of aggressive and harmful behaviors (Grubbs & Exline, 2016). To illustrate, entitlement predicts workplace deviance such as theft and sabotage (Harvey et al., 2014), co-worker abuse (Harvey & Harris, 2010), and relational conflicts with supervisors (Harvey & Martinko, 2009). Entitlement has also been identified as the primary component of narcissism driving the relationship between narcissism and counterproductive work behavior (Grijalva & Newman, 2015).

Further, other contextualized versions of entitlement predict a variety of deviant outcomes (e.g., Chowning & Campbell, 2009). To illustrate, academic entitlement involves expectations of high grades without actually studying and is associated with academic cheating (Chowning & Campbell, 2009), employee entitlement involves expectations of special treatment such as financial bonuses without high performance and is linked to falsifying hours on a timesheet (Fisk, 2010; see also Huseman et al., 1987), and consumer entitlement involves expectations of special treatment in the retail environment and predicts aggressive and degrading responses to customer service personnel (Fisk & Neville, 2011). This existing research on contextualized entitlement reveals that the domain in which entitlement is embedded dictates the extent to which exactly individuals feel entitled (e.g., grades vs. money) and how deviance manifests (e.g., academic cheating vs. time theft). Based on this logic, technological entitlement should be ideally positioned to predict computer abuse because of the conceptual match between the predictor and outcome variables with regard to technology.

Given the evidence for a positive relationship between entitlement (of all forms) and deviance, we narrow in on how this entitlement-deviance relationship manifests in the work technology context. To begin, we theorize that technologically entitled employees are more likely to simply ignore technology policies—believing that they are above the rules and thus do not have to adhere to organizational security norms. In fact, we expect that entitled individuals are more likely to disregard organizational technology policies even if the personal costs for following the rules are low or punishment for breaking the rules is highly likely (Zitek & Jordan, 2019). These individuals also prioritize their own technological needs and convenience above others’ and that of the organization as a whole. Because entitled individuals are more selfish and less empathetic, employees with high technological entitlement are expected to blatantly disregard the risk(s) that their actions may pose to others (Campbell et al., 2004; Zitek et al., 2010). Altogether, technologically entitled employees are predisposed to believe that they can do what they want with technology, even if it is unethical, unauthorized, or illegal.

The foregoing discussion leads us to predict that technologically entitled employees will be significantly more likely to engage in computer abuse, as compared to employees with lower technological entitlement, and that technological entitlement predicts computer abuse above and beyond general entitlement (as supported by our arguments regarding the value of contextualization). As such, we offer the following hypotheses:

H1: Technological entitlement is positively related to computer abuse.

H2: Technological entitlement predicts computer abuse above and beyond general entitlement.

## Moderation by Technology Restriction

We generally expect a positive relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse; yet consistent with Hong et al.’s fifth guideline (i.e., to model interactions among context-specific factors), there are also situations that may magnify this association. Indeed, ample research has demonstrated that trait-expressive behaviors are activated by situationally relevant cues (e.g., nurturance in response to a request for help or anxiety in response to a threat; Tett et al., 2013; see also Tett & Burnett, 2003). This interaction between traits and situations is captured by trait activation theory, which acknowledges that some triggering events can reveal natural propensities more readily than others based on a conceptual alignment between the trait and event (Judge & Zapata, 2015; Tett & Burnett, 2003). In the case of technological entitlement, we expect that employees’ perceptions of technology restriction serve as a contextually relevant trigger of technological entitlement, which contributes to computer abuse (as shown on the right side of Figure 1).

Employees with high technological entitlement strongly believe that their technology needs are “exceptional,” and expect to be able to use both company- and personally owned technology at work in any way they please. Therefore, policies that constrain technology use arguably serve as a direct threat to the self-perceived special status of technologically entitled employees. The resulting frustration and anger associated with restrictions likely encourage behaviors designed to reestablish control over the technological environment. In other words, when technologically entitled employees experience threats to their technological freedom (thus calling their special status into question), they lash out as a means of reasserting their beliefs about deserving exceptional technology privilege. To provide an example of how this might manifest, we would expect highly technologically entitled employees to become agitated when access to technological resources and systems are denied, and subsequently channel their frustration toward gaining access through unauthorized means that open a company up to additional risks (e.g., using proxy servers or a private VPN to access blocked social media platforms). In this way, the restricted access to technological resources and systems would serve as a contextually relevant trigger for computer abuse. In particular, we examine two manifestations of technology restriction at varying levels of specificity (acceptable use policy and remote access policy), which we describe in more detail below.

First, regarding how technology restrictions are communicated, the majority of companies have a broad acceptable use policy regarding how employees are allowed to use technology at work (SANS, 2020). These policies generally outline acceptable and unacceptable technologyrelated behavior, such as placing limitations on how employees use company-owned technology (e.g., desktops, laptops, and workstations) as well as how employees use personal mobile devices and work technology for personal reasons during work hours (Society for Human Resource Management, 2020). Findings regarding the impact of acceptable use policies on computer abuse are mixed. Some studies indicate that these types of policies effectively deter computer abuse outcomes (e.g., D’Arcy & Hovav, 2007; Desire, 2017), whereas others suggest they are unrelated (Blanke, 2008; Foltz, 2000) or that they even directly (Posey et al., 2011) and indirectly fuel computer abuse (Lowry et al., 2015). By arguing that employees respond to restrictive policies differently depending upon their level of technological entitlement, the current study identifies one potential reason for these inconsistent results across past studies. Overall, we expect that broad restrictions on technology use at work that are communicated through acceptable use policies will trigger the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse.

Beyond examining broad acceptable use policies, it is also helpful to assess technology restrictions at differing levels of specificity to demonstrate the generalizability of our theorized moderator. Companies often have more specific subpolicies nested under an organization’s broader acceptable use policy. Given its prevalence and its association with strong employee reactions (e.g., Liu, 2021), the current paper also investigates whether a specific subpolicy—remote access to work technology—moderates the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse. Remote access refers to the ability of employees to log on and use work technology from another computer system outside of the physical workplace. Not surprisingly, workers tend to prefer flexible work schedules and remote work options (International Workplace Group, 2019; PwC, 2013); however, there are also downsides. For example, enabling employees’ remote access to organizational systems and data introduces security and privacy concerns (Hodge, 2020). Accordingly, companies often place restrictions on the conditions and opportunities under which employees are allowed to work from remote locations.

Employees who are technologically entitled are marked by a strong belief that they deserve to use technology anytime and anywhere, which makes them more likely to become aggressive when denied the ability to perform work-related tasks from their preferred remote location. Thus, we expect that remote access restrictions will trigger computer abuse among technologically entitled employees. Altogether, we hypothesize that perceptions of technology restriction—in the form of both broad acceptable use policies and more specific restrictions on remote access to work technology—will ultimately enhance computer abuse among technologically entitled employees:

H3: The relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse is positively moderated by higher perceptions of technology restrictions for both (a) acceptable use policies and (b) remote access policies.

## Methods

To test our hypotheses, we conducted three studies with independent samples of working adults. In Study 1, we measured past computer abuse behavior and perceptions of restrictions on broad technology use in a field study. In Studies 2 and 3, we used experimental methods and modeled computer abuse intent with vignettes. The vignettes manipulated technology restrictions such that we investigated restrictions on personal- and company-owned technology use at work in Study 2 (H3A) and restrictions specifically on remote access in Study 3 (H3B). First, however, we created a measure of technological entitlement that we used in all three studies. The instrument development process is described as part of Study 1 and in more depth in the Appendix.

## Study 1

## Overview and Design

In Study 1, we conducted a two-wave field study where we collected data on the independent and dependent variables at two different time points. This time-lagged approach was used to support our arguments of causal precedence and mitigate the risk of common method bias (Podsakoff et al., 2003) by minimizing the cognitive accessibility of responses to previous questions (Hawk & Aldag, 1990; Podsakoff & Organ, 1986). Participants completed the surveys over the course of a month, with about two weeks between the first and second survey.

## Sample

Data was collected by a third-party market survey organization in December 2018 and January 2019. Participants had been working with their current organization for at least three months on a full-time basis. Compensation for survey completion varied and was dependent on the individual agreements between the respondents and the survey organization. There were 187 participants in Study 1 with an average age of 52.1 years (SD = 10.9) and 74% were male. Demographic information for the Study 1 participants is in Table 1.

## Measures

Technological entitlement (Time 1): We developed a measure of technological entitlement in four steps, drawing on the guidelines provided by Hinkin (1995), DeVellis (2011), and Chu and Chau (2014). Further details of our four-step instrument development process appear in the Appendix. Briefly, in the first step (domain specification), we developed a definition of technological entitlement by drawing on general entitlement theory (Grubbs & Exline, 2016; Foster et al., 2015). In the second step (item generation and content validity), we employed a deductive item generation method (Hinkin, 1995) and developed eight items based on the subscale on entitlement from Foster et al.’s (2015) Grandiose Narcissism Scale, modifying the wording to the work technology context (e.g., “I deserve to get what I want in regards to my technology at work, even if other employees are not able to do so”). To match our definition, these items were designed to capture “an inflated sense of deservingness and exaggerated expectations specifically with regard to work technological resources, uses, and privileges.” Thus, the technological entitlement items specifically mention expecting/deserving more than other people or getting special treatment and reference the technology context at work.

In this step, we also evaluated the content validity of the items using 35 undergraduate and graduate business students. Students are an appropriate population for testing content validity “given that the primary requirement is the intellectual ability to evaluate the consistency between scale items and construct definitions” (Colquitt et al., 2014, p. 6; Hinkin & Tracey, 1999; Schriesheim et al., 1993). Furthermore, the population of business school students represents the emerging workforce, which is part of our target population, making this group ideal for evaluating content validity (Haynes et al., 1995). All of the items demonstrated strong content validity, based on benchmarks provided by Colquitt et al. (2019). Results from the content validity exercise, including the proportion of substantive agreement (p<sub>sa</sub>) and the substantive-validity coefficient (c<sub>sv</sub>) indicated high content validity (see Appendix).

We next completed the third step (i.e., instrument purification) for scale development per Chu and Chau’s (2014) guidelines by conducting an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring in SPSS version 24 (IBM, 2016). The results supported a unidimensional structure with all of the items loading over 0.51, thus meeting the 0.40 threshold recommended by Hinkin (1995). In keeping with contemporary psychological practice (Smith et al., 2012; Hayes, 2005) and other IS research on scale development (Thatcher et al., 2018), we also considered practicality and use of the Technological Entitlement Scale in terms of scale length. As discussed by Hayes (2005), in the context of practical administration, any reduction of items in a scale is helpful, with the goal of a short form being to “reduce the number of items to between three and five without substantially sacrificing measurement quality” (p. 13). We thus eliminated the two items with the lowest factor loadings and eliminated one item due to its redundancy with another item (see Appendix). Table 2 provides the final 5-item measure based on a 7-point Likert scale. As the fourth and final step, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to confirm the theoretical structure of technological entitlement and ascertain its distinction from the related constructs of general entitlement and narcissism. The results again supported a unidimensional structure of the Technological Entitlement Scale, all of the items had factor loadings over 0.63, and the three-factor model (which differentiated between technological entitlement, general entitlement, and narcissism) had superior goodness-of-fit relative to the one- and two-factor models (see Appendix).

The estimates of reliability for the five item Technological Entitlement Scale were acceptable (α sample B = 0.86; α Sample C = 0.90; see Table A2 in Appendix). The 5-item measure was used to operationalize technological entitlement in all three studies, (Study 1, α = 0.89) with a 7- point response scale.

Technology restriction (Time 1): To measure technology restriction, we modified items from the D’Arcy and Hovav (2007) scales on computer monitoring and preventive security measures to assess employee perceptions of restrictions on personal- and company-owned technology devices and use. Examples from the 6-item scale included “My company has strict rules for use of personal mobile devices at work,” and “My company restricts employees’ use of company-owned devices” (α = 0.81).

Computer abuse behavior (Time 2): Items from Lowry et al.’s (2015) reactive computer abuse scale were used to measure computer abuse behavior. Respondents were asked to indicate the frequency (1 = “Never” and 6 = “Very frequently”) with which they have engaged in certain computer abuse behaviors. The 10-item scale included items such as “Misused computer system access privileges,” and “Taken computer resources without approval” (α = 0.86). To address potential floor effects associated with underreporting, we used the natural log of the composite as our dependent variable.

Control variables (Time 1): Consistent with existing behavioral security studies in IS (e.g., Herath & Rao, 2009; Lowry et al., 2015), we controlled for age, gender, sanctions (Herath & Rao, 2009; 5 items, α = 0.83), and social desirability response bias (Hays et al., 1989; 4 items, α = 0.67). We also controlled for job satisfaction (Cammann et al., 1983; 3 items, α = 0.94) to account for general disposition toward the organization, annual salary, and the percentage of time spent working remotely (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011). Finally, we included general entitlement using the entitlement subscale of the Grandiose Narcissism Scale (Foster et al., 2015) to demonstrate the incremental validity of our technological entitlement construct beyond this less specific form of entitlement (4 items, α = 0.84). There were also three quality control items embedded in the survey (e.g., “Indicate agree as your answer”), and only participants who responded correctly to all three items were included in the analysis.

## Analysis

We tested the fit of the latent variables in Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998-2012; see Appendix Table A3) and we tested our hypotheses using SPSS version 24 (IBM Corp, 2016) and the PROCESS macros for SPSS (Hayes, 2013). In testing our hypotheses, we ran three models in a stepwise fashion. The first model (Table 3, Model 1) included all of the control variables as well as general entitlement, and the second model (Table 3, Model 2) added the main effect of technological entitlement to demonstrate the incremental variance accounted for by this focal variable. We then ran the third model (Table 3, Model 3) in SPSS using the PROCESS macro for simple moderation (Hayes, 2013), which included perceptions of technology restriction as a moderator of the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse. Interaction terms were mean-centered (Aiken & West, 1991). Adhering to best practices for model comparison (Chin, 1998; Cyr, 2008), we empirically compared the R<sup>2</sup> values associated with each of the respective models to determine Cohen’s f <sup>2</sup> (Cohen, 1988) and associated criteria to evaluate the magnitude of these effects.<sup>2</sup>

## Results

Results from Study 1 are provided in Table 3 and support the respective hypotheses. As shown in Model 2 of Table 3, technological entitlement is significantly related to computer abuse, $\beta ~ = ~ 0 . 1 7 , ~ p ~ < ~ 0 . 0 5 ,$ supporting H1. Further, technological entitlement explained the variance in computer abuse beyond that explained by general entitlement, supporting H2. In addition, Model 1 in Table 3 shows that general entitlement is significantly related to computer abuse behavior; however, after technological entitlement is included in Model 2, general entitlement no longer has a statistically significant relationship with computer abuse.

The results also support H3a, as the interaction between technological entitlement and perceived restriction on technology use is significant in Model 3, β = 0.12, p < 0.05 (see Figure 2). This indicates that the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse is stronger when perceptions of organizational technology restriction are higher. Findings remained robust when control variables were excluded (see the note under Table 3). In Study 2, described next, we set out to replicate findings from Study 1 with an experiment.

## Study 2

## Overview and Design

To replicate findings from Study 1 and build confidence using a multimethod approach, we designed an experimental vignette in Study 2. We collected data in two waves, with one week between survey administrations. Data on the independent variable, technological entitlement, was collected in Wave 1 and data on the outcome variable, computer abuse intent, was collected in Wave 2. The moderator, perceptions of technology restriction, was derived from experimental manipulation presented in the vignette in Wave 2.

## Sample

The sample was collected by a third-party market survey organization in January and February 2020. Participants were representative of the working population in the United States and had been working with their current organization for at least three months on a full-time basis. Compensation for survey completion varied and was dependent on the individual agreements between the respondents and the survey organization. There were 339 participants with an average age of 43.7 (SD = 12.5), and 55% were male (see Table 4).

We used the 5-item scale described in Study 1 to measure technological entitlement in Study 2 (α = 0.88). The vignettes used for this manipulation described a hypothetical employee, Taylor, working at a hypothetical company, MerraInc. The manipulation for technology restriction was included in the first part of the vignette. The Low Technology Restriction condition was designed to convey a great degree of freedom in how employees were allowed to use personal and mobile technology for work. Specifically, the vignette for the Low Technology Restriction stated that:

“Compared to other companies, MerraInc employees have a lot of freedom regarding how they use technology at work. For example, MerraInc employees are allowed to use personal mobile devices while at work, can access company data remotely, have unlimited access to the company WiFi, and can freely access the internet at all workstations.”

The vignette for the high technology restriction condition, on the other hand, was designed to convey very little freedom regarding technology use at work and stated:

Compared to other companies, MerraInc employees have very little freedom regarding how they use technology at work. For example, MerraInc employees are not allowed to use personal mobile devices at work, cannot access company data remotely, are prohibited from accessing the company WiFi from personal mobile devices, and have very limited access to the internet at workstations.

The last part of the vignette describes an opportunity for the hypothetical employee, Taylor, to steal data from a company database. We measured computer abuse intent by asking respondents to indicate how strongly they agreed with statements beginning with the stem “If I were Taylor” that corresponded to computer abuse acts such as, “I would export the data from the MerraInc databases” and “I would steal the MerraInc data” (α = 0.94, 5 items). As in Study 1, we used the natural log of the composite as our dependent variable.

## Analysis

We first tested the fit of the measurement model for all latent variables in our model using Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998- 2012; see Appendix Table A3 for fit statistics). We then tested and confirmed the differential effects of the two experimental conditions by running a manipulation check. Participants’ responses to the question—“The technology policies at MerraInc are restrictive”—were compared across conditions and indicated that the manipulation was successful; compared to responses among participants in the low condition (X = 2.73, SD = 1.82), responses among participants in the high condition (X = 5.3, SD = 1.57) were significantly higher, F (1, 358) = 205.04, p < 0.001.

Next, we considered whether technologically entitled participants systematically rated technology restriction as higher, compared to participants with lower technological entitlement. Aligned with our theoretical arguments, individuals at or above the mean on technological entitlement (i.e., $Z _ { \mathrm { T E } }$ of 0 or above) rated the hypothetical technology policies as significantly more restrictive in both low and high conditions, compared to individuals below the mean of technological entitlement (i.e., $Z _ { \mathrm { T E } }$ below 0). While this systematic difference supports our overarching theoretical argument that technologically entitled individuals are generally more sensitive to technology restrictions of any kind, it could also potentially attenuate the experimental effect (Salkind, 2010). That is, although the experimental manipulation of technology restriction was shown to be effective, its effect significantly varied according to participants’ levels of technological entitlement. Given that our theory is dependent upon perceived levels of technology restriction, testing our interaction hypothesis by examining the manipulation condition might generate confounded results.

For this reason, we calculated two interaction terms to account for both (1) the interaction between technological entitlement and the experimental condition, and (2) the interaction between technological entitlement and restriction perceptions of the respective experimental condition. For this second interaction term, we used participants’ responses to the manipulation check, or the unique perception of high technology restriction. We provide results from models including this second interaction term in our table, and report results (see the note under Table 5) from a model where both interaction terms are included to demonstrate the robustness of our claim that it was the perception of high technology restriction that was driving the hypothesized moderation effect. Notably, the decision to focus on perceptions, as captured by a manipulation check, has been utilized by other experimental work (see Melwani & Barsade, 2011).

For hypothesis testing, we used stepwise main effects models and PROCESS macros (Hayes, 2013, Model One) in SPSS version 24 (IBM, 2016), with mean-centered interaction terms in our final model, as we did in Study 1. The first two main effects models from the stepwise regression provided results for H1 and H2. Hayes’s (2013) PROCESS macros Model 1 allowed us to evaluate the moderating effect of perceived technology restrictiveness on the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse, corresponding to Hypothesis 3A. In Table 5, we report results with control variables from Hayes’s (2013) Model 1. As described above, we also ran Hayes’s Model 2 (2013), which is a model that includes both previously described interaction terms. Again, including both interaction terms in our model allowed us to determine whether it was the perception of restriction (as opposed to the restrictive experimental condition) that was amplifying the effect of technological entitlement on computer abuse; these results are reported at the bottom of Table 6 in a note. We ran all models with and without control variables, as certain researchers argue that control variables should be excluded from experiments because of the design and random assignment to conditions (see Simmons et al., 2011; Simonsohn et al., 2014). Results from Model 3, which excludes control variables, are reported at the bottom of Table 5. Consistent with Study 1, we used the f <sup>2</sup> approach (Cohen, 1988) for model comparison and effect size computation.

## Results

Results of the main effects model are shown in Table 5 and suggest that technological entitlement is positively associated with computer abuse intent, $\beta = 0 . 2 8 , p < 0 . 0 0 1$ , supporting H1 (see Model 2 in Table 5). In addition, technological entitlement explained an additional 4.4% of variance in computer abuse intent beyond that explained by general entitlement, supporting H2. Results from Table 5, Model 3, confirm that the interaction between technological entitlement and perceptions of technology restriction remained positive and statistically significant, $\beta = 0 . 1 2 , p < 0 . 0 1$ . Thus, results from Study 2 also support H3a, as the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse was stronger when perceptions of restriction on employee technology use at work (acceptable use policy) were higher (see Figure 3 for plots). As described above, technology restriction includes both the condition and the perception of the condition. The interaction results in Table 5 are based on the perception of technology restriction, as we confirmed that technologically entitled employees systematically rated technology restriction higher $( Z _ { \mathrm { H i g h T E } } = 0 . 1 0 , Z _ { \mathrm { L o w T E } } = - 0 . 0 9 , t \mathrm { - s t a t i s t i c } = 1 . 8 1 , p <$ 0.05). Results supporting H3a remained robust when the interaction between technological entitlement and technology restriction condition was included in the model and when control variables were excluded from the model (see the note under Table 5).

## Study 3

## Overview and Design

In Study 3, we replicated the results from our prior studies, and designed an experimental vignette that allowed us to determine whether perceptions of restriction on a more specific aspect of work technology use—remote access to work technology—positively moderated the relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse intent. This allowed us to establish the generalizability of our moderator to remote access policies, which may be conceptualized as type of subpolicy that falls under the broader umbrella of an acceptable use policy. We expect that restrictions on remote access to work technology will serve as a particularly salient trigger to technologically entitled employees.

There were 156 participants in Study 3 with an average age of 41.95 (SD = 12.8), and 51% were male. Additional demographic information for the Study 3 participants is shown in Table 6.

## Measures

We used the same 5-item scale as the previous studies to measure technological entitlement (α = 0.91). We used the same set of controls as the previous studies (see Appendix Table A4).

Experimental vignette: We developed a slightly different experimental vignette for Study 3, in which we manipulated the level of technology restriction specifically on remote access to work technology. The vignettes had the same number of words in the scenario to maintain equity across conditions. The low technology restriction condition conveyed a great degree of freedom regarding how and when employees were allowed to remotely access work technology. Specifically, the vignette for the low technology restriction condition stated:

Compared to other companies, MerraInc employees have a lot of freedom regarding how and when they may use technology for work. MerraInc allows employees to work from home and employees are able to access the company’s network, systems and data from any location. MerraInc employees may conduct business from any personal device (e.g. cell phone, laptop, desktop) at any time. This means that MerraInc employees are able to complete work while away from the physical workplace at any time and from any location.

The vignette for the high technology restriction condition, on the other hand, was designed to convey very little freedom regarding remote access to work technology, and stated:

Compared to other companies, MerraInc employees have hardly any freedom regarding how and when they may use technology for work. MerraInc completely forbids employees from any type of remote work and employees are not allowed to work from home under any circumstance. MerraInc employees cannot access the company’s network, systems and data from anywhere other than the office, even if there is an urgent issue. This means that MerraInc employees are not able to complete any work while away from the physical workplace.

The last part of the vignette describes an opportunity for the hypothetical employee, Taylor, to steal data from a company database. We measured computer abuse intent with the same items as those in Study 2 (α = 0.94, 5 items) and, again, used the natural log as our dependent variable.

## Analysis

We evaluated goodness of fit for the latent variables in our measurement model using Mplus (Muthén & Muthén, 1998- 2012, see Appendix Table A3). For the manipulation check, we asked respondents to indicate the degree to which they agreed with the following statement: “The MerraInc technology policies for remote work do not meet my expectations for personal technological freedom.” As in Study 2, we created two interaction terms to capture the interaction between (1) technological entitlement and the technology restriction condition, and (2) technological entitlement and the technology restriction perception by using responses to the manipulation check. We ran the main effects models in SPSS and ran the interaction models using Hayes’s PROCESS macros, Model 1 and Model 2 (for robustness). Again, we ran models with and without control variables and, for clarity, report results from the model with control variables derived from Hayes’s (2013) Model 1.

## Discussion

Despite substantial anecdotal and qualitative evidence that entitlement may result in greater computer abuse, the topic has received relatively little empirical scrutiny in the IS literature. In the current paper, we extend existing research on general entitlement to develop and measure technological entitlement, a novel construct contextualized to the IS domain. Consistent with context-sensitive theorization principles (Whetten, 2009), because technological entitlement focuses specifically on the belief that one is more deserving of special technology resources and privileges than are other individuals, we hypothesized that it is better positioned to predict IS outcomes of interest than general entitlement. To test this prediction, we created and validated an instrument to measure technological entitlement (i.e., the Technological Entitlement Scale), and designed three studies to investigate the relationship between this new construct and computer abuse.

Across these three studies of working adults, we found converging evidence (using both survey and experimental methodologies) that technologically entitled employees pose a direct risk to the IS security of organizations—even beyond the risk associated with general entitlement. An initial behavioral study determined that technological entitlement is positively related to computer abuse and that perceptions of technology restriction in the workplace strengthen this relationship. Experimental evidence from Study 2 replicated these findings. In Study 3, we manipulated the context of restrictiveness to establish the generalizability of our moderator. We found that restrictive remote-access policies amplify the technological entitlement-computer abuse relationship similar to the way that general acceptable use policies do in Studies 1 and 2. Overall, our findings provide insight into the psychological make-up of malicious insiders, supporting the argument that technological entitlement is a defining feature of those who commit deviant technological acts in work settings. Below, we expand on the theoretical and practical implications that accompany our findings.

## Theoretical Implications

This paper informs theory in several ways. To begin, the robust relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse supports the value of incorporating context into theorization (Johns, 2017; Whetten, 2009). By aligning the foci of the variables in our theoretical model to the technology context, we enlarge the scope of entitlement theory and advance its application to the field of IS (Johns, 2006, 2017; Rousseau & Fried, 2001). The advantage of theory contextualization is that it allows for the generation of richer and more actionable advice specifically designed to address problems and questions that arise in the IS domain (Hong et al., 2014; Weber, 2003). In our case, we used an IS frame of reference among our empirical constructs— technological entitlement, technology restriction, and computer abuse— to gain insight into when malicious insiders commit computer abuse. This contextualized approach allowed us to improve the explanatory power of our entitlement model and is aligned with context-specific approaches to personality research, which contend that contextualized measures of personality traits (such as entitlement) are stronger predictors of performance than noncontextualized measures (Schmit et al., 1995; Shaffer & Postlewaite, 2012).

We also identified technology restriction as an important boundary condition of the technological entitlement-computer abuse relationship (Hong et al., 2014). Aligned with the commonly accepted tenet of research on personality—that traits such as entitlement are expressed in response to traitrelevant situational cues (Tett & Burnett, 2003; Tett et al., 2013)—we found that employees were more likely to express (trait) technological entitlement by committing computer abuse in response to situational cues such as restrictive technology policies. Our identification of this moderator not only supports interactionist approaches to work behavior (Tett & Burnett, 2003) but offers insight into the “interplay between technology, users, and usage context” (Hong et al., 2014, p. 119). At the same time, we extend Hong et al.’s (2014) work on theory contextualization by applying it to develop a new technology-centric construct. While not an original focus of the guidelines created by Hong et al. (2014), construct development in IS may be considered an application of theory contextualization. Our paper offers an exemplar of how to leverage theory contextualization principles to migrate constructs developed in outside disciplines to IS.

Finally, our research helps inform theory by offering a potential explanation for why technology policies have an inconsistent relationship with computer abuse. Although technology policies are intended to deter computer abuse, certain studies suggest they are not always effective (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011; Lowry et al., 2015; Posey et al., 2011). Our results suggest that the efficacy of technology policies on deterring computer abuse may be conditional on employee technological entitlement; in other words, deterrent methods such as stringent technology policies may be more effective for employees with lower (versus higher) technological entitlement. In this way, our research provides a potential explanation for mixed results on policy effects (D’Arcy & Herath, 2011), revealing that technology use policies can actually trigger computer abuse among technologically entitled employees.

## Practical Implications

We also consider several practical implications stemming from our work on technological entitlement. Although technical aspects of threat control are considered a primary means to combat insider threats, security experts recommend taking a more holistic approach to prevention by conducting ongoing screening of employees (Ernst & Young, 2016), and personality is considered a crucial part of insider threat mitigation (Intelligence and National Security Alliance [INSA], 2017). To borrow from the INSA report (2017), personality mapping is not a smoking gun but rather a smoke alarm that there is increased risk for malicious insider behavior. As such, while we do not advocate technological entitlement dictating employment, promotion or termination decisions, we do believe that information on personality (along with user behavior analytics, security incidents, life events, past behavior, etc.) may help companies by providing more input into the overall potential for malicious insider behavior. Having established that greater technological entitlement is directly associated with an increase in reported computer abuse behavior, we consider gathering information about technological entitlement a viable means for companies to more accurately gauge risk and supplement existing threat assessments.

Insider attacks are incredibly costly and difficult to detect, remediate and contain (Ponemon, 2016, 2020; see also Lee & Lee, 2002). Thus, identifying technological entitlement as an individual attribute that characterizes malicious insiders likely has significant economic implications for organizations. To better quantify these economic implications, we estimate the financial impact associated with varying degrees of the prevalence of technologically entitled employees in a company. We understand that this is an approximation because many elements go into malicious insider attacks, but we still think it is useful to estimate the financial impact of technological entitlement. According to the Ponemon Cost of Insider Threat report (2020), the average total expenditure on malicious insider incidents in 2020 among the global companies in the sample was about \$4.1 million USD and the average company size was 18,935 employees (apart from costs associated with other types of insider threats such as negligence or external actors). Based on the average relationship between technological entitlement and computer abuse identified across our three studies, an employee with higher technological entitlement (i.e., a 1 point increase in technological entitlement) “costs” an additional \$47.40 USD per year in malicious insider threat mitigation alone. This is calculated based on information in the Ponemon report (2020), wherein the average annual cost for average-sized companies (with 18,935 employees) is \$4.1 million, yielding a per-employee cost of about \$215.47. We multiply this by the average effect size of 0.22 from our three studies, such that a one-unit increase in technological entitlement corresponds to \$47.40 per technologically entitled employee (\$215.47 × 0.22d = \$47.40).

Based on this estimate, if the average-sized company experienced a 10% increase in the percentage of technologically entitled employees, the company would risk spending an additional \$89,776 USD per year to mitigate the risk associated with these employees (10% of 18935 is ≈ 1894 employees × \$47.40 ≈ \$89,776 annual cost). We believe that these estimates provide an opportunity for companies to improve planning and budgeting around cybersecurity investments. According to Ponemon (2020), the majority of costs associated with insider threats go toward post-incident responses, mostly personnel and technology to resolve incidents after the events occur. Proactive measures such as user behavior analytics and employee training and awareness provide significant cost savings (\$3.42 and \$3.02 USD million, respectively; Ponemon, 2020) by reducing cyberrisk. Integrating employee screening of technological entitlement is a low-cost way for companies to not only supplement and improve risk evaluation (as noted above) but to also inform companies as to which employees and/or units should be prioritized with regard to these types of monitoring and training investments.

Our findings also have implications for creating and implementing policy on employee technology use. Existing research shows that employee perceptions of technology policy fairness affect technology-related deviance and resource misuse (Henle et al., 2009). For example, when changes in security policy are deemed infringements on freedom (Lowry et al., 2015) or when policies are considered unjust (Posey et al., 2011), the risk of employee computer abuse increases. Relating this to our paper, we also found a strong positive link between perceptions of restrictive technology policies and computer abuse across multiple studies and determined that this relationship was stronger among technologically entitled employees. In other words, highly technologically entitled employees respond more negatively to perceived restrictions on technology use. Thus, this subgroup of employees is particularly important to consider when attempting to mitigate perceived injustice with these types of restrictions. With employees in general, and particularly with those who are technologically entitled, every attempt should be made to involve them in the process of policy building to encourage buy-in, provide information about why restrictions are necessary, and treat employees consistently, such that nobody gets special treatment or is perceived as getting special treatment (Lord, 2018). Organizations that work toward establishing technology policies that are procedurally and informationally fair will better mitigate security risks associated with technological entitlement.

Lastly, we note a potential implication of our findings regarding employee perceptions of remote access policy. In 2020, remote work was more prevalent than ever before because of mandated physical distancing requirements resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic (Gartner, 2020). However, companies have begun to request that employees return to the physical workplace after allowing employees to work remotely (PwC, 2020), which could potentially be perceived as taking away a once-cherished freedom. In addition, other companies have introduced extremely stringent guidelines to mitigate risks associated with remote access to work technology (Morrison, 2020). Our findings suggest that these trends—both being forced to return to the physical workplace after experiencing a remote environment or the heavy monitoring of remote access—may be perceived as technology restrictions imposed by the organization, which could increase the information security risk posed by technologically entitled employees. As such, our technological entitlement model and theorizing play a prescient role in understanding the evolving work technology environment. Organizations should carefully consider employee perceptions when deciding how to move forward with disabling or downgrading remote work options and implementing restrictions on remote workers.

## Limitations and Future Research

Like any study, our research is subject to potential limitations. We acknowledge that it is difficult to establish whether one variable actually causes another due to a myriad of alternative explanations that must be explored and ruled out. However, the current study took several steps to evaluate the rigor of the connection between technological entitlement and computer abuse. We triangulated our findings across both experimental and behavioral methodologies and replicated our findings in multiple samples. In Study 1 and Study 2, we used a timelagged design to measure technological entitlement at least one week prior to measuring computer abuse, which helped establish the temporal precedence of our independent variable—lending support to our argument that technological entitlement precedes computer abuse.

Based on feedback from a helpful reviewer, to better understand the nomological net of technological entitlement, we also conducted post hoc analyses to determine whether technological entitlement was associated with employee rank in an organization. As suspected, managerial rank was indeed positively related to technological entitlement, such that managers had significantly higher levels of technological entitlement compared to nonmanagers in each of our three studies.<sup>3</sup> Although beyond the scope of the present study, we appreciate a reviewer bringing our attention to another contextual moderator of technological entitlement, and consider this an important area to examine in the future. It would be interesting to consider that the link between managerial status and computer abuse identified by Lowry et al. (2015) may be explained by managers having higher technological entitlement. We encourage researchers to build off this observation and consider how technological entitlement potentially mediates the relationship between managerial status and computer abuse in future work. Other areas that are ripe for additional inquiry include investigations on how technological entitlement develops over time within individuals, as well as the examination of alternative mechanisms through which technological entitlement relates to abuse more generally (per Guideline 6 in Hong et al., 2014).

The effect sizes associated with our independent variable (technological entitlement) and moderator (perception of technology restriction) noted at the bottom of each of the results tables are considered small per Cohen’s f <sup>2</sup> benchmarks (1988), which is not surprising because our dependent variable has a low base rate. At the same time, we note that our models used logged outcomes, which affects the interpretation of effect size. For example, by applying Ford’s (2018) methods for calculating percentage change in logtransformed models (i.e., exponentiate the coefficient for the independent variable, subtract 1, and then multiply by 100 to calculate percentage increase or decrease), we found that a one-unit change in technological entitlement is associated with an 8% increase in computer abuse, on average across the three studies. Specifically, the percentage change was 3.05% in Study 1, 9.42% in Study 2, and 10.52% for Study 3. These results suggest that the effects of technological entitlement on computer abuse are certainly not small. Regardless of magnitude, effects can be important to the extent that they change how people understand a relationship or phenomenon (Prentice & Miller, 1992), and there is ample evidence that small effects can have an impressive impact on both theory and practice (Bosco et al., 2015; Cortina & Landis, 2009). The effects from our studies successfully corroborate our contextualized theory and have the potential to change how researchers and practitioners think about IS-related phenomena, particularly in regard to deviant behavior in IS contexts.

In terms of prevalence, our findings from across three studies confirm that technological entitlement is normally distributed, meaning that about 16% of the working population has relatively “high” (i.e., > +1SD) technological entitlement and about 2% of employees have “extreme” (i.e., > +2SD) levels. Consistent with the notion of normality, the average technological entitlement was close to the midpoint on a 7- point scale (?? = 3.28 in Study 1, ?? = 3.34 in Study 2, and ?? = 3.60 in Study 3) and the variance was similar in all three studies (s = 1.43, 1.44, and 1.67 for Study 1, 2, and 3, respectively). These results support the contention that employees vary considerably in technological entitlement, with a non-negligible portion possessing high levels (approximately 1 in 5 employees would be categorized as having high or extreme technological entitlement). It is worth considering, however, that research on person-environment fit has shown that individuals with similar personality characteristics—in this case, individuals with relatively high technological entitlement—are attracted to specific industries, jobs, and/or organizations (Schneider, 1987). In future research, it may prove valuable to examine the prevalence of technological entitlement by industry or job type to determine whether there is a systematically greater risk of computer abuse (as attributed to technological entitlement) in certain industries or jobs compared to others.

Finally, although technological entitlement appears to be generally problematic with regard to computer abuse outcomes, we encourage others to explore whether there are situations in which technological entitlement is not detrimental or may even be desirable. Notably, some studies have shown a positive association between general entitlement and creativity, which the authors explained by linking entitlement with the need for uniqueness (Zitek & Vincent, 2015; Studies 2, 3a, and 3b). Moving forward, we recommend exploration into how technological entitlement is associated with various outcomes, both negative (e.g., cyberloafing, resource misuse) and positive (e.g., technological innovation). Lastly, we recognize the need for future research to address the cross-cultural boundaries of our findings, as employees in our studies were from the United States and culture represents an important context to explore (Whetten, 2009).

## Conclusion

We contribute to IS research by developing the novel construct of technological entitlement, validating a measurement instrument, establishing that it has a positive association with computer abuse intent and behavior, and identifying boundary conditions of this relationship. Through three studies, we found strong support for our contextualized model and demonstrated that technological entitlement can predict computer abuse beyond general entitlement. We hope that our work leads to further exploration into how technological entitlement is related to computer abuse as well as other key IS variables.

## Instrument Development and Content Validity

We integrated guidelines from Chu and Chau (2014) and best practice recommendations from Hinkin (1995) and DeVellis (2011) to develop a scale measuring technological entitlement. We first specified the domain and developed a definition of technological entitlement in the work context—corresponding to Stage 1 of Chu and Chau’s (2014) process. We define technological entitlement as the persistent belief that one is more deserving of technological resources than are other individuals, which manifests in the expectation that one should be afforded special technology access and privileges. For the subsequent stages of the instrument development, we enlisted three independent samples. Sample A was used for content validation, Sample B was used for the EFA, and Sample C was used for the CFA. All participants were undergraduate students at a large public university who completed the surveys for extra credit in a 200-level or 400-level course.

Corresponding to Chu and Chau’s (2014) second stage, we developed items that corresponded to exaggerated expectations about technology, specific to the workplace. To do this, we reviewed scales on general entitlement, including Campbell et al. (2004) and Foster et al. (2015), and adapted eight items to reflect the work technology context. These items can be found in Table A1. We then evaluated the content validity of the items. Thirty-five undergraduate and graduate students completed a content validity exercise as outlined by Anderson and Gerbing (1991), which we evaluated using criteria from Colquitt et al. (2019). ## Instrument Refinement: Exploratory Factor Analyses

In the instrument refinement stage, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis and exploratory discriminant factor analysis with an independent sample, Sample B. The standard model for measurement in psychology is reflective (Bollen, 2002) because psychological traits and characteristics tend to be conceptualized as latent variables. We likewise consider technological entitlement to be a reflective construct. We performed principal axis factoring in SPSS on the eight items from the Technological Entitlement Scale. We used principal axis factoring because this is recommended over principal components analyses in the case of theory building (Worthington & Wittaker, 2006). Hinkin (1995) recommends retaining only items with factor loadings exceeding 0.40. All of the items factor loadings exceeded 0.40 on the single factor solution. Using the same sample, we ran an exploratory discriminant factor analysis with the Technological Entitlement Scale alongside items from related scales: narcissism and general entitlement. We used the 16-item adaptation of Raskin and Terry’s (1988) 40-item Narcissistic Personality Inventory [NPI], commonly referred to as the NPI-16 (Ames et al., 2006), to measure narcissism.<sup>4</sup> To measure general entitlement, we used Campbell et al.’s (2004) Psychological Entitlement Scale, which includes nine items such as “I honestly feel I’m just more deserving than others” and “I demand the best because I’m worth it.” The responses were scaled such that 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree. The reliability coefficient was α = 0.81.

Next, we simultaneously entered the eight items from the Technological Entitlement Scale along with the items from general entitlement and narcissism measures. We used principal axis factoring with varimax rotation. When all of the items were included, the eight items from the Technological Entitlement Scale loaded on one factor, and there were no cross-loadings that exceeded 0.40. Additionally, no items from the scales of general entitlement or narcissism loaded above 0.40 on the latent technological entitlement construct.

In keeping with contemporary scale development (e.g., Hayes, 2005; Thatcher et al., 2018), as a final step we shortened our measure. Items 2 and 6 had the lowest factor loadings at 0.58 and 0.51, respectively, so we decided to drop these items. We also dropped Item 5 (“I deserve more technological freedom at work than other people”) because it was very similar to Item 1 (“I expect to have more technological freedom than the average employee”). The practice of eliminating redundant items is a way of “increas(ing) the efficiency of administration and decrease costs without substantially lowering measurement reliability and validity” (Hayes, 2005, p. 13). We reran the factor analysis with the five items, and the results were consistent with the eight-item scale. The five items were used in the confirmatory factor analysis, described below.

## Instrument Finalization: Confirmatory Factor Analyses

To establish further support for the five-item measure and test its theoretical structure (Byrne, 2005), we next performed confirmatory factor analyses in Mplus (Múthen & Múthen, 1998-2012) using Sample C. These analyses were intended to show that technological entitlement was unidimensional, exhibited acceptable fit with data, and was distinct, although positively related to other co nstructs in its nomological net (i.e., narcissism and general entitlement). The factor solution was evaluated according to overall $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ RMSEA, SRMR, and CFI based on guidelines from Hu and Bentler (1999). Using Sample C (n = 183), we tested various different models to demonstrate the distinctness of the five-item Technological Entitlement Scale from measures of general entitlement and narcissism. We tested a onefactor solution where all items loaded on a single factor. We then tested two different two-factor solutions, each of which loaded technological entitlement onto the same latent factor as either general entitlement or narcissism. Finally, we tested a three-factor solution (all items loading on their theoretical factors).

There were items within the NPI-16 and the PES that had very similar wording with other items on the same scale (e.g., “I like to be the center of attention” and “I really like to be the center of attention” for the NPI-16), meaning there was a risk of correlated error variance on those pairs of items. Following recommendations from Byrne (1994) and Kline (2005), we decided a priori to allow residuals (within scales) on these pairs of items to covary, as failure to do so potentially biases coefficients in latent variable models (Cole et al., 2007). Three sets of items on the NPI-16 were allowed to covary and one set of items on the PES were allowed to covary for all factor models.

The three-factor solution fit the data significantly better than the other factor solutions. Based on results from the chi-square difference tests where the three-factor model is used as the nested model relative to the respective models, the three-factor model fit significantly better than the one- and two-factor models. The Technological Entitlement Scale is associated with—yet unique from—measures of general entitlement and narcissism. Moreover, the three-factor solution provided acceptable goodness-of-fit indices for the RMSEA (ε = 0.055), and SRMR (0.066) indicating that the model was sufficiently explanatory, per Hu and Bentler’s (1999) recommendations. All of the scale means, standard deviations, reliability estimates, and correlations from both Sample B and Sample C are provided in Table A2. We note that the development of the Technological Entitlement Scale was based on a 5-point Likert response scale, which existing research suggests would have psychometric properties (i.e., reliability and validity) that extend to 7-point scales (see Colman et al., 1997). At the same time, as noted in our methods section, we switched to using the 7-point Likert scale because it potentially captures more variance (Colman et al., 1997; Nunnally, 1978).
