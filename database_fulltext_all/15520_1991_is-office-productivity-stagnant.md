# Is Office Productivity Stagnant?

By: Raymond R. Panko
University of Hawaii
2404 Maile Way
Honolulu, Hawaii 96822

## Abstract

Has office productivity been stagnant in recent years, despite massive spending on information technology (IT)? This concern has been raised in both academic publications and the business trade press. This article examines the basis for that concern, which, if true, would have profound implications for both IT researchers and practitioners.

Keywords: Office productivity, productivity, output per hour, investment, payoffs, impacts

ACM Categories: J.4, K.4.3, K.6.0

## The Office Productivity Crisis

In a series of publications, Stephen Roach of Morgan Stanley has painted a bleak picture of information technology's productivity payoffs (Gay and Roach, 1986; Roach, 1984; 1985; 1987a; 1987b; 1988; 1989). He has argued that office productivity has been virtually stagnant in recent years, despite massive investments in information technology (IT).

The idea that office productivity is stagnant is nothing new. In the 1970s and early 1980s, Alan Purchase of SRI International and many other writers argued that office productivity was stagnant (Panko, 1990b). Those early analysts called for increased IT spending as the key to unlocking office productivity. They noted that the average production worker in manufacturing and agriculture had many times the capital investment of the average office worker. By closing this investment gap with IT, they believed, companies could close the gap in productivity.

In contrast, Roach (1987a) now argues that while the capital spending called for by early analysts has come about, the promised payoffs have not: "Fifteen years into the 'high-teching' of America and the U.S. economy is still trapped in a quagmire of low productivity growth. While there are many factors behind such disappointments, technology has quite simply not delivered its long-awaited productivity payback" (p. 1).

Roach's concerns have been published widely. Based primarily on Roach's analysis, FORTUNE (Bowen, 1986) decries “The Puny Payoff from Office Computers,” while The New York Times (Schneider, 1987) speculates that “Services” might have been “Hurt by Technology.” Business Week (1988) calls Roach's surprising findings “The Productivity Paradox.” These and other published accounts (Neikirk, 1987) and Roach's widely read technical reports add fuel to top management's increasing skepticism about the efficacy of IT spending (Moynihan, 1990). If Roach's indictment of IT's impact on productivity remains unchallenged, it will stand as a serious indictment of past investments in information technology and pose a roadblock to future commitments. Roach's analysis and conclusions therefore demand careful scrutiny.

## General Productivity Measurement

To understand Roach's analysis, it is first necessary to understand how productivity is defined and measured. In general, productivity tries to measure efficiency—how much output can be produced with as few inputs as possible. Yet this simple statement is surprisingly difficult to operationalize.

In the United States, productivity estimates are prepared by the U.S. Department of Labor's Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Although the term "productivity" is used in many different ways in the management and IT literature (q.v., Deprez, 1986), the labor economics literature follows BLS terminology and measurement concepts quite rigorously. So have most studies concluding that office productivity is stagnant. BLS measurement techniques are discussed in detail in the BLS Handbook of Methods (U.S. Department of Labor, 1988).

In BLS productivity measurement, both input and outputs are measured in terms of units instead of prices or costs. By focusing on units, productivity focuses on the underlying efficiency of work processes. For example, if units of output rise by 20 percent and units of input rise by 10 percent then productivity rises by 9.1 percent regardless of whether input and output prices are rising, falling, or staying the same. While prices and costs are certainly important, they are the province of other measures of efficiency.

If input or output data are available only in terms of dollar amounts, deflation is performed to remove the effects of price changes (U.S. Department of Labor, 1988). For example, if sales are \$100 million one year and \$113 million the next, then dollar sales rose by 13 percent. But if inflation in that product category was 5 percent, then unit sales must have risen by 7.6 percent. Economists refer to deflated dollar figures as constant collars. In productivity calculations, constant dollars are often used as a surrogate for units of input or output.

In BLS methodology, productivity is computed by dividing all outputs by all inputs. Unofrtunately, it is often impossible to measure or even identify all inputs and outputs. As a result, the BLS produces a number of different productivity measurements, each with different strengths and weaknesses. No single productivity statistic should be viewed as definitive or easy to interpret. Whenever possible, multiple productivity, measures should be used.

## Output per Hour

The previous section addressed productivity in general. This section addresses the most widely known BLS productivity series, output per hour or OPH. Output per hour is computed by dividing units of output by the number of hours worked to produce them.

OPH is usually quite easy to compute from existing data. In addition, output per hour is important to labor economists, because if OPH is rising, then wages can be increased without causing a one-for-one increase in inflation. In contrast, if OPH is stagnant, any wage gains will tend to fuel inflation.

But OPH is misleading because it attributes all output increases to a single input factor, namely labor. Obviously, output increases are due to many inputs, such as capital and energy. Later, multifactor productivity statistics, which explicitly take multiple input factors into account, will be discussed.

## Output per hour for aggregate sectors

The BLS produces a number of output per hour data series. The broadest and best-known series are those for large aggregate sectors of the economy, specifically those shown in Figure 1.

In practice, GDP and GNP rarely differ by even 1 percent, but GDP is used in productivity estimation. To convert dollar output to unit output, GDP is “deflated” to constant dollars.

## Private Business

The broadest OPH series is not for the entire economy but rather for private business. This excludes both public administration (government) and non-profit corporations. $^{1}$ These are significant exclusions. Public administration accounts for about 12 percent of the GDP (Quinn, 1988), and non-profit corporations are also very important to the economy. Unfortunately, OPH for private business is often quoted when reporters discuss productivity trends in the entire economy.

Public administration and non-profit corporations are excluded because there is no good measure for their output. For most parts of the economy, the contribution to GDP is measured by the value of sales. But in government and non-profit institutions, services are often performed free or at a heavily subsidized price. In these cases, using “sales” to estimate output is meaningless. Where contribution to the GDP cannot be based on sales, the Department of Commerce simply equates the value of output to the cost of producing it. If the cost of producing it is \$1000, then the output is valued at \$1000. For productivity measurement, this approach is a disaster. In an industry whose output is measured by its input, output always appears to grow at the same speed as inputs. So measured productivity growth is always zero, regardless of how rapidly productivity is really growing. $^{2}$

## Non-Financial Corporations

There are serious measurement problems for other parts of the economy as well. In some other industries, notably finance, insurance, and real estate (FIRE), there may be sales data, but deflation estimation is highly problematic (Baily and Gordon, 1988). The main problem is a lack of natural units of output. For instance, in depository institutions, should the output measurement include deposits, loans, or interest payments? Without natural units of output, deflation cannot be done reasonably. Because of this and other measurement problems (Baily and Gordon, 1988), the BLS reports OPH data for non-financial corporations, which exclude the FIRE division of the economy as well as public administration and non-profit corporations.

## Manufacturing

Even when non-profit and FIRE exclusions are made, the measurement picture is still somewhat murky. For example, in the services division of the economy there are often no natural units of output on which to base deflation figures. Overall, roughly a third of the GNP comes from industries where measurement problems are severe and in which output is measured largely in terms of input (Levitan and Werneke, 1984).

For these and for historical reasons, the BLS produces an even narrower aggregate productivity measurement—OPH for manufacturing. While measurement problems in manufacturing are comparatively small, manufacturing has only 10 percent of the GNP and only 18 percent of total employment (Quinn, 1988)

## Office Output per Hour in Aggregate Sectors

The BLS does not produce any data series for office productivity. In fact, “office work” is not even a standard employment category in BLS data. So studies that have estimated office productivity have had to define office work and produce their own productivity methodologies.

To estimate the size of the office work force, most analysts have equated office work with either the old white collar category or the first five major categories of the new Standard Occupational Classification System (SOCS) (Executive Office of the President of the United States, 1987). $^{3}$

Some divisions of the economy shown in Figure 1 are especially office-rich, including finance, insurance, and real estate (93 percent), wholesaling (71 percent), and public administration (68 percent). Within services, 78 percent of all employees in professional services are in the first five categories of the SOCS.

Other divisions of the economy are office-poor. In mining and manufacturing, only about 40 percent of all workers are counted, and in construction, only 23 percent fall into the first five categories of the SOCS.

Although the BLS does not produce any productivity statistics for office workers, a number of analysts have tried to use something like the BLS methodology for OPH to measure office output per hour (OOPH). The first efforts were often flawed. For instance, some early analysts divided that GNP or GDP by the number of office workers. But as discussed above, GNP and GDP calculations often equate output to input, making productivity growth appear to be zero. These calculation problems are especially strong in public administration, services, and FIRE, which collectively have over half of all office workers (Panko, 1990a). Output measurement problems in these divisions produce not just a one-time drop in productivity but a systematic understanding of productivity growth. This is precisely the kind of artifact that would make office productivity appear to be stagnant or at least growing very slowly, even if it is really growing at a good pace.

Most newer analyses divide output for private business by the number of office workers less office workers in public administration. This is a step forward, but it is not enough. It still does not treat non-profit organizations properly, and there are still weaknesses in FIRE and services measurement that tend to understate productivity growth in these office-rich divisions of the economy.

Even if these problematic sectors of the economy could be removed from the analysis, a fatal problem would remain. Office workers in the remaining parts of the economy would represent a fairly small percentage of employment—only 30 percent to 40 percent (Panko, 1990a). This is important because all single-factor productivity statistics become progressively less meaningful as the single factor's percentage of total inputs decreases. In a highly labor-intensive industry where there are few other inputs, output per hour for total labor will be a good productivity measure. At the opposite extreme, one would never divide a company's output by the number of hours worked by its janitors and expect the number to make sense as a measure of “janitorial productivity.” Office work falls somewhat between these extremes. This issue will be discussed later, in the section on substitution. But for now, the discussion of industries in which office employment is relatively small will be deferred.

With these problems, it is inappropriate to infer from aggregate sector data that office productivity is stagnant.

## Divisional Output per Hour

Below the level of aggregate sectors of the economy are the 10 major “divisions” shown in Figure 1. Given the problems with measuring output in large aggregate sectors, perhaps problems would be less if output per hour was measured in individual divisions.

But the opposite is the case. First, measurement problems at the aggregate sector level can be magnified at the division level. As noted above, in public administration almost all output is estimated from input, and in FIRE and services, about half of all output is estimated from input (Kendrick, 1988). Analysts have also argued that there are severe measurement problems in construction, retailing, and transportation (Baily and Gordon, 1988). To publish OPH estimates for these divisions would make little sense.

There are other problems with the way that contributions to the GDP are calculated at the divisional level. Baily and Chakrabarti (1988) note that “Contrary to a widespread impression, analysts measuring productivity do not start with industry data and work up to the aggregate. Instead, they measure changes in aggregate output, then allocate the growth by industry. This procedure has the potential for serious errors” (p. 7). So divisional output measurement is less accurate than output measurement for aggregate sectors. For these reasons, the BLS refrains from producing division-level OPH measurement, except for the manufacturing division, where measurement problems are smaller.

Despite these inaccuracies, the private American Productivity Center does calculate divisional output per hour estimates. Roach (1987a), furthermore, has extended the American Productivity Center numbers to produce OOPH estimates at the divisional level. Roach's analysis indicates that output per office worker disappointment is due primarily to divisions where there are known (FIRE and services) or suspected (construction, retailing, and transportation) measurement problems (Baily and Gordon, 1988). Because these measurement problems tend to produce precisely the kind of stagnancy or even declines shown in Roach's analysis, there is no way to know if the office output per worker crisis seen in division-level data is real or a data artifact.

While there is evidence of stagnant output per office worker in some other divisions with fewer output measurement problems (mining, trade, some manufacturing industries), the percentage of office workers is fairly low in these industries, and this violates the principle that office labor must be a high percentage of total inputs. (Interpretation problems raised by the violation of this principle are discussed later in this article.) Overall, at both the division level and the national level, the data fail to show reliably that office productivity has been stagnant.

## Output per Hour for Selected Industries and Services

In addition to output per hour series for aggregate sectors of the economy, the BLS also produces OPH series for selected individual industries and services. For individual industries, the BLS usually measures specific outputs, such as the number of cars produced in the automobile industry. Because of such detailed output measurement, output per hour for selected industries and services is generally believed to be more accurate than OPH for aggregate sectors or divisions.

Unfortunately, industry-level OPH series are available primarily in manufacturing, where outputs are fairly easy to measure but where the percentage of office workers is low. In other divisions of the economy, industry-level OPH measurements have been made in only a limited number of industries. For FIRE, only one industry is measured, namely commercial banking, which accounts for only 11 percent of FIRE's contribution to the GDP. In services, the BLS offers only three industry OPH series: hotels, motels, and tourist courts; laundry and cleaning services; and beauty and barber shops. None of these industries has many office workers. Worse yet, none is from the professional services industry group, which dominates services today. For this reason, OOPH analysts have generally ignored industry-level OOPH measurement.

Yet to do so is wrong, because while these selected industries represent only a part of the economy, they offer clear views of productivity, and the picture they present gives little ground to those who argue that office productivity is stagnant.

Commercial banking is one industry where office workers dominate employment and where outputs are measured reliably. Data published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics indicate that OPH grew in this industry very rapidly from 1980 to 1987—3.3 percent per year (U.S. Department of Labor, 1990b). Because about 90 percent of all workers in FIRE are office workers, OOPH growth must be almost identical to overall OPH growth. So while Roach's (1987a) analysis shows divisional office OPH strongly declining in FIRE in recent years, the one FIRE industry for which OPH can be estimated quite accurately shows good productivity growth. In addition, a detailed industry study has pinpointed computer use as a major reason for output per hour growth in commercial banking (Brand and Duke, 1982).

Public administration also offers a combination of office-rich “industries” (called functions) and good productivity measurement. Although public administration was not included in Roach’s analysis, it would be odd if public administration’s OOPH was rising given the general problems with OOPH in the entire economy.

In public administration, the BLS has conducted extensive industry-level OPH measurement for the U.S. federal government (Ardolini, 1979). Using this database, Panko (1985) isolated data from functions that were office-intensive, so that OPH would be a reasonable surrogate for OOPH. This analysis found that OPH grew 1.7 percent annually in the U.S. Federal government's office-intensive functions from 1967 to 1981, when the study's data ended. In comparison, output per hour in private business grew only 1.4 percent. In fact, from 1977 to 1981, OPH in office-intensive functions in the federal government outgrew even OPH in manufacturing. A less precise averaging of recent date indicates that OPH in office-intensive functions has grown about 1.8 percent annually between 1982 and 1987 (U.S. Department of Labor, 1990b).

For communications, another office-rich industry, the impact of technology has long been apparent. Division-level OPH estimates from the American

Productivity Center, which are probably in the case of communications where output is fairly measurable, indicate that OPH growth in communications was 4.3 percent annually from 1973 to 1979 and 3.9 percent annually from 1979 to 1985 (Roach, 1987a).

In conclusion, statements that OOPH has been stagnant are due to analyses of data either with output measurement problems that tend to understate productivity growth or from industries with fairly low fractions of office workers. Therefore, single-factor productivity measurement may be misleading. In contrast, in the few cases where office employment is high and where output measurement problems are low, there is no evidence that office output per hour has been stagnant in recent years. In fact, the evidence indicates considerable productivity growth.

## Substitution Effects

In order to justify a single-factor productivity analysis, the preceding section discussed only industries where office labor was very high. Would things change if analysts could validly use data from industries and divisions where output measurement problems are low but where the percentage of office workers is also low, such as manufacturing?

## Multifactor productivity analysis

As noted earlier, while output per hour (OPH) is fairly easy to measure in many instances, labor economists have never been comfortable with it as a productivity measure because it effectively relates all output to a single input factor, namely hours of labor. In reality, output is produced by many input factors such as capital, energy, materials, purchased services, research and development, and so on.

Beginning in 1983, the BLS began to publish productivity measures that took multiple inputs into account. These are called multifactor productivity statistics. An ideal multifactor productivity measurement would take into account all input factors. In practice, however, available data do not allow this. The BLS currently produces two multifactor productivity analyses that include some, but not all, inputs. First, there is capital-labor multifactor productivity, which includes both capital and labor. Second, there is KLEMS multifactor productivity, which includes capital (K), labor, energy, materials, and outside services. Although KLEMS contains more input factors than capital-labor productivity, it is only available for manufacturing.

Multifactor productivity measures are useful because they allow us to break output per hour growth into two components—growth in intrinsic (multifactor) productivity and the substitution of other factors for labor or substitution of labor for other factors. This allows an interpretation of the importance of substitution when considering output per hour.

## The importance of substitution in multifactor productivity

Table 1 (for capital-labor productivity) shows that capital substitution accounted for 69 percent of OPH growth in private non-farm business sector and 24 percent of OPH growth in manufacturing. Table 2 (for KLEMS multifactor productivity) shows substitution contributing 53 percent of measured output per hour growth in manufacturing.

Norsworthy and Malmquist (1985) collected similar capital-labor multifactor productivity for Japan. From 1963 until 1973, substitution accounted for 92 percent of OPH growth for manufacturing in Japan. From 1973 to 1978, the figure was 70 percent. These higher substitution figures for Japan are due to that country's higher rate of capital investment.

Thus, even for total labor, which is a large input factor in most industries, OPH is as much a measure of substitution effects as it is a measure of underlying productivity growth. For smaller factors of production, single-factor productivity measures are almost exclusively measures of substitution and say almost nothing about the intrinsic productivity of the input factor.

For instance, consider “capital productivity,” which might be produced by dividing output by units of capital input. This was actually done in the Norsworthy and Malmquist (1985) study. From 1965–1973, capital productivity dropped by 0.1 percent per year in the United States, and from 1963–1973, capital productivity dropped even faster (4.1 percent per year) in Japan. From 1973–1978, capital productivity declined 0.9 percent annually in the United States and 0.8 percent in Japan.

These numbers, read in the same way as single-factor office productivity studies are often read, would indicate that capital's productivity has been plummeting and that Japan has been having even worse capital productivity troubles than the United States. Of course this is nonsense. Because capital is a small input factor, single-factor capital productivity is almost entirely a measure of substitution. While substitution directions are not always expected or easy to explain, in this case it is clear. Capital tends to be substituted for labor, so negative numbers merely indicate that substitution far outweighs multifactor productivity growth. In fact, in terms of output per hour of labor, the worse the capital productivity numbers look, the better.

## Natural directions in substitution

Consider the following question: Do we expect output per hour for labor to rise when things are going well? Almost everyone will answer, "Yes." But should they? Rationally, if labor is strongly substituted for other input factors, then OPH will decline even if things are going well, just as "capital productivity" tends to decline. So why have we been so strongly conditioned to expect OPH to rise when things are going well? Is there then a "natural" direction to substitution that causes other factors to be substituted for labor, but not the reverse?

The answer is that while there are no “natural” directions of substitution, it is true that labor’s cost has traditionally been high relative to other inputs. Therefore, while there is no a priori requirement that other factors be substituted for labor, labor’s high cost tends to make this the case in practice.

What about office output per hour? Do the same dynamics occur, so that we should expect OOPH to increase if things are going well? Certainly, office labor is expensive; therefore, as in the case of total labor, we would expect capital and some other factors of production to be substituted for office labor, giving it a natural direction.

But what about the relationship between office labor and non-office labor? Now a problem occurs. Will companies add non-office labor to increase the productivity of office workers—for example adding factory workers to substitute for office workers? It seems more likely that, in innovations such as computer-integrated manufacturing, the opposite will occur. More office workers will need to be hired in order to decrease shop floor employment. Thus, we would expect substitution between office and non-office labor to increase non-office OPH and decrease office OPH.

In fact, falling computer prices may accentuate the substitution of office labor for non-office labor. As an input factor falls in price, it tends to be substituted for other input factors. Because computer prices have been falling very rapidly, a considerable amount of computer substitution is likely. This could increase office OPH, or it could be used to increase non-office OPH in a way that will require more hours of office labor input, or both effects could occur. Studies by Baily and Chakrabarti (1988) and by Osterman (1986) show that a number of substitution effects will falsely make office OPH appear to be declining when computers are used intelligently.

Therefore, we must change our conditioned thinking about office OPH. We must not blindly expect office OPH to increase when things are going well. The opposite could happen—OOPH could very likely decrease, just as in the case of capital productivity. While OPH is usually (fairly) easy to interpret, office OPH is a meaningless number whose increases and decreases by themselves mean nothing.

In Roach's (1987) analysis of division data, output per information worker in manufacturing increased only 4.2 percent from the average of the 1970s to 1986, while output per worker grew by 30.1 percent and output per production worker grew by 45.9 percent. Assuming these numbers are accurate, what do they mean? Do they mean that true underlying office productivity has been stagnant? Has IT not been substituted for office labor adequately? Has office labor been substituted for production labor? Or has office labor gone into unmeasured product or service improvements or manufacturing? All are plausible, but none can be selected based on current evidence.

There are some cases in which office OPH can be expected to behave like total OPH, almost always increasing when things are going well. However, with the reasoning given above, this could only happen in parts of the economy where office-non-office substitution is plausibly expected to be small. And this means divisions of the economy in which office labor is dominant and non-office labor is few in number. In looking for cases in which measurement problems are small and the percentage of office workers is high, note the examples given earlier: commercial banking, communications, and office-intensive functions of the U.S. federal government. In all three cases, OOPH has shown a strong pattern of productivity growth.

## Is Information Technology to Blame?

Even if office productivity was proved to be stagnant, could poor information technology investments reasonably be blamed? Many statements regarding office crises mention large spending on IT (e.g., Roach, 1985). Certainly, IT spending is considerable in the economy. It has stabilized in recent years—at approximately a third of all capital investment by business (Roach, 1989). But this still does not answer the question of whether IT spending is large enough to produce measurable changes in office productivity.

## The size of expected impacts

While capital investment is important, it does not necessarily produce very large gains. In Table 1 (capital-labor productivity) capital substitution only increased OPH by 0.6 percent (not 6 percent) per year for private non-farm business and by only 0.67 percent in manufacturing.

Table 2 looks specifically at manufacturing, where capital substitution impacts are expected to be large. But the table shows that capital substitution increased output per hour by only

0.54 percent per year from 1949 to 1983 and by 0.69 percent from 1973 to 1983. The Norsworthy and Malmquist (1985) study shows even smaller amounts of capital substitution in U.S. manufacturing, but their data were collected primarily during the productivity doldrums of the 1970s.

To pick a single number, it appears that current levels of U.S. capital spending produce about a 0.6 percent increase in output per hour for total labor through substitution. This is a fairly small impact. For office technology, the impact is likely to be even smaller, if total capital spending increases OPH by 0.6 percent per year, and if high-tech capital spending is a third of all spending, then, as expected, high-tech spending would increase total OPH by only about 0.2 percent per year.

As noted above, office workers account for roughly half of all workers. So if all information technology is aimed at office workers—and this is by no means certain—capital spending on IT should increase the OPH growth per office worker about 0.4 percent per year. Even compounded, this would be only 4.1 percent in a decade or 8.3 percent in 20 years.

Some would argue that IT's impact might be larger, because IT capital investments generally have shorter lives than many other capital investments like buildings and would therefore need a higher rate of return to be acceptable. But many other capital purchases have lifetimes of about five years, and even with substantially higher rates of return, it is difficult to expect IT capital investment to increase the OPH of office workers more than about 10 percent in a decade. Given the measurement and interpretation problems discussed earlier, it is doubtful that such small annual impacts could be separated from the “noise” in the analysis.

This weak link between impacts and technology spending has also been noted by Roemer (1988), who said, “Asking why the vast investment in computers has not had a discernible impact on productivity growth is a little like asking why the pull of gravity is not noticeably stronger when the moon is on the other side of the earth than it is when the moon is above us” (p. 428).

In the past, capital investment in information technology was even lower. Roach (1985; 1987a) gives some good estimates for information technology growth and general capital growth and confirms that IT's share of capital investment was smaller in the 1970s and early 1980s than it is today. Thus, for studies using data from before the late 1980s, ascribing any measured impact to IT capital investment must be considered very carefully.

## Impacts in high-IT industries

Of course information technology is not uniformly spread across industries. In some industries, where the high-tech capital endowment is high and is growing rapidly, the impact of technology may be measurable. (If endowment is low, no impact can be expected no matter how rapid the growth.) And growth is needed because productivity series measure productivity growth. If endowment is high but not growing, little OPH growth can be expected because of information technology.

Roach (1987a) has studied IT investments extensively. In particular, he has built indices of both IT endowment and IT growth at the division (and slightly finer) level. His results indicate that there are two technology-rich industries: finance and insurance, and communications. Finance and insurance has 2.2 times the IT capital per worker as the average U.S. industry, and IT growth has been 2.7 times the average. This makes finance and insurance well positioned to show technology-induced OPH growth. Communications, in turn, has 4.3 times the average IT capital intensity. Although its IT capital growth rate is only 60 percent of the national average, its base is large enough to make even this slower growth rate produce a larger-than-average impact on the division's performance.

As discussed previously, productivity growth has been high in commercial banking—the one specific industry in finance and insurance where productivity data are available—and high in communications. In addition, moving beyond productivity to the broader world of financial performance, other evidence exists that IT spending is effective. A number of studies have related IT spending and financial performance in the insurance industry (q.v. Harris and Katz, 1990). Except for some early studies, which were done when IT capital spending was lower, these studies have shown strong links between IT spending and profitability. While this is not directly applicable to productivity effects for IT, the real issue is the appropriateness of IT spending, not specifically its impact on productivity alone.

## Conclusions

Is office productivity stagnant, and is information technology to blame? The primary conclusion of this article is that if IT were taken to court, it would be acquitted.

First, it is by no means clear that the victim is really dead. Productivity is difficult to measure in general, and office output per hour can only be measured with some validity in industries where the percentage of office workers in the work force is very high and where output measurement problems are low. Roach's (1987a) evidence for stagnant office productivity comes primarily from divisions of the economy where productivity measurement accuracy in general is poor. His evidence also stems from situations where substitution is a priori as likely to increase office output per hour by supporting office workers with technology as it is to decrease office output per hour by substituting office workers for non-office workers. Under these conditions, measured office output per hour is an inappropriate statistic.

Only three collections of industries meet the more strict criteria for measurement: commercial banking, communications, and office-intensive functions of the U.S. federal government. All three have experienced substantial productivity growth. Thus, in the few cases where measurement is likely to be meaningful, there is no evidence of stagnant productivity.

Second, even if a body was found, IT has a good alibi. While IT is certainly large in relative terms, it accounts for only a small portion of all office spending. Even if all IT expenditures went to office workers and furthermore, went only toward productivity improvement, the impact would be too small to measure, except in a few specific industries. In the few places where IT spending is high and where output per hour can be measured with good accuracy—namely in commercial banking and communications—significant productivity growth has been seen. In addition, researchers looking beyond productivity to profitability have seen strong relationships between IT spending and profitability in the IT-intensive insurance industry.

Unfortunately, this picture is merely negative. It says that IT would not be convicted in a court of law, or in proper academic debate. But is office productivity increasing, decreasing, or staying the same? The simple fact is, we don't know. Office productivity is certainly a critical concern that should not be minimized. This study has not proven that office productivity is not stagnant. It has merely argued that existing analyses claiming stagnant office productivity are not supported.

## Recommendations

The most desirable recommendation would be to conduct definitive studies of office productivity. Unfortunately, it does not appear that the problems involved in office productivity measurement will be overcome soon. Technology spending will rise, strengthening the plausibility of relationships between IT spending and performance. But measurement and interpretation problems will continue to be severe in BLS data. While the BLS could provide further insight by expanding its industry studies in FIRE and services, it is not likely to happen to the degree needed for a conclusive case. While studies to assess directions for substitution in IT could be done, they do not appear to be imminent.

More fundamentally, it is questionable whether it really makes any sense to talk about “the impact of IT” for the total economy or large segments of the economy. How one uses IT would seem to be far more important than simply how much one spends. No “technological imperative” produces impacts. IT impacts, to the extent that they occur, come in the form of thousands or even millions of individual IT projects where benefits are laboriously built instead of received automatically from some deus ex machina.

Perhaps, given measurement principles and the non-automatic nature of payoffs, it is time to stop conducting general IT impact studies for the entire economy and instead, consider studies of the management of IT impacts for departments, individual firms, and individual users. While more than a dozen attempts have been made to build such a theory (Panko, 1991), a good deal of work still needs to be done.

However, when a complete theory emerges, the focus will probably be on producing IT impacts deliberately, rather than merely measuring them. Ultimately, measurement is likely to be a problem-finding and feedback mechanism within a more general impact engineering process. Fundamentally, the wrong questions have been asked. The real question for everyone is “how do we produce the impacts we wish to have?”
