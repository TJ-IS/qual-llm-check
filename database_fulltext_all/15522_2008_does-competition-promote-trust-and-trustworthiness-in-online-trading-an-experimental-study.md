Ab strac t: We investigate whether greater market competition improves or inhibits the ability of feedback systems in Internet markets to deliver trust and trustworthiness to the marketplace. Our investigation is grounded in the theory of signaling from information economics. Using methods from experimental economics, we create a laboratory online market where sellers face a moral hazard. We manipulate the level of market competition and the nature of the social network behind the feedback system and study the affect on trust, trustworthiness, and market efficiency. We find that competition in strangers networks, where market encounters are one-shot and reputation information is communicated through outside parties, improves trust, trustworthiness, and market efficiency. The efficiency advantage that partners networks, where a buyer can maintain a repeated relationship with a seller, have over strangers networks largely vanishes with the introduction of competition. This is because the difference in the pattern of social networking largely disappears. Overall, encouraging competition leads to more effective feedback systems in Internet markets. We discuss implications for trader strategy and Internet market design.

Key words and phrases: competitive markets, experimental economics, feedback systems, information economics, moral hazard, online markets, reputation, signaling theory, social networks, trust.

Well-func tioning markets, be they brick-and-mortar or Internet, must strike a delicate balance between competition and cooperation. Competition is a main driver of the social benefits associated with trade. But these benefits can be realized only if traders cooperate by making good on the agreement, for example, delivering items as promised. Many Internet markets elicit this cooperation through feedback systems. These gather information about the past dealings of individual traders in the social network underlying the market, and then make this information available to all traders. In doing so, feedback systems aim to facilitate cooperation by inducing both trust and trustworthiness among traders.

Our research question is whether greater market competition improves or diminishes the ability of Internet feedback systems to deliver trust and trustworthiness to the marketplace. The answer has implications for trader strategy and Internet market design. Signaling models derived from principles of information economics exhibit equilibria with a high degree of trust and trustworthiness, as well as ones with a very low degree. So achieving a high degree of trust and trustworthiness is a matter of what traders can manage to coordinate. By trust, we point to an individual’s willingness to commit to do something where the cooperation of other people is crucial to the outcome. Trustworthiness, on the other hand, involves the individual’s capacity to deliver to others, as they expect, and in the process, creates value for the group as a whole.<sup>1</sup> Using methods from experimental economics, we create a laboratory online market where sellers face a moral hazard. We systematically manipulate the level of competition and the nature of the social network behind the feedback system, and then observe how these changes affect trust, trustworthiness, and market efficiency.

Barnes coined the term social network, conceived as “a set of points some of which are joined by lines. The points of the image are people, or sometimes groups, and the lines indicate which people interact with each other” [2, p. 43]. With regard to market trust and trustworthiness, the important interactions are among those who share reputation information, and among those who trade with one another. Granovetter [20, p. 490] discusses two types of marketplace social networks, distinguished by the pattern of interaction through which reputation information gets distributed to traders. The first pattern of interaction is one’s own past dealings with that person, associated with what we will call a partners network because it involves repeated interactions among the same traders. In partners networks, traders cull reputation information from their own trading history. The second kind of social network delivers reputation information through trusted informants. This we will call a strangers network because it is most relevant when interactions are one-shot (not repeated). In strangers networks, traders must rely on reputation information received from other traders.

The advantage of Internet markets is that they enable traders to break through geographical constraints to trade in larger and more competitive pools (e.g., [18, 25, 28]). Transactions tend to be one-shot (e.g., [29]), so that Internet markets are largely strangers networks. To facilitate the flow of reputation information, many Internet markets have formal feedback systems (e.g., Amazon.com, Cnet, eBay, Half.com, and Yahoo), allowing traders to post evaluations of those they exchange with for the benefit of other traders (e.g., [8, 15, 16, 21, 29]). In brick-and-mortar markets, reputation information tends to be carried through networks where people have prior noncommercial relationships [10, 17], often involving advice from trusted opinion leaders [23]. In Internet markets, however, reputation information is carried through impersonal networks in that information posted to the feedback system is made available to all market traders. So both information and trade interactions in strangers networks on Internet markets tend to be more anonymous than in brick-and-mortar markets.

Amazon’s feedback system for the sale of used books provides an illustrative example of how Internet feedback systems work. Amazon invites independent booksellers, private individuals, as well as professional dealers to post their offerings, including price and a description of the book’s condition, on Amazon’s site. In making a purchase, a buyer transmits payment through Amazon, which takes a percentage, while the seller ships directly to the buyer. The moral hazard problems inherent in the seller’s side of the deal—stipulating the book’s condition and the shipping—are addressed through a feedback system in which buyers are invited to rate the transaction on a scale, and post comments that future buyers can view when deciding whether to make a purchase from that seller. The idea behind this system, as with all Internet feedback systems, is to provide traders with the reputation information necessary to pursue tit-for-tat strategies in their trade relationships, punishing those who have been untrustworthy and rewarding those who have been trustworthy.

The economic theory of asymmetric information suggests that how well feedback systems curtail moral hazard is a matter of coordination (see [32] for a review). Information about a trader’s reputation is termed a signal, and signaling is associated with two very different types of market equilibria. In one type, buyer discrimination on the basis of the signal is high: they trust and buy only from sellers with good reputations. For their part, sellers strive to maintain good reputations by being trustworthy. Buyer and seller behavior are mutually reinforcing in that trustworthy sellers reward buying and discriminating buyers reward trustworthiness. This is known as a separating equilibrium and it is associated with a high volume of successful trade and so high market efficiency [9, 34]. But in the second type of equilibrium, buyer discrimination on the basis of the signal is low; they trust and buy little from sellers who, in fact, are generally not trustworthy. Buyer and seller behavior are again mutually reinforcing. This is known as a pooling equilibrium and it is associated with a low volume of trade, so that low market efficiency results [1]. All traders are better off under the high-trade separating equilibrium than under the pooling equilibrium. But the extent they achieve separating or pooling is a matter of how well they coordinate around the signals.

Bolton et al. [5] investigated a laboratory market in which sellers face a moral hazard concerning whether to ship after receiving a buyer’s payment. The only extrinsic motivation for trust and trustworthiness comes from the flow of reputation information through feedback systems and from how this information is used by traders. Sellers were faced with no competition in that a buyer may or may not have been able to buy from just one preassigned seller and the price of a transaction was fixed. In one treatment, the preassigned seller was a long-term partner, in the other treatment, a one-time stranger. While both strangers and partners networks exhibited trust, trustworthiness, and successful trade, these attributes were substantially higher under the partners networks. So the strangers networks common to Internet markets exhibited a performance gap relative to the partners networks prevalent in brick-and-mortar markets.

The data analysis showed that one of the key reasons for the gap is that, relative to partners networks, buyers in strangers networks have less incentive to trust because the value of the reputation information produced by trusting (or buying) goes to someone other than oneself. Likewise, sellers in strangers networks have less incentive to be trustworthy. The cost of disappointing a buyer, resulting in less future business, is partially borne by other sellers. So, in sum, there are fewer incentives for trust and trustworthiness in strangers networks than in partners networks.

Competition acts on incentives and arguably could help to increase performance in markets with strangers networks and might even narrow the performance gap. For example, buyers having multiple sellers to choose from (where there is matching competition but a fixed price) increase the incentive for sellers to be trustworthy, as reputation is a factor buyers will surely take into consideration. This in turn could encourage more buying, with the ultimate consequence of more transactions. Yet this positive outcome is not a foregone conclusion, if only because a lot of market competition includes competition on price. Price competition, if it squeezes hard on profit margins, might diminish seller incentives to be trustworthy, leading to less buying and ultimately fewer transactions.

The experiment we present here extends the work of Bolton et al. [5] to an investigation of the effects of competition on both strangers and partners networks. We will see that competition, including price competition, increases the effectiveness of strangers networks and, in fact, closes the gap with partners networks. So competition appears an important, positive contributor to the effectiveness of the feedback systems that Internet markets rely upon.

## Experimental Design

## Background of Experimental Economics Approach

The ultimate objec tive of our researc h ag enda is to understand how to make feedback systems in Internet markets more effective. Theory suggests that fostering competition might be one way of doing so. The advantage of an experimental approach in an investigation such as this is that the laboratory provides a test bed in which we can construct a market with the desired specifications and then sharply manipulate the factors we wish to study, to see how they interact and ultimately affect trade. In this way, we gain insight into what makes for a better trading system. Laboratory experiments have aided in the design of trading institutions including online auction platforms [24], the Federal Communications Commission’s spectrum auctions, and the American Medical Association’s Resident Matching Program [33].

Our experiment manipulates two factors—competition and social network. We look at three competition conditions: (1) a buyer chooses whether to trade with one seller based on reputation (no competition), (2) a buyer chooses from two sellers based on their individual reputations but price is fixed (matching competition), and (3) a buyer chooses between two sellers based on price offers as well as reputation (price competition). We look at two social networks—strangers and partners. Our experiment has a 3 × 2 full factor design; that is, we cross all conditions of each factor to create six distinct kinds of markets, which we discuss in detail below. Of course, not all of these markets will have immediate field interest; for example, there may be few field markets with stranger networking and only matching competition. But it is the systematic manipulation of factors, moving conditions in and out of the market, that enables us to identify causal relationships and so to gauge the impact the factor has on market performance.

The traders in our experiment are college students who are given financial incentives to trade well. The use of financial incentives to motivate subjects is one of the hallmarks of experimental economics [14]. Laboratory experiments using financial incentives have been used to investigate decision-making problems not only in economics (e.g., [22]) but in psychology (e.g., [35]) and business (e.g., [4]) as well. Laboratory studies often use college students as a first step in a research program. If we know that students can solve the problem at hand well, then it increases our confidence that the general population can do so; at the very least, it shows that there are circumstances under which people can solve the problem. If students do poorly, the data may provide insight into the difficulty. Either way, the initial experiments provide the baseline for follow-up studies that either pursue elaborations of the problem or check robustness with regard to issues such as the population sampled (e.g., [11]). In this particular instance, college students are arguably an apt group to sample because they are commonly familiar with and engaged in Internet markets.

## Overview of the Six Treatments

The experiment has six treatments (see Table 1). Each treatment concerns a particular type of market. In all markets, transactions transpire over 15 rounds, each round beginning with the matching of buyers and sellers for the purpose of trade. The markets in each treatment are distinguished along two dimensions. The first dimension is the type of network—a partners network or a strangers network. In markets in a partners network, each buyer can interact exclusively with a single seller if they choose so, and so the traders can effectively pair for the duration of the market. In a strangers network, buyers and sellers interact at most once, and so traders effectively rotate pairings through the market. The second dimension is competition, with no competition, matching competition, or price competition. In markets with no competition, buyers have no choice with whom they are matched, although they can choose not to buy from the matched seller. In markets with matching competition, buyers choose between two sellers on the basis of reputation information only; the price is fixed. In markets with price competition, buyers choose between two sellers on the basis of both reputation information and price offers.

No subject participated in more than one treatment of the experiment; each treatment is done in a single session. Within each treatment, subjects participated in two runs of the associated market. This enabled a check for experience effects.

## Treatments Where Markets Have No Competition

At the beginning of the laboratory session, participants are assigned to buyer and seller roles, with an equal number in each role. The roles are fixed for the entire experiment. The no competition treatments look at the strangers versus partners result exhibited in Bolton et al. [5] in a slightly different experimental context. The authors had participants rotate between buyer and seller roles; here we fix the roles. Data from the present experiment will show that the stranger–partner gap identified in the earlier study is robust to this feature.

Traders interface by computer. In each of the 15 rounds of a market, a buyer and a seller are matched to interact as illustrated in Figure 1.

At the beginning of the round, both the seller and the buyer have an income of 35 laboratory francs (35F). The seller offers an item for sale at a fixed price of 35F. The item has a value of 50F to the buyer. The seller’s cost of providing the buyer with the item (i.e., the costs associated with executing the trade, shipping, handling, etc., as well as production costs) is 20F. These are production costs where either the seller produces the item after he or she knows the demand, or the product is produced before the buyer’s decision is known but costs are not sunk (e.g., when the item can be resold at a price equal to production costs). Beyond the reasons given in the text, the precise amounts (35F, 50F, etc.) were chosen to give subjects appropriate incentives to trade, and to maintain comparability with other studies of buyer–seller games as surveyed by Bolton et al. [6].

## Treatments Where Markets Have Matching Competition

To investigate the impact of matching competition, we modify the baseline buyer– seller interaction to allow the buyer, in each round, to choose between two sellers on the basis of reputation histories. As before, the buyer can also choose not to buy (see Figure 2).

In the partners network, after the first-round market, the buyer chooses between the seller he or she last bought from and a new seller he or she was not previously matched with. So the buyer can always choose to maintain a longer relationship with a seller. But in each round, he or she can also choose to switch to a new seller. Hence, matching competition in the partners network does not necessarily imply a lasting partners relationship, but it allows buyers to build one. Buyers who are not chosen in one round become alternates to partners in the succeeding round.

In the corresponding strangers network, however, lasting buyer–seller relationships cannot be developed. After the first round, the buyer chooses between the seller he or she was last matched with but did not buy from, and a new seller he or she was not previously matched with. Hence, buyers cannot do repeat business with the same seller because they always have to choose between two sellers they have not previously traded with.

In these treatments, two-thirds of the participants are assigned roles as sellers and onethird roles as buyers. Sellers are not shown the profile of the seller they are competing with. In all other respects, the setup and procedures for the treatments with matching competition are the same as for the treatments without matching competition.

## Treatments Where Markets Have Price Competition

To investigate the impact of price competition in strangers and partners networks, we follow the same procedure as above. In addition, sellers must simultaneously post a selling price anywhere in the range from 0F to 100F for the buyer to see prior to choosing a seller (see Figure 3). As a result, a buyer can choose between two sellers, or not to buy at all, on the basis of both reputation and price information. A seller’s reputation profile includes only information about his or her shipping history, not his or her past price postings. (See Appendix B.) So price competition always allows buyers to select sellers according to their reputation, as in matching competition, but adds price as another dimension of the competition. In all other respects, the setup and procedures for the treatments with price competition are the same as for the treatments with matching competition.

## Data Collection

In all, 216 subjects participated in the experiment with 36 subjects in each treatment. No subject participated in more than one treatment. Subjects were students, mostly undergraduates, from various fields of study. They volunteered through an online recruitment system. Cash was their only incentive to participate.

Upon arrival in the laboratory, participants were seated at computers, separated by partitions. They were then asked to read the instructions. When all were finished, the experimenter read the instructions out loud in order to enter them into public knowledge. To get familiar with the software, subjects played several practice games, sometimes as buyer and sometimes as seller, with the computer in the opposite role making its moves at random.

Upon completion of the treatment, one of the two markets was chosen at random and each subject was privately paid his or her earnings for that market in cash plus a \$5 show-up fee. The exchange rate for earnings was \$0.02 per franc. Total earnings per subject ranged from \$5 to \$20 with an average of \$15.80.

## Data Analysis

We b eg in the analysis b y looking at how the treatments compare with respect to observed gains from trade. We then look specifically at trust and trustworthiness, as measured by rates of buying and selling. Finally, we look at the influence of price competition.

Within any treatment of the experiment, we do not observe any statistically significant experience effects in play across the two runs of the market, as we reported earlier. Specifically, within each market, we ran contingency table tests comparing frequency of trades per round across early and late runs of the market, with the fifteenth and last round excluded due to low frequency of trade. No test was significant at any standard level. For this reason, in the following analysis, we do not distinguish between the first and second markets within treatment, but aggregate the data.

## Gains from Trade

The ultimate market measure of whether competition increases or diminishes the effectiveness of a social reputation network is the net effect on gains from trade. For each of the six treatments, Figure 4 displays the gains from trade as a percentage of the maximum achievable. The maximum achievable gains from trade are the total payoffs achieved if all possible transactions are successfully completed minus the total payoff achieved if there are no buys. The gains are further broken out into amounts received by sellers and by buyers. They show differences across treatments, both within and across network configurations. In particular, the total gains from trade are quite different across strangers and partners when there is no competition, but similar when there is price competition. As competition increases, the pattern of distribution of gains from trade among buyers and sellers differs across strangers and partners networks.

The data, indicated in the column headings of table, are censored in that the frequency of transaction one can observe is limited by the number of opportunities we provided traders in the experiment. We draw three main observations from Figure 4 and Table 2:

Observation 1 (Effects of Matching and Price Competition in Strangers Networks on Gains from Trade): In strangers networks, relative to no competition, both matching and price competition increase the total gains from trade by about the same amount. Relative to no competition, buyers gain from both types of competition while sellers lose from price competition.

Relative to a strangers network without competition, the introduction of matching competition in a strangers network significantly increases total gains from trade by 41 percent (0.237/0.576). The further addition of price competition dampens these gains by a small and insignificant amount (as measured by the Price coefficient). The total gains from price competition are still significantly greater than in case of no competition (Wald test, two-tailed $p { < } 0 . 0 0 1 $ ). The gains from matching competition primarily go to buyers, a significant 154 percent increase (0.146/0.095) over no competition; the analogous gains to sellers are small and insignificant. Adding price to matching competition further increases buyer gains by a small, insignificant amount, but sellers incur a significant 29 percent decrease (–0.126/0.435).

Observation 2 (Effects of Matching and Price Competition in Partners Networks): In partners networks, relative to no competition, matching competition increases the total gains from trade; these same gains are erased by the addition of price competition. Relative to no competition, buyers gain from both types of competition while sellers lose from price competition.

In partners networks, the total gains from trade from adding matching competition are 9 percent $( ( 0 . 2 3 7 - 0 . 1 6 2 ) / ( 0 . 5 7 6 + 0 . 2 1 6 ) )$ and weakly significant (Wald, two-tailed $p = 0 . 0 5 6 )$ ). The total gains from adding price competition, relative to no competition, are negative $( - 0 . 0 5 = 0 . 2 3 7 - 0 . 0 3 5 - 0 . 1 6 2 - 0 . 0 9 1 )$ but not significant (Wald, two-tailed $p = 0 . 1 9 5 )$ . We can see from Table 2 that buyers capture significant gains from adding matching competition with no significant effect on sellers. Further adding pricing competition has no significant effect on buyers but significantly reduces what sellers capture.

Observation 3 (Performance Gap Between Strangers and Partners Networks): Introducing either matching or price competition erases the significant performance gap between strangers and partners networks.

Consistent with the findings of Bolton et al. [5], with no competition, the total gains from trade in partners networks are 38 percent (0.216/0.576) higher than in strangers networks. The coefficient of the partners variable shows the difference to be highly significant. The result can not be explained by differences in the communication channel between buyers and sellers (e.g., [7, 12, 16, 31]), or by the distances or anonymity between buyers and sellers [19]. Both were kept constant across treatments. When we add matching competition to treatments without competition, however, the total gains from trade in partners networks are 5 percent $( 0 . 2 1 6 - 0 . 1 6 2 )$ higher than in strangers networks, but not significantly so (Wald test, two-tailed $p = 0 . 2 0 7 )$ ). When we add price competition, the difference between partners and strangers networks completely disappears: total gains from trade in the partners networks appear to be less now than those in strangers network by 3 percent, but this difference is not significant (Wald test, two-tailed $p = 0 . 3 9 3 )$ .

To summarize these results, we observe that the addition of competition among sellers, be it matching or price, has much the same effect on trader role-shares that elementary economic theory would lead us to expect—buyers gain and sellers lose. However, in the strangers networks overall gains from trade increase from both types of competition, whereas they change little for partners networks. The net result is that the performance gap disappears.

Table 3 sheds further light on why it vanishes. In partners networks without competition, the buyer cannot switch away from his or her assigned seller. In the partners networks with competition, the buyer can and does switch 19 percent of the time under matching competition, and this behavior rises to 42 percent under price competition. The temptations offered by matching competition, and especially price competition, effectively break down partnering. That is, with the addition of competition, the transaction patterns in partners networks look like those in strangers networks. The performance gap vanishes because competition causes the difference in the pattern of social networking to vanish.

Having observed the pattern in gains from trade, we turn next to examining the underlying trust and trustworthy behavior in these markets, represented by the actions of buying and selling. ## The Influence of Price Competition

One might have thought that price competition would weaken seller trustworthiness because competition pushes the incentives for trustworthiness lower. But, in fact, trustworthiness under price competition diminishes only somewhat, and the gains from trade under price competition are not appreciably different than under matching competition within either partners or strangers networks.

Observation 6 (Effects of Price Competition on Both Partners and Strangers Networks): Buyer choice of seller in the price competition markets is heavily weighted toward reputation over price. Prices stay well above the zero profit levels implied by perfect competition.

We begin by comparing pricing in our markets with elementary economic theory. If we ignore the moral hazard issue for a moment, and assume that sellers always ship, then the economic theory of perfect competition implies that competition for buyers should push seller price offers to 35F (the marginal cost of producing). Figure 7 shows the average prices per round in the price competition markets. The average chosen price was 43,00F for the strangers network and 44,70F for the partners network. Even if we restrict attention to rounds when price patterns have settled down, rounds 6 through 15, the observed round averages are significantly higher than the competitive price of 35F (t-test, n = 10 rounds, two-tailed $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ for both networks). Average round prices across strangers and partners networks, rounds 6 through 15, are not significantly different both for chosen prices comparison as well as not chosen prices comparison (t-test, n = 10 rounds, two-tailed $p = 0 . 1 6 2$ and $p = 0 . 2 5 1$ , respectively). Also displayed in Figure 7, once prices have settled down (again rounds 6 through 15), price is not much of an indicator of selection: within markets, the average chosen price per round is not notably different than the average not chosen price per round (t-test, $n = 1 0$ rounds, two-tailed $p = 0 . 0 9 6$ for strangers and $p = 0 . 5 0 4$ for partners).

We next look directly at the effects of reputation information. Table 5 shows that frequency of buyer choice is always higher with better reputation independent of price. A combination of worse price and better reputation tends to be selected by buyers over combinations of better price and same, respectively worse, reputation. While a quick read of Table 5 would suggest that there is no clear tendency to choose the better price when the feedback score is the same, keep in mind that no single measure of reputation is likely to capture how every person judges the better reputation. For example, some people may weight recent seller behavior differently than earlier behavior, and people who do so may use different weighting schemes. Hence, what looks like indifference in the table may not look precisely like that to everyone. That said, the basic results reported here are robust to other simple measures of reputation, such as the number of ships minus the number of no ships, patterned after eBay’s feedback score.

## Conclusion

The researc h question that we pursued in this paper is how greater market competition influences the ability of Internet feedback systems to deliver trust and trustworthiness to the marketplace. We finish by summarizing our main findings from the laboratory experiment and then discuss how these findings apply to Internet markets in the field. We also offer some limitations and future research directions.

## Summary of the Main Findings

As we explained in the introduction, the key to reputation information creating trust, trustworthiness, and trade efficiency in the marketplace is buyer discrimination on the basis of the information. Calculating over all our markets, buyer trust is rewarded by trustworthiness 88 percent of the time, indicative of the high signal value of reputation. The regression involving buyer choice presented in this paper shows how remarkably discriminating buyers are with regard to reputation information even in the face of price competition. When given the choice of two sellers, it takes a large price break to convince the average buyer to do business with the seller of lesser reputation.

We find that adding competition—either through matching competition or price competition—to strangers networks, the kind of networks most associated with Internet markets, significantly increases gains from trade by promoting trust and trustworthiness. The addition of matching competition complements the strangers network in disciplining seller behavior. When we add price competition, transaction prices stabilize at a level well above marginal production costs, and so building a good reputation continues to be a profitable strategy.

We also observe that competition largely erases the advantage of partners networks over strangers networks in promoting trade. The reason for this is twofold. First, while competition (again via matching or price) increases the effectiveness of strangers networks, it has a far smaller influence on the effectiveness of partners networks. Second, when given the chance, buyers switch sellers quite often, so that with the introduction of competition, the difference in the pattern of social networking dissipates. This further supports our conclusion that competition is a powerful tool for promoting trust and trustworthiness in strangers environments such as Internet market platforms. With competition, the performance of the strangers networks common to Internet markets is similar to the partners networks that are more prevalent to brickand-mortar markets.

## Discussion

The setup of our laboratory markets reflects the factors that signaling theory in information economics tells us are in general the factors critical to building reputation. This theory is well established [32], and in this sense, our results should extend to a broad swath of Internet markets in the field (with some caveats).

The willingness to pay exhibited by buyers in the present study as well as in the eBay field study by Resnick et al. [30] suggests that there are strong returns to Internet sellers who invest in a reputation for reliability. In both studies, sellers who are reliable receive substantially higher prices. The same result suggests that Internet market operators would do well to equip their sites with trader search capabilities that permit sorting on reputation factors as well as price. This could have the double advantage of outfitting buyers with trust information they have a demonstrated interest in, and spurring sellers, who know that this information is easier to evaluate, to provide more reliable, trustworthy service.

Our results also suggest that, all other things equal, markets with a higher degree of competition are likely to be safer markets. Internet market feedback systems can be made safer by lowering barriers that impede competition. Finally, we note that markets with repeated relationships via partners networks without competition perform on a par with strangers networks including competition. So, in markets with unavoidably low competition, one way to increase the safety of the market is to encourage longer-term buyer–seller relationships, perhaps by making it easier to find one’s old transaction partners in the market.

## Limitations and Future Research

Probably the most important limitation of the present study has to do with the nature of the relationship traders had in our market. Specifically, in both partners and strangers networks, traders were, by design, anonymous to one another beyond what they could see of the others’ trading history. This reflects a rather common circumstance in many Internet markets, but not all of them. One direction for future research would be to consider markets where subgroups of traders have relationships outside of the marketplace as is the case in markets for certain types of items (see examples in McMillan [26]). One hypothesis would be that partnering tendencies are stronger in these kinds of networks, and so might exhibit greater trust and trustworthiness than comparable strangers networks even when both kinds of markets exhibit comparable levels of competition. Mobius et al. [27] also present evidence that is suggestive of this hypothesis.

In the present study, we considered competitive markets in which each buyer could choose between two sellers. An interesting extension would be to consider markets where there are even more sellers to choose from. On one hand, the greater selection pressure might spur sellers to greater trustworthiness. On the other hand, the greater pressure on price might lead to lower profit margins and so lower incentives for trustworthiness. Along these same lines, it would be interesting to consider markets where sellers have distinctive products or services for sales, impeding competition, or multiple homogeneous items for sale, which would allow more attractive sellers to grab more of the market.

Finally, it would be good to complement the present lab experiment with a version done under field conditions with traders experienced in the field market. The advantage of a lab experiment is that the control that the laboratory affords permits sharp manipulations of variables such as the nature of the social network. In field experiments, we surrender some of this control but gain external validity.

For exogenous partner markets: You will be matched with the same trading partner every round. For endogenous partners: In each round, the buyer is matched with the “previous seller” and a “new seller.” The previous seller is the last seller the buyer chose to buy from in previous rounds. The new seller is a seller that the buyer was not matched with in the previous round. In round 1, both sellers are new sellers (if neither is chosen then one is arbitrarily designated the previous seller in the succeeding round).] All partner pairings are anonymous: Your identity will not be revealed to the person you are playing with either before, during, or after the game.

## Seller’s Feedback History

For each round, the computer will record whether selected sellers chose ship or not ship (if the seller did not get to decide, the computer records nothing). This feedback will then be made available to all future buyers that are matched with this seller. The feedback will include a summary of the number of times the seller shipped in the past, as well as a round-by-round history of their shipping decisions, beginning with the most recent decision. Buyers will see this feedback history prior to making their buy decision.

## Number of Markets

You will participate in two markets, each with 15 rounds, one market played after the other. You will have the same role, buyer or seller, in both markets. But the order in which you interact with trading partners will be different in the second market than in the first. Information about seller decisions in the first market will not be available to participants during the second market.

## Money Earnings

You will be paid your earnings for one market. The market to be paid will be selected by a random draw of a card at the end of the session. Each market has an equal chance of being selected, so you should do the best you can in both markets. The laboratory francs you earn for the selected market will be exchanged at a rate of \$0.02 per franc. You will be paid the resulting sum plus a \$5 show-up fee in cash.

## Practice Games

When the monitor gives the okay, play some practice games. Your partner for the practice games will be the computer. It has been programmed to choose its moves at random. The practice games will allow you to experience the game from both the buyer and seller’s perspective. Practice until you feel comfortable with the game and its rules. Information about your decisions will not be used or displayed to other participants during the actual markets.
