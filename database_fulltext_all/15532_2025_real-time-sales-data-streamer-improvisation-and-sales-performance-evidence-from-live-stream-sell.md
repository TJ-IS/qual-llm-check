# REAL-TIME SALES DATA, STREAMER IMPROVISATION, AND SALES PERFORMANCE: EVIDENCE FROM LIVE STREAM SELLING<sup>1</sup>

Yumei He Management Science Area, A. B. Freeman School of Business, Tulane University New Orleans, LA U.S.A. {yhe17@tulane.edu}

Ni Huang Department of Business Technology, Miami Herbert Business School, University of Miami Coral Gables, FL, U.S.A. {nhuang@miami.edu}

Lingli Wang Department of Economic Information Management, School of Information, Renmin University of China Beijing China {wang.ll@ruc.edu.cn}

Yan Sun DAMO Academy, Alibaba Group Hangzhou, China {sunyan.sy@alibaba-inc.com}

Live stream selling has surged as an innovative form of e-commerce, supporting high social interactivity and fast-paced sales for large-scale audiences. However, live streamers must manage complex, concurrent tasks— such as presenting products, engaging with viewers, and employing various sales tactics to facilitate consumer purchases, while dealing with uncertainty in foreseeing viewers’ reactions and time pressure for selling products. This study examines the role of real-time sales data in influencing streamers’ impromptu decisionmaking and sales outcomes in live stream selling. On the one hand, real-time sales data might exacerbate the already complex decision-making, overwhelming and distracting streamers. On the other hand, it may help streamers improvise their tactics and utilize time more efficiently and effectively to improve sales performance. In partnership with a leading global live stream selling platform, we analyzed data from a randomized field experiment to unravel whether, how, and under which conditions the provision of real-time sales data affects live stream sales performance. The streamers in the experiment were randomly assigned to either the treatment group, which had access to real-time sales data for presale products, or the control group, which had no such access. Our results reveal that product sales of presale products in the treatment group increased by approximately 40.21% compared to those in the control group. Further, the observed main effect can be explained by streamers’ data-driven improvisation in promotional, linguistic, and presentational pace tactics. Moreover, heterogeneity analyses reveal that the positive effect of real-time data on sales performance is more salient for streamers with likely better improvisational skills and for products with higher uncertainty, thereby providing suggestive evidence of streamer improvisation as the underlying mechanism. This study contributes to the literature on data-driven decision-making, evidencing the importance of real-time data for enhancing decision-making processes and outcomes. Our findings also offer guidance for implementing real-time data infrastructure in live stream selling platforms.

Keywords: Live stream selling, data-driven decision-making, real-time sales data, improvisation theory, randomized field experiment

## Introduction

Live stream selling,<sup>2</sup> which supports real-time sales activities streamed on the internet with a streamer hosting live sales events and interacting with viewers, has emerged as an innovative e-commerce model in recent years (Ki et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2023). For example, the market size of live stream selling in the United States is expected to grow by 35% in 2026 (Yltävä, 2025), and China’s live stream selling market is projected to reach approximately 1.12 trillion USD by 2026 (Ma, 2024). Such trends have led industry experts to consider live stream selling as the “next wave of the e-commerce revolution” (Arora et al., 2021) and “the future trend of ecommerce” (Shacknai, 2021).

In live stream selling, a streamer hosts live sales events in a virtual room by demonstrating products, interacting with the audience, and employing various sales tactics<sup>3</sup> to encourage consumer purchases (Feng et al., 2024; Song et al., 2022). Compared to traditional e-commerce that relies on archival text, images, and pre-recorded videos for product information provision, live stream selling features real-time videostreaming, and streamer-viewer interactions (Fei et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022a). In contrast to TV-home shopping, wherein the sales videos are often pre-recorded, rehearsed, edited, and follow predetermined scripts (Ki et al., 2024), live stream selling requires streamers to interact with a large-scale audience, acting on the fly and responding to viewer inquiries in real-time (Lytle et al., 2020).<sup>4</sup> Therefore, the effectiveness of live streams largely hinges on how the streamer improvises in unforeseen circumstances (Wu et al., 2023).

Prior research suggests that historical data can be a useful resource for planned decision-making, which involves analyzing archival data to develop plans for the future (e.g., Berman & Israeli, 2022; Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016). However, the challenges posed by the fast-paced, complex, and uncertain nature of live stream selling limit the usefulness of historical records in streamers’ real-time decision-making. Streamers face multitasking challenges in live stream selling that require them to quickly adapt their decision-making processes. For example, streamers need to simultaneously present products, respond to viewer inquiries, and facilitate transactions in live stream selling (Gao et al., 2023). Additionally, the real-time interactions in live stream selling introduce uncertainty because viewers’ behaviors are unpredictable, thereby requiring streamers to improvise in the moment (Li et al., 2022). Further, streamers often operate under time pressure as they need to complete product presentations and sales within a limited time frame (Guo et al., 2021).

This study seeks to understand the value of real-time sales data in live stream selling. Real-time sales data refers to sales data (e.g., transactions, revenues) that is made available simultaneously as it is generated and processed (Hackathorn, 2004). In live stream selling, real-time sales data can be displayed in the streamer’s sales dashboard during live streams, thereby enabling streamers to monitor product sales as they occur. Unlike historical data that describes past events, real-time sales data provides streamers with up-to-themoment information regarding product sales and consumer purchase behaviors. This can be particularly helpful in impromptu decision-making scenarios, which are characterized by unforeseen situations, multitasking complexity, and time pressure (Lerch & Harter, 2001).

Note that it remains unclear whether and how real-time sales data influences streamer behavior and sales performance. On the one hand, given the already complex, hectic decisionmaking environment in live stream selling, processing realtime sales data could further deplete the streamers’ cognitive resources, thereby potentially leading to information overload and distraction from their primary activities (Gonzalez, 2005). Further, under time pressure, streamers might ignore real-time sales data and continue to follow their past experiences to make decisions (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Yu, 2016). On the other hand, providing real-time sales data might be beneficial for streamers to make informed decisions, improvise on their sales activities to meet constantly changing circumstances, and utilize their limited time more efficiently and effectively to enhance sales effectiveness. Taken together, we seek to empirically answer the following research questions:

RQ1: What is the impact of real-time sales data provision on streamer sales performance in live stream selling?

RQ2: What improvisational tactics do streamers utilize given the provision of real-time sales data, and how do those tactics contribute to sales performance in live stream selling?

RQ3: What are the boundary conditions on the effectiveness of real-time sales data provision on sales performance in live stream selling?

In collaboration with Alibaba’s Taobao Live (hereafter referred to as the partner platform), a leading global live stream selling platform, we report the results of a randomized field experiment on the platform. Specifically, the partner platform implemented the experiment over December 1-3,

2020, to assess the effectiveness of providing real-time sales data to streamers on presale products. In the experiment, streamers were randomly assigned to either the treatment group (wherein they had access to real-time sales data on presale products displayed on a streamer-facing dashboard) or the control group (wherein the streamers did not have access to real-time sales data on presale products). In our research context, presale products refer to newly launched products available for pre-order in a live stream before the online merchants finalize production, with order fulfillment and delivery scheduled for a future date (Li & Zhang, 2013). The partner platform focused on presale products in the experiment because such products typically lack or have limited historical data and consumer preference information, such as online reviews (Yu et al., 2015). Offering real-time sales data on presale products in live stream selling allows streamers to gauge consumer interest and help merchants assess consumer demand before committing to production, thereby reducing potential inventory issues.

Our analyses reveal several important findings. First, the sales outcome of presale products in the treatment group significantly increased by approximately 40.21% compared to that in the control group, demonstrating the value of real-time sales data for streamers during live stream selling. Second, our exploration of the underlying mechanisms reveals that the provision of real-time sales data improves sales by influencing streamers’ improvisation in terms of employing various sales tactics. Specifically, we observed that the streamers in the treatment group were more likely to employ: (1) promotional tactics (i.e., urging consumers to place an order), (2) linguistic tactics (i.e., using affect-related words more frequently), and (3) presentational pace tactics (i.e., speeding up the speech and shortening the average streaming time for products). These tactics further facilitate the improvement of sales performance. Furthermore, by exploring the boundary conditions, we found that the positive effect of offering realtime sales data to streamers was more salient for presale products with a higher level of uncertainty (e.g., products carried by small businesses and experience goods) and for streamers who likely possessed better improvisational skills (e.g., top-rated streamers and more popular streamers).

This study contributes to the related literature on data-driven decision-making (e.g., Berman & Israeli, 2022; Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016) and live stream selling (e.g., Zhao et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2022). While prior research on data-driven decision-making has primarily considered the effectiveness of historical data and analytical tools for planned decisionmaking (e.g., David et al., 2016; Hoffman et al., 2018; Lilien et al., 2004), this study is among the first to empirically investigate how individuals leverage real-time data to enable improvisation in their impromptu decision-making process in exigent, fast-paced environments. Given the empirical tension that real-time data can be either distracting/cognitively burdening or helpful, we show that streamers can adapt and improvise on their sales tactics relying on real-time sales data, which in turn improves their sales performance.

Further, our study contributes to the stream of work on live stream selling platforms by examining the effectiveness of an information technology (IT) artifact design—that is, providing real-time data via a streamer-facing dashboard to facilitate streamers’ in-the-moment decision-making. Extant research on live stream selling platforms has explored various characteristics related to streamers, viewers, and streamerviewer interactions that influence streaming outcomes (e.g., Lo et al., 2022; Lu & Chen, 2021; Zeng et al., 2022). Limited work has explored how IT artifact designs can help streamers with real-time decision-making. Our research demonstrates the effectiveness and the underlying mechanisms of real-time sales data that enable and support streamers’ improvisation of sales tactics and boost product sales.

Our findings provide practical implications for streamers, online merchants, and live stream selling platforms. Based on our results, live stream selling platforms should consider investing in the development and integration of real-time data analytical tools to support streamers. Besides, our heterogeneity analyses suggest that platforms should consider streamers’ improvisational skills and product uncertainty when providing real-time sales data. Our results suggest that even though top-rated and popular streamers can benefit from the provision of real-time sales data, non-top-rated and less popular streamers may not. To address this, live stream selling platforms should consider implementing adaptive learning mechanisms and training programs to help those streamers enhance their improvisational skills. Additionally, our findings indicate that real-time data tools are particularly valuable for products with relatively high uncertainty, as measured by products from small businesses or experience goods. Thus, platforms could stress the use of real-time sales data to live streamers for those products where such real-time feedback is particularly informative and useful.

## Literature Review and Theoretical Background

## Data-Driven Decision-Making

This study is related to the literature on data-driven decisionmaking, particularly information technology (IT)-based designs that facilitate individuals’ decision-making processes (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016). Previous studies on datadriven decision-making have primarily focused on the effects of various analytical tools and approaches in influencing decision-making in different contexts (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021). For example, Hoffman et al. (2018) found that presenting the visualized job testing score to human resource managers can enhance hiring decision quality by mitigating decision bias and errors. Berman and Israeli (2022) concluded that retail stores that adopt descriptive dashboards to present historical sales information experienced a rise of 4%-10% in average weekly revenues. Lu et al. (2022) revealed that the historical descriptive chart of rating led to consumers distorted perception of average rating, resulting in inferior choices violating well-established decision rules.

However, limited attention has been paid to understanding how the provision of real-time data affects impromptu decision-making and its subsequent outcomes. Real-time data differs from historical data in several important ways. First, historical data provides insights into past trends and patterns for guiding long-term, planned decisions about the future (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2019). In contrast, real-time data, which is continuously updated and immediately made available, can be particularly useful in facilitating impromptu, on-the-spot decision-making (Lerch & Harter, 2001; Gonzalez, 2005). In conditions with high intensity of uncertainty and time pressure, access to real-time data can help decision makers by providing them with current understanding of the situations in which they are operating. Second, historical data supports decision-making through analytical reasoning and deliberation, often characterized as “System 2” thinking, which involves systematic, deliberative, and cognitive processing (Kahneman, 2011).

In comparison, real-time data enables decision makers to engage in rapid, spontaneous judgments based on heuristics and intuition, falling under the umbrella of “System 1” thinking that relies on experience and expertise (Milkman et al., 2009). Real-time decision-making in live stream selling is characterized by rapid pace, high interactivity, and continuous feedback, requiring decisions to be made under time constraints (Lerch & Harter, 2001), cognitive pressure (Starcke & Brand, 2012), and uncertainty (Gonzalez, 2005). Under these conditions, streamers employ intuition to take spontaneous actions and adapt on the fly, and their immediate decision-making is often unable to engage in thorough deliberation. Therefore, streamers’ real-time decision-making primarily operates through “System 1” thinking, which emphasizes fast, automatic, and intuitive processes (Evans & Stanovich, 2013).

Adding to the prior research on data-driven decision-making, this study examines the efficacy of providing real-time data in terms of facilitating individual decision-making in an exigent environment characterized by complexity, unpredictability, and time pressure. We contextualize our investigation in the live stream selling context, an environment that requires streamers’ quick thinking, adaptability, and ability to make decisions on the fly. In such situations, the streamers’ ability to improvise and adapt based on real-time data is important, providing an ideal context to empirically investigate whether and how real-time data provision can influence streamer behavior as well as streaming outcomes.

## Live Stream Selling Platforms

The prior literature on live stream selling platforms can be categorized into three main camps: (1) the characteristics and economic value of live stream selling, (2) the influencing factors for streaming outcomes, and (3) the strategic actions of sellers and streamers on the platforms. The first body of work seeks to examine the unique characteristics and business value of live stream selling (Ki et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). Distinct from traditional TV-home shopping and e-commerce, live stream selling features realtime social interactions, wherein streamers engage with viewers as potential customers and respond to service requests from large audiences in real time (e.g., Ki et al., 2024). Meanwhile, prior research has quantified the business value of live stream selling for online merchant sales (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, live stream selling has both direct-selling and indirect-advertising effects, which are important for the survival of online merchants (Li et al., 2022).

The second group of studies explores various factors related to streamer-viewer interactions, as well as streamer characteristics that influence streaming outcomes. For example, previous work suggests that real-time social interactions (Lo et al., 2022; Song et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022)—including live comments (Lu & Chen, 2021) and bullet screens (Zeng et al., 2022), social presence indicators (Lu et al., 2021), and adequate information cues (Wang et al., 2022a)—are significant drivers of consumer purchases in live stream selling. Moreover, prior research has documented several streamer characteristics that affect consumer purchases in live stream selling, such as the streamer’s attractiveness and competency (Gao et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2024), narrative and content (Hwang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022c), vocal and linguistic characteristics (Gao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), displays of emotion (Bharadwaj et al., 2022), communication styles (Zhang & Wang, 2023), and promotion strategies (Zhang et al., 2023).

The third body of research employs analytical models to understand the operational aspects of sellers’ and streamers actions on live stream selling platforms (Chen et al., 2025). It explores key operational issues in live stream selling—for example, what the optimal timing to introduce a live stream selling channel is (Du et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Pan et al.,

2022), what types of live streamers sellers should collaborate with (Hou et al., 2021), how sellers should design contracts with streamers (Qi et al., 2022), and which pricing and discount strategies sellers should employ in live stream selling (Mao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2021a).

Our study contributes to the second category of literature by investigating how IT artifact designs can support streamers real-time decision-making and enhance sales performance. While previous literature has recognized the critical role of streamers’ live activities—such as real-time social interactions—in driving consumer purchases (Zeng et al., 2022), it has not yet explored how to leverage artifact designs to facilitate streamers’ impromptu decision-making. This work aims to fill this gap in the literature by examining the provision of real-time sales data that might enable streamers to monitor real-time product demand during a live stream, improvise on their sales activities, and improve sales performance.

## Improvisation Theory

Improvisation theory, which originated from jazz music performance, refers to the framework that explains how individuals (or organizations) adapt and perform in situations characterized by uncertainty and time pressure (Johnson-Laird, 2002). The act of improvisation involves “a spontaneous action in response to unanticipated occurrences that is characterized by the convergence of planning and execution” (Mannucci et al., 2021, p. 614). Improvisation theory proposes that effective improvisation leads to better performance outcomes, and improvisation has been recognized as a critical skill for dealing with uncertainty in highly turbulent business contexts (Lewin, 1998; Weick, 1998), such as new product development (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Vera et al., 2016), entrepreneurial activities (Hmieleski et al., 2013), organizational learning (Mannucci et al., 2021), sales management (Ahmed et al., 2022; Banin et al., 2016), and the development and utilization of information systems (Njenga & Brown, 2012).

In this regard, examples of particularly relevant work include Banin et al. (2016), which considers improvisation among salespeople as a descriptive decision-making process, and Stein (2011), which theorizes improvisation as a framework for analyzing real-time decision-making. The extant literature acknowledges that improvisation exhibits two critical elements: (1) spontaneous thinking and actions, and (2) the utilization of intuition (Weick, 1998). First, improvisational behaviors are not completely prescripted (Crossan, 1998). This enables individuals to deviate from planned decisions and adapt their behavior at the spur of the moment. Second, improvisation is essentially a descriptive decision-making process (Banin et al., 2016), a process fundamentally built on the concept of “System 1” thinking (Kahneman, 2011), in which individuals apply their intuitions to make real-time decisions under time constraints (Milkman et al., 2009; Yu, 2016).

In the context of live stream selling, streamers often face unanticipated situations and need to make quick decisions to engage with their audiences and effectively promote products (Lytle et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023). Thus, we draw upon improvisation theory to understand how streamers can leverage real-time sales data to facilitate spontaneous thinking and adaptive actions to enhance their performance and drive sales outcomes. It is notable that prior literature on improvisation theory has primarily examined situations in which decision makers can observe the physical presence of their audience. For example, jazz musicians can gauge the audience’s response through applause, cheers, or body language (Johnson-Laird, 2002). Similarly, in face-to-face sales interactions, salespeople can read customers’ nonverbal cues and adjust their pitches accordingly (McFarland et al., 2006). In contrast, live stream selling presents a unique challenge, as there is an absence of the physical presence of the audience (Wongkitrungrueng et al., 2020); this lack of physical presence can hinder streamers’ ability to improvise effectively. This study extends improvisation theory to the live stream selling context, examining whether and how the provision of real-time sales data can enable effective improvisation in a fast-paced, uncertain environment where the physical presence of the audience is absent.

## Real-Time Sales Data Enables Improvisation in Live Stream Selling

Synthesizing the related literature on improvisation theory (e.g., Weick, 1998), live stream selling (e.g., Guo et al., 2021), and sales management (e.g., Singh et al., 2020), we suggest that real-time sales data can be either beneficial or detrimental for a streamer’s improvisation in sales tactics, which in turn affects sales performance.

On the one hand, real-time sales data could benefit improvisation and improve sales performance by providing timely demand information for the currently promoted product, thereby enabling streamers to monitor consumers’ immediate, factual behavioral feedback (Lerch & Harter, 2001; Watson & Zheng, 2005). Without real-time data, streamers may rely on prior experiences or historical records to guide their sales tactics in the moment (Milkman et al., 2009; Xie et al., 2025), potentially leading to suboptimal or erroneous decisions when improvising (Vera & Crossan, 2004). For example, a streamer may continue urging consumers to buy a historically popular product, unaware of the current lack of interest in the product from viewers. In contrast, akin to musicians improvising in response to audience reactions (Weick, 1998), real-time sales data may enable streamers to flexibly adapt sales tactics in terms of narratives and linguistic use (Wang et al., 2022c), promotional strategies (Zhang et al., 2022), and selling time optimization (Chen et al., 2022) to persuade viewers and enhance sales performance.

On the other hand, the exigent and unpredictable decisionmaking environment in live stream selling poses challenges for improvisation. The extant literature on real-time decisionmaking has documented that decision makers might not be able to instantly process the information due to cognitive resource constraints (Gonzalez, 2005) or conflicts in attention among spontaneous activities (Lerch & Harter, 2001). Similarly, processing real-time sales data could add complexity to the already demanding multitasking responsibilities of streamers, potentially leading to information overload, diverting their attention, and disrupting ongoing streaming activities (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). Streamers may struggle to improvise and make effective impromptu decisions regarding which sales tactics to implement and when to implement them (Yu, 2016), ultimately hurting sales performance. Given these competing perspectives, we seek to empirically test the role of real-time sales data provision in streamers’ improvisation and sales performance in live stream selling.

## Randomized Field Experiment

## Research Context

In collaboration with one of a leading e-commerce platform in Asia, we analyzed data from a randomized field experiment implemented on its live stream selling platform. The field experiment focuses on the real-time data of presale products—new product releases that consumers can order before the products are made available on the market, with orders fulfilled on a future scheduled date (Li & Zhang, 2013). Consumers typically pay a deposit upfront and are charged the remaining balance on a predetermined later date. Due to the lack of extensive sales history and limited customer reviews, live stream selling is particularly effective for educating consumers on these products. In contrast, non-presale (regular) products are well-established products in the market and have a history of customer interactions, including reviews and ratings. Although the platform had implemented a streamer-facing dashboard displaying various metrics, such as real-time sales data for live streamed products, this feature was initially limited to non-presale products. As the partner platform further developed the dashboard, it recognized the potential value of extending real-time sales data to presale products, thereby presenting a unique opportunity for us to quantify the value of real-time sales data through this randomized field experiment.

## Experimental Design

The partner platform conducted the randomized field experiment over December 1-3, 2020, during the early days of the holiday season, when a large number of presale products were introduced into the market through live stream selling. As the demand for presale products was unpredictable, the platform provided real-time sales data for those products to help streamers assess consumer interest and assist sellers in managing production and inventory. Figure 1 depicts the experimental design, and Figure 2 illustrates an exemplary interface of the streamer-facing dashboard that displays realtime sales data. Prior to the experiment, the platform had already implemented a real-time sales dashboard for nonpresale products. During the experiment, the platform extended to presale products for a randomly assigned group of streamers. The key distinction between the dashboards of the treatment and control groups lay in the access to real-time sales data for presale products. For presale products, only streamers in the treatment group had access to real-time sales data in their dashboards, displayed in columns labeled “total amount of deposit” and “total remaining balance”—columns specific to presale products’ real-time sales data—whereas streamers in the control group did not have access to such data. Meanwhile, for regular products, streamers in both the treatment and control groups had access to real-time sales data. To minimize the risk of cross-group contamination, each product was exclusively sold in either the treatment group or the control group.

The experimental randomization was implemented at the streamer level. On December 1, 2020, the platform randomly assigned each streamer to either the treatment or the control group based on the last digit of their account ID. Streamers whose account ID ended in 0, 1, 3, or 8 were assigned to the treatment group, while the remaining streamers were assigned to the control group. In both groups, the data on the dashboard was updated every five seconds. The experiment ended at 11:00 pm on December 3, 2020, when all streamers were granted access to sales data for presale products on the dashboard.

## How Does the Provision of Real-Time Sales Data Affect Sales?

We performed our main analysis at the stream-product level for two reasons. First, following Bharadwaj et al. (2022), the granular stream-product level analysis enabled us to precisely identify the effects of real-time sales data on product sales performance. Specifically, while treatments were delivered at the streamer level, the actual variation originated from the streamers’ presentation of products during each stream. With the data organized at the stream-product level, we were able to focus on the sales outcome of presales products, which ultimately received the focal treatment in the experiment. Second, analyses at the stream-product level allowed us to explore additional insights into the potential mechanisms of the real-time sales data on product sales. In the experiment, the sales dashboard displayed the presale data for each individual product. As streamers make improvisation decisions based on the product-level data presented to them, analyzing the impact of real-time sales data at the same level ensured an accurate estimation of the treatment effect (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2019). ## What Are the Plausible Underlying Mechanisms?

Our exploration of mechanisms delves into examining whether and how the provision of real-time sales data for presale products affects sales performance through improvisation; this exploration is structured around two aspects: (1) examining the mediation effects of improvisation and (2) testing the boundary conditions for suggestive evidence of improvisation.

## Mediation Analyses on Sales Tactics

In our study context, we considered the sales tactics of streamers in the control group as a benchmark; if a streamer in the treatment group were to improvise on sales tactics for presale products using real-time sales data, we would observe significant differences in sales tactics across groups. We delved into the streamers’ audio scripts in their live streams, which directly captured streamers’ sales tactics and presentation (Guo et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2024). The streaming scripts were transcribed from the audio of live stream records by Universal ASR—an online end-to-end, state-of-the-art automated speech recognition system (ASR) for Mandarin (Gao et al., 2020)—and divided into episodes of each product in a live stream, thereby aligning with the unit of empirical analysis. Building on improvisation theory (e.g., Johnson-Laird, 2002; Zenk et al., 2022) and its application in sales management (Banin et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2020), we categorized sales tactics from the literature into three groups: promotional tactics, linguistic tactics, and presentational pace tactics.

To adapt their definitions and descriptions to the context of live stream selling, we interviewed streamers to understand how each sales tactic manifests in this specific setting.<sup>7</sup> Based on the qualitative data, we identified specific tactics within each group that streamers agreed on to reflect the live streamer’s streaming tactics and that have the potential to change consumers’ purchase decision-making, boiling down to measures related to streamers’ promotional tactics (Zhang et al., 2022), linguistic tactics (Wang et al., 2022c), and presentational pace tactics (Chen et al., 2022).

First, similar to how a musician monitors the audience’s response and promotes active participation in live performance (Johnson-Laird, 2002; Zenk et al., 2022), streamers can screen real-time sales data to adapt promotional tactics to encourage consumer purchases. We refined definitions and keyword markers through streamer interviews. The promotional tactics include recommendations, urging to place an order, providing presale information, and announcing inventory information (further details in Appendix C). Additionally, similar to how a musician improvises their use of melody and chord sequences in response to audience preferences, streamers may improvise their use of linguistic tactics (Liu et al., 2023). The provision of real-time sales data directly reflects prospective consumers’ reactions to streamers’ performance, thereby guiding streamers to use affective language to evoke their engagement and, ultimately, purchase (Lin et al., 2021; Bharadwaj et al., 2022) or leverage cognitive words to provide rational appeals and justify a consumer’s purchase (Lin et al., 2024).

For example, a faster speech rate may indicate that the streamer is deviating from the planned script and utilizing it to signal excitement and urgency to the audience (Gao et al., 2023). As the speed of speech can influence the effectiveness of persuasion (Miller et al., 1976), it could translate into better sales performance. Meanwhile, just as a jazz musician can extend or shorten a passage based on the audience’s engagement (Johnson-Laird, 2002), streamers can allocate an appropriate amount of time to streaming a product to optimize the sales effort and maximize the sales potential (Bharadwaj et al., 2022). For instance, streamers adopt urging or affective expressions, rather than deliberative reasoning, as they reflexively interpret real-time sales trends and adapt their sales tactics to the situation, allowing streamers to maintain audience attention and drive purchases (Weick, 1998; Barsade, 2002). Similarly, by adjusting speech pace or streaming time, streamers can improvise pace and efficiently allocate time to products based on real-time sales data, prioritizing items that show strong performance metrics (Moorman & Miner, 1998; Kahneman, 2011).

## Heterogeneity Analyses

To enact improvisation, two elements are pivotal: the uncertainty in decision-making that necessitates improvisation and the decision maker’s improvisational skills (e.g., Crossan, 1998; Vera & Crossan, 2004). In seeking further evidence that improvisation is the primary mechanism at play, we explored boundary conditions under which the effect of real-time sales data provision on sales performance is more pronounced.

Heterogeneity by product uncertainty: We first explored uncertainty as a moderator, as prior studies suggest that improvisation is more likely to occur in uncertain decisionmaking situations (Banin et al., 2016; Zenk et al., 2022). In our study context, we used product ownership as a proxy for uncertainty and thus distinguished products from brand stores or small businesses. Products from brand stores have established brand recognition, thus reducing uncertainty for streamers who can draw on existing brand knowledge and customer perceptions when presenting these products (Bei & Gielens, 2023). Consequently, the need for improvisation may be less pronounced, as streamers can use established brand narratives and customer familiarity to guide their sales strategies. In contrast, small-business-owned products lack brand recognition, thereby introducing higher uncertainty into streamers’ selling events. In these scenarios, real-time sales data becomes more relevant for in-the-moment decision-making, as streamers cannot rely on established brand knowledge.

## Falsification Tests

We first conducted a falsification test by examining the impact of real-time sales data on the group of streamers who did not use the real-time sales dashboard during the experiment. In our study context, for the provision of real-time sales data to be effective, one necessary condition is that streamers must have utilized the real-time sales dashboard in live streams during the experiment. Streamers who did not browse the dashboard in the experiment could not have relied on real-time sales data to facilitate their real-time decision-making. Therefore, for streamers in the treatment group who did not utilize the real-time sale dashboard in a live stream, the provision of real-time data in the treatment group would not have helped improve sales performance, compared to the sales performance of streamers in the control group. Accordingly, we reestimated Equation (1) for the sample in which a streamer did not browse the dashboard. As presented in Column 1 of Table 9, the coefficient of the treatment indicator is insignificant $( \beta = . 0 . 0 4 1 , p > 0 . 1 )$ . This finding suggests that the observed effect of real-time sales data on sales performance was indeed driven by streamers’ utilization of the real-time sales dashboard.

The second falsification test aimed to rule out the spillover effects of our treatment on non-presale products. In live stream selling, a streamer can introduce both regular and presale products in the same stream. This raises the concern that the experimental treatment might have unintentionally affected the sales performance of non-presale products. To address this concern, we reestimated Equation (1) on the sample comprising non-presale products. As reported in Column 2 of Table 9, the results suggest that the sales of nonpresale products did not significantly differ across the experimental groups $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 0 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , indicating that the observed increase in sales for presale products was unlikely to have been at the expense of a sales downturn in nonpresale products.

Consequently, the insights derived from real-time sales data for presale products were uniquely tailored to each presale product and did not carry over effectively to nonpresale products. Second, streamers both in the treatment and control groups already had access to real-time sales data for non-presale products. Non-presale products’ own real-time data already informed the decision-making for streamers in both groups. The value of real-time sales data for non-presale products was also related to its relevance to the focal product. As a result, the real-time sales data provided for presale products could not have influenced sales tactics for nonpresale products, which explains the insignificant variations in sales of those products.

Further, we considered the proportion of regular products in a live stream to reflect how a streamer manages regular (nonpresale) and presale products in the same live stream. If the presence of regular products had interfered with the treatment, we would expect that a higher ratio of regular products could potentially influence streamer improvisation, thereby attenuating the treatment effect on sales performance for presale products. To empirically test this, we constructed the measure High Non-Presale % to indicate whether the proportion of regular (non-presale) products among all products in a live stream exceeded the median value, with 1 indicating that the ratio exceeded the median, and 0 otherwise. We performed moderation analysis, and the results in Column 3 of Table 9 indicate an insignificant interaction effect between Treatment and High Non-Presale % $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 0 , \mathfrak { p } >$ 0.1). This result suggests that the treatment effect remains consistent regardless of the ratio of regular products in the live stream.

Third, to investigate the potential interference between treatment and control groups due to competition effect, namely, that the treatment group might divert attention away from the control group, we analyzed two key metrics—the number of views and visits—as proxies for market competition, as they directly reflect viewer traffic and market presence in live stream selling (Peng et al., 2025). The results of t-tests show no statistically significant differences in either the number of views or the frequency of visits between the treatment and control groups at both the stream level and streamer level (all p values > 0.1). These findings suggest that our intervention, providing access to real-time sales data for presale products, primarily affected streamers’ behavior during their live streams. Potential viewers browsing the platform before entering a stream remained unaware of these behind-the-scenes variations, which occurred during the live stream. Consequently, our treatment was unlikely to have influenced new viewer acquisition or overall viewership metrics. Instead, once viewers were in the stream, streamers could leverage real-time sales data to swiftly improvise their sales tactics, enhancing their ability to convert existing viewers’ interest into purchases.

## Alternative Mechanism

One alternative explanation for the observed treatment effect could be that streamers might have altered the prices of presale products upon gaining access to real-time sales data. Rather than streamer improvisation in sales tactics, it could have been the price discount that led to more purchases. However, we note that streamers in live stream selling generally do not have the discretion to adjust prices during a live stream (Ji et al., 2023). The industry standard practice is for streamers and merchants (or brands) to have binding contracts long before the live streams, typically outlining prenegotiated terms related to pricing, promotions, and commission fees (Hou et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2024).

Our interviews with streamers and platform executives also confirm that the pricing strategy of products is a predetermined parameter that is separate from streamers’ realtime sales tactics (see details in Appendix B). An unanticipated price adjustment or promotional change made during a live stream could elicit negative reactions from viewers (Hou et al., 2021). For example, if a streamer suddenly offers a product at a discount price, consumers who had previously purchased the same product at a higher cost may demand a price match or refund. Such actions could disrupt the overall viewer experience and; therefore, streamers are unlikely to adjust the price in live streams.

To test this alternative explanation, following the process of analyzing streamers’ audio scripts, we extracted keywords that indicate the pricing adjustment, as presented in Table 10. We observed an extremely low frequency of keywords related to price adjustment $( \mathrm { M } _ { p r i c i n g a d j u s t m e n t } = 0 . 0 0 0 5 )$ compared to other sales tactics extracted from the streamer script, such as recommendation $( \mathrm { M } _ { r e c o m m e n d a t i o n } = 0 . 0 5 2 6 ) $ , urging $( \mathbf { M } _ { u r g i n g } =$ 0.0249), provision of presale information $( \mathbf { M } _ { p r e s a l e } = 0 . 1 0 8 5 ) \mathrm { . }$ and provision of inventory information $( M _ { i n \nu e n t o r y } = 0 . 0 5 6 9 )$ Thus, we concluded that adjusting product prices in the moment is a rare practice in live streams. Additionally, a t-test on the occurrence of keywords related to pricing adjustment between the treatment and control groups revealed insignificant differences $( p > 0 . 1 )$ . Further, we performed mediation analysis, including Pricing Adjustment as a mediator in the same PROCESS model from our main mediation analyses. The results did not yield a significant mediation effect. The coefficient of the path from Treatment to Pricing Adjustment is insignificant $( \beta = 0 . 0 0 0 1 , p > 0 . 1 ) .$ , as is the indirect effect from Treatment to Sales Performance through Pricing Adjustment $( \beta = - 0 . 0 0 0 3 , p > 0 . 1 )$ , with a 95% CI, including 0. These findings demonstrate that Pricing Adjustment was not a mechanism at play in this context.

## Comparing Real-Time Sales Data to Historical Demand Records

Here, we examine the interplay of real-time sales data with a typical form of historical data, that is, historical demand records in live stream selling. Prior research suggests that salespeople improvise more when they lack prior product knowledge and therefore face greater uncertainty. Although streamers cannot access historical demand records during live streams, they can preview those aggregated demand/sales records for the to-be-streamed products prior to streaming (Qi et al., 2022). This prior knowledge can reduce reliance on realtime data by enabling streamers to recall past experiences and inform their sales tactics. Conversely, the absence of historical demand records could compel streamers to utilize real-time data for more effective improvisation during live streams.

We observed the historical demand/sales records for some of the presale products in our dataset. We constructed the variable Historical Records to indicate whether a presale product had historical demand/sales records in the month before the experiment $( 1 = \mathbf { y } \mathbf { e s } , 0 = \mathbf { n } \mathbf { o } )$ . We then estimated the moderating effect of historical sales records on the relationship between real-time sales data and sales performance for presale products. Column 1 of Table 11 indicates an insignificant and negative interaction effect between real-time sales data and historical demand records (β $= ~ - 0 . 4 0 9 , ~ p ~ > ~ 0 . 1 )$ . Meanwhile, our subsample analysis revealed that the treatment effect was significant for presale products without sales records $( \beta = 0 . 5 7 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ but insignificant for those with sales records $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 2 , p > 0 . 1 )$ Comparing the treatment effect estimated in Columns 2 and 3 suggests a marginally significant difference between the two coefficients $( p < 0 . 1 )$ , thereby indicating possible interactions. As our empirical tests offer mixed evidence on the possible role of historical demand records in the relationship between real-time sales data and sales performance, we refrained from asserting conclusions on the said moderation relationship.

Instead, our findings suggest uncertainty as a necessary condition for real-time sales data to take effect, which is consistent with previous research indicating that uncertainty necessitates improvisation to improve performance (Hmieleski et al., 2013). In situations where streamers lack prior knowledge of a product, they face greater ambiguity in predicting customer demand and preferences. Under such circumstances, real-time sales data becomes a crucial resource for streamers to adapt their sales tactics and improvise effectively during live streams. While previous studies have revealed that historical data facilitates planned decision-making by deliberation on past experiences (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016; Huang et al., 2022), our study provides pioneering empirical evidence that real-time data facilitates spontaneous decision-making through improvisation. This suggests that real-time data plays a distinct role in facilitating decision-making, compared to historical data.

## Discussion

## Main Findings

Our study examines whether, how, and under which conditions the provision of real-time sales data improves sales performance in the context of live stream selling. In partnership with a globally leading live stream selling platform, we obtained data from a randomized field experiment wherein real-time sales data for presale products were integrated into the sales dashboard for streamers in the treatment group. Our empirical analyses reveal that the introduction of real-time sales data generated an increase of 40.21% in sales of presale products in the treatment group, compared to sales in the control group. Further, our mechanism analyses show that the provision of real-time sales data improves sales by supporting streamers in improvising on sales tactics, specifically through urging prospective consumers to place an order, more frequently employing affect-related expressions, speeding up speech, and shortening streaming time for presale products. Moreover, we found that the efficacy of real-time sales data is contingent on certain boundary conditions. Specifically, the positive effect of real-time sales data provision on sales performance is more pronounced for products with a higher level of uncertainty and for streamers with a higher level of improvisational skills.

## Implications for Research

This study offers important implications for research. First, it contributes to the literature on data-driven decision-making by exploring how real-time data can be leveraged to facilitate improvisation in an exigent and uncertain environment. While extant literature has explored the effects of historical data and analytical tools for planned decision-making (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2019; Berman & Israeli, 2022), limited research has examined how real-time data enables decision makers to navigate unforeseen scenarios under time pressure. Our research addresses this gap by documenting field evidence that real-time sales data facilitates streamer improvisation in sales tactics, thereby leading to better sales performance. Notably, improvisation involves spontaneous actions, intuition, and realtime adaptability, aligning with the core characteristics of “System 1” thinking. Accordingly, by establishing improvisation as the key mechanism, our study provides evidence that real-time decision-making operates primarily through “System 1” thinking. Our findings reveal that in the high-pressure, fast-paced context of live stream selling, even when processing quantitative data, decision makers rely on intuitive rather than deliberative processing, with experienced streamers applying “skilled intuition” in leveraging real-time data—thereby advancing our understanding of how “System 1” thinking manifests in data-driven environments.

Note that while prior research suggests that real-time decision support systems facilitate immediate decision-making by affecting users’ cognitive activities (Lerch & Harter, 2001), thereby reducing bias and improving operational efficiency (Ketter et al., 2012; Golrezaei et al., 2014; Watson & Zheng, 2005), our study shows that in live stream selling, real-time data enables streamers to improvise on their sales tactics, thereby indicating that real-time data supports impromptu decisionmaking through a mix of intuitive responses (Stein, 2011). Related to an emerging research stream on real-time feedback (Rivera et al., 2021; VanEpps et al., 2021; Adomavicius et al., 2019), our study provides implications on the impact of realtime data on live stream sales performance, a novel research context in which the decision maker’s improvisation is crucial.

Additionally, our research evidences the distinct value of realtime sales data compared to historical demand records in driving sales performance. Previous studies have revealed that historical data supports decision-making by providing deliberative processing of past experiences (Brynjolfsson & McElheran, 2016; Huang et al., 2022), whereas our study reveals that real-time data facilitates spontaneous decisionmaking through improvisation. This contrast emphasizes the unique value of real-time data for impromptu decision-making in uncertain, fast-paced environments such as live stream selling, illustrating the economics of emerging real-time data analytics (Brynjolfsson et al., 2021).

Second, this study advances the literature on live stream selling platforms by proposing a real-time IT-enabled platform design to facilitate streamers’ spontaneous decision-making and improve streaming outcomes. Previous literature on live stream selling has focused on identifying its characteristics (e.g., Ki et al., 2024) and investigating how factors related to streamers, viewers, and streamer-viewers interactions affect consumer purchase and viewer engagement (Lu et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2022). However, little attention has been paid to exploring platform designs to support streamers’ real-time decision-making. This work fills this gap by examining the effectiveness of an IT-enabled platform design (i.e., real-time sales data provision) to enable streamers’ improvisation in sales tactics and, consequently, enhance streaming performance.

Third, our paper also provides implications for improvisation theory by extending it to the context of live stream selling, where the physical presence of audiences is absent. Due to the lack of face-to-face interactions, an online environment may restrict a decision maker’s ability to improvise (Njenga & Brown, 2012). For example, the absence of immediate feedback and nonverbal cues from the audience can limit decision makers’ capacity to understand what is happening in the moment, which is crucial for them to spontaneously adapt their approach (Vera & Crossan, 2004). Our research suggests that the provision of real-time sales data can mimic the physical presence and feedback of audiences, thereby empowering streamers to engage in effective improvisation. This extension of improvisation theory to the context of live stream selling indicates the potential of technology to facilitate improvisation in online environments in which traditional forms of audience feedback are limited.

## Implications for Practice

Our research provides managerial implications for stakeholders of live streaming platforms. To begin with, our research encourages more live stream selling platforms to provide real-time data infrastructure for streamers’ live sales events. Our research demonstrates that the deployment of realtime data assets can generate beneficial returns to the streamer and the platforms, thereby suggesting that platforms should invest more in their real-time data assets. Notably, although our study focuses on presale products due to the available experiment opportunity, the insights gained in this study extend to other products. According to our interviews, the necessity for improvisation in live streams is not exclusive to presale items but applies to all products. Additionally, our heterogeneity analyses suggest that live stream selling platforms should tailor real-time data dashboards to streamers’ improvisational skills and product uncertainty. To maximize the effectiveness of realtime sales data, the platforms could offer incentives for toprated or popular streamers to use real-time metrics, especially when promoting products carried by small businesses or experience goods. Further, the divergence in effects between popular and less popular streamers, as well as between experience and search products, is particularly noteworthy. To bridge the performance gap of more (vs. less) popular streamers, platforms could implement adaptive learning mechanisms to help less popular streamers enhance their improvisational skills. For instance, platforms should introduce coaching modules and interactive dashboards to strengthen less-popular streamers’ ability to effectively leverage real-time sales data. Additionally, platforms could allow streamers to opt out of real-time sales data for search goods or provide targeted training programs to help them adapt sales tactics based on unique product characteristics when interpreting data from the streamer-facing dashboard.

## Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge that our research has several limitations, which also open avenues for future research. First, given the relatively short-term period of this experimental investigation, future research with access to longer-term data could examine the treatment effects over an extended time window. This would provide insights into the sustainability of the value of real-time sales data provision and enable streamers and platforms to develop strategies to maintain or enhance the positive impact. Second, in our research context, streamers seldom spontaneously altered prices during a live stream. However, price adjustment might be feasible in other live streaming contexts, and such flexibility could change market competition (Wang et al., 2022b). Future research could thus examine the interaction of real-time sales data with pricing strategies on sales performance in contexts where decision makers are open to pricing changes, thereby answering questions such as how real-time sales data affects pricing decisions, price elasticity, and market competition. Third, our analysis was constrained by technical limitations on the partner platform, which precluded the retrieval of engagement-related metrics. If such data becomes available, future work could explore how streamers’ improvisational tactics—as responses to their access to realtime sales data—might influence consumer engagement. Fourth, while our study did not manipulate the presentation format of real-time sales data, we acknowledge its potential influence on shaping streamers’ real-time decision-making and thus recognize it as a promising area for future research. For example, it would be interesting to derive insights from exploring how the inclusion of a distance-to-goal indicator (e.g., the amount remaining to earn) could impact streamers’ spontaneous decision-making and streaming performance.

## Comparing Live Stream Selling and TV-Home Shopping

To distinguish live stream selling (LSS) from traditional television-home shopping (TVHS), we synthesized key characteristics that emphasize LSS’s improvisational demands and data-driven nature (see Table A1). The comparison focuses on two key aspects that differentiate LSS from TVHS: th nature of real-time interactions and the availability of real-time data.

First, LSS platforms enable multiple forms of real-time interactions between a streamer and a large crowd of audience, thereby enabling immediat mutual feedback and tactical sales adjustments (Bharadwaj et al., 2022). These platforms afford viewers with features such as live comments, likes, and follows to communicate their thoughts, demands, and requests in real-time (Ki et al., 2024). Therefore, unlike TVHS, where the sales script is largely predetermined or even pre-recorded (Fritchie & Johnson, 2003), LSS is far more dynamic and unpredictable. The demand for immediate response during live streams compels streamers to employ improvisational tactics to meet consumer needs and optimize ongoing sales tactics. To effectively manage the fast-paced environment, streamers must multitask in the moment and maintain a high-energy level to gauge consumer engagement and influence their purchase decision-making (Jiao et al., 2023). Conversely, TVHS is a one-way communication channel in which a host has limited interaction with the audience (Chen & Tsai, 2008; Fritchie & Johnson, 2003; Yen, 2018). Consequently, hosts can adhere to planned behaviors and require less energy to sustain viewer engagement and drive purchase behavior (Stephens et al., 1996)

Second, as evidenced by prevailing industry practices, many live stream selling platforms utilize real-time data analytical tools, such as viewer metrics and engagement levels on the real-time data dashboard for streamers, which inform immediate tactical decisions. In LSS, the ability to pivot sales tactics based on real-time insights is not only advantageous but often critical for success. In contrast, TVHS hosts perform without access to concurrent data, thereby lacking the opportunity for data-driven improvisation.

## Expert Interviews

Interview Summary: To improve the understanding of regulatory practices and the utilization of real-time sales dashboard as a means to improvise in live stream selling platforms, we conducted in-depth interviews with a wide range of stakeholders. We followed protocols established by Zenk et al., 2022, in which a model for improvisation was developed through interviews.

Participants: Our interview sample was participants with experience in live stream selling, including both high-profile streamers with upward of 400 million followers to smaller, medium-sized influencers. We initially reached out to 103 individuals, including live streamers, professional streamer company managers, and platform executives. After multiple rounds of communication, we secured interviews with 10 willing participants (see Table B1). Each participant received 1,000 RMB (approximately 140.67 USD) as compensation once they completed the interview. Our interviewees were carefully selected for their experience and influence on the live stream selling platform, reflecting a diverse group of participants (Zenk et al., 2022). Each participant had substantial experience in dealing with real-time sales dashboards. These interviews spanned the top three live stream selling platforms—including Taobao Live, Douyin (TikTok in China), and Kuaishou—thereby ensuring that our findings have high generalizability. The interviewed subjects operated across various industries, including jewelry, fast moving consumer goods, fitness equipment, food, and educational content.

Interview procedure: First, we briefly introduced the purpose of our interview. Then, we asked interviewees questions about their backgrounds. Based on their responses regarding the usage and experience of real-time sales dashboards, we curated questions to follow up on how their improvisation is accomplished after their utilization of real-time sales data. Then, we discussed the regulatory practices related to pricing and new product release in a live stream. Each interview lasted for one hour and was recorded with the participants’ consent.

Interview introduction to participants: “This interview aims to understand the user experience related to real-time data features in live stream selling platforms. We understand that during a live stream, you can access real-time sales data via a specialized data dashboard for both presale and non-presale products. We sincerely invite you to share insights into your user experience and usage history. Please note that all data collected will be used exclusively for aggregate analysis and your personal information will remain confidential. We deeply appreciate your willingness to contribute to this project.”

Key findings: From the interviews, we made five key observations. First, the participants acknowledged the widespread adoption of real-time data across platforms. During our experiment back in December 2020, other live stream selling platforms, with the exception of Taobao Live, had not yet implemented real-time data. However, in recent years, the application of real-time data has become widespread across multiple platforms. All participants, even small steamers (e.g., P5), indicated the frequent use of real-time data in their live streams.

Second, the participants described severe challenges in real-time broadcasting and confirmed both the need for and the practice of improvisation. In live streams, streamers are involved in multiple activities that demand improvisation. For example, P2 reflected: “The streamers of our platform are under great time pressure in the streams. They have to engage in a lot of interconnected sales activities, such as welcoming new arrivals, enhancing streaming atmosphere, introducing products, engaging in live comments, prompting sales, encouraging new followers, or dealing with urgent matters.” Further, P8 firmly stated: “Live stream selling has placed high demand for the streamers, both emotionally and cognitively. Those who struggle with multitasking may find it difficult to monitor the sales dashboard while streaming.” The challenges appeared to be even more pronounced for new or small streamers who lack adequate capacity to act in the here and now. For example, P5 expressed: “Especially for new streamers, memorizing scripts and monitoring the activity in the live comments can be overwhelming, as there are many tasks to juggle, and they are not ye skilled. Adjusting based on real-time data can be challenging (as it is equivalent to adding another task), and they cannot match up to the experienced live streamers.” Similarly, P8 commented on streamers’ competency: “Some streamers are not talented at multitasking. Monitoring real-time sales data could be incredibly tough. However, those streamers of higher competency can be able to.” Moreover, the need for improvisation was mentioned by P1: “Only those who are very responsive and flexible can do the job of a live streamer, which requires strong improvisation.”

Third, the interviews revealed the specific improvisational strategies employed after having access to real-time sales data. Notably, the sales tactics identified from the interviews closely aligned with those tactics we found to be effective in the exploration of our mechanism, which w summarize below.

Promotional and linguistics tactics: It is noted that all the streamers agreed regarding the application of the tactic of urging prospective consumers to order, thereby aligning with our analysis in the PROCESS model that urging plays a significant role in translating the effect of real-time sale data provision for presale products on the sales performance. P5 indicated that “urging to order is the most effective and commonly used tactic after seeing the sales data.” Moreover, the top streamer, P4, expressed that “urging can create the atmosphere for viewers to place the order impulsively.” With regard to the use of emotional words, the majority of the participants—including P1, P2, P3, P5, P6, P8, and P10—acknowledged applying it as a tactic in response to observing real-time sales data. For example, P2 and P3 specifically noted: “Seeing the positive figures on the sales dashboard, I can’t help but express the emotion.” Even P8, who is a leading streamer in the paid-knowledge domain (a subsector of education), emphasized the value of using emotional words. He commented that “emotional language catch eyes and, I tend to use it if the product sells.”

Presentational pace tactics: While the choice of products introduced in a stream is predetermined, the pace at which each product is presented can vary. All streamers acknowledged that after screening the numbers on the dashboard, they modified the rhythm and timing of sales pitches. For example, P2 shared that “I usually allocate more time to the products that are selling well in the moment, according to the (real-time) dashboard.” A similar response from P4 suggested the use of the same presentation strategy. With regard to the speech pace, the majority of participants indicated they usually speak more quickly reading the sales data on the real-time dashboard. Moreover, P9 inferred that “this tactic (speaking fast) creates a rapport with emotion,” thereby suggesting a link between accelerated delivery and fostering an emotional connection with viewers.

Fourth, our interviews revealed that making on-the-air adjustments in pricing and promotion is not feasible. The promotional framework, which includes both pricing and promotional offers, is usually negotiated and locked in prior to the live streams. Once set, these terms are expected to remain stable in the entire presale period. For example, the top-tier streamer, P4, mentioned that even for low-value products, adjusting promotions or pricing would require a minimum lead time of one week. For high-value items, this negotiation period can last for up to two months. There are two reasons for this. The first reason is limited discretion in real-time. In the interviews, seasoned streamers and platform managers emphasized that pricing strategy is a predetermined parameter and is separate from the streamer’s real-time sales tactics. Notably, there is usually a binding contract between the streamers and retailers or brands. This contract typically outlines prenegotiated terms regarding pricing, promotions, and commission fees. Therefore, streamers generally do not have the discretion to adjust prices in real time. The second reason is their concerns regarding negative audience reactions to sudden changes. The participants expressed concerns regarding adjusting price or promotion in the moment. Any unanticipated price adjustments or promotional changes made during a live stream could elicit negative reactions from viewers. For example, if a streamer suddenly offered a product at a reduced price, viewers who had previously purchased the item at a higher cost may demand refunds or price adjustments. Such a scenario could generate confusion and disrupt the overall viewer experience, thereby undermining the streamer’s credibility. Therefore, such negative consequences can prevent streamers from altering the price in the moment.

Fifth, whether or not the product is presale does not influence how the real-time sales dashboard is utilized. When asked whether the product type, presale or non-presale, influenced their improvisation tactics, almost all participants provided a definitive response—the product category (presale product or non-presale product) did not significantly impact their approach. Instead, they dynamically adapted their strategies based on the real-time data they observed in the moment. In particular, P1 stated, “the presale product is just a product sold in advance, and no more than that. Regardless of the product type, we make improvisations.”

## Alternative Measures of Moderators

Here, we report additional analyses with alternative measures of moderators. First, we consider the alternative measure of uncertainty by distinguishing search goods from experience goods. Experience goods are associated with a greater level of uncertainty, as their quality is difficult to determine before a purchase (Dimoka et al., 2012; Nelson, 1970). In contrast, search goods have well-defined attributes that consumers can evaluate before purchase, maintaining a lower level of uncertainty (Nelson, 1970). For streamers, selling experience goods might involve a greater level of uncertainty compared to selling search goods, as more information and engagement in more persuasive efforts are required to ameliorate consumers’ perceived risks and encourage purchases (Weathers et al., 2007). In those scenarios, streamers wil have to rely on improvisation (Hmieleski et al. 2013), utilizing real-time sales data to adapt their sales tactics, mitigate uncertainty, and enhance sales performance. We constructed the variable Experience Good based on Taobao’s product category taxonomy (He et al., 2019). Taobao’s three-layer taxonomy includes 1,050 product categories at the most detailed level. To ensure reliability of the classification, two independent researchers conduct cross-validation exercises to agree on the classification. After three rounds of discussions with the partner platform, we labeled a product as an experience good (Experience Good = 1) if the quality was difficult to be determined prior to purchase; otherwise, we classified it as a search good (Experience Good = 0). Due to space constraints, the classification details are not included here but are available upon request.

The regression result in Column 1 of Table D1 indicates a positive and significant interaction effect between Treatment and Experience Good, which validates our argument that improvisation is more effective under greater uncertainty $( \beta = 0 . 6 7 8 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . This finding is consistent with our exploration regarding the interaction effect from Brand-Owned Good. Furthermore, Columns 2 and 3 of Table D1 present the regression results for experience and search goods, respectively. We observed a positive and significant treatment effect for experience goods $( \beta = 0 . 3 8 0 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but a negative and significant treatment effect for search goods $( \beta = - 0 . 4 3 4 , p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The adverse effect for search goods suggests that relying on real-time sales data might prompt streamers to enact sales tactics misaligned with consumer expectations. For instance, emphasizing urgency or affective language may appear incongruent with the objective characteristics of search goods (Chu et al., 1995). This mismatch can result in diminished trust or perceived pressure, negatively impacting sales performance. Meanwhile, we acknowledge that our analyses on experience (vs. search) goods exhibit imbalance in subsample sizes (e.g., experience goods represented 92.4% of our sample). Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Second, we alternatively considered streamer popularity as the proxy for streamers’ improvisational skills in our analysis. More popular streamers often exhibit a greater competency in their improvisation, as they are effective and experienced in interacting with a large numbe of viewers. The insights gleaned from the streaming experience of more popular streamers can potentially inform their sales tactics when they have access to real-time sales data, thereby leading to more effective improvisation that turns into better sales performance. We measured streamer popularity by the number of followers a streamer attracted prior to our experiment. If the total number of followers exceeded the median value, Popular Streamer equals 1, otherwise 0. Table D2 presents the results after we introduce the interaction term Treatment × Popular Streamer in addition to Popular Streamer. The result of Column 1 suggests a positive interaction effect $( \beta = 0 . 3 3 8 , p < 0 . 1 )$ and the follow-up subsample analysis identifies that the positive treatment effect is primarily sourced from the more popular streamers $( \beta = 0 . 4 9 3 , p$ < 0.01). In addition, it is noteworthy that the effect of real-time sales data is unexpectedly negative for less popular streamers $( \beta = - 0 . 1 5 7 , p$ < 0.05), which is consistent with the findings of our interview with streamers and platform executives. Those results align with improvisation theory, which posits that individuals with greater expertise or higher competency (i.e., more popular streamers) are better equipped to leverage real-time data for spontaneous decision-making (Vera & Crossan, 2004). By contrast, less popular streamers often struggle with improvisation, which can result in ineffective sales tactics and thereby unexpectedly hurt their sales performance.
