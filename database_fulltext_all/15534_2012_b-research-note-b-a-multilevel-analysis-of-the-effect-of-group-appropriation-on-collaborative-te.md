his study develops a comprehensive model to predict and explain the use of collaborative technologies (CT) and the task performance of individual users as a result of using CT. The integrated model attempts to capture how the individual user’s extent of use of CT is a function of both the technical features and the structures embedded within or created by the interactions among the technology, group, and organization. The model developed is tested using data collected from a national bank with 279 members working in 40 different workgroups. A hierarchical linear model (HLM) is used to test the hypotheses generated from the model. Results show that our integrated model provides a more complete explanation of the use of CT and task performance beyond those of the individual-level factors. The study is an early effort to develop an integrated theory to provide comprehensive insight into individual use of CT in a group or organizational context.

## 1. Introduction

Technology adoption and use have been among the key research areas in information systems (IS) research for the last two decades. Much effort has been devoted to understanding adoption and use of technology at the individual level in various contexts. This stream of research assumes that the decision about whether or not to adopt and use a specific information technology (IT) is purely an individual decision shaped by the user’s individual perceptions of various cognitive or affective factors, such as perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PE) (Davis et al. 1989, Venkatesh and Davis 1996).

Nonetheless, most of the IS used in organizations today are hardly “individual-based.” Instead, they are used by a group of individuals, and the maximum effect of such IS can only be realized if group members within a workgroup use the target technology together. Some examples of such technologies include group electronic calendars, electronic meeting tools (e.g., Skype and MSN), electronic approval systems, electronic bulletin boards, document libraries, and group project management tools. We call this group of IT collaborative technologies (CT).

Cooper and Zmud (1990) suggested a six-stage model of the diffusion process, which consists of (1) initiation, (2) adoption, (3) adaptation, (4) acceptance, (5) routinization, and (6) infusion. Our study is interested in examining individual users’ extent of use of CT and its resulting performance outcomes at the adaption and acceptance stage, i.e., after the initial adoption stage but before the CT has become a routine of the users’ work and infused into organizational processes.

This paper argues that in understanding the use of CT, individual-level constructs are not comprehensive enough because social processes moderate the effect of technology on users’ behaviors (Markus and Silver 2008). Similar to individual use of a communication medium, such as the telephone, individual use of CT is influenced by the expectation about others’ usage (Markus 1987). Therefore, individual usage of a CT is not necessarily determined by the individual perception of the technology but is susceptible to the influence of institutional and situational conditions (Watson-Manheim and Belanger 2007).

Specifically, we argue that the decision to use CT is shaped by both individual-level and group-level factors. Although some individuals within a workgroup may perceive a particular CT to be very useful and easy to use (high PU and PE), it is unlikely that they would use the system extensively. This is because, unlike individually oriented technologies, these individuals cannot realize the benefits of the system if only a few people in their workgroup use it. For example, a person cannot realize any benefit of a group meeting schedule function of a CT (e.g., automatic searching for an earliest possible meeting date for the entire workgroup) by sharing his or her personal calendar if only he or she, or a limited number of their workgroup members, are willing to share their calendars. Therefore, for an individual user, the decision to use CT is different from that of individually oriented technologies. Consequently, examining the adoption and use of CT by using an individual-level perspective may only attain a partial understanding of the whole picture. Hence, the main objective of this study is to develop an integrated model, which considers both individual- and grouplevel factors, that can better predict and explain the use of technologies that are implemented for a group of people (i.e., CT).

The above discussions suggest that, because team members are collectively responsible for team outcomes, their collective agreement on how to use the CT to accomplish their work becomes an important consideration. Adaptive structuration theory (AST) (DeSanctis and Poole 1994), given its focus on appropriation, becomes well suited for examining this issue and is thus adopted as one of the theoretical lenses of our study. In particular, consensus of appropriation (COA) and faithfulness of appropriation (FOA), two important concepts in AST, provide a unique perspective to investigate the usage of CT.

In another vein, although the relationship between IS use and an individual user task performance has been well researched (Dennis et al. 2001), and there is some empirical evidence suggesting that use of group support systems (GSS; a form of CT) positively affects overall group-level performance (Benbasat and Lim 1993), the relationship between CT use and individual members’ task performance is still largely unexplored. To fill this gap, this study also attempts to examine whether use of CT leads to improved individual task performance.

Sarker et al. (2005) convincingly argue the importance of considering the interactions among group members when studying technology adoption by groups. Building on Sarker et al. (2005), we pinpoint several group-level factors that may interact with various individual-level factors to affect use of CT. Nonetheless, this study differs from that of Sarker et al. (2005) in two aspects: First, while their focus is on initial adoption, our focus is on post-adoption use (but before the CT becomes part of the organizational routine). Second, in their study, group-level constructs (e.g., group valence) were modeled and analyzed using individual-level data. In our study, while we aggregated individual-level data to form group-level data, these group-level factors were modeled explicitly as group-level constructs by taking group membership into consideration. Thus, our study is able to pinpoint and examine the effects of workgroup differences.

This study contributes to the academic literature in three ways: First, by building and testing a model that encompasses both the individual- and group-level factors, this study develops a better understanding of CT use. Second, by explicitly modeling grouplevel factors using group-level constructs, this study integrates group-level appropriation into individual technology use. Third, through the integrated model, this study highlights the substitutive relationship between group-level appropriation and individuallevel drivers of technology use (e.g., PU and PE). All in all, these contributions generate new insights into individuals’ use of technology in team contexts. In terms of contribution to the practice, by identifying the roles and influences of multiple-level factors with regards to CT use, this study also contributes to practice by providing specific guidelines for preparing more refined implementation strategies for CT so as to increase usage and system success.

This paper consists of five sections, including the introduction. The next section develops our theoretical model and hypotheses. The data collection procedure and sample characteristics are described in the third section. The fourth section reports the data analyses and results of hypotheses testing. The last section discusses the findings and offers concluding remarks.

## 2. Theory Development

## 2.1. Collaborative Technologies

In this paper, we treat collaborative technologies and groupware as being synonymous. Ellis et al. (1991) define groupware as “computer-based systems that support groups of people engaged in a common task (or goal) and that provide an interface to a shared environment” (p. 40). As argued and illustrated in the introduction, individual group members’ use of such technologies is more likely to be influenced by other group members’ opinions and stance than other types of IS. Therefore, the appropriation status of CT, such as agreement among group members on how to use the system, likely has significant influence on the individual’s intention to use CT, the level of use, and the individual’s task performance resulting from the use of CT.

## 2.2. Research Framework and Hypothesis Development

As explained earlier, because of the nature of CT, it is necessary to examine both individual- and grouplevel factors to provide a more complete understanding of CT use. However, it is equally important to ensure that the integrated model, while having sufficient explanatory and predictive powers, is also reasonably parsimonious for theoretical neatness and for conducting empirical validations. To begin our quest, we draw from two of the most predominant research perspectives in the IS literature: one from the individual level and one from the group level. For the individual level, the most widely used perspective has been the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis 1989) and the various extensions of TAM (hereafter TAM++), including the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) (Venkatesh et al. 2003). For the group-level perspective, we draw from the adaptive structuration theory (AST) (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). To integrate the individual-level and group-level perspectives, we draw from the social comparison theory (Baron and Roper 1976, Sanders and Baron 1977), which has been applied to a wide range of phenomena, including the group polarization effects in the GSS literature (e.g., Sia et al. 1999, 2002).

Figure 1 depicts our research model which shows how four independent/moderating variables, two at individual level and two at group level, together affect CT use, which, in turn, affect task performance. In the following sections, we elaborate on our theoretical development and choice of theoretical perspectives.

2.2.1. Individual-Level Technology Acceptance Research (TAM). Developed from the theory of reasoned action (Ajzen and Fishbein 1980) to fit the context of IS, TAM has been widely used to explain individual IS adoption and use. Although it was originally developed to explain IS adoption, TAM, together with the various versions of TAM++, has been extended to examining postadoption use (e.g., Bhattacherjee 2001, Hsieh et al. 2008, Karahanna et al. 2006). TAM postulates two major beliefs as antecedents of intention to use a system: perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease-of-use (PE). PU is defined as the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance. PE refers to the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort.

In the various versions of TAM++, researchers have added more factors to the original TAM. However, none of these factors has been shown to be as powerful as PU and PE in predicting adoption or use of IS (Kim and Malhotra 2005). Lee et al. (2003) identified 21 variables that affect the four key constructs in UTAUT (see Straub and Burton-Jones 2007). It would not be wise to include all variables in UTAUT into our integrated model because after adding the other group-level variables, for a model of this size it would be difficult, if not impossible, to conduct empirical validation. Finally, as mentioned, utilitarian values, such as PU and PE, remain the most important factors in predicting adoption and use (e.g., Kim and Malhotra 2005). Considering all these and to keep the model testable, we decided to include only PU and PE as the individual-level factors in our model.

2.2.2. Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST). Building on Giddens’ (1987) structuration theory, DeSanctis and Poole (1994) developed the adaptive structuration theory (AST) to understand the dynamics and effects of GSS, a specific type of collaboration technology. According to AST,<sup>1</sup> group task performance does not result directly from the characteristics of tasks and technology, but instead from the appropriation of IT by group members under the structures of group and organization. Group structures are rules and resources provided by the technology, task, process, organizational culture, group norm, and group members’ knowledge (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). The process of creating such structures is called structuration.

Structuration is not necessarily a reactive and passive adaptation to the external environments, but has proactive and creative aspects that group members continuously refine and renovate throughout their membership tenures (Giddens 1987). IS users choose certain structures from among numerous structural potentials, and then adapt them to create their own structures. For example, of the many features available, users may choose to use certain features but not others. Users may also choose to use a feature in ways that are not intended by the designer of the system. Furthermore, consider the situation of using a group meeting scheduling function of a CT. Instead of “linking” or “uploading” their personal calendar (this was the original intent of the designer on how the system ought to be used), users may choose to respond to a meeting request on an ad hoc basis (i.e., users adapt and create new structures). This process of active choice and adaptation is what DeSanctis and Poole (1994) define as appropriation.

In the context of CT, based on AST, to achieve the original objectives as intended by the organization or designer of the technology, group members need to appropriate technology in a manner that reflects: (1) consensus on appropriation (COA), which denotes the degree of agreement among group members on how the technology should be used (Sambamurthy 1989), and (2) faithfulness of appropriation (FOA), which means the extent to which the technology is used in a manner consistent with the general objectives and procedures that it aims to promote and use (Chin et al. 1997).<sup>2</sup>

COA signifies the level of agreement among group members regarding how a particular IS should be used (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). As explained, the benefits of the use of a CT can only be realized if the entire workgroup or a sufficiently large number of members in that workgroup collectively adopt and use the CT. Initially, group members can have different ideas and opinions until agreement prevails on how to use the CT for their group work and tasks (DeSanctis and Poole 1994). Group members gradually resolve such disagreements by education and training on the systems and communications within the workgroup (Wheeler and Valacich 1996). Eventually, members may reach agreement on how to use the technology through such a structuration process.

2.2.3. Integrating Individual- and Group-Level Perspectives: Social Comparison Theory (SCT). We predict that high COA at the group level is expected to drive individual members working in the same group to one of the extremes of either high or low usage. The social comparison theory (SCT) (Baron and Roper 1976, Sanders and Baron 1977) provides a theoretical explanation for our prediction. SCT postulates that an individual member of a workgroup often forms an initial stance on an issue faced by the group. However, when exposed to other members’ views regarding the issue, he or she tends to compare his or her view to that of the other members. During this process of comparison, the initial position tends to be shifted towards those of the majority or those with the strongest opinion. SCT has been applied to explain various group and social phenomena, including the group polarization effects in group decision making (e.g., Sia et al. 2002) and initial adoption of technology by groups (Sarker et al. 2005).

Applying SCT to our context, at the initial stage, group members are more likely to have different opinions on how and which specific functions or features of the CT are to be used, i.e., low COA. As explained, the full benefit of CT can only be realized if the entire group collectively adopts and uses the CT. Therefore, as explained and illustrated earlier, even if some individual members see the benefit (e.g., high PU and PE) of using a CT, their usage of the CT is likely to be limited and infrequent. That is, the extent of use (frequency, intensity, and overall dependency) of the CT is likely to be low.

After the members have had opportunities to interact and discuss with other members, consensus on how to use the CT slowly emerges (high COA). Following the prediction of SCT, individual members tend to gravitate towards the majority’s view or those with the strongest opinion. If the majority’s stance or the strongest opinion is a negative one (limited use or not using any of the features; e.g., of the 10 features available, the majority’s stance is to use one or none of the features), then, based on SCT, individual members are likely to adjust their stance toward the negative side, thus limiting their use of the CT to only a smaller set of features. This, in turn, lowers the individual member’s extent of use of the CT. By contrast, if the majority’s stance or the strongest opinion is a positive one (most or all features ought to be used in a certain way; e.g., all 10 features available are fully utilized), then individual members are likely to adjust their stance toward the positive side, thus increasing their individual extent of use of the CT.

Our argument above parallels that of Markus (1987) who applied the critical mass theory<sup>3</sup> to investigate the usage pattern of interactive media, such as email, which are parts of CT. Markus postulates that there are only two stable states of an interactive medium usage in a community: all or nothing. That is, either all members use it (universal usage) or no one uses it. Markus argues that less-than-universal usage implies that many users experience higher costs and/or lower benefits than they would if usage were universal. To illustrate, she uses the example in which only half of a project team uses a new communication system. A person who wants to reach all members would have to use this system as well as other means to reach those members who do not use this system. This incurs high cost to the person and thus he or she would likely not use the system. Therefore, in the stable state, either the team will not use this new system and will divert back to the old way (e.g., phone call or some other means) or all members will use this system. Therefore, critical mass theory also provides an explanation for our prediction that high COA, which is achieved after consensus on how to use CT has been reached (i.e., a steady state in critical mass theory’s term), polarizes individual users’ extent of CT use (frequency, intensity, and overall dependency). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> COA (at the group level) has a polarizing effect (either increases or decreases) on individual members’ use of collaborative technologies.

TAM asserts that two individual user beliefs, PU and PE, exert direct influence on individual adoption and use of IT (e.g., Davis 1989, Davis et al. 1989). However, the social constructionists contend that the objective and absolute assessment of IT qualities is an illusion (Lee 1994), and argue that the characteristics of IT emerge as users interact with the system and with other users (Carlson and Zmud 1999). IT use is both a function of individual beliefs of the technical features, such as PU and PE, and of numerous structures embedded within the technology, group, and organization (Watson-Manheim and Belanger 2007). We theorize that when there is high group consensus on CT use, the effects of individual perception on technical features, such as PU and PE, would fade out.

We again draw from the social comparison theory (SCT) (Baron and Roper 1976) to explain the moderating role of COA. At the initial stage, without any frame of reference, members of a workgroup form their initial attitude toward a CT relying solely on their own perceptions of the technological characteristics, such as PU and PE, of the CT in question. These beliefs on PU and PE ultimately affect the use of the system<sup>4</sup> (mediated through attitude and intention as postulated by the theory of reason action (TRA) and theory of planned behavior (TPB)). However, after the workgroup has had opportunities to interact and talk with the other members, and when consensus among group members on how the system ought to be used has emerged (i.e., high COA), individual members’ beliefs of PU and PE become less important in determining their use of the system. This is because, according to SCT, once a consensus has emerged, group members are likely to shift their initial position and go along with the group (Sanders and Baron 1977). Therefore, even members who have negative evaluations of the PU and PE of the system are likely to go along with the decision of the group and use the system in the agreed-upon way. As an example, suppose that some members of a workgroup have a low evaluation of the PU and PE of a group meeting scheduling system. However, the workgroup has reached an agreement that members should link their personal calendars to the system so that the system can automatically search for the earliest possible meeting date for the workgroup (high COA). With agreement within the workgroup on how it should be used, those members who have low evaluations of the PU and PE of the system would have to yield to the majority and strong consensus, thus using the system according to the way agreed upon by the team. Most importantly, this also suggests that the influence of individual evaluation of the PU and PE of the system on individual usage of the system becomes weakened because the individual member has yielded to the consensus of the team.

On the other hand, when there is low consensus on how to use the technology (low COA), according to SCT, there is no basis for individual members to adjust their initial stance, which is formed based on their perception of the technological characteristics of the system. Therefore, the influences of PU and PE remain unchanged and significant. In short, when group members have high COA, the influences of PU and PE on CT use are reduced. In contrast, when COA is low, the influences of PU and PE remain significant. Note that different groups, when facing the system, may undergo different structuration processes (DeSanctis and Poole 1994, Salisbury et al. 2002). Therefore, even if the same CT is introduced to different workgroups within an organization at the same time, at any given time after the introduction of the CT, each workgroup is likely to have a different level of COA. Taken together, we propose the following hypotheses.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2A).</sup> The level of COA (at the group level) negatively moderates the effect of PU on collaborative technology use (at the individual level) such that the relationship becomes weaker with increasing levels of COA.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2B).</sup> The level of COA (at the group level) negatively moderates the effect of PE on collaborative technology use (at the individual level) such that the relationship becomes weaker with increasing levels of COA.

It is worth pointing out that although subjective norms are not part of the original TAM as conceptualized by Davis et al. (1989), it is an important construct in UTAUT and in some versions of TAM++ (Venkatesh et al. 2003). Subjective norms, a form of social influence, have been defined as a “person’s perception that most people who are important to him think he/she should or should not perform the behavior in question” (Fishbein and Ajzen 1975, p. 302). Therefore, one might argue that by including subjective norms, UTAUT has taken collective influences into consideration. However, we take the same position as Burton-Jones and Gallivan (2007), that subjective norms have been conceptualized at the individual level, and subsequent empirical tests of the UTUAT have measured the construct at the individual level without verifying the actual influence from the group level. Most importantly, we believe that subjective norms do not capture the group-level effects that are needed to better explain and predict the decision to use CT.<sup>5</sup> As opposed to subjective norms, which were originally conceptualized as an individual-level construct, COA and FOA are conceptualized and consistently used as grouplevel constructs. Utilizing group-level constructs has the advantage that researchers are able to specifically examine the effects of workgroup differences. Furthermore, subjective norms are broader in scope than are COA and FOA because it goes beyond the influence of the group. It incorporates the influence of superiors and other co-workers, who may not be part of the employee’s workgroup. Finally, by differentiating COA from subjective norms, this study further contributes to the literature by more clearly pinpointing a specific type of norm and its effects in the context of CT use.

## 2.2.4. Collaborative Technologies Use, Faithfulness of Appropriation (FOA), and Performance.

(a) Extent of use of CT

Although the empirical evidence in the literature regarding the relationship between information systems (IS) use and individual task performance is mixed, the general belief is that there is a positive relationship between them (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006). One of the reasons for this mixed result may be that past research has measured IS use in many different ways (Burton-Jones and Straub 2006).

As indicated earlier, the extent of use of the system is reflected by the frequency and duration of use, as well as a user’s overall dependency on the system. Despite the previously mentioned early studies providing mixed empirical results on the relationship between users’ extent of use of information systems and individual task performance, this may be attributable to the many ways in which early research measured IS use. More recent research has affirmed this positive relationship (e.g., Sundaram et al. 2007). Although there are few empirical studies specifically examining the effect of CT use on individual task performance, there have been some studies investigating the effectiveness of group support systems (GSS), a form of CT, on individual performance. Unfortunately, these studies have also produced inconclusive results (see Dennis et al. 2001 for a review). Nonetheless, Benbasat and Lim (1993) and Dennis et al. (2001) conclude that, in general, there is a positive relationship between GSS use and individual performance when some moderators are taken into account. Following this, we propose H3 as follows.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Extent of use of collaborative technologies (at the individual level) is positively related to individual task performance.

## (b) Faithfulness of use (FOA) of CT:

In this study we are mainly interested in the extent of use of CT at the individual level. However, we believe that how a CT is used may also affect performance. FOA is the extent to which a system is used in alignment with the system designer or the organization’s original intentions. We assume that designers or organizations have certain intentions when designing or implementing a system. AST (DeSanctis and Poole 1994) proposes that the appropriation status of instruments and tools determines the outcomes of using them in tasks. Specifically, users’ faithful appropriation according to organizational or designers’ original intentions is more likely to produce the originally intended value of IS. The GSS literature also provides evidence that IT value can be better realized in organizations whose members use the target IT according to the organization’s original intentions (e.g., Dennis et al. 2001, Limayem et al. 2006).

This positive relationship between FOA and group performance has been shown to translate into a positive relationship between FOA and an individual performance. Theorizing in the context of GSS, Limayem et al. (2006) argue and show empirically that faithful appropriation of the decision guidance in GSS would lead to individual members’ systematic scanning of the decision search space, and the resulting filtering and categorization of information. This enables faster information integration and processing, thus leading to reduced decision time and more positive perceptions of the decision outcome on the part of individual group members.

In the same vein, we believe that a positive relationship between FOA and an individual group member’s task performance holds for CT in general. CT are designed with the general intention of supporting workgroups, as well as individual members within the workgroups, such that they can perform efficiently and effectively. To realize this intention, designers and organizations often need to impose certain structures in ways in which both the workgroups and individual members within the workgroups can benefit. Therefore, groups that faithfully adopt such structures would find that these structures not only greatly enhance overall group performance, but also improve individual performance (Chin et al. 1997). As an example, when using a group meeting scheduling system, if a workgroup faithfully appropriates the structures imposed, the system should be able to automatically search and schedule the earliest possible meeting times when promoted. This not only greatly improves the overall effectiveness and efficiency of the workgroup but also saves the time and effort of individual members because they no longer need to use emails or the phone to send individual inquiries or responses. Similarly, if a workgroup faithfully appropriates the structures imposed by a document management system, the system not only can greatly enhance the overall group performance, but group members will also find that it takes less time to search for relevant information, that it is easier to compile reports, and that it improves their overall capabilities in executing tasks. Following the above arguments on the effects of FOA on individual task performance, we propose the following hypothesis.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> FOA (at the group level) is positively related to individual task performance.

## 3. Research Methodology

## 3.1. Multilevel Approach

Because our study investigates both individual- and group-level factors regarding individual CT use and task performance (i.e., individual cognitive beliefs and group appropriation), we used the multilevel approach that considers both individual- and grouplevel variables simultaneously. The hierarchical linear model (HLM) can handle data in different levels of hierarchy to separate the influences of individuals and groups on any dependent variables.<sup>6</sup>

## 3.2. Data Collection and Sampling

We collected data from the IT department of a nationwide banking institution that implemented and used collaborative technologies. The system runs on the bank’s Intranet and consists of several functions of a typical collaborative system. Table 1 describes some of the key functions of the system. ## 4.2. Hypothesis Testing

We used Windows SPSS/PC version 12.0 for descriptive statistics and HLM 6.02 to test the multilevel model. To reduce the possibility of multicollinearity, we performed group mean centering on all predictors before conducting the analysis. Following Podsakoff et al. (2003), we took several precautions to minimize common method bias (CMB). First, to increase response candidness, we presented the respondents with detailed information about the precautions taken to ensure confidentiality of their individual responses. Also, to decrease respondent evaluation apprehension, we assured the respondents that there were no right or wrong answers to the measures in the survey. Furthermore, to test for potential CMB, we performed several tests as recommended by Podsakoff and Organ (1986) on the data. The detailed test results are reported in Appendix 2. The results suggest CMB is not a problem in our data set. Still, to control for any potential of CMB, following Podsakoff et al. (2003), we statistically partialed out common method variance by controlling for respondents’ general attitudes toward technology in the analyses. More details are reported in Appendix 2. Since the workgroups that participated in our study varied in size, we controlled for group size in our data analysis. Finally, we controlled four individual demographic factors: age, gender, organization tenure, and educational level, that are known to affect technology use. The results reported in the rest of the paper are after controlling for general attitudes toward technology, group size, and the four demographic factors. ## 5. Discussion and Conclusion

Our study began with the premise that collaborative technologies (CT) adoption and use are more complicated than are individually oriented technologies, which have been the major subject of investigation in technology adoption and use research. Integrating individual-level technology adoption research and AST, we have developed a model that considers both individual- and group-level factors that might affect CT use and individual task performance. A series of hypotheses regarding PU and PE, and the moderating roles of COA and FOA were generated. Drawing from social comparison theory (SCT), we theorized that the influences of PU and PE could be overridden by the degree of appropriation. Specifically, for workgroups in which consensus has emerged on how to use a CT (high COA), when individual perception of PU and PE of a CT does not coincide with the majority of group members, group consensus on how to use the CT may weaken the effects of individual-level factors, such as PU and PE.

Our hypotheses were supported by our data. Our results indicate that the relationships between PU or PE with use of CT are affected by COA. In particular, PU and PE have strong predictive power when group members have lower consensus on appropriation (COA) of the target technology. However, when

COA is high, the influences of PU and PE on individual use of CT are weakened, and individual use of CT is influenced more by group consensus and opinions; the higher the level of consensus among group members on how to use the CT, the higher the level of individual use of the CT.

This finding is consistent with the literature on social comparison theory and group polarization (Baron and Roper 1976, Sanders and Baron 1977, Sia et al. 2002). Previous research on group polarization suggests that group members are likely to be influenced by group pressure when they perceive consensus (Sia et al. 2002). Thus, although some individual group members may not perceive CT as being useful or easy to use, they are likely to yield to the group consensus and use the CT according to the way agreed upon by the group. COA not only has a direct influence on individual CT use, but can also mitigate the influences of PU and PE on individual CT use.

The finding regarding the substitutive relationship between group-level appropriation (COA) and individual-level drivers of technology use (PU and PE) is an important one and warrants further attention. As today’s organizational work nature increasingly becomes more collaborative and team-oriented, technology support in organizations also becomes more collaborative in nature. The substitutive relationship found indicates the deficiency when examining CT using the theoretical lens from the individual-level perspective. As theorized by critical mass theory (Markus 1987), for individual team members, the benefits of using such team-based technologies cannot be fully realized without the full participation of all team members. Therefore, group-level factors need to be taken into consideration to better understand and explain individual members’ adoption and use of team-based technologies, such as CT. This substitutive relationship between individual- and grouplevel factors not only serves as a foundation for future research of CT but also points to a new direction to examine technology use in team contexts, which is radically different from the traditional individualbased technology adoption and use research.

Consistent with the majority of the prior empirical findings on the relationship between individual IS use and individual task performance, we also found that individual extent of CT use (as measured by frequency and duration of use, and overall dependency on the CT) is positively related to individual task performance. That is, the higher the extent of use of CT, the higher the task performance. Finally, we also found that workgroups that had high FOA achieved higher levels of individual task performance. FOA is the consistency between the actual manner in which a CT is being used and the organization’s original intent. High FOA signifies that the technology is used in the ways that it was originally intended to be used. Although this result is consistent with that of Dennis et al. (2001), in their study, FOA was modeled and analyzed using individuallevel data. In our study, while we aggregated the individual team member $' \mathrm { s }$ perception of FOA to form group-level FOA, using a more sophisticated statistical technique (HLM), we were able to explicitly take group membership into consideration. Thus, our study is able to precisely pinpoint and examine the effects of workgroup differences. Therefore, this finding extends and adds to the literature on GSS and AST.

## 5.1. Limitations and Future Research

As with any study, ours has several limitations. The first is related to our sample size. As mentioned, although our sample size is a respectable one when compared to some other multilevel organizational studies, confidence with the results obtained and the conclusions drawn could be strengthened with a larger sample size. This suggests the need for future replications of our study. However, despite the relatively small sample size, we were able to detect statistically significant effects that are consistent with our theory-based predictions. This suggests that sample size may not be an issue with this study.

The second limitation is that we did not examine group performance in this study. It would have been ideal if we could have examined whether higher FOA indeed leads to higher group performance, as suggested by AST and the literature on GSS. However, our sample precluded us from gathering such data. Related to this, we employed self-reported measures to capture individual task performance and use of CT. A better alternative would be to use objective measures, such as supervisor ratings or the results of employees’ annual performance appraisals for task performance and system-captured data for the extent of CT use. Self-reported measures are also more subject to common-method bias. Unfortunately, these objective measures were not accessible to us at this research site. It also needs to be pointed out that our data were collected from individual users of a CT using one single method, a survey questionnaire. In the ideal case, data should be collected from multiple sources using different measurement methods. Therefore, when interpreting our results, one has to be aware of the potential threat of common-method bias. Nonetheless, we took steps procedurally when collecting data to reduce the threat of common-method bias. As reported in the results section, we also statistically controlled for the potential threat of commonmethod bias in our analyses.

There are several avenues for future research. First, this study highlights the importance of taking grouplevel factors into consideration when investigating IT adoption and use. It clearly points to the need for future research to better understand how group processes affect individual use of technology and the specific mechanisms through which these processes influence such behavior. Our study only reveals the effects of COA and FOA on individual use behavior and subsequent task performance. Future research should examine other factors that may affect individual use of technology and the resulting task performance.

Second, this study collected only cross-sectional data to examine post-adoption use of CT at a given timeline. To provide a richer understanding of the ongoing structuration process, it would be necessary to collect longitudinal data. This is definitely an avenue for future research.

Finally, in our study, we assume that an individual’s CT use behavior is only affected by the primary workgroup that he or she is associated with. In today’s organizational working environment, it is common for an individual to be associated with multiple workgroups. Therefore, the structuration and appropriation patterns of a workgroup may affect the structuration and appropriation patterns of another workgroup through shared membership. Investigating such “spill-over” effect would be another interesting future research direction. In particular, future research can investigate how this spill-over effect at the workgroup level affects adoption and use of CT at the organizational level. This is especially interesting when there are conflicts among different workgroups in structuration and appropriation patterns.

## 5.2. Contributions

The major contribution of our study is to develop a comprehensive understanding of the post-adoption use of CT. We argued at the outset that the nature of CT, and therefore the adoption and use decision of CT, is very different from that of individually oriented systems. While the rich body of literature on technology adoption, which focuses solely on individuallevel factors, may well serve the individually oriented technologies, this body of literature needs to be extended or modified to better understand CT. As technology use in organizations has increasingly become more collaborative in nature, the need to develop a more comprehensive understanding of CT becomes more pressing. Our integrated model is a first step towards filling this gap. Specifically, this study has advanced the technology adoption literature by incorporating the influences of a salient social aspect in today’s organization, i.e., workgroups. This not only enriches our understanding of CT use, but also opens up a new perspective in this area of research.

Building on Sarker et al. (2005), we specifically model group-level factors in our conceptualization and analyses. Although multilevel analysis is not totally new to the IS discipline, applying it to understanding technology use is novel. By doing so, we are able to more specifically understand the effects of group differences. We hope this study has opened a new method of enquiry to researchers who are interested in studying technology adoption and use in groups or organizations. In particular, organizations are naturally nested in hierarchical structures. Therefore, many of the phenomena in organizational research are multilevel in nature, with the behaviors or actions at a lower level often affected by some other factors at higher levels. This is also true for most of the issues related to IS research. Other than research related to CT, considerable IS research naturally involves multiple levels. With the increasing capability of technology, IS support for organizations has moved from individual to higher levels, such as teams, departments, or even entire enterprises (e.g.,

ERP, CRM) with decisions made at one level affecting the decisions and outcomes at other levels. Our research contributes to the literature by highlighting the need to reexamine some of the classic issues in IS research with the new perspective of viewing the phenomena at multiple levels.

Our research may also have implications for research in multiteaming. We observe that group characteristics, such as COA and FOA, have significant effects on use of CT. However, in some organizations, it is common that an individual may be a member of multiple workgroups at the same time. When there are conflicts or a lack of shared practices across workgroups, individual member use of CT or task performance may be affected negatively. Therefore, our study serves as a foundation for future inquiries in this direction.

From the managerial perspective, our findings caution practitioners that in order to increase adoption and to enhance the effectiveness of CT in terms of improving task performance, organizations must nurture constructive and positive consensus among group members, rather than focus solely on individual perceptions or the technical features of CT.

In summary, our study is clearly a first step towards creating a more comprehensive understanding of technology use and its impacts beyond the wellaccepted single-individual-level view of technology adoption. We believe that future research in this direction will contribute rich knowledge for this important area of research.

## Appendix 2. Testing and Controlling for the Potential Threat of Common Method Bias (CMB)

Following Podsakoff et al. (2003), we took several precautions to minimize common method bias (CMB). First, to increase response candidness, we presented the respondents with detailed information about the precautions taken to ensure the confidentiality of their individual responses. Furthermore, to decrease respondent evaluation apprehension, we assured the respondents that there were no right or wrong answers to the measures in the survey.

Next, we applied Harman’s one-factor test to the data (Podsakoff and Organ 1986). We performed an exploratory factor analysis on all the variables. No single factor was observed, and no single factor accounted for a majority of the covariance in the variables, suggesting that the common method bias is not a major concern in the present study.

To further ascertain that CMB was not a threat, we also conducted another two sets of confirmatory factor analysis for the four individual-level constructs (Podsakoff and Organ 1986), one with four factors specified and another with only one factor specified. When method variance is a significant problem, a single factor model should fit the data as well as a more complex model (McFarland and Sweeney 1992). The results obtained by using LISREL $( \chi ^ { 2 } =$ 14107, df = 71; RMSEA = 0.060; NFI = 0.98; NNFI = 0.99; $\mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 9 9 )$ indicated an acceptable fit for the four-factor model. The four-factor model’s fit was compared with the fit of the one-factor model (<sup>2</sup> = 1163300, df = 77; RMSEA = 0.27; NFI = 0.83; NNFI = 0.81; $\mathrm { C F I } = 0 . 8 4 )$ . The difference between chi-square values $\left( \chi ^ { 2 } = 1 \right.$ 149103, df = 6, p < 00001) revealed that the four-factor model yielded a better fit than the single-factor model.

Finally, following Podsakoff et al. (2003), we statistically partialed out common method variance in our analyses. We especially controlled for the respondents’ general attitudes toward technology in the analyses. General attitude toward technology reflects individual differences that systematically lead people to express positive or negative evaluations of the various aspects of their technology (Gopal et al. 1992). We measured general attitude toward technology using six items developed by Gopal et al. (1992), which consists of three dimensions: (1) level of comfort, (2) degree of respect, and (3) challenge. The Cronbach’s alpha of these six items was 0.903. The variables in our research model were respondents’ evaluations of various facets of their technology and work outcomes. Hence, controlling for general attitude toward technology allowed us to factor out common method variance that can be ascribed to such attitudes in our analyses.
