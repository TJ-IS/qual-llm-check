# Issues and Opinions

# Publication Opportunities in Premier Business Outlets: How Level Is the Playing Field?

Joseph S. Valacich, Mark A. Fuller, Christoph Schneider

Department of Information Systems, College of Business, Washington State University, Pullman, Washington 99164 {jsv@wsu.edu, mark@wsu.edu, schneiderc@wsu.edu}

Alan R. Dennis

Information Systems Department, Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, Indiana 47405, ardennis@indiana.edu

his paper reports an analysis of the proportion of faculty publishing articles in premier business journals (i.e., Tthe ratio of authors of premier business journal articles to total faculty of a discipline) across the disciplines of accounting, finance, management, marketing, and information systems (IS) for the years 1994–2003. This analysis revealed that over this period the management discipline had on average the highest proportion of faculty publishing in premier journals (12.7 authors per 100 management faculty), followed by finance (9.4 authors per 100 faculty), marketing (9.2 authors per 100 faculty), IS (5.5 authors per 100 faculty), and accounting (4.8 authors per 100 faculty). A further analysis examined these ratios for the different disciplines over time, finding that the ratios of authors to faculty have actually decreased for the disciplines of marketing and IS over this time period but have remained stable for the disciplines of accounting, management, and finance. Given steady growth in faculty size of all disciplines, the proportion of faculty publishing articles in premier journals in 2003 for all disciplines is lower than their 10-year averages, with IS having the lowest proportion in 2003. A sensitivity analysis reveals that without substantial changes that would allow more IS faculty to publish in the premier journals (e.g., by increasing publication cycles, number of premier outlets, and so on), IS will continue to lag far below the average of other disciplines. The implications of these findings for IS researchers, for institutions and administrators of IS programs, and for the IS academic discipline are examined. Based on these implications, recommendations for the IS discipline are presented.

## Introduction

At most research universities in the United States, hiring, promotion, and tenure decisions are heavily based on researcher productivity (Swanson 2004); most often, researcher productivity is measured as the number of publications in general and the number of premier publications in particular (Fishe 1998, Gomez-Mejia and Balkin 1992, Henderson et al. 1990, Swanson 2004, Swidler and Goldreyer 1998).<sup>1</sup> This widely accepted method of assessing research productivity is grounded on the assumption that scholars in different disciplines have relatively equal opportunities to publish in the premier journals of their respective fields. However, given different numbers of faculty and different numbers of premier journals in various business school disciplines, how level is the playing field?

One key component influencing this equity issue is how different institutions—and disciplines—define the concept of a premier outlet. What is considered a premier journal for purposes of promotion and tenure decisions can be quite different across institutions, even if those institutions are similarly categorized as research extensive or research intensive by the Carnegie Foundation (2000).<sup>2</sup> More significant to this discussion is how prior research has demonstrated that there is a vast discrepancy between the ratio of authors of premier journals to total faculty for different business disciplines (Swanson 2004). Specific to the IS community, in a recent interview, Professor William King— Founding President of the Association of Information Systems (AIS)—noted that “the ratio of ‘A’ [IS] journal papers published each year to the total AIS membership is ridiculously low” (Lytros 2005b, p. 9). Perceived inequities in both the number of premier journals and the number of relative opportunities in those journals can have many potential impacts on individual behavior, as well as downstream effects on the discipline as a whole.

Typically, within a given university, representatives of each discipline negotiate with the dean’s office to develop a list of journals that will be considered premier outlets for purposes of promotion and tenure. Disciplines with a higher number of premier journals often justify this difference by the larger sizes of the disciplines and the higher number of scholars in the respective fields—not an unreasonable justification. However, such a justification requires an accurate assessment of the size of the discipline and then an accurate assessment of the actual number of premier publication opportunities in that discipline; in many cases, however, such assessment may be based on legacy perceptions and inaccurate data. Organizational politics also will play a role in such a negotiation, as disciplines with more influence at a particular institution may have an advantage in the process of categorizing premier outlets. Finally, institutions need to be careful not to confuse the number of journal outlets with the opportunities for authors to publish in these outlets, because such opportunities are a function of the number of journals, the frequency at which each journal is published (e.g., quarterly, monthly), the number of articles in a typical issue, and the pattern of coauthorship that is common in the discipline. Also, opportunities for publishing outside one’s home discipline should be considered in any such discussion.

This scientometric<sup>3</sup> paper examines the relative differences in the proportion of authors publishing in the premier journals across business disciplines, as measured by the ratio of authors of premier journal articles to faculty members in a discipline. This assessment provides insights for making cross-discipline comparisons, enabling more informed discussions regarding the relative productivity of various disciplines as well as the relative equity of using common standards for annual performance reviews and promotion/tenure policies.

This paper is organized as follows. We briefly review existing relevant research regarding publication opportunities. This is followed by a discussion of the journal selection and data-collection process. Then the results of the analyses are presented. Finally, the implications of the findings are explored and recommendations for the discipline of IS are provided.

## Prior Relevant Research

Over the past several years, researcher productivity has received a good deal of attention in various business disciplines (e.g., Athey and Plotnicki 2000, Dennis et al. 2006, Swanson 2004, Trieschmann et al. 2000). Within this broad topic area, researchers have examined the distinction between research performance and MBA program performance (Trieschmann et al. 2000), reviewed publication opportunities within certain disciplines (e.g., Athey and Plotnicki 2000, Beatty and Goodacre 2004), analyzed the value of premier publications (Swidler and Goldreyer 1998), determined interdisciplinary differences in the proportion of faculty publishing in the premier journals (Swanson 2004), or analyzed the appropriateness of research standards for promotion and tenure in IS (Dennis et al. 2006).

In 2000, for example, Trieschmann et al. provided a detailed analysis of researcher productivity as measured by article page count in different premier journals. The focus of that study was to compare research performance and MBA program performance across different business schools. Given its focus, the study examined the combined research productivity of eight business disciplines rather than the distinct publication opportunities across the different disciplines. Nevertheless, relevant to our research, this study created a methodology for identifying the premier outlets for each discipline, which we apply here.

Various researchers have also examined the productivity of scholars and their institutional affiliation to rank schools and authors. For example, Athey and Plotnicki (2000) examined researcher productivity by author and institutional affiliation within IS. In that analysis, only research published in a broad range of IS-related journals was considered. Similarly, Chua et al. (2002) focused on examining researcher productivity in a large number of IS journals, among other research goals.

Swanson (2004), in contrast, examined the proportion of faculty publishing in the premier journals across different business school disciplines but limited his analysis to the accounting, finance, marketing, and management disciplines (using variations of the journal list provided by Trieschmann et al. 2000) for the period 1980–1999. One of the key findings of Swanson’s study was that there exists a stark discrepancy among the proportion of faculty publishing in premier journals across the different disciplines, with a significantly lower proportion of faculty publishing in accounting than in the fields of finance, management, or marketing (averaged across the different journal sets analyzed). Notably missing from this analysis was the discipline of IS, a field that has undergone tremendous growth over the past 10 years.

Although researchers continue to explore the topic of research productivity and publication from a variety of perspectives, a thorough analysis comparing the proportion of faculty publishing in the premier journals of different disciplines that includes the IS discipline has not been conducted to date. As argued by Baskerville and Myers (2002) as well as Peffers and Ya (2003), the status of the IS discipline has changed considerably (matured) over the past decade, warranting its inclusion in such comparisons. Following Swanson’s (2004) approach, this article will attempt to fill this void by including the IS discipline in such an analysis, focusing on the proportion of authors publishing in premier outlets of the aforementioned disciplines over the period 1994–2003. Thus, our analysis will address the following overarching question: How does the proportion of faculty publishing in the premier journals in the IS discipline compare to those in the accounting, finance, management, and marketing disciplines? In the following section, we will describe our data collection and analytic approach to address this question.

## Methods

To determine how the proportion of faculty publishing in the premier journals in IS compares to those in the different business school disciplines, we calculated an authors/faculty ratio (to control for the differences in faculty sizes). We determined the number of authors who published articles in the disciplines premier publication outlets and the faculty sizes of each discipline during the time period analyzed. This enabled us to calculate the authors/faculty ratio, as well as to examine the changes in this ratio over time. Given that business school scholars routinely publish work outside their “home” disciplines, we assessed the extent to which this “cross-discipline publishing” occurred and its influence on the results. We then conducted sensitivity analyses to determine the effects of various alternatives for increasing the proportion of authors publishing in premier journals within the IS discipline.

## Faculty Size per Discipline

To determine faculty size across disciplines, we obtained the numbers of doctoral-level faculty from the Association to Advance Collegiate Schools of Business (AACSB) for the period 1994–2003 (see Table 5). The AACSB collects these data from its members as part of its annual salary survey. Although not all faculty members of the respondent schools strive to publish in premier business school journals, publishing research is an integral part for maintaining AACSB accreditation. As estimating the number of faculty actually trying to publish in the premier journals is a very subjective (and nearly impossible) task, we decided to use the faculty numbers provided by the AACSB as a proxy, assuming that the ratio of faculty members trying to publish in the premiers to total (reported) faculty is fairly equal across the different business school disciplines. Using these data also helps in comparing this study with earlier research (e.g., Swanson 2004).<sup>10</sup>

## Sensitivity Analyses

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. Specifically, we first added the data from Management Science to our data set, as this is often considered a premier journal in many business school disciplines. Further, the two premier IS journals (i.e., MISQ and ISR) have not always published at full capacity during the time period analyzed. We therefore examined the relative proportions of faculty publishing in the premier journals of the different disciplines if ISR and MISQ had published at peak capacity. Finally, we used the most recent data of our analysis to determine the potential effects additional volumes of the premier IS journals would have on the authors/faculty ratio and how these changes would affect the relative differences across disciplines.

## Results

## Faculty Size Across Disciplines

An ANOVA done on the mean faculty size over the time period 1994–2003 found a significant difference in the number of faculty across disciplines $( F ( 4 , 4 5 ) =$ 2864, $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ (cf. Swanson 2004). Specifically, this analysis found accounting to have the largest faculty (M 368870, SD 3274) and IS to have the smallest faculty $( M = 1 , 9 2 2 . 4 0 , S D = 5 6 8 . 8 )$ , with the other disciplines lying in between these extremes (see Table 6 for pairwise comparisons of the different disciplines).

To examine the changes in faculty size over time, we also analyzed the faculty size data using regression (cf. Swanson 2004). Specifically, for each discipline, we regressed the faculty size on time, finding that the faculty size in all business school disciplines examined grew significantly during the time period analyzed (see Figure 1 and Table 7). Overall, IS appears to have grown the most over the period analyzed.

## Discussion

The results of our analysis can be quickly summarized. First, our results show that faculty size and proportions of authors publishing in premier journals for the business school disciplines of accounting, finance, management, marketing, and IS between 1994 and 2003 are not the same.<sup>14</sup> Specifically, management has the greatest proportion, with 12.7 authors per 100 faculty members, followed by finance with 9.4 Authors/100; marketing has approximately 9.2 Authors/100, while IS and accounting have about 5.5 and 4.8 Authors/100, respectively.

Second, in addition to relative overall differences, there are also differences in the trends during this time period. Specifically, while the number of faculty in all disciplines of AACSB member schools has increased significantly, the proportion of authors publishing in premier journals has kept pace only for some of these disciplines. Thus, there has been a significant decrease in authors per 100 faculty members in marketing and IS. For the management, accounting, and finance disciplines, the ratio of authors to faculty members has remained constant over the period of this analysis. The results of a cross-discipline publishing analysis for the years 1994, 1998, and 2003 show that faculty members in the different disciplines publish their work primarily in the premier journals of their own disciplines. For the years analyzed, the differences across disciplines in terms of the amount of work published in non-home-discipline premier journals was minimal and did not alter the relative results of our analyses. This is an important finding, because one possible interpretation for such differences in Authors/100 per discipline is that some faculty in some disciplines do more cross-publishing than others. This appears not to be the case, at least for the set of journals in this analysis.

Third, a sensitivity analysis showed that including Management Science in the journal set did not alter the results in a meaningful way, as scholars from all business school disciplines have the opportunity to publish articles in Management Science. ## How Did We Get into This Situation?

It appears that the decrease in the Authors/100 ratio in the IS discipline is to a large extent based on the growth of the discipline while the number of authors publishing in the premiers stayed constant. However, despite growing submission rates (see Benbasat 2000, 2001; DeSanctis 2003), the two premier IS journals have not always published at full capacity, suggesting that the reasons for the current situation might be multifaceted.

In this empirical piece, we did not attempt to explain how we got into this situation. Nevertheless, one plausible explanation for the current situation could be that the IS discipline has acted with the zeal of a neophyte—in other words, the IS discipline might have tried too hard in its pursuit of legitimacy. Swanson (2004) used Ellison’s (2002) q-r theory to explain differences in publication activity across different disciplines. This theory suggests that social norms develop over time regarding what is considered acceptable quality (Ellison 2002). Specifically, there will be a shift toward an emphasis on methodological rigor (r quality) instead of on a submission’s contribution (q quality). The search for legitimacy of the IS discipline, and the accompanying debates over rigor and relevance of IS research (e.g., Benbasat and Zmud 1999, Davenport and Markus 1999, Lee 1999, Lyytinen 1999), can be seen as contributing to a trend of higher norms for what is considered acceptable r quality (see also DeSanctis 2003, Robey 2003). As no research study is without flaws (Dennis and Valacich 2001, McGrath 1982), many papers get rejected due to deficiencies in r quality, as editors tend to implement rather than overrule reviewers’ suggestions (see also Saunders 2006, Starbuck 2005), leading to a decrease in publication output.

Whereas Ellison (2002) originally assumed that editors would overrule reviewers in an attempt to fill journal slots, Swanson (2004) offers two compelling reasons for editors’ reluctance to overrule the reviewers: the editors’ dependence on competent reviewers, and the editors’ own reputation, as they do not want to be regarded by reviewers as having standards below the norm. This reluctance to overrule reviewers’ recommendations can lead to a journal not publishing at full capacity. Further, the authors’ time needed to increase a paper’s r quality takes time away from more productive research efforts (Ellison 2002), leading to a lower article submission rate (Swanson 2004), which can eventually diminish a journal’s publication pipeline (however, the growth of the field can, by itself, lead to an increase in overall submissions).

Another factor leading to editors’ reluctance to overrule the reviewers particular to the IS discipline is the two-tier review system. Whereas in February 2005 the editorial board members of premier journals in other business school disciplines were comprised primarily of full professors (around 90%), only about half (around 49%) of the editorial board members of ISR and MISQ were full professors. Given that junior reviewers and editors focus relatively more on methodology (i.e., r quality) than on contribution (i.e., q quality; cf. Starbuck 2003), junior associate editors are more likely to follow reviewers’ recommendations based on r quality. Furthermore, in a two-tier review system, an editor has to overrule the reviewers as well as the associate editor when deciding to accept a high-q paper that has a slightly lower r quality.<sup>16</sup>

Ongoing debates about the diversity and legitimacy of the IS discipline (e.g., Benbasat and Weber 1996; Benbasat and Zmud 2003; DeSanctis 2003; Galliers 2003; Piccoli and Ives 2003; Robey 1996, 2003) are also likely to have caused an exclusion of high-q quality papers from certain journals (see Dennis et al. 2006). Combined with an increased focus on methodological rigor—also partly fueled by the field’s quest for legitimacy—this has led to a situation where the IS field appears to “eat its young” (Robey 2003, p. 355). This in turn limits the acceptance of high-q quality papers (despite growing submission rates) and at the same time places barriers for young IS faculty to publish a sufficient number of premier articles to meet tenure criteria at many institutions (Dennis et al. 2006). Although the present empirical study does not attempt to discover which factor primarily contributed to the current situation, the results are likely to have important implications for a variety of constituents.

## Implications for IS Researchers

Independent of the mechanism at play, the premier journals within the IS discipline have imposed limits on the number of publications. Such limitations may lead to greater perceptions of unfairness, potentially leading to dissatisfaction and various unproductive behaviors of individual researchers (e.g., Adams 1963, 1965; Bandura 1986; Crosby 1976; Sweeney et al. 1990; Vroom 1964). Through a variety of mechanisms, the current inequitable situation would likely negatively influence the productivity of at least some members of the IS community (see also Dennis et al. 2006).

## Implications for Academic Institutions and Administrators

In addition to the implications for individual researchers, these results also have important implications for institutions and administrators of IS programs. The implications of these results clearly demonstrate that all business disciplines are not playing on a level field when it comes to publishing in premier business outlets. If the institutions wish to establish an equitable situation across disciplines, then administrators need to be aware of the present situation and account for these differences in publication opportunities (irrespective of the underlying reasons) when making policy decisions, resource allocations, salary decisions, and, most important, promotion and tenure decisions. For example, Swanson argued that “the magnitude of the differences [in the proportion of faculty publishing in premier journals] is sufficient to indicate that publishing a major article in accounting is more competitive than publishing a major article in finance, management, and marketing” (2004, p. 248).

The results of our study support Swanson’s conclusion and indicate that the situation is similar for IS. As a result, there are some detrimental effects to IS departments: Lower (perceived) researcher productivity and lower perceived publication in the highest quality outlets will lead to lower stature in colleges, relative to other departments. This in turn will lead to less IS presence on tenure and promotion committees as well as less IS department input into strategic decision issues and key policymaking committees. We have conducted a separate analysis on the implications of overly high-publication requirements on the promotion and tenure process (see Dennis et al. 2006).

## Implications for the IS Discipline

Fewer relative publication opportunities in premier journals may not only impact individual researchers, institutions, and administrators, but may also have widespread deleterious effects on the IS discipline as a whole. For instance, limited publication opportunities may result in lower promotion and tenure rates across the IS discipline when compared to other business disciplines. Lower promotion and tenure rates throughout the IS discipline will likely have tremendous long-term (negative) impacts on the discipline. For example, lower promotion and tenure rates across the IS discipline are likely to foster widespread perceptions that IS faculty are less-skilled researchers than faculty in other disciplines. Additionally, and more critically, lower promotion and tenure rates in IS will cause greater faculty turnover within IS departments. This turnover will act to weaken programs over time, both in terms of political power within specific institutions as discussed previously (i.e., affecting resources, leadership opportunities, key committee assignments, and so on) as well as in terms of intellectual continuity, damaging academic program quality at all levels (but especially at the doctoral level). This situation may create a death spiral for the IS program within some institutions.

## Recommendations

To maintain the incentive for faculty to submit their highest quality work to the premier IS journals, we envision some changes for the IS discipline as a whole. As argued above, keeping the number of premier publication opportunities relatively low and constant as the field continues to grow in size acts to diminish our overall productivity. To address this situation, we make four broad recommendations.

First, increasing the number of publication slots in the discipline’s premier journals is one way to deal with external limits to publishing in the discipline’s premier journals (e.g., moving from a quarterly to monthly publication cycle).<sup>17</sup> To accommodate the growth of the field, Zmud recently suggested that the

IS discipline needed more journals that were “universally agreed ‘top’ journals” (Lytras 2005a, p. 2). Our analysis supports this by demonstrating that at least three IS premier journals need to publish bimonthly, or two IS premier journals need to publish monthly (in addition to the articles published in Management Science), to “level the playing field” (i.e., to reach the average Authors/100 ratio of the remaining disciplines under the 2003 situation).

Similarly, for accounting, Swanson (2004) suggested adjusting the sets of premier journals in the different business school disciplines (and potentially considering a fourth accounting journal as a premier) to alleviate the present situation. Although we agree with Swanson on the potential negative implications of the present inequity, large-scale changes—for example, achieving broad acceptance of a new journal as a new premier in a field—will be slow in developing, and elevating an existing journal not currently widely recognized as a premier outlet is likely much more difficult than expanding the number of articles within the current premier journals (e.g., through increasing the number of issues published each year or increasing the size of the existing issues; see Saunders 2006). Of course, making sure the journal has an adequate pipeline of quality papers to publish before expansion makes this a difficult chicken-or-the-egg problem (i.e., although we need more premier slots to get more high-quality submissions, we cannot expand the number of publication slots until we have an ample backlog of quality articles).

Second, we recommend taking a broader view of what constitutes publishable work in premier IS journals. While we agree with Benbasat and Zmud (2003) that IS research must include the IT artifact, we also believe that we may need to take a broader view when considering what is publishable IS research, so as not to miss the next “new thing” (Ives et al. 2004). Such a broad view will also serve to broaden our community (DeSanctis 2003), with a subsequent increase in high-quality submissions (thus dealing with the chicken-or-the-egg problem). In other words, encouraging greater diversity in the problems addressed, theories applied, and methods utilized for studying IS phenomena, without biases to favor specific research methods, topics, or philosophies, can help to motivate IS researchers to submit more and higher quality work to the discipline’s premier outlets (rather than to high-quality outlets in other disciplines). In recent years, both MISQ (see, e.g., Saunders 2006, Weber 2002) and ISR (Sambamurthy 2005) have made a pledge for greater diversity in terms of topics and methods; thus, the two premier IS journals are taking steps to address the current situation.

Our third recommendation echoes Saunders’s (2006) recent call for establishing realistic standards for the review process. Such pleas for a greater emphasis on a manuscript’s contribution (i.e., q quality)—while still placing an emphasis on a paper’s rigor (i.e., its r quality)—can also help to effectively deal with the chicken-or-the-egg problem. Although such calls are worthwhile, it can hardly be expected that norms concerning acceptable quality (in terms of q and r) will change in the short run. Consequently, at least in the short run, it should be the senior editors’ role to “step in” when reviewers (and associate editors) recommend rejection of high-q quality papers due to lacking r quality. Over the long run, the discipline’s premier journals will need to continue to “train” reviewers, editors, and the broader community on what acceptable quality (especially in regards to q quality) means. While this can be regarded as a long-term process, MISQ’s recent editorials about “developmental reviews” (Saunders 2005a, b) can be seen as a step in the right direction.

Finally, in addition to trying to address these problems from within the discipline, we must also take steps to educate our colleagues about the relative differences in premier publication opportunities across disciplines (see also Dennis et al. 2006). Such steps should include a concerted effort to inform and educate administrators of the relative differences across disciplines so that the broad range of policy, resource, and personnel decisions that are made within institutions better reflect these differences. Senior members of the IS community who are often called on to write external reviews for promotion and tenure cases can also aid this education process by developing “standard language” for describing the publication-opportunities situation (see Appendix B for an example). IS department heads could develop a short summary of the analyses presented here and distribute this information to administrators of other disciplines, to deans, and to promotion and tenure committees within their college or school.

As can be seen from the discussion above, many avenues can be taken to address this situation. However, substantial change—whether from within or from the outside—will likely be slow, given the inherent inertia of the academy. Nevertheless, we call on the discipline as a whole to work toward changing the current inequitable situation—both for the sake of maintaining and increasing the stature of IS programs (Dennis et al. 2006) and for rectifying the narrowing opportunities for IS scholars such that we no longer “eat our young” (Robey 2003).

## Limitations and Conclusions

Although we have attempted to provide an unbiased picture of the current situation, the present analysis has several limitations. First, this study has only examined full research papers and research notes. Thus, introduction to special issues or comments and replies were excluded from the analysis. Furthermore, as mentioned above, we obtained faculty numbers from AACSB surveys; thus, these numbers are based on self-reports from member schools, and not all member schools have completed the surveys. Therefore, the numbers are a slight underestimation of the actual numbers of doctoral-level faculty in these schools. However, there is no reason to believe that the distribution of members belonging to the various disciplines differs between respondents and nonrespondents. Thus, all disciplines would be affected equally by any underestimation, and, consequently, our analysis would display an underestimation of the publication opportunities for all disciplines. On the other hand, the faculty numbers serve as a proxy for the number of faculty actually trying to publish in the premier journals and are thus an overestimation, which would also affect all disciplines equally.

Additionally, our cross-discipline publication analysis did not examine whether any researchers changed schools during the time period under consideration. Although this might have happened, we believe that this too would not have affected our results, as it is unlikely that individuals change disciplines when accepting a position at a different university.

Last, the IS discipline is an extremely diverse discipline, where many researchers publish their best work in hybrid journals outside the IS mainstream, such as computer science or economics (which have not been covered in our cross-publishing analysis). Further, we excluded the premier journals of the disciplines production/operations management, insurance, international business, and real estate from our analyses. However, it is our experience that researchers from all business disciplines submit research to premier outlets beyond those examined in this research (e.g., management, marketing, and IS researchers often publish work in top psychology outlets, while accounting and finance researchers often publish work in top economics outlets). Thus, how we chose to operationalize the list of premier journals—using prior research as a guide—is a clear limitation to these results and must be taken into account when interpreting these findings. Given that each university operationalizes what constitutes a premier publication somewhat differently, finding the perfect set of premier journals to perform this analysis would be next to impossible. Nevertheless, future research should include a broader set of high-quality journals both within and outside those studied here to understand the implications of using a broader set of premier journals when making cross-discipline comparisons.

Although the present study presents a rather provocative view of the current playing field, more research is needed to assess the consequences of these differences. One area of investigation could center on determining the degree of awareness among business school administrators and whether faculty review policies reflect real or perceived differences across disciplines. Another area of investigation could examine whether there are indeed negative effects on faculty motivation arising from perceptions of unfairness as well as how faculty members are responding to these perceptions. Future research could also test the q-r theory or other theoretical mechanisms using archival analysis of submissions and past editorial acceptances and rejections. Last, investigations could examine relative opportunities using a broader definition of premier outlets, including those from computer science, economics, psychology, and so on, as well as a more inclusive operationalization of the faculty size construct to include faculty located outside AACSB institutions. Though different analyses that could be performed abound, the analysis reported here has the potential to spur many lively discussions with the IS community as well as the broader reaches of the academy. It is through these discussions that awareness of relative differences (inequity) can be made known, providing a foundation through which change—both within institutions as well as within the IS discipline—can be motivated.

## Appendix B

Sample standard language for describing differences in publication opportunities in external review letters:

Studies by Valacich, Fuller, Schneider, and Dennis (2006 —ISR) and Swanson (2004—CAR) showed that various business school disciplines have different opportunities for publishing in elite discipline-specific journals. Swanson (2004) found that there exists a stark discrepancy in the opportunities for publishing in premier journals across different business school disciplines. Specifically, he demonstrated that “the magnitude of the differences [in the proportion of faculty publishing in premier journals] is sufficient to indicate that publishing a major article in accounting is more competitive than publishing a major article in finance, management, and marketing” (p. 248). Likewise, in their analyses including the IS discipline, Valacich et al. show that, normalized for differences in faculty size, publishing in IS is more competitive than publishing in finance, marketing, and management. In relative terms of faculty size and publication slots for authors, publishing one article in an IS premier journal is equivalent to publishing 1.5 articles in a marketing or finance premier journal, or two articles in a management premier journal. Such differences in baseline opportunities make the application of equal standards across disciplines very problematic.

In absolute terms, Dennis, Valacich, Fuller, and Schneider (2006—MISQ) showed that, on average, only about 18 IS faculty worldwide (of a typical Ph.D. graduating class of 275 people) publish one or more premier IS article within their first six years after graduation. In other words, only 6.7% of each graduating class publishes one IS premier article by the time they reach tenure, and only five individuals (1.7%) publish two or more IS premier articles.
