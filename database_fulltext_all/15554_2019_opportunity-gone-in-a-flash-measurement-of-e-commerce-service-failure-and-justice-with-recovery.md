# Opportunity gone in a flash: Measurement of e-commerce service failure and justice with recovery as a source of e-loyalty

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Customer opportunism Functional failure Information failure System failure Perceived justice E-loyalty

## A B S T R A C T

Online flash sales (OFS) are an opportunity for customers to purchase goods with large discounts for a limited period, along with the increased risk of service failures due to heavy demand for such ofers. The current work examines the impact of OFS e-commerce service failures, as well as that of perceived customer opportunism (PCO), on perceived justice with service recovery (PJWSR), post-recovery satisfaction (SSR), post-recovery perceived switching cost (PSC) and e-loyalty. By using a mixed-method approach, the current work develops a novel framework for OFS e-commerce service failure. Failure is conceptualized as functional, information, and system failures, with unique sub-dimensions/measurement items underlying each. Our findings suggest that functional and information failures have a negative efect on PJWSR, PCO has a positive efect on PJWSR, PJWSR has a significant efect on SSR/PSC and SSR positively afects e-loyalty, with no moderation of PSC on this relationship. This work contributes to the e-commerce service failure and customer opportunism literature by proposing a new contextual scale for measuring OFS e-commerce service failures and the impact of recoveryinduced justice on a customer's loyalty. Leveraging this work, e-tailers need to make judicious decisions to avoid specific failures during OFS by designing robust e-commerce systems and, in the case of a failure, to have a responsive recovery protocol to enhance PJWSR and subsequent e-loyalty.

## 1. Introduction

The rapid growth of e-commerce has led to the proliferation of multiple e-tailers, creating immense competition in an increasingly saturated online market space [1–4]. While e-tailers rely on innovations like social media connections, personalized recommendations, dynamic product customizations and m-commerce to get loyal customers, another tactic commonly used to create short-term site trafic is online flash sales (OFS), which also attracts opportunistic customers with closer associations to another e-tailer [5,6]. An OFS is an event when etailers sell goods at heavy discounts, 50% or sometimes even higher, for a very short time up to a maximum of few hours, with few units of a product available on ofer [7–10]. An OFS provides a customer with the opportunity to procure desirable products/brands at very low prices, and an e-tailer the chance to generate extra-sales and clear inventory [11,12]. There is also a severe time constraint, sometimes only a few seconds, for the buyer to make a largely impulsive choice. Some evidence suggests that customers buy much more, both in value and volume, during an OFS than on regular shopping occasions [7,8].

However, enhanced site trafic along with back-end technology constraints may lead to OFS failures like crashed website, out-of-stock products, delayed/incorrect deliveries or failed payments, which warrant recovery [8,13,14].

Generally, any service failure leads to strong customer dissatisfaction and the need for an efective recovery [15,16]. The same applies to an opportunistic customer. However, the response to e-commerce service failure recovery is markedly different for such a customer, with enhanced positive evaluations for the recovery process [17]. The attribution theory indicates that service failure for an opportunistic customer is equally undesirable, but with the attribution of the failure to self, the dificulty of managing it through recovery is somewhat reduced [18]. An OFS is a time-pressured failure-prone environment, but one which presents the opportunistic customer a chance to secure large financial gains. A few prior studies explore customer behaviour in similar online environments, however, none examine e-service failures or the consequences of recovery of those failures, as well as customer opportunism, on perceived justice (PJWSR) and its subsequent outcomes, like post-recovery satisfaction (SSR), post-recovery perceived switching cost (PSC) and e-loyalty [12,19–22]. Hence, there are two research questions that this work attempts to answer: 1) What are the various meanings of an OFS e-commerce service failure? and 2) How does PJWSR of the failure, for an opportunistic customer, afect e-loyalty?

An OFS e-commerce service failure is proposed to be composed of information, functional and system failures and their respective sub dimensions and measurement items. A comprehensive scale development process suggested by Hinkin, Tracey and Enz [23] is followed. Following a comprehensive literature review, in-depth interviews with thirty respondents who experienced an OFS failure were conducted. The in-depth interviews helped in evoking dimensions and their respective measurement items, which were mapped to existing items in the extant literature. Next, survey data was collected from 374 respondents with a structured questionnaire and a rigorous quantitative empirical validation was done using partial least squares-based structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM). The paper draws theoretical un derpinning from, as well as contributes to, the theories of customer opportunism and e-service failure, with its interesting findings. This work extends the work of Tan et al. [24] by proposing a novel scale for measuring e-commerce service failures in the context of consumer opportunism. Further, the strong role of judicious recovery of such failures for invoking e-loyalty of an otherwise opportunistic customer, mediated by SSR and PSC, is also a novel insight.

Next, we discuss the literature on opportunistic behaviour and eservices failure, followed by the hypothesized model. Subsequently, the methodology, including conceptualization of the OFS e-commerce service failure and data collection, are highlighted. The paper concludes with the analysis of results, general discussion, theoretical and managerial implications, limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Customer opportunism

Williamson [25] defines opportunism as “self-interest seeking with guile which includes but is scarcely limited to more blatant forms, such as lying, stealing, and cheating and involves subtle forms of deceit”. In an opportunistic relationship, customers keep their intentions opaque to a supplier and engage with a new supplier if there is a short-term opportunity to gain substantial financial benefits [26]. In the domain of e-commerce, there is a lack of any tangible evidence about the quality of product/service available, causing lower levels of certainty, security and trust [27]. Hence, customers demonstrate loyalty-driven beha viours towards an e-tailer which displays consistent product/service quality and minimizes risks [28]. Reciprocally, e-tailers also invest in trusted customers through various loyalty programs, implying variable costs like rewards, redemptions, IT investments, advertising costs, business overheads, R&D and vendor contracts, with the expectation that the customer will always buy from them, even if at a price pre mium [29]. From the agency theory purview, the mutual trust between the customer (principal) and the e-tailer (agent) ensures the longevity of the exchange relationship and prevents any opportunistic behaviou from either party [30]. Many-a-times however, for example during an OFS from another e-tailer, a customer may find an attractive but rather uncertain opportunity to buy a product/service at a much lower cost than what their regular e-tailer is ofering at, and may decide to display an opportunistic behaviour by opting for the short-term economic savings over a trusted relationship. The desire to seek short-term gains may prevail over the potential risk of a service failure [31]. This is because substantially lucrative deals, like the ones in an OFS, reduce the customer risk aversion and service-delivery expectations, causing them to prefer an outcome with less certainty but a higher utility [32].

## 2.2. E-commerce service failure

E-services, including e-commerce, leverage information and communication technologies for efective value delivery, with the provider, the receiver, and the channels of service delivery as three key components [33]. Thus, e-commerce service failure occurs whenever an e-commerce website is incapable of ofering the required end-product/ service to the customer due to unavailability of the said product/service at the time of transaction, or there is unwillingness by the customer to accept/buy the product/service delivered, or there is an absence of the necessary technological/logistical capabilities essential for a customer to receive the product/service satisfactorily [34].

There is substantial research examining the impact of e-commerce service quality and its success measured as an intention to re-use [35], continuance intentions [36], and e-loyalty [36–40]. However, besides a few works [2,33,41,42], none of those discusses e-commerce service failures. Tan et al. [24] outline why e-commerce service failure is clearly diferent from an equivalent ofline service failure or an e-commerce service success and needs its own line of research. Within the works examining e-commerce failures, Holloway and Beatty's [41] framework is not based on a comprehensive theory and e-commerce service failure components are inductively proposed [24]. Forbes et al. [42] propose various e-commerce service failures, like slow/unavailable service, faulty packaging, defective products, products out of stock, ambiguous pricing, incorrect information, website failures, and customer requirements failure, without any clear underlying dimensions. Wang et al. [2] consider e-service failures as a unidimensional construct, termed as failure severity, with limited discussion on its dimensions. Additionally, the ecommerce service failure and justice due to its recovery are treated as independent antecedents to loyalty, when the two are related chron ologically. Further, there are no empirical studies measuring e-commerce service failure or examining customer response to recovery of such failures in a context evoking customer opportunism, which serves as this work's unique positioning [12,19,22,43].

## 2.3. OFS E-commerce service failure: a typology

The e-commerce service failure framework by Tan et al. [24], who broadly classify e-service failure as functional, information and system failures, is used as the theoretical underpinning of the current research. The functional capabilities of a website are the primary source of a customized yet flawless purchase process and overall positive purchase experience [24,44]. The deficiency of such capabilities implies a functional failure which reflects the performance setback in e-commerce services. A disproportionate trafic pressure may cause the website functionalities to be compromised rendering it incapable of servicing the customer's requirements during the purchase process [24,45,46]. These failures may include the website crashing, becoming inaccessible during the purchase process, or increasing the customer eforts for making a purchase during a time-constrained event [38,47].

The quality of information presented by a website is well-documented as a salient enabler of accurate customer purchases [44]. In their work, Holloway and Beatty [41] indicate the incorrect listing of items as a key concern facing customers during acquisition. For a customer attempting to purchase something in a limited time, availability of accurate and credible information determines the customer's objective fulfilment [44]. Extant research [48,49] indicates that accuracy, timeliness and relevance are key attributes that determine information quality, hence an absence of these attributes would constitute information failure for a time-pressured customer. Additionally, incomplete information about product specifications, ofers, hidden charges, firm policies, and security features, otherwise critical for a customer to make quick informed decisions, also constitute information failure [49].

The overall system quality of an e-commerce website decides the service process eficiencies and is largely driven by the back-end technology which ensures efective value delivery for the end-customers [50]. It serves as a key enabler for streamlining the e-tailer's performance in a demanding environment [51]. Thus, a system failure would include an absence of attributes, both of the website and ancillary services, that inhibit efective product/service delivery to the end-customer [24]. Thus, system failure encompasses all such systemic failures, beyond the functional and information failures, that disable necessary capabilities for a value-laden transaction for a customer during a pur chase occasion.

## 3. Proposed framework

An e-commerce service failure, like any other service failure, war rants recovery and, as some works [52,53] indicate, there should be a strong negative impact of the individual failures on a customer's perceived justice. Independent of the failure type/intensity, the customer's opportunism is expected to enhance that justice, given failure self-attribution, and is measured in the study with PCO, defined as the self reported purposeful customer behaviour, opaque to the primary etailer, to secure unilateral profits through an OFS by another e-tailer [31,32]. Based on Schoefer et al. [54], we define perceived justice with service recovery, here PJWSR, as the customer's cognitive and hedonic evaluations of the eficacy of the overall recovery process, including apology and the subsequent compensations received after the service failure, and is composed of procedural, interactive and distributive justices [55]. Distributive justice indicates the customer's perception of fairness of the monetary/non-monetary compensation received as part of the post-failure recovery process [56]. Procedural justice refers to the customer's belief about the adequacy of the flexibility and eficiency of the recovery process itself, while interactive justice refers to the extent of fairness, honesty, courtesy and empathy with which service providers communicate with the customers during the recovery process [56,57].

Extant literature suggests that all three components of perceived justice from the recovery should have a significant positive influence on post-recovery satisfaction, post-recovery switching costs and resultant customer loyalty [2,55,57–60]. Based on Bruce [61], SSR is defined as a state of mind that represents the composite of a user's overall evaluation of the compensation received after a service recovery is completed. PSC is defined as the user resistance to moving to another e-tailer after an OFS failure recovery is complete, due to inherent financial, psychological and time costs [2,62]. Unlike most related works in IS literature, this study considers PSC as the switching costs post-recovery of an ecommerce service failure, and not as a static variable [2]. This is because switching costs can be diferent post-recovery as the recovery eforts themselves can improve seller-buyer commitment [63]. Finally, e-loyalty indicates the post-recovery customer intention to display repeated purchase behaviour, driven by a positive attitude towards the etailer [64]. From a temporal viewpoint, the process from e-commerce service failure to e-loyalty is comprised of three important episodes: ‘during-failure’: the time when the OFS failure occurs; ‘during-recovery’: the time after the OFS failure while the recovery is ongoing; and ‘post-recovery’: the time after the recovery process is completed. Hence, OFS e-commerce service failure and PCO are ‘during-failure variables, PJWSR is a ‘during-recovery’ variable and PSC/SSR/e-loyalty are ‘post-recovery’ variables. ## 3.1. Functional failure and PJWSR

Tan et al. [24] suggest that the functional capabilities of an ecommerce website support customers in accomplishing their transactional objectives. An OFS e-commerce website's lack of such capabilities to address the customer's desire of securing a rare financial opportunity creates negative experiences. These negative experiences can cause customers to lose out on a good deal or make a wrong impulse purchase, leading to a sense of injustice which would require immediate recovery measures [8,38,65]. The recovery eforts by an e-tailer are aimed to convince such customers, failed by the website's functional failure, to reengage by creating service-recovery induced justice [66]. Higher the quantum of functional failure, higher will be the customer dissonance which will, in turn, lower the perceived justice of a specific service recovery efort of an e-tailer [67,68]. Therefore, we propose:

H1. Functional failure of the OFS e-commerce service will be negatively associated with PJWSR.

## 3.2. Information failure and PJWSR

The availability of relevant information on an OFS e-commerce website during an online purchase enhances the predictability of a successful purchase experience for a customer and is a crucial determinant of positive evaluations [69,[78]]. In a time-pressured environment, like an OFS, inaccurate content displayed on the website can lead to negative outcomes in the form of wrong product selections or other misinformed decisions [8,70]. Further, insuficient information about return policies, warranties, cancellations, and security protocols may create doubts about the veracity of the claims made on the OFS website and may either dissuade customers from completing a purchase, or make them acquire deals with no redress available in case of any failure [11,24,71]. Such failures cause a breach of faith for the customer, which can undermine the perception of justice even after service recovery [67,72–74]. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H2. Information failure of the OFS e-commerce service will be negatively associated with PJWSR.

## 3.3. System failure and PJWSR

System failures enhance the overall transactional cost for the user and create a feeling of financial/psychological loss due to their inability to procure the correct product/service in the available time [75]. In case the procurement process is technically inefficient and incapable, a customer's purchase process may get terminated even before attaining the end-objective [76]. Such failures prevent a customer from securing an attractive deal and the resultant dissatisfaction needs to be recovered by the e-tailer for restoring justice [77]. Thus, we argue that major system failures during a purchase occasion can subdue overall customer experiences, prevent efective value delivery, and negatively influence the sense of PJWSR, even after the seller attempts recovery [8,67,72,73] and hypothesize:

H3. System failure of the OFS e-commerce service will be negatively associated with PJWSR.

## 3.4. PCO and PJWSR

Expectation-disconfirmation theory suggests that customers use their expectations, modelled by prior experiences, to evaluate service performance [24,79]. Such expectations are violated when a service failure occurs. A customer's decision to engage with a service is a careful evaluation between/of the rewards of successful acquisition and the risk associated with the failure to do so [79]. E-commerce services present three primary failure risks for a customer: undesired outcomes, unnecessary costs and process disruptions [24,80]. The rewards and risks are even higher during an OFS. However, an opportunistic customer, displaying high PCO, willingly exposes herself to these enhanced risks for larger gains, and with little expectation of a successful acquisition. Such a customer may feel to have been treated with injustice concomitant to the type/quantum of failure. However, due to self-attribution of that failure and the impersonal and transactional engagement with the e-tailer, any form of recovery is considered judicious and enhances the overall effectiveness of the recovery process [26,52,53,81,82]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H4. PCO of a customer participating in an OFS will be positively associated with PJWSR.

## 3.5. PJWSR, SSR and PSC

Multiple works use justice theory as a theoretical underpinning to examine service recovery efectiveness and its impact on post-recovery customer satisfaction in various contexts [2,56,60,83–85]. Prior lit erature suggests that post-recovery satisfaction is determined by the customer perception of being treated fairly throughout the complaint handling process, measured by PJWSR [59,60,67]. An opportunism displaying customer, who blames herself for the failure and considers a recovery as more judicious, should be highly satisfied with the OFS etailer, probably more than she would be under normal circumstances. Hence, in alignment with works like [59], all components of PJWSR are expected to have a significant positive influence on SSR.

Extant literature also indicates that switching costs are reflections of the time, efort and money, as well as psychological costs, to be in curred by a customer while changing his/her service provider [38]. A higher value of these costs implies deterrence and resistance on the part of a customer to shift to a new vendor. Wirtz & Mattila [86] argue that efective service recovery, through customer empowerment or compensation, reduces customer switching probability. Thus, the percep tion of justice with eficacious service recovery, after an OFS e-commerce service failure, should have a significant negative relationship with post-recovery switching intentions for a customer [87]. In other words, higher post-recovery switching costs reflect the success of the recovery process and consequently, the opportunistic customer, who is delighted to have received a judicious recovery, may decide to “stick” with the new e-tailer. [87]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5/6. PJWSR is positively associated with SSR/PSC.

## 3.6. SSR, PSC and e-loyalty

Customer satisfaction is a well-known predictor of behavioural intentions. A satisfied customer purchases the same product/service repeatedly from a service provider and resists the competitive ofers [88]. In the context of OFS e-commerce service failure, SSR represents the composite of an opportunistic customer's evaluations once the recovery is complete, and if positive, is expected to enhance customer e-loyalty [2,62]. This is because appropriate handling of customer complaints, which results in a restored sense of justice and overall customer sa tisfaction, is essential for evoking loyalty-driven behaviour [60].

Further, e-commerce markets generally have lower switching costs, with all vendors ofering similar services and minimal human-touch, yet switching costs do have a strong efect on customer loyalty [2]. The capability of an e-tailer to enhance switching costs and customer loyalty is a primary success driver in e-commerce [89] Previous works largely indicate that switching costs are key enablers of customer loyalty [90]. Hence, in the current context, where the relationship between a customer and e-tailer is an outcome of opportunistic behaviour by the customer for a short-term gain, building higher switching costs by enhancing customers' perception of justice through efective recovery can become a driver for customer e-loyalty [2,38]. Based on the above arguments, we propose:

H7/8. SSR/PSC is positively associated with e-loyalty.

## 3.7. Moderating role of post-recovery switching costs

Previous literature lends substantial support to the positive moderation role of switching costs on the satisfaction-loyalty relationship [38,91]. In the case of OFS e-commerce service failure, after a judicious recovery efort has been made, enhanced switching costs deter a customer from shifting to a new vendor [2]. On the other hand, customers dissatisfied with service recovery, combined with low switching costs, are highly prone to changing their e-tailer [90]. In other words, once an opportunistic customer receives recovery after a failed e-commerce service, her intention to display loyalty-oriented behaviours, post-recovery, will primarily be a function of SSR and its positive interaction with PSC [91]. Thus, we hypothesize that:

H9. PSC positively moderates the relationship between SSR and eloyalty.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. OFS e-commerce service failure: conceptualization

Tan et al. [24] e-service failure framework, which serves as the theoretical underpinning, is generic and the operational meanings of each of the three failures, as well as the measurement items, may be diferent in the context of an opportunistic relationship. This mandates a unique inquiry for further sub-dimensions and measurement items for OFS e-commerce service failure. We use standard scale development protocols to develop the sub-dimensions/measurement items for OFS ecommerce service failure and then, empirically test the proposed model [23].

A combination of deductive and inductive approaches, as suggested by Tanwar and Prasad [92], was used. To do so, we drew on the Tan et al. [24] framework and scales used in other studies as part of a deductive approach. The inductive approach involved item generation through respondent voices from in-depth interviews. Respondents were selected with the first qualifier of having faced an OFS failure in the last six months and the second qualifier of having depicted opportunism by engaging in the OFS. For the interviews, initially, 174 students of a prominent business school, who fulfilled the first requirement, showed a willingness to participate. Given that a sample size of 25–30 relevant respondents is considered adequate for in-depth interviews to attain saturation, 75 of those students were chosen randomly to ensure a fair mix of all types/intensities of failures encountered [93]. To fulfil the second requirement, the shortlisted students were asked two questions: “Did you participate in the OFS to exploit an opportunity for financial savings?” and “Did you feel that by participating in the OFS, you were unfair to your regular e-tailer?” Thirty of the students gave an afirmative answer to both questions and were selected for final interviews. However, the selected students were not told the actual reason for selection, which was masked as a random process, to prevent any bias in their subsequent answers. The selected participants were probed on the details of the failure they experienced. Each interview lasted for around 30 min and data was collected until saturation of information was achieved, resulting in thirty-seven initial items [94]. A thematic ana lysis of the respondent voices ensured deeper interpretations and categorization of their comments into patterns, matching the literaturebased conceptualizations, and measurement items as depicted in Appendix A [95]. These themes were proposed as sub-dimensions of OFS e-commerce service failure.

Next, two experts from online sales business were asked to rate the items, on a scale of ten, for their association with each construct, with a higher value depicting a higher association. Fifteen items, which ensured the inter-rater reliability was above 0.80, were allocated to functional, information and system failures and were converted to questionnaire items. Based on the items, functional failure is defined as the lack of functional and technical capabilities of a website, reflected by scalability/response and ergonomics capabilities, that prohibits a user from securing the desired transactional value during an OFS [21,38]. The scalability/response failure is defined as the inability of an OFS e-commerce system to accommodate the increasing demand as well as ofer timely information, resulting in a customer losing out on a quick value-for-money deal [44]. The ergonomics failure is defined as the extent to which customers perceive an OFS website to be user-un friendly, non-intuitive, and cumbersome to use and navigate, preventing them from exploiting an attractive deal in a reasonable time [96]. The information failure represents an intentional, or otherwise, misrepresentation of information, through limited accuracy of content and assurance of transaction security, by the OFS e-tailer reducing customer trust [70,74]. It is proposed to reflect two dimensions, accu racy failure and assurance failure. The accuracy of content failure represents the extent to which the OFS website fails to accurately display the deals available to a customer. The assurance failure is defined as the partial or total absence of a third-party service that helps instil confidence in an OFS e-tailer's business practices, like authentication seals, digital certificates, or insignias, and create believability in the deals displayed [97]. Finally, the system failure is the failure of other back end or support services of the OFS e-tailer which leads to non-delivery of expected values to a customer and the resultant negative experiences.

## 4.2. Quantitative data collection

For the quantitative data collection. the sample was drawn from the staf and students of a prominent business school in India, with the first filter that they should have participated in a particular OFS, conducted by an e-tailer twenty-nine days prior to the survey, and faced a failure. The OFS was active for 10 h on that specific day, starting from 9 a.m. in the morning, and represented the ‘during-failure’ episode. A day after the OFS failure, an email was sent out by the e-tailer to all the failed customers who had logged into the website. The email (paraphrased) mentioned that the day marked a big one for the owners and the company, but unfortunately the experience of customers was less than pleasant. It further admitted that the company did not live up to the promises and looked forward to serving the customers better in the future. The cause was attributed to overwhelming demand, with trafic twenty times the normal, and close to a billion website hits that day.

Subsequent to this email, over the next few days, the customers who received the email were individually contacted by the company for their specific failures, which were redressed through compensations like refunds, equivalent deals, vouchers, special discount codes, product replacements and in some cases, premium memberships. The apology email as a respondent selection filter not only indicated an admission of failure by the e-tailer for those respondents but also ensured the beginning of the recovery process, since an apology represents the bare minimum initiative for restoring a failed customer's self-esteem [60]. To ensure the presence of service recovery, the second filter was that all respondents should have received the common apology email as well as a subsequent compensation. Thus, the whole timeframe from receiving the apology to receiving the compensation represented the ‘during-recovery’ episode, while the period after the recovery until data collection was the ‘post-recovery’ episode.

While the items for the remaining constructs were directly adopted from standard scales, those for PCO were borrowed from [26] selectively by the same two experts from online sales business, since not all items were applicable in this context, as shown in Appendix A. An online questionnaire was sent with the finalized items, with these two qualifiers, to 942 randomly selected staf and students of a business school for a small incentive in return. Of those, 412 were eligible for the study and filled the questionnaires, of which only 374 questionnaires were complete in all respects. The response rate was, thus, 39.7% for the overall questionnaires sent and 90.8% for those who qualified. Demographically, 76% of the respondents were male; 84% were aged between 18 and 30, 14% were aged between 30 and 45 and the remaining 2% were aged between 45 and 60. The grand mean of the construct, PCO, on a scale of five, was 4.41 with respective averages of PCO1, PCO2, PCO3 and PCO4 at 4.39, 4.39, 4.46 and 4.37. Further, none of the respondents gave a rating below four for any of these items, indicating significant self-reported customer opportunism in the sample. For the current sample, the ‘during-recovery’ episode varied from three to ten days, depending on the compensation, while the ‘postrecovery’ episode varied from nineteen to twenty-six days.

## 5. Analysis and results

## 5.1. Measurement model

The data was split randomly in half, such that 187 responses were used to evaluate the measurement model and remaining to evaluate the structural model, in accordance with the two-step approach [98]. The measurement model was run using PLS-SEM software SmartPLS3.0 and the results are depicted in Table 1. One item of ergonomics failure, ERF3, and two items of SSR, SSR1 and SSR2, did not load well (factor loading < 0.70) on their respective constructs and were dropped from further analysis. The test for internal consistency of each construct, the composite reliability (CR), was more than the recommended value of 0.70 [99]. The convergent validity was established as all the average variance extracted (AVE) values for constructs were above the recommended cut-of of 0.5 [100]. All the AVE values were above their corresponding maximum squared correlation (MSC) values for each first-order construct indicating discriminant validity [101].

Common method bias (CMB) was checked using a post-hoc Harman one-factor test [102]. The largest factor, after running an EFA with all the items, accounted for only 22% of the variance, indicating a possible lack of CMB.

## 5.3. Moderation analysis

The study proposes the moderating efect of PSC on the relation between SSR and e-loyalty. The moderation test was conducted using hierarchical moderated regression analysis (HMRA) [107]. Both the independent and moderator variables were mean centred, and the moderation efect was classified depending on the significance of the coeficient of the interaction term. Examination of the interaction term in Table 2 reveals that the relation between SSR and e-loyalty is not moderated by PSC and hypothesis H9 is not supported. The lack of moderation was also confirmed separately by Haye's method [108].

## 6. General discussion

An OFS represents an event when some people, loyal to another etailer, become risk-prone and opportunistic in their decisions while looking for attractive deals [109]. A failure in such a context and the efect of its recovery on perceived justice, post-recovery satisfaction, post-recovery switching costs and e-loyalty is the domain of current investigation. Aligned to earlier research [110], our study finds that a functional failure, reflecting scalability, response and ergonomics failures, even in an opportunistic relationship, negatively influences PJWSR (H1). People trying to buy goods during an OFS are desperate because of time and stock constraints and understand that excessive demand on the website may cause functional failures. Yet, such failures during OFS cause customer disappointment and have a negative efect on the PJWSR. The greater the extent of the functional failure, the higher the customer disappointment and may need a stronger recovery efort to evoke perceived justice.

The findings also suggest that an information failure, reflecting accuracy and assurance failures, experienced during OFS negatively influences PJWSR (H2), a finding consistent with prior studies [72,73]. Thus, the perception of being deceived due to an information failure has a negative impact on perceived justice. Opportunistic customers, who largely hold themselves liable for any failure, still feel cheated by an e-tailer and are not easily satisfied by the recovery eforts. Therefore, well-strategized compensations should be sent out to these customers to appease them after the information failure. Further, a weak negative but insignificant efect of system failure on PJWSR (H3) is interesting and may concede that a system failure, measured in the way we do, is found more tolerable by opportunistic customers and is more easily recoverable. Additionally, PCO is found to have a strong positive efect on PJWSR (H4). This implies that an opportunistic customer, with failure self-attribution and lower expectation of acquisition from an OFS, considers a service recovery as more judicious, compared to a non opportunistic customer, even though (s) he is equally disenchanted with the failure.

Next, the results suggest that PJWSR has a significant efect in mi tigating the negative experiences customers have in an OFS failure, makes them satisfied, and lowers their intention to switch (H5/H6). The finding aligns with prior literature on perceived justice and implies that efective service recovery measures after e-commerce service failure can enhance customer satisfaction and increase the “stickiness” of an otherwise opportunistic customer [2,57]. Therefore, online retailers should be very careful about making sincere recovery eforts, financial or in-kind, after a service failure. Further, SSR was found to have a strong efect on e-loyalty (H6) which indicates that the satisfaction from the overall recovery process goes a long way in creating loyal customers [88]. It also represents an irony because a relationship which starts with opportunism ends with loyalty, making the e-commerce service failure a “blessing-in-disguise” for the e-tailer who recovered it judiciously. PSC is found to have no efect on e-loyalty (H7), a finding in contrast to earlier literature [2]. It indicates that in an opportunistic environment, where switching e-tailers in easy, PSC is of no consequence to the loyalty, which is primarily driven by the satisfaction with recovery. Hence, the only way an OFS e-tailer can get a customer “hooked” to its website is by creating justice through efective service recovery and post-recovery satisfaction. This is also confirmed by the insignificant moderation efect of PSC (H9).

## 7. Theoretical implications

Our research makes some key academic contributions through an intersection of e-service failure and customer opportunism. First, despite substantial literature available on service failures and the efect of recovery on justice, few works empirically investigate the failures of ecommerce services [24]. The current work fills the important research gap of measuring e-commerce service failure and its implications in an environment, like OFS, where customers are opportunistic, look for the best deals, overlook their current ties, have lower expectations, and attribution of failures is on oneself [12,24,52,53,109]. In the domain of e-service failure, which is a recent area of research, Tan et al. [24] ofer a generic framework with e-service failure composed of functional, information and system failure. We extend their work by proposing new sub-dimensions under each failure, albeit in a unique context, leading to novel measurements for e-commerce service failure. This work pro poses that functional failure reflects scalability, response and ergonomics failures, information failure reflects assurance and accuracy failures, and system failure is a unidimensional construct. Thus, at the sub-dimension level, this framework departs from that of Tan et al. [24] and the operational meanings and consequent sub-dimensions, as well as their measurement items, are a novel contribution. Further, the specific failures proposed by Holloway & Beatty [41] and Forbes et al. [42] find evidence in our measurement items, albeit in a structured theory-driven framework.

Second, in the domain of consumer opportunism, multiple studies have attempted to identify the customer behaviour in similar environments, like group-buying or deal-of-the-day, but they do not measure or examine the role of e-service failure in the process [12,19,21,43]. The findings of the work confirm that the dynamics of an e-service failure and its recovery for an opportunistic customer are diferent from those for a customer in a regular shopping environment, with the opportu nistic customer easier to recover despite her dissatisfaction due to failure. This work contributes to the literature leveraging attribution theory by suggesting that in case an opportunistic customer faces an ecommerce service failure, (s) he will direct the locus of failure away from the e-tailer and hence, feels more justified once recovery is received, independent of the type/quantum of failure [32]. This is evident from the strong influence of PCO in enhancing perceived justice from service recovery.

Third, this study reafirms the importance of PJWSR for an opportunistic customer after e-commerce service failures. Many studies invoking justice theory have explored the efect of ofline service failures and their recovery on perceived justice, though none are in the domain of e-services failure [60,83]. Our findings depict the relative importance of each e-commerce service failure on the recovery induced justice, reflecting the three components, which is a major contribution of this work. The only prominent work in e-services failure domain, Tan et al. [24], qualitatively propose the impact of three e-services failures on disconfirmed process expectancy, without investigating the efects of failure recovery on perceived justice. Hence, this work represents an initial efort to unify the fields of e-services failure and recovery-induced perceived justice. Additionally, not all e-commerce service failures, like system failure, are found to afect PJWSR in contrast to expectations from justice-inducing models [2,41,57,60]. This implies that systemic glitches, otherwise most damaging on a regular occasion, are considered outside the control of the e-tailer and customers are ready to forgive [53]. However, customer disappointment that arises due to functional and information failures still requires service recovery eforts, even if of a lower degree, from the e-tailer to generate a sense of justice. Finally, through a hierarchical model, depicted in Fig. 1, with PJWSR as during-recovery and SSR/PSC as post-recovery variables leading to e-loyalty, our work presents a distinct model which depicts the conversion of an opportunistic relationship to one with loyalty, enabled by e-service failure recovery.

## 8. Managerial implications

For the practitioners, first, our framework of e-commerce service failure indicates that the functional, information and system failures are all important and deserve attention. For users, a functional failure implies a lack of features that make a website scalable, responsive and ergonomic. Based on the proposed measures of each component of functional failure, OFS e-tailers need to ensure the deployment of appropriate back-end capabilities that prevent an OFS website from being too slow or completely unresponsive. Further, a scalable and responsive website is of no use if it is not intuitive and uncluttered enough for the user to navigate through quickly during a time-constrained event. A possible way to guarantee this is to create a simpler and intuitive OFS website, which is diferent from the regular one and puts a lesser strain on the e-tailer's servers. An information failure for users implies lack of accuracy and assurance, and thus, e-tailers should ensure that the website always presents correct, updated and complete information about the products on-deal and that the policies about the OFS are clearly articulated under terms and conditions, warranties and return policies. Finally, system failures imply failed deliveries, non-refunds, incorrect prices charged, and automatic cancellations during an OFS and should be clearly avoided.

Second, our findings highlight that PJWSR, an outcome of recovery employed by the OFS e-tailer, and SSR, a post-recovery variable after an OFS failure, have a strong influence on e-loyalty. Therefore, managers should be extremely concerned about providing appropriate service recovery that induces a high sense of distributive, procedural and in teractive justice among customers after a service failure during an OFS. The study results indicate that opportunistic customers who experience functional and information feel deprived, yet find the specific service recovery more judicious as an outcome of their opportunism. Therefore, the e-tailer can adopt targeted service recovery eforts for specific customer groups (opportunists vs. otherwise) who experience a specific type of failure, versus those who do not. It might be useful to use technology to segregate these groups and then, focus on unique recoveries immediately. However, this only implies prioritization since all three failures are critical and need to be recovered, though with different intensities.

As Cranage and Sujan [13] note, to enhance customer loyalty, failures should be avoided at all costs and if they do occur, they must be managed honestly under transparent policies. Hence, e-tailers should try to reduce failures during OFS by ensuring a smooth and seamless experience for the customers through adequate preparations and simulations, several months in advance. The e-tailer should be techno logically equipped with an adequate number of servers and other related technologies, such as cloud services, to handle the extra trafic during an opportunism-inducing event like OFS [14]. But if the failure does happen, they need to create justice through efective recovery, as envisaged in this work, to “latch” on to customers who engaged with them out of opportunism in the first place.

## 9. Limitations and future directions

The present study has certain limitations. The first limitation is that the empirical validation of our model was performed on data from only one country. However, India was an apt choice for conducting this study because OFS has become a regular phenomenon and is advertised with much intensity. In future, the generalizability of this model could be enhanced by conducting this study in other countries. A second limitation is that the respondents were those who had not been able to purchase from a particular OFS in the past, and hence may not have reported accurate information from their memory, even though the event happened only a month before the study. Future researchers can replicate this work through a longitudinal study spanning across the three temporal episodes. A final limitation exists as the respondents of the study did receive various compensations beyond the apology, however, the investigation did not measure it. Even though PJWSR measures the perceived efectiveness of the recovery process, the dynamics of the model may change based on the type/intensity of compensation received. There are two types of service recoveries: psychological and tangible [111]. The former consists of primarily an apology to the customer, for creating empathy, and the latter includes a reperformed service, an exchanged product, or a refund/in-kind compensation [112,113]. Future researchers can examine the efects of diferent recovery interventions, psychological or tangible, on perceived justice as experienced by customers during an e-commerce service failure.
