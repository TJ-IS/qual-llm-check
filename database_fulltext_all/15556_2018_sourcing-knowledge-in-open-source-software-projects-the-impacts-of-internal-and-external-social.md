# Sourcing knowledge in open source software projects: The impacts of internal and external social capital on project success

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Open source projects Diversity Development di<sup>f</sup>erence Participant di<sup>f</sup>erence Connectedness Success

## A B S T R A C T

Open source software (OSS) development has become an essential element of IT strategy: many <sup>fi</sup>rms seek OSS as a strategic means of unlocking the business value in external developer communities and internal knowledge sources. However, integrating external and internal sources of social capital is challenging and identifying appropriate integration strategies is crucial for the success of such endeavors. This study examines the simultaneous e<sup>f</sup>ects of external and internal sources of social capital, in the form of participant and project di<sup>f</sup>erences, and examines how they interact to in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS project success. We propose a taxonomy of participant di<sup>f</sup>erences (language, role, and contribution) and project di<sup>f</sup>erences (development environment and connectedness) and postulate their main and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects on project success. Using a four-year panel data set of 329 SourceForge projects, we show that development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence has a curvilinear relationship with success and that connectedness reduces the positive impact of role and contribution diversity on project success. We also show that when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is moderate, the impact of role diversity is the most positive. We present the implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings for theory and practice.

## 1. Introduction

In recent years, open and collaborative models of innovation, such as open source software (OSS) development, have attracted the attention of practitioners and researchers alike. Many technology <sup>fi</sup>rms traditionally known for closed organizational structures and propriety software development, such as IBM, Microsoft, and Facebook, have embraced the strategic opportunities that open source development models o<sup>f</sup>er. They are actively pursuing open platforms and promoting hackathons inside and outside their organizational boundaries in order to source new knowledge, identify innovations, and create new business value (Santos et al., 2013). For the year 2016, revenue from OSS products is estimated to have exceeded 50 billion euros (\$57 billion), up from under 8 billion euros in 2008 (Statistica, 2016). Five out of Fortune 10 <sup>fi</sup>rms and 40% of Fortune 50 <sup>fi</sup>rms are today developing next generation software solutions using open platforms such as GitHub. For instance, Microsoft and Facebook have the most active open source development communities on GitHub, with Microsoft boasting the highest number of contributors and more than 258 software projects under development (Octaverse, 2016).

While OSS development holds many interesting opportunities, not all OSS projects are successful (Ehls, 2017). To understand the success or failure of OSS projects, some researchers have used a social capital lens (Chou and He, 2011; Grewal et al., 2006; Singh et al., 2011; Wang, 2005; Xu and Jones, 2010) to examine how social relationships create resources for action (Coleman, 1988; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Social capital theory has primarily considered how social capital, derived from an external network of suppliers, customers, vendors, and business partners, leads to the development of intellectual capital within organizations in the form of new or improved products or processes (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). This theory is applicable to OSS development because OSS is an intellectual resource developed via the social action of freelance developers.

While social capital theory has traditionally focused on external sources of social capital, such as those acquired by an organization’s network of suppliers, customers, vendors and business partners, an organization’s internal resources, such as its employees, can also be valuable sources of social capital. In particular, the external and internal social capital can complement each other by interacting in ways to create business value (Adler and Kwon, 2002; Cuevas‐Rodríguez et al., 2014; Stam and Elfring, 2008; Walter et al., 2007). In the OSS context, internal social capital relates to an OSS project’s team of software developers, testers, and active users, while external social capital relates to contributors from other OSS projects who may be interested in and can contribute to the target project by virtue of their connectedness and the similarity in their project development environments. Several OSS projects use the same OSS platform and developers often work concurrently on multiple projects, transferring ideas, knowledge, interfaces, and software code from one project to another (Fershtman and Gandal, 2011; Grewal et al., 2006; Méndez-Durón and García, 2009; Peng and Mu, 2011; Peng et al., 2013). Unlike traditional organizations, OSS project boundaries are permeable, impermanent, and somewhat ill-de<sup>fi</sup>ned, allowing for easy <sup>fl</sup>ow of people and resources within and across project boundaries (Seidel and Stewart, 2011) Further, OSS projects di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional projects in this way: key stakeholders do not de<sup>fi</sup>ne their success in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial outcomes. Because OSS projects di<sup>f</sup>er from traditional projects in the ways described, we must develop unique theories for OSS projects.

In this study, we contend that (1) both internal and external social capital contribute to OSS success, and (2) internal and external social capital may interact in unique ways to in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS success. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne OSS success in terms of developer contributions to OSS source code. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition is consistent with examinations of technical success and we use it because code contributions represent the lifeblood of OSS projects (Daniel and Stewart, 2016). We de<sup>fi</sup>ne internal social capital as di<sup>f</sup>erences among participants (developers, etc.) in an OSS project, and external social capital as di<sup>f</sup>erences among OSS projects on OSS platforms. We propose a taxonomy of internal and external social capital in terms of participant diferences (language, role, and contribution di<sup>f</sup>erences across participants) and project diferences (di<sup>f</sup>erences in development environments of OSS projects and their connectedness to other OSS projects). We postulate the main and moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects of these two types of social capital on project success and test these e<sup>f</sup>ects using four years of data on 329 SourceForge projects. The research question of interest is: Does internal and external social capita influence OSS project success, and if so, how?

Our study makes several contributions to social capital and open source research. First, it bridges the gap between internal and external sources of social capital, o<sup>f</sup>ering a more nuanced and holistic view of the interaction between the two sources in OSS projects. This holds signi<sup>fi</sup>cance because prior OSS research has considered either internal social capital (e.g., Chou and He, 2011) or external social capital (e.g., Grewal et al., 2006), but not both at the same time. Our study examines the simultaneous e<sup>f</sup>ects of and interaction between these two sources of social capital on OSS project success and shows that the more-is-better approach may not be the right strategy for <sup>fi</sup>rms. We also provide tentative explanations for some of the inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported in the prior OSS literature. For example, Chou and He (2011) found that two out of three types of social capital (relational and cognitive capital) did not impact OSS project outcomes, while studies in face-to-face settings report the importance of relational social capital (Putnam, 1995; Shumaker and Brownell, 1984) and cognitive social capital in electronic networks of practice (Constant et al., 1996). Similarly, internal social capital (measured as team homogeneity or closeness in internal networks), is linked to higher e<sup>fi</sup>ciency and lower administrative overhead in one study (Zenger and Lawrence, 1989), but others report negative e<sup>f</sup>ects on creativity and knowledge assimilation capabilities of the team (Pelled et al., 1999). We explain these mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings by suggesting that external social capital must be examined to understand the impact of the internal social capital.

## 2. Conceptual development

Social capital is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as “the sum of the actual and potential resources embedded within, available through, and derived from the network of relationships possessed by an individual or social unit (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). The central tenet in socia capital theory is that networks of relationships constitute a valuable resource (a collectivity owned capital called “social capital”) that facilitates the creation of intellectual capital (e.g., new knowledge) through the processes of knowledge exchange and combination. Social capital consists of three dimensions: structural capital, cognitive capital, and relational capital. “Structural capital” relates to the con<sup>fi</sup>guration of linkages and ties that provides access to network resources such as knowledge embedded within network members. “Cognitive capital” refers to shared language, codes, and narratives that enable e<sup>f</sup>ective communication and interpretation of knowledge among knowledge sources and recipients. “Relational capital" is the trust, norms, and obligations that facilitate fric tionless sharing and acceptance of knowledge.

We distinguish between the social capital that is internal to an OSS project and the social capital that is external to it. We consider internal social capital in terms of participant diversity within the project team. The notion of diversity as a resource is well-researched in the management, sociology, and political science literatures (Cheong et al., 2007). Diversity embedded in institutional structures can manifest as social capital (Arneil, 2006; Reagans and Zuckerman, 2001). In particular, similar people can share cognitive schemas and relate easily to each other. Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s (1998) study of cognitive and relational dimensions can explain the impact of internal social capital.

External social capital refers to a project’s relationship with the broader social structure of the OSS network through which the project can access necessary resources (Oh, Chung and Labianca, 2004). In this study, external social capital is operationalized in terms of project di<sup>f</sup>erences. OSS projects constitute conglomerations of developers and active users who share ideas, expertise, and code to collectively build intellectual capital in the form of useful OSS software (Singh et al., 2011). A social network can help OSS development by sourcing valuable knowledge from the community and from other projects on the network. However, social networks also di<sup>f</sup>er considerably in terms of their size and knowledge resources. These social network di<sup>f</sup>erences impact the consequent interaction, collaboration, and knowledge-sharing among members in those projects. Nahapiet and Ghoshal’s (1998) study of structural dimensions o<sup>f</sup>ers insight for understanding external social capital.

Studies investigating the e<sup>f</sup>ects of internal diversity and external social networks on teams have provided mixed and inconclusive results (Horwitz and Horwitz, 2007; Sparrowe et al., 2001). For instance, knowledge resources that are available through external social networks may not be appropriately assimilated due to the diversity formulation embedded in the structure of an institution (Reagans and Zuckerman, 2001). These ideas are developed further below to postulate our hypotheses in the context of open source platforms.

## 2.1. Participant diferences

Because participant di<sup>f</sup>erences drive how participants relate to each other, participant diversity embodies an aspect of interna social capital. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne diversity as meaningful di<sup>f</sup>erences across participants (Harrison and Klein, 2007). This construct is important because it determines the degree to which participants can relate to and understand each other’s skills and resources to build useful and functional OSS products (Daniel et al., 2013). Participant diversity has previously been conceptualized in terms of demographic and job-related di<sup>f</sup>erences such as employee tenure (Chen et al., 2010; Ren et al., 2015), temporal dispersion (Colazo and Fang, 2010), culture (Alfaro, 2010; Daniel et al., 2013), role or function (Daniel et al., 2013; Kankanhalli et al., 2007; Peters and Karren, 2009), disparity or reputation (Daniel et al., 2013), and skills (Giuri et al., 2010; Singh and Tan, 2010). Diversity in<sup>fl</sup>uences work performance in collocated teams (Joshi and Roh, 2009) and global virtual teams (Alfaro, 2010; Chen et al., 2010; Kankanhalli et al., 2007; Peters and Karren, 2009; Schilling et al., 2013). However, research on collocated teams suggests that participant diversity may act as a double-edged sword (Horwitz and Horwitz, 2007; Milliken and Martins, 1996). Team homogeneity makes it easier for team members to trust each other. However, homogeneity also leaves little room for knowledge exchange among team members with similar information, limiting the generation of new knowledge.

Harrison and Klein (2007) provide a comprehensive taxonomy of three types of participant di<sup>f</sup>erences: separation diversity, disparity diversity, and variety diversity. Separation diversity refers to di<sup>f</sup>erences in lateral position or opinion among unit members, such as in values, beliefs, or attitudes. Separation diversity is highest when half of unit members are on one end of the continuum and half are on the other end. Disparity diversity describes the vertical di<sup>f</sup>erences in proportion of socially valued assets or resources held by unit members. Disparity diversity is highest when one member holds the bulk of the resources. Variety diversity refers to differences in kind, source, or category of relevant knowledge or experience among unit members. Variety diversity is highest when each member represents a distinct category.

Such a taxonomy can potentially explain prior inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings because a development team comprised of developers who are extreme on one type of diversity but homogenous in another type of diversity may impact project outcomes in idiosyncratic ways. The validity of this taxonomy was demonstrated in a study of academic start-ups in Austria (Breitenecker et al., 2011). The authors found that separation diversity, disparity diversity, and variety diversity impact <sup>fi</sup>ve di<sup>f</sup>erent project characteristics: cohesion, communication, feedback, trust, and work norms. In the context of OSS projects, we re-conceptualize Harrison and Klein’s typology as language diversity, role diversity, and contribution diversity for reasons described below.

Language diversity, which is akin to Harrison and Klein’s (2007) separation diversity, refers to di<sup>f</sup>erences in the languages spoken by participants in OSS projects. These di<sup>f</sup>erences can indicate disparities in opinions, beliefs and goals, especially those related to group goals (Krauss and Chiu, 1998). Language in<sup>fl</sup>uences attitude change; social perception; personal identity; social interaction; intergroup bias and stereotyping; and attribution (Krauss and Chiu, 1998). Speakers of markedly di<sup>f</sup>erent languages see distinct mental images of similar states of a<sup>f</sup>airs (Krauss and Chiu, 1998). For example, the word control has a positive connotation in the United States. In France, it suggests lack of trust (Claes and Bois, 2002). Such di<sup>f</sup>erences can make relationships among participants di<sup>fi</sup>cult because people <sup>fi</sup>nd it more pleasurable to interact with others who have similar psychological characteristics, because that interaction veri<sup>fi</sup>es and reinforces their own beliefs, a<sup>f</sup>ect and expressed behaviors” (Harrison et al., 2002). Language di<sup>f</sup>erences, which lead to di<sup>f</sup>erences in beliefs and goals discourage connections and sti<sup>fl</sup>e knowledge creation.

Role diversity is similar to Harrison and Klein’s (2007) notion of variety diversity, Williams and O'Reilly’s (1998) information diversity, and Miner et al. s (2003) categorical variability. Information diversity refers to the variety of information available to a project team. It is dependent on the roles of each participant in OSS projects (Koka and Prescott, 2002). OSS participants often have roles based on their personal knowledge and skills. These roles can include active users who report bugs and make posts, administrators who start projects, and developers who write source code. Participants in each role hold a unique perspective of the application and therefore can increase opportunities for knowledge exchange and create opportunities for the development of unique code contributions.

Finally, contribution diversity re<sup>fl</sup>ects di<sup>f</sup>erences in how much participants add to the software development process. Some participants may make a single contribution over the lifetime of an OSS project; others may invest signi<sup>fi</sup>cant time and e<sup>f</sup>ort (Pham et al., 2013; Pinto et al., 2016; Zhou and Mockus, 2012). OSS projects typically have a meritocratic structure (Bird et al., 2007; Dahlander and O'Mahony, 2011; Raymond, 2001; Roberts et al., 2006), in which participants with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant contributions gain responsibilities and authority within the project. They are able to shape the future direction by, for instance, approving or disapproving code changes (Bird et al., 2007; Kogut and Metiu, 2001; Lindberg et al., 2016; Roberts et al., 2006). This meritocracy creates a power structure in OSS projects that maps to Harrison and Klein’s (2007) concept of disparity diversity. It facilitates productive relationships among participants that can facilitate the development of intellectual capital.

## 2.2. Project diferences

We focus on project di<sup>f</sup>erences as an instantiation of external social capital, which can in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS project success. Projects have di<sup>f</sup>erent patterns of relationships between them and other projects. Each pattern is associated with a distinct level of knowledge exchange and combination such that social capital is converted to intellectual capital at different rates (Nahapiet and Ghoshal. 1998) While participant di<sup>f</sup>erences (i.e., diversities) focus on the people contributing to a given OSS project, project di<sup>f</sup>erences focus on the processes by which knowledge, experience, and code are transferred across OSS projects due to the sharing of participants across OSS projects (Peng et al., 2013). Participant di<sup>f</sup>erences re<sup>fl</sup>ect internal social capital (i.e., internal to an OSS project), while project di<sup>f</sup>erences re<sup>fl</sup>ect external social capital (i.e., social capital sourced from other projects).

In this study, we consider two project-level di<sup>f</sup>erences: development environment and connectedness. We focus on these two projectlevel factors because they both impact a project’s access to knowledge and participant attention. We de<sup>fi</sup>ne development environment diference as the degree to which projects are dissimilar in terms of the technologies used, such as domain knowledge; programming language; and development and execution environment. Development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence impacts knowledge transfer and use in much the same way as the cognitive dimension of social capital (represented by shared representations, interpretations and systems of meaning) impacts knowledge transfer and use (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Because of the OSS licensing terms, developers in projects with a similar environment can easily share source code and documentation (e.g. websites, FAQs) among related projects. This means that developers do not have to learn new programming styles, development, or execution environments for every new OSS project (Haefliger et al., 2008). It allows developers to move code from one project to another, reuse the same libraries, or build new applications by drawing from similar projects (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010)

Connectedness diference, also called degree centrality in the literature, is the degree to which one project connects to other projects in a network (Brass et al., 2004). OSS projects often exist within networks composed of participants working on distinct projects (Grewal et al., 2006; Hu et al., 2012; Oh and Jeon, 2007; Peng et al., 2013). Projects are considered to be connected when one participant engages with two or more projects (Peng et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2011). The developer connects two nodes (projects) in the OSS network. Network connections among projects facilitate knowledge sharing; high project connectedness allows developers and active users to move easily among projects, transferring knowledge and reusing code from one project to another (Peng et al., 2013). In this sense, project connectedness maps closely to the structural dimension (network) of social capital described by Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) because it provides developers access to connected projects on the OSS platform, and an opportunity to adapt and reuse relevant tools, know-how, and interfaces among these projects

## 3. Research hypotheses

We use a social capital perspective to theorize relationships between internal and external social capital (participant diversities and project development environment and connectedness) and OSS project outcomes, and in particular, to explore how these variables interact to in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS project outcomes. Fig. 1 provides an overview of our constructs and their interrelationships.

## 3.1. External social capital: development environment diference across projects

OSS licensing guidelines require OSS projects to share their source code and associated documentation with all interested parties. Therefore, when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is low and a target project is similar to other projects in an OSS network, developers and active users can easily transfer software features, resources, and code from other projects to the target project. While the target project may <sup>fi</sup>nd itself competing for the participant s attention, it overwhelmingly bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the knowledge transfer that manifests as feature suggestions and code contributions to the target project. For example, projects may use the same or a similar run-time environment (RTE). An RTE is the set of platforms, libraries, variables, and other necessary <sup>fi</sup>les that standardize code and allow seamless execution of programs irrespective of underlying hardware and software di<sup>f</sup>erences (Gunnerson and Wienholt, 2005). By abstracting applications from the underlying operating system, similar RTEs like the Java Runtime Environment and the Common Language Runtime allow OSS developers to build portable and reusable applications without worrying about the di<sup>f</sup>erences in underlying hardware on which they may be operated, and allow active users to test and debug those applications on a variety of di<sup>f</sup>erent user platforms (Gunnerson and Wienholt, 2005). When the target project di<sup>f</sup>ers from other projects in terms of development environment (including RTE) reuse is less valuable and less feasible because translation and porting cost too much (Frakes and Nejmeh, 1986). This logic leads us to argue that low levels of development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence allow knowledge exchange and facilitate code contribution to the target project.

As development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence increases to a moderate level, the shared codes and languages decrease. Developers and active users <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to reuse resources from other projects and contributions decrease. Shared codes and languages are forms of cognitive capital (a dimension of social capital), and a decline in this capital adversely a<sup>f</sup>ects the combination capability of developers and active users. Individuals learn by associating ideas with what they already know (Reagans and McEvily, 2003). If the project uses a di<sup>f</sup>erent development environment, participants may <sup>fi</sup>nd it too di<sup>fi</sup>cult to adapt features from the other projects. However, as the development environment difference increases from a moderate level to a high level, we expect to see a reversal and an increase in project contributions. This occurs because the project is su<sup>fi</sup>ciently di<sup>f</sup>erent from other projects so that it no longer competes with those projects for developer e<sup>f</sup>ort and attention. The target project can easily capture a developer s dedicated attention and corresponding contributions. Further, the di<sup>f</sup>erences among projects foster creativity and novel solutions (Sampson, 2007). Hence, we expect the relationship between project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence and OSS success (project contribu tions) to follow a U-shaped quadratic relationship:

## 3.2. External social capital: connectedness among projects

Connections formed by participants between OSS projects represent an important conduit for the <sup>fl</sup>ow of knowledge and resources from one project to another (Grewal et al., 2006; Peng et al., 2013; Singh et al., 2011). Participants in highly connected projects can access knowledge in the form of source code (Hae<sup>fl</sup>iger et al., 2008), developer skills, or prior experience (Hahn et al., 2008). Because knowledge integration from many sources is important for the success of OSS projects, we argue that a project’s connectedness a<sup>f</sup>ects its ability to attract developer knowledge and contributions through network connections. The transferred knowledge may represent explicit knowledge in the form of documented ideas (Kuk, 2006) or technical artifacts such as code (Hae<sup>fl</sup>iger et al., 2008; Howison and Crowston. 2014) that can benefit the target project. In addition, tacit knowledge may also transfer to the target proiect as the connecting participant learns of di<sup>f</sup>erent development methods (Singh et al., 2011) and gains hands-on experience (Nonaka and Toyama, 2003) to incorporate in the target project. This transfer of knowledge may lead to increased contributions to the target project in the form of product features and/or reused code from other projects. Hence, we suggest:

H2. Connectedness has a positive relationship with OSS project success.

## 3.3. Internal social capital: participant language diversity

Because OSS projects often employ globally distributed participants, they regularly include participants who speak different languages. Language di<sup>f</sup>erences can cause “separation” among members of a collective because it is through language that people encode and communicate perceptions and associations that show a common outlook (Barley et al., 1988). The way people use language can reveal opposing metaphors for work and role expectations (Gibson and Zellmer-Bruhn, 2001).

The diversity of languages hinders knowledge exchange and combination, critical for new intellectual capital creation (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998) in several ways. First, the relational dimension of social capital motivates participants to engage in knowledge exchange and combination behaviors through trust (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Language di<sup>f</sup>erences negatively in<sup>fl</sup>uence interpersonal communication, trust, and work environment in a multi-cultural team (Iles and Hayers, 1997; Schweiger et al., 2003), thus impacting their ability to make meaningful contributions. Second, language di<sup>f</sup>erences create uncertainty in the team agenda and goals because team members are not sure they have communicated e<sup>f</sup>ectively with each other (Chevrier, 2003). Third, because many participants join OSS projects to identify with and learn from expert software developers (Fang and Neufeld, 2009; Lakhani and Wolf, 2005; Von Krogh et al., 2012), projects with high language diversity may hinder participants’ learning and contributions, resulting in lower project success. Language diversity is highest when half of the OSS team speaks one language and the other half speaks a di<sup>f</sup>erent language (Harrison and Klein, 2007). By hindering identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, these circumstances yield the most team con<sup>fl</sup>ict, the lowest amount of communication among group members speaking di<sup>f</sup>erent languages, and the lowest success (Harrison and Klein 2007). This expectation leads us to hypothesize:

H3. Language diversity negatively impacts OSS project success.

## 3.4. Participant language diversity and development environment diference between projects

While development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is expected to have a U-shaped relationship with OSS project success, we expect this relationship to interact with language diversity. Language diversity, through its impact on internal social capital, will tend to have the most negative impact when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is very high or very low, and the least negative impact when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is moderate. The rationale for this moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect is as follows: OSS projects compete with each other for participant effort and attention (Santos et al., 2013). When there is low development environment difference between the target and other projects, participants frustrated by separation induced by language diversity may easily leave the target project for other projects with similar development environments. When developers perceive alternate and target projects as similar, they can e<sup>f</sup>ortlessly transfer their skills and knowledge to alternate projects, to the detriment of the target project. In the presence of low development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence, language diversity should therefore have a stronger negative impact on OSS projects.

When development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is high, the impact of language diversity on OSS project success will also be strongly negative. In such circumstances, participants will <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to join another project, but contribution still slows because not enough projects exist with which to share resources and standards. Development environment di<sup>f</sup>erences create barriers for coordination. These di<sup>f</sup>erences make it harder for developers to move technology, code snippets, policy, standards, and licenses from other projects to the target project. Language di<sup>f</sup>erences make it more di<sup>fi</sup>cult for project participants to leverage innovative ideas from projects with di<sup>f</sup>erent development environments. The separation and coordination challenges result in fewer code contributions.

We expect the most positive impact of language diversity on OSS project contribution when the project operates in an environment characterized by a moderate level of development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence. In this case, the target project experiences minimal competition with alternate projects for resources. At the same time, the target project can bene<sup>fi</sup>t by sharing standards, code snippets, policy choices, etc. with some projects, and language diversity yields the least negative impact on contributions. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4. Development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence moderates the negative impact of language diversity on project success, such that the negative relationship between language diversity and OSS project success has a more negative slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is high or low, and a less negative slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is moderate.

## 3.5. Participant language diversity and connectedness among projects

The target project’s position in the network, or connectedness, also likely interacts with the impact of language diversity on project success. As stated in H3, high language diversity leads participants to make fewer contributions to the target project because it creates separation that inhibits the ability of participants to identify with each other. In the presence of language diversity, if a project is connected to many projects because participants are working on those other projects, participants may use the time and e<sup>f</sup>ort you would expect them to spend on the target project on the connected projects. Thus, the negative impact of high language diversity grows even stronger (more negative) for highly connected projects. Said another way: the low internal social capital yields more negativity in the presence of high external social capital. In contrast, if the target project is less connected and participants do not work on other projects, they may be less aware of competing projects and continue to contribute to the target project. This occurs even if separation makes it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify with participants. Language diversity has a weaker (less negative) moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect. Hence, we propose:

H5. Connectedness positively moderates the negative impact of language diversity on OSS project success, such that when connectedness is high, the impact of language diversity on project success is more negative than when project connectedness is low.

## 3.6. Internal social capital: participant role diversity

Progress in the creation of intellectual capital often occurs by bringing together knowledge from disparate sources and disciplines (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). A diverse group often brings in diverse knowledge, perspectives, and experiences, and in doing so, fosters new and innovative ideas (Harrison and Klein, 2007). Furthermore, implementing innovative ideas requires a variety of knowledge types including awareness knowledge and “how-to” knowledge (Rogers, 1995).

Awareness knowledge relates to use of the software in context, which can be provided by active users (Wang et al., 2015). Based on their use of the application, users can o<sup>f</sup>er information about usage contexts.

How-to knowledge embodies the technical knowledge required to create the software product; developers typically provide this knowledge in OSS projects. Based on their role as developers, they can o<sup>f</sup>er contributions founded on the application technical details. Developers may test the software; however, their awareness of potential uses may be limited. Users, partly because they may be numerous, typically test the software in a wider set of organizational environments and computer con<sup>fi</sup>gurations. Administrators have knowledge about the platform used to support the application development and, perhaps, historical knowledge about the evolution of the application.

Participants in each of these roles (i.e. role diversity) allow the project to leverage a variety of useful perspectives (Daniel et al., 2013). When a project has high role diversity (i.e., participants are equally dispersed in the roles of developer, user, and administrator), it gains knowledge from developers about writing code, from users about usability, and from administrators about the platform. The project bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from di<sup>f</sup>erences in experiences among unit members and this leads to greater creativity and innovation (Harrison and Klein, 2007), which facilitates OSS development. Hence:

H6. Role diversity positively impacts OSS project success.

## 3.7. Participant role diversity and development environment diference among projects

We expect the positive impact of internal social capital, arising from role diversity, on OSS project success to be the most positive when the development environment includes moderate levels of di<sup>f</sup>erence. An OSS project with high role diversity can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from combining ideas, skills, and expertise from related projects. However, the amount of bene<sup>fi</sup>t depends upon the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence between that target project and other projects. If development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is low, then most projects are similar to the target project and the target project participants likely engage only with very similar projects. For example, developers’ experience may include only Java projects, and active users’ experience may center on applications about communications. Participants have a narrow range of experience. Lacking breadth of experience, these developers and active users may <sup>fi</sup>nd it di<sup>fi</sup>cult to leverage role diversity or identify feature ideas for the application. The developers have less expertise to help them implement the feature ideas. Thus, the positive impact of internal social capital in the form of role diversity is reduced when there is low development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence.

Role diversity o<sup>f</sup>ers the most bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the target project when it operates in a situation characterized by moderate environment di<sup>f</sup>erence. The project can bene<sup>fi</sup>t most from role diversity if it is both similar to and di<sup>f</sup>erent from other projects in terms of environment, technology platform, programming language, etc., to allow for cross sharing of ideas and code. For instance, active users with experience in multiple environments can ensure that the application works well in those environments by reminding participants of potential challenges in those other environments. Developers can access new technical ideas while also identifying enough overlap in techniques to apply them easily when implementing ideas from active users. In this case, the external social capital complements the internal social capital.

If development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is too high, a target OSS project may receive fewer contributions from developers, active users, and administrators because that project is too di<sup>f</sup>erent to gain knowledge from other projects. The technical skills that developers gain from participating on other projects in the environment cannot be easily transferred to the target project to implement user ideas, Similarly, users may make only nominal contributions in their role because they cannot recommend features from other projects. Because of this, the positive impact of role diversity will be weakened when the environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is too high. Hence, we expect development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence to moderate the relationship between role diversity and project success as follows:

H7. Development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence moderates the positive impact of role diversity on OSS project success such that the positive relationship between role diversity and OSS project success has a more positive slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is moderate and a less positive slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is high or low.

## 3.8. Participant role diversity and connectedness among projects

For a project with high role diversity (i.e., substantial how-to knowledge from developers and know-what knowledge from active users), all participants must focus on the target project to reap the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of role diversity. For such projects, connection to many projects may hurt, rather than help, project contribution. Connected projects distract participants from the target project (Daniel and Stewart, 2016). Because of the distraction, participants are not e<sup>f</sup>ective in their roles in the target project, whether that role is developer, active user, or administrator. For instance, if a developer contributes to two projects, he may exhibit less e<sup>f</sup>ectiveness in translating active user feature suggestions into features on the target project. Thus, the bene<sup>fi</sup>t of role diversity is weaker in the presence of connections to other projects. Our logic coincides with what Cuevas‐Rodríguez et al. (2014) <sup>fi</sup>nd: they <sup>fi</sup>nd that the positive impact of internal social capital on uniqueness in radical product innovation is strongest when there is weak external socia capital. Hence:

H8. Connectedness moderates the positive impact of role diversity on OSS project success such that, when connectedness is high, the positive impact of role diversity on project success is less positive than when connectedness is low.

## 3.9. Internal social capital: participant contribution diversity

Contribution diversity allows OSS projects to provide a positive interaction experience for both active and less-active participants. Harrison and Klein (2007) argue that organizations di<sup>f</sup>er in the concentration of valued social assets or resources, such as status or reputation across participants, which they call disparity diversity. Contribution diversity, as a form of disparity diversity, is highest when one participant (or a small number of participants) holds more reputation than all other participants. While Harrison and Klein (2007) argue that disparity diversity tends to have negative outcomes, recent work suggests positive outcomes (Anderson and Brown, 2010; Groysberg et al., 2011; Halevy et al., 2011).

We believe contribution diversity provides a positive interaction experience for OSS participants because active developers enjoy showcasing their expertise to an audience of appreciative peers. Zhang and Zhu (2011) show that, as audience size increases, so does the motivation of an OSS participant to contribute. Because participants often volunteer and do not receive <sup>fi</sup>nancial compensation from the project, this recognition and status is essential to maintain the morale and interest of key participants. The attention of a developer community, a scarce resource, may translate into future project leads and/or job prospects. Although less-active developers tend to garner less attention, they want to learn from and be mentored by the active developers. Further, they often aspire to gain attention, in the future, for themselves (Daniel et al., 2013). While contribution diversity incentivizes active developers to contribute, maintaining their esteemed status, it also fosters contributions from less-active developers, who aspire to become experts themselves.

Contribution diversity also facilitates coordination through its positive impact on participant relationships. People with clear status di<sup>f</sup>erences rarely argue with each other (Gould, 2002) because of clearly de<sup>fi</sup>ned power and authority structures. This demonstrates an e<sup>f</sup>ective enactment of the cognitive dimension of social capital. When a project includes clear senior/active developers, the junior/less-active developers are more intent on learning from senior developers and not in arguing with them. In this sense, participant di<sup>f</sup>erences act as a coordination mechanism (He and Huang, 2011). Without such di<sup>f</sup>erences, participants may engage in long and unproductive debates (He and Huang, 2011) that waste time and demotivate those who could be making contributions. In essence, a project with both senior/active developers and junior/less active developers allows development of relationa norms (an aspect of social capital). Therefore, higher contribution diversity makes it more likely that the project achieves superior outcomes in terms of participants’ contributions.

Further, the expected impact of contribution diversity on outcomes is consistent with strati<sup>fi</sup>cation status theory. This theory suggests that higher strati<sup>fi</sup>cation leads to positive outcomes if participants perceive the strati<sup>fi</sup>cation (i.e., the distribution of power) as being fair and just, but to negative outcomes if the distribution of power is perceived as unfair (Groysberg et al., 2011; Halevy et al., 2011; Lazear and Rosen, 1981). Participants generally perceive OSS hierarchies as being fair. This occurs because an OSS developer’s status is based on code contributions, and even junior/less-active developers can assume leadership status by virtue of their contributions. Hence, we expect such contribution diversity, through its impact on participant relationships, to positively impact project success. This expectation leads to the hypothesis:

H9. Contribution diversity positively impacts OSS project success.

## 3.10. Participant contribution diversity and development environment

As with other types of participant diversity, we argue that development environment interacts with the impact of contribution diversity on project success. If the target project is too similar to other projects in terms of development environment (development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is low), it cannot get enough novel ideas from other projects to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the strong hierarchy that contribution di<sup>f</sup>erences o<sup>f</sup>er. The ideas available will have already been dealt with by the hierarchy. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t of contribution diversity is highest for projects that have moderate development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence with other projects. If the target project is too di<sup>f</sup>erent from other projects in terms of development environment, it cannot attract useful ideas from other projects. Nor can the developers use the ideas from other applications and the decision-making abilities provided by the hierarchy prove useless. The contribution diversity allows the project to make optimal use of the best level of ideas available to the project. Hence:

H10. Development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence moderates the impact of contribution diversity on OSS project success, such that the positive relationship between contribution diversity and OSS project success has a more positive slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is moderate and a less positive slope when development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence is high or low.

## 3.11. Participant contribution diversity and connectedness among projects

When a community lacks connection to other communities, participants <sup>fi</sup>nd it easier to develop certain norms and codes that facilitate work (Boland Jr. and Tenkasi, 1995). In a less-connected organization, participants can develop stronger norms and trust when compared to a more-connected organization (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). In this way, a less-connected project <sup>fi</sup>nds it easier to di<sup>f</sup>erentiate between senior/active developers and junior/less-active developers. When the target project shares participants with connected projects, the participants will (as an example) get an alternate view about how to designate a senior developer in the target project. The knowledge that <sup>fl</sup>ows across the network of a well-connected project diminishes the potency of the hierarchy provided by the di<sup>f</sup>erentiation between senior and junior developers. The di<sup>f</sup>erentiation is less of a motivator for developers and does not facilitate coordination. For example, participants may perceive a di<sup>f</sup>erence between their global status within the set of connected projects and their local status in the target project.

For unconnected projects, the only signi<sup>fi</sup>cant di<sup>f</sup>erences among participants are those among participants inside the target project. However, for connected projects, the global, platform di<sup>f</sup>erences may impact participants’ perceptions by creating ambiguity about their status (position in the hierarchy) within the project. This, in turn, may create ambiguities and disagreements on key decisions, such as the future evolution of the software and the identity of the decision-makers. Such a dysfunctional situation may occur because requirements were not clearly set at the start of the project (Scacchi, 2002) and so developers must frequently prioritize, recalibrate, and select among con<sup>fl</sup>icting potential features (Yamauchi et al., 2000). Developers may spend less time on application development (using ideas from other projects) and more time in arguments that inhibit success. This dynamic leads to our <sup>fi</sup>nal hypothesis:

H11. Connectedness negatively moderates the impact of contribution diversity on OSS project success, such that when connectedness is low, contribution diversity has a stronger positive impact on project success than when connectedness is high.

## 4. Methods

## 4.1. Data collection

Data was collected from OSS projects hosted at SourceForge Research Data Archive (SRDA), a database of OSS projects at the University of Notre Dame (Van Antwerp and Madey, 2008). SourceForge is one of the largest virtual workspace hosting OSS projects. Project-level information available from SRDA includes when these projects were registered, their application’s development stage, intended users of the application, the number of times they have been downloaded, and submission and correction of bug reports.

Our study sampled projects that registered on SourceForge between Jan 1, 2005 and Dec 31, 2005, with an initial sampling frame of 21,714 projects. We captured the activity in these projects for the next six years, up until December 2011. A longer panel was not possible due to unavailability of some types of data (e.g. releases) we use in this study in SRDA database post 2011. Inactive or abandoned projects with no activity during the six-year observation period were dropped from the sample. A project was considered active if at least one commit was made during each year or if the project had made a release during each year during six year observation period. However, a large number of the projects had limited data available for the years 2010–2011, leading us to utilize data from 2005 to 2009. This screening resulted in a sample size of 3021 projects. Out of these 3021 projects, only 581 projects had complete data available in SourceForge database for our variables of interest. Since most of our constructs are at the project team level of analysis, we excluded projects with fewer than three members from the study sample. The <sup>fi</sup>nal sample consisted of a balanced panel of 329 projects with a total of 1316 observations over four years. This sample represented a variety of application types, such as software development tools, management applications, statistical analysis software, online sales tools, education related programs and entertainment applications.

## 4.2. Operationalization of variables

OSS project success was operationalized as the number of artifact closures associated with a project, such as addressing bug reports and feature requests. As bug <sup>fi</sup>xing and feature additions are a critical part of OSS development, closures represent a project’s success in terms of the amount of technical development (Crowston et al. 2003). Closures indicate a successful resolution of a bus report or feature request (Wen et al., 2013) and is similar to measures used for software engineering task completion (Herbsleb and Mockus, 2003). Task completion measures the amount of e<sup>f</sup>ort exerted by developers on a project (Herbsleb and Mockus, 2003). Such operationalization is also consistent with the OSS literature (Chengalur-Smith et al., 2010; Daniel et al., 2013; Wen et al., 2013).

To measure contribution diversity, we <sup>fi</sup>rst recorded a participant’s contribution as the sum of all the artifacts closed by the participant in a year. Following recommendations in prior work (Harrison and Klein, 2007), we used the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of variation of these participant contributions as a proiect-level measure of contribution diversity for each vear

We measured language diversity using Blau’s index (1977) of the language spoken for each project’s participants, as documented in the SourceForge database. Blau’s index represents the probability that two participants taken at random from the project (with replacement) have the same language. A value of 0 indicates that two developers spoke the same language, representing low language diversity, and a value of 1 indicates high language diversity. As participants in a project change over time, the Blau index for the project also changes over time giving us a time-variant measure of language diversity.

Role diversity, re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the degree to which participants were dispersed across roles, was also operationalized using Blau (1977) index by categorizing participants as developers, administrators, and active users during each recorded year in the sample. Administrators were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such in each project. Participants were categorized as developers if they managed releases or made a commit during a year, and as active users if they reported bugs, requested features or support, or participated in project-de<sup>fi</sup>ned forums during a year, but were not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as developers using the criteria above. The role diversity measure was time-variant as project roles changed over time. A preliminary analysis of projects con<sup>fi</sup>rmed movement of participants in and out of project roles over the four-year duration of our study. We calculated an index to represent the probability that two participants taken at random from the project had the same role. This index varied between 0 (if all participants were identical on this attribute, indicating no role diversity) and 1 (if all participants were di<sup>f</sup>erent, indicating high role diversity).

Project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence represents the dissimilarity between a target project and other active OSS projects. This construct was measured in terms of three dimensions: type of the project, programming language, and development and execution environment (Jones, 1984; Sacks, 1994). Each of these dimensions represents a di<sup>f</sup>erent type of expertise: project type, such as gaming, internet application, etc., represents the application domain knowledge, whereas the other dimensions represent the tools knowledge that comprises the knowledge of process, data, and functional architecture (Kim and Stohr, 1998). Software engineering research has shown that the similarity of domain and tools a<sup>f</sup>ect the amount of knowledge that can be reused from one project to another (Banker and Kau<sup>f</sup>man, 1991; Lee and Litecky, 1997). Our project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence measure is similar to that used in the existing literature (Singh et al., 2011).

To measure a project’s development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence, we <sup>fi</sup>rst computed its development environment position using a vector $\mathbf { F } _ { \mathrm { p } } = ( \mathbf { F } _ { 1 } . . . \mathbf { F _ { k } } )$ , where k was the total number of categories under the 0 dimensions, and $\mathrm { F _ { i } }$ was an indicator variable that equaled 1 if the project p fell under category i. A project can fall under several categories within a single dimension. For example, if a project $ { \mathbf { \tilde { p } } } ^ { \prime }$ was developed to run on gnome and win32 and project $\mathbf { \dot { q } } ^ { \prime }$ was developed to run on win32, KDE, and JRE, then the projects $ { \mathbf { \ddot { p } } } ^ { \ }$ and ‘q’ were similar as both the projects were compatible with win32. This similarity enhanced the chances of knowledge and code reuse across projects. In this example, the total number of categories, k, would be 4 (gnome, win32, KDE, and JRE). Project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence between the two projects p and q, was then calculated by using the uncentered correlation of the vectors $\mathbf { F _ { p } }$ and $\mathbf { F } _ { \mathrm { q } }$ (Ja<sup>f</sup>e, 1986) as

$$
\text { Development   Environment   Difference } _ {\mathrm{pq}} = 1 - \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{p}} \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{q}} ^ {\prime} / \operatorname{sqrt} \left[ (\mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{p}} \mathbf {F} _ {\mathrm{p}} ^ {\prime}) (\mathbf {F} _ {q} \mathbf {F} _ {q} ^ {\prime}) \right]
$$

Project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence varied from 0 to 1, with a value of 0 indicating low di<sup>f</sup>erence and 1 indicating high di<sup>f</sup>erence between two projects. We calculated the environment di<sup>f</sup>erence of a target project with other active projects for each of the four years. We then summed these measures and divided it by the number of such projects to calculate the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence measure for the target project for each year.

Project connectedness represents the number of other projects connected to the target project (Freeman, 1979) by developers working simultaneously on multiple OSS projects, sharing knowledge and ideas across these projects. In our operationalization, any two projects were considered connected if they shared one or more developers. This was in line with literature in social network theory on degree centrality for groups (Carrington et al., 2005) and its operationalization for knowledge and innovation di<sup>f</sup>usion among groups (Tsai, 2001). Project connectedness for projects $\mathrm { P C } ( \mathrm { P _ { k } } )$ , was calculated based on listed developers in December 2005, using the following formula (Nieminen, 1974):

$$
P C (P _ {k}) = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} a (P _ {i}, P _ {k})
$$

where $a ( P _ { i } , P _ { k } ) = 1$ if and only if $( P _ { i } , P _ { k } )$ are directly connected by (one or more) common developers, else it is 0and n = number of projects in the network, other than the target project.

Project connectedness was calculated for each year as number of developers (including administrators) change over a period of time, and was normalized across all projects. Table 1 summarizes our constructs and their measures.

We controlled for six potential sources of variation in OSS project success. First, we controlled for the total number of participants in a project, as a larger number of participants may indicate more resources (time, skills) available to the project (Butler, 2001) and is likely to lead to a greater number of contributions. Second, we controlled for the type of license (using three categories: weak copy left, strong copy left and neither), as prior studies show that license choice a<sup>f</sup>ects user interest (Stewart et al., 2006). Third, we controlled for the registration time and date when the project was <sup>fi</sup>rst registered on SourceForge; this measure captures both the amount of time the project has had to attract developer interest and the possibility that there could be external events that may impact these projects. Since applications vary in the size of the audience they seek to reach, we controlled for the scope of the application using two variables: the number of intended audiences and the number of topics. Intended audiences on SourceForge are groups like system administrators, developers, or academics that represent the types of people the developers think will use the application. Topics include keywords under which the project is catalogued such as games or databases. Finally, we controlled for the number of releases because a new release may prompt additional interest in the application.

## 5. Analysis and results

## 5.1. Data analysis

Table 2 displays descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations between our constructs of interest. A comparison with descriptive statistics reported in prior studies indicated that our sample appears similar to prior work in terms of the number of developers and distribution of licenses (e.g. Lerner and Tirole, 2002; Stewart and Gosain, 2006a, 2006b). As the dependent variable measure was a non-negative skewed count data, we used negative binomial moderated regression analysis for hypotheses testing (Allison, 1999). This analysis is a particular case of Poisson regression. To meet the underlying assumptions of this statistical technique, we transformed our participant count. release count. and connectedness measure using a natural logarithmic transformation. since this data were left-skewed in our sample. As suggested by Gelman and Hill (2007), we mean-centered the variables before calculating the interaction variables to control for multicollinearity among the variables. Further, to test for multicollinearity, we calculated Variance In<sup>fl</sup>ation Factors (VIF) for each variable (Greene, 2003). VIF is a measure how much of the variance of the estimated regression coe<sup>fi</sup>cients are in<sup>fl</sup>ated as compared to when the predictor variables are not linearly related, and a value of 10 is indicative of high multicollinearity. Our VIF values were all below 3, and coupled with low correlations between independent variables (see Table 2), multicollinearity was unlikely to be a concern in our model estimation. A test for serial correlation in panel data (Drukker, 2003) indicated no evidence of serial correlation.

## 5.2. Hypothesis testing

We tested our hypotheses using three models. Model 1 was a baseline model with only the control variables, Model 2 added the main e<sup>f</sup>ects to Model 1, and Model 3 added the moderation e<sup>f</sup>ects to Model 2. Chi-square tests of the change in incremental variance in the dependent variable, using log likelihood ratio test, was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant between Models 1 and 2 (-2LL = 233.31, p-value < 0.001), and also between Models 2 and 3 (-2LL = 97.07, p-value < 0.001). We present the results of our statistical analysis in Table 3, while Table 4 summarizes our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

Our analysis found that project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence had a curvilinear relationship with project success, indicated by a statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coe<sup>fi</sup>cient of square of the project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence $( \beta = 1 8 1 0 . 7 8 , \textrm { p < } 0 . 0 1 )$ , as expected from Hypothesis H1. Fig. 2 plots the predicted value of project success at di<sup>f</sup>erent values of development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence, holding all other independent variables at their mean value. This <sup>fi</sup>gure shows that the predicted value of project success is higher at high and low values of project environment di<sup>f</sup>erence than at moderate values. As development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence increases from its minimum value, project success decreases, and reaches a minimum point and then subsequently increases with further increase in project environment di<sup>f</sup>erence.

We also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the non-linear relationship using the three-step approach proposed by Lind and Mehlum (2010). The <sup>fi</sup>rst step is to check the direction and signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the squared term. As mentioned above, the coe<sup>fi</sup>cient is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, a necessary condition for the existence of U-shaped relationship. In the next step, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that slopes at both ends of the data range are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and in their respective directions. As expected the slope at the low end of the range is negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathrm { d y } / \mathrm { d x } = - 9 3 . 6 5 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 1 )$ , while at the high end of the range it is positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant $( \mathrm { d y } / \mathrm { d x } = 1 7 6 . 7 0 , \mathrm { p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). In the last step, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that the in<sup>fl</sup>ection point (the point at which the slope of the U-shape changes) lies within our sample range. The turning point is given by delta method $( \mathrm { R a o } , 1 9 7 2 ) , - \mathrm { \Delta \mathrm { B _ { D E D i f f } } } / ( 2 * \mathrm { \Delta \mathrm { B _ { D E D i f f S q } } } ) , \mathrm { i s ~ - 0 . 0 0 6 , }$ which lies within the 95% con<sup>fi</sup>dence interval of the turning point [−0.021 0.022]. These results provide the necessary and su<sup>fi</sup>cient conditions (Lind and Mehlum, 2010) to support hypothesis H1.

To con<sup>fi</sup>rm the main e<sup>f</sup>ects of the three diversities, we plot the marginal e<sup>f</sup>ect for each of the three diversities (Jaccard and Turrisi, 2003). ## 6. Discussion

This study takes a social capital perspective to theorize about how project characteristics (such as project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence and connectedness), in conjunction with participant di<sup>f</sup>erence (diversity), in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS project success. We illuminate how external social capital interacts with internal social capital to in<sup>fl</sup>uence success. We employ social capital theory to understand the relationships among these constructs, their impact on knowledge exchange and combination, and the creation of intellectual capital.

Our analysis of four years of data from 329 SourceForge projects found that project di<sup>f</sup>erence represents an important contingency in the relationship between participant di<sup>f</sup>erence and project success. These relationships were con<sup>fi</sup>rmed for role diversit and contribution diversity, but not for language diversity. Table 5 summarizes the key <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study.

## 6.1. Implications for research

Management scholars have often emphasized the importance of building an organization’s social capital, both internal and external. However, research has rarely examined the optimal con<sup>fi</sup>guration of a network and its interaction with organization parti cipant di<sup>f</sup>erences.

This study speci<sup>fi</sup>cally examines how project di<sup>f</sup>erence and participant di<sup>f</sup>erence jointly in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS project success. The complex interactions between project and participant di<sup>f</sup>erence on project success suggest, <sup>fi</sup>rst, that more social capital is not always better, and that, second, we should take a more nuanced view of the interactions among the di<sup>f</sup>erent types of social capital and how these factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence project success. Our study shows that the interactive e<sup>f</sup>ect of organizations’ internal and external social capital may depend on the organization’s capability, competitive positioning, and environment (Mehra et al., 2006). For example, it has been observed that at low level of external social capital, the relationship between entrepreneurial orientation and performance is weaker for OSS organizations with high internal social capital than for those with low internal social capital (Stam and Elfring, 2008).

While prior research has predominantly focused on the bene<sup>fi</sup>cial e<sup>f</sup>ects of knowledge sharing among a diverse participant base, we note that knowledge sharing also has negative e<sup>f</sup>ects. For example, learning about other projects may motivate the participant to withdraw attention from the target project and move on to connected projects. The fact that the positive and negative e<sup>f</sup>ects may coexist suggests that we need a nuanced view of these relationships: strategies that seem to work with one (less-connected) OSS project may not work with another (more-connected) project.

In addition, our research also underscores the conditions that can help organizations achieve optimal balance between interna and external social capital. By simultaneously examining internal and external social capital, we gain a better understanding of the conditions under which interactive in<sup>fl</sup>uence is detrimental to project performance. Our research contributes to IS strategy research by suggesting that an imbalance between internal and external social capital may hold negative consequences on a OSS project’s performance outcomes.

Our study also shows that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of role diversity on OSS project outcomes is moderated by project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence and is non-linear. While prior research (Daniel et al., 2013) has examined the main e<sup>f</sup>ect of role diversity, the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect is new to the literature. The positive impact of role diversity is highest for moderate levels of development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence. High role diversity in combination with high development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence can create an information overload that overwhelms the developer attempting to transfer and apply knowledge from one connected project to another. Furthermore, the nonlinear nature of the moderating e<sup>f</sup>ect demonstrates that role diversity does not always have a positive e<sup>f</sup>ect on OSS project outcome, as previously thought. Nor does environment di<sup>f</sup>erence always have a negative outcome.

Although there is a clear hierarchy of developers in OSS projects, the extent to which this hierarchy influences OSS software development is also understudied. Daniel et al. (2013) demonstrated that increased variation in this hierarchy (contribution diversity) positively in<sup>fl</sup>uences OSS project outcomes. The current study shows that the e<sup>f</sup>ect of contribution diversity on project outcomes is contingent upon project connectedness and project development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence. While contribution diversity may establish a clear hierarchy and motivate a developer to contribute code to ascend the hierarchy, this e<sup>f</sup>ect may be attenuated for highly interconnected projects. When projects are highly interconnected, developers may move from one project to another opportunistically: they contribute code to the project where they can ascend the hierarchy fastest. We need additional research to examine these non-linear moderating e<sup>f</sup>ects.

Lastly, our study helps partially reconcile inconsistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings reported in the prior literature. Chou and He (2011) reported that relational and cognitive social capital have no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on expertise integration in OSS projects, although structural capital does. Similarly, Wasko and Faraj (2005) observed that relational social capital did not have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant impact on contributions in electronic networks of practice, although cognitive social capital sometimes did. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings are inconsistent with observed evidence regarding the impact of social capital in traditional organizations. Our analysis suggests that the impact of social capital inside an organization or network may di<sup>f</sup>er based on social capital outside of it. Our analysis also suggests that the interaction between internal and external social capital can adversely impact the formation of intellectual capital.

## 6.2. Implications for practice

Our results hold important implications for organizations (such as BMW, Starbucks, and IBM) that are currently experimenting with crowdsourcing strategies and building strategic alliances to gain knowledge from external sources. Although organizations may expect these investments to enrich or complement their internal software development process, our analysis shows that such expectations may not be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Depending on the extent and type of project and participant di<sup>f</sup>erences, organizations’ externall sourced capital may help or hinder project success in unanticipated ways.

For example, although it is believed that language diversity is detrimental to organizational outcomes (i.e., code contribution), our study did not <sup>fi</sup>nd evidence to support this belief. Similarly, it is also believed that a <sup>fl</sup>at organization, with relatively low levels of role diversity, is a desirable structure for innovative work such as software production. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the opposite may actually be the case, and that the role clarity created by high role diversity is bene<sup>fi</sup>cial. Our work adds to the discussion on interactions of network e<sup>f</sup>ects inside the <sup>fi</sup>rm and outside of it (Cuevas‐Rodríguez et al., 2014; Walter et al., 2007). It also suggests that optimal results come when the internal social capital matches the external social capital. Attracting the best developers is often di<sup>fi</sup>cult and expensive (Krishnamurthy, 2005). An organization could, instead, focus on creating conditions where internal and external social capital can be best leveraged for e<sup>f</sup>ective software development.

Technological changes are often a major threat to organizational strategies; even industry leaders may struggle to cope with unanticipated changes. Our work provides some insight into how organizations can respond more e<sup>f</sup>ectively to technological changes without adversely a<sup>f</sup>ecting their project outcomes. For example, in April 2008, when the Ruby on Rails (Rails) development environment migrated to GitHub, the sudden in<sup>fl</sup>ux of Rails-based projects in the GitHub pool changed the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence for all projects. The in<sup>fl</sup>ux drastically reduced the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence among Rails projects and increased the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence among non-Rails projects in the Mozilla open source archive (Krishnamurthy, 2005). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that this unexpected change could have been leveraged for maximum project success by increasing the role diversity of Rails projects to compensate for the decreasing development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence, and by decreasing the role diversity for projects not using Rails (e.g. those using Mozilla). However, this alignment of development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence and role diversity should not be the only strategy. A quick look at GitHub’s top programming languages over the years shows that the dominant programming languages and environments have frequently changed over time. A project that used Java and may have had low development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence at one time (because there were many other Java projects), may have high development dif ference at another time when Java is less popular.

The results in this study may also guide OSS project leaders on how to choose team members for their projects, based on their <sup>fi</sup>t with the OSS development environment. For example, administrators and project managers could continuously monitor their development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence and alter their team compositions to be continuously aligned with development environment di<sup>f</sup>erences (Mares, 2015). Although a project’s connections with other projects and development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence with other projects is outside project leaders’ control, participant di<sup>f</sup>erences (diversity) can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced through recruitment practices to improve project success. If a project leader <sup>fi</sup>nds that the development environment di<sup>f</sup>erence has changed, and that more role diversity is desirable, she could achieve the desired developer composition in a few di<sup>f</sup>erent ways: either by choosing active developers or by encouraging some current active users to become developers.

Finally, our research has implications for those who manage and own platforms such as SourceForge and GitHub. While these platforms o<sup>f</sup>er vast repositories of OSS projects for developers to choose from, platform managers can also invest in tools to improve connectedness between active projects. Doing so would help developers draw knowledge, experiences, and code from one project for reuse in others.

## 6.3. Limitations and future research

This study, like most, su<sup>f</sup>ers from a few limitations. First, while we build our arguments around knowledge exchange and combination, we did not directly measure knowledge exchange or combination processes. Like similar research (Grewal et al., 2006), we inferred knowledge exchange. Future research may use surveys or interviews to detect when knowledge transfer occurs, what type of knowledge is transferred, barriers to knowledge transfer, and the positive and negative e<sup>f</sup>ects of knowledge transfer. We also did not explore all possible indicators of success (Crowston et al., 2006). Our measure is consistent with examinations of the technica success of OSS projects (Daniel and Stewart, 2016; Grewal et al., 2006). Alternate measures, such as page views or the number of times the application was downloaded, that are more consistent with market success, would be useful to consider in future research (Grewal et al., 2006).

Second, we focus our theorizing around a developer’s motivation to contribute to a project. While prior research on OSS developer motivations provides a strong foundation for our work (Lakhani and Wolf, 2005; Von Krogh et al., 2012), we did not consider developer ability and how it may impact OSS project contribution. Future research can usefully add to our work by considering the role of developer ability in addition to, or in lieu of, developer motivations on OSS contributions. Similarly, this study focuses on the development of social capital, not on the types of tasks completed. Every OSS project o<sup>f</sup>ers a variety of tasks, such as <sup>fi</sup>nding bugs correcting bugs, adding features, and documenting code. It is possible that diversity and social capital impacts these tasks di<sup>f</sup>erently. Better understanding of these relationships represents an opportunity for future research.

Third, we employed SourceForge data from 2005 to 2010. Given the age of this data, one may question whether <sup>fi</sup>ndings from our 2005 data are applicable today. Although social capital and its role in the formation of intellectual capital was as important a topic then as it is now, the number of OSS projects and the population of OSS developers have both grown since 2005, which may or may not alter the underlying dynamics of project and participant di<sup>f</sup>erences. Hence, future research may examine the generalizability of our study using more recent data. Likewise, the extent to which our <sup>fi</sup>ndings generalize to large OSS projects that do not share a platform with other OSS projects (e.g. Linux) remains unclear.

Last, this work encourages consideration of how other aspects of the network dictate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of diversity on outcomes. We did not consider the potential contribution of for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t software development <sup>fi</sup>rms, such as IBM, that increasingly seek to contribute strategically to OSS projects (August et al., 2013; Spaeth et al., 2015). For-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms can act as an alternate resource for knowledge and skills, in addition to serving as a source of trained OSS developers. While our research focused on projects that are connected via shared participants, projects can also connect to each other by virtue of funding from common sponsors (e.g. IBM) or by being associated with the same foundation. Future research should consider whether these connections yield similar resources and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts compared to projects connected by shared participants. Future research could explore why and how for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms in<sup>fl</sup>uence knowledge sharing, the development of social capital in OSS projects, and the associated outcomes

In addition, this work demonstrated that the project’s location in the network changes the impact of diversity. However, we examined only a few of the ways the target project connects to other projects.

We hope this research encourages examination of how other project patterns alter the impact of diversity. Future research could consider other patterns of connections, such as cohesion, strong tie connections, or weak tie connections. For instance, it is possible that weak tie connections do not impact the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of disparity diversity in the same way that strong tie connections do. It is possible, for example, that weak tie connections do not transfer as much information about status in projects as strong tie connections.
