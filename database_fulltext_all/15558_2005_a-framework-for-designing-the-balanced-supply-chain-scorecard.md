# A framework for designing the balanced supply chain scorecard

Jong Han Park<sup>1,2</sup>, Jae Kyu Lee<sup>2</sup> and Jung Soo Yoo<sup>1</sup>

## Abstract

Effective measurements of Supply Chain Management (SCM) in the context of the Balanced Scorecard (BSC) have become more important. In the existing SCM performance studies, there are three limitations. First, the SCM measures are not reconciled with the BSC. Second, SCM measures have not comprehensively considered the goals that can be achieved by SCM strategies and solutions. Third, the measures have not identified the characteristics of industry, product, and production strategy. In this paper, we propose a framework for the Balanced Supply Chain Scorecard that considers the literature on the BSC and SCM, SCM solutions, and product characteristics. We analyze the causal relationships between the SCM solutions and product characteristics against measures. The identified relationship between the measures and SCM solutions can be used to evaluate the impact of SCM solutions. Using a case study, we find that the importance of measures significantly depends on the product characteristics. This study can be used to expand the BSC into the area of Strategic Enterprise Management systems, thus to relate performance measures to business activities. ## Introduction

In today’s business world, Supply Chain Management (SCM) is one of the key factors for enhancing organizational effectiveness and competitiveness. Especially in the current era of global marketing and outsourcing, many companies adopt SCM for their business operations, and companies realize the need for accurate SCM performance measurement. Despite its importance, little attention has been given to the performance evaluation of the supply chain and its metrics (Gunasekaran et al., 2001), as pointed out by Lee & Billington (1992).

According to Kaplan & Norton (1996), to present a clear picture of organizational performance, a company needs to concentrate not only on financial performance measures but also on non-financial measures. The Balanced Scorecard (BSC) measures corporate performance from four perspectives: financial, customer, internal business process, and learning and growth. If a system such as Strategic Enterprise Management (SEM) (Brignall & Ballantine, 2004) incorporates the BSC in its database, it can become a powerful executive information system. So the SCM measures should be included as a part of the BSC.

In designing the SCM performance measures, we observed three problems that should be resolved. The first observation is that the SCM measures are often not reconciled with the BSC, which is also influenced by the company’s other business activities (Gunasekaran et al., 2001). To take SCM into account, the notion of the BSC needs to be extended in two ways. First, the internal business process perspective should be expanded to include the inter-organizational process for the communication and collaboration of SCM between suppliers and customers. Second, from the customer perspective, consideration should be given to the demand chain process. Thus, with regard to the demand chain, there is an overlap between the customer perspective and the internal business process perspective.

The second observation is that the SCM measures in current literature do not fully consider the goals that the popular SCM solutions provide, such as Supplier Relationship Management (SRM), Collaborative Planning, Forecasting, and Replenishment (CPFR), Electronic Data Interchange (EDI), and desktop purchasing.

The third observation is that a standard set of performance measures cannot fit every particular company, because corporate characteristics significantly influence the importance of measures. For example, product characteristics influence the company’s supply chain strategy significantly, thus different concerns are required for different companies (Fisher, 1997). However, most studies so far have suggested a common set of SCM performance measures without considering the company’s characteristics and specific situation, such as: market, corporate strategy, product type, outsourcing and SCM strategy (Beamon, 1999; Brewer & Speh 2000; Lapide, 2000; Gunasekaran et al., 2001).

To resolve these problems, we propose a framework for the Balanced Supply Chain Scorecard (BSCS) that considers the literature on the BSC and SCM, the SCM solutions, and the characteristics of companies. The SCM solutions in this study include EDI, CPFR, reverse auction and tendering, desktop purchasing, SRM, and Product Lifecycle Management (PLM). In terms of the characteristics of companies, this study limits its scope to the product characteristics, such as whether the products are functional products or innovative products. According to Fisher (1997), the product types in the supply chain are characterized by the features of the product’s demand predictability, lifecycle, variety, and supply chain structure.

To derive the measures of the BSCS, we start with the 29 measures in 11 objectives proposed by Kaplan & Norton (1996, 2000, 2004). Next, we study the SCM literature and identify 45 measures, from which we derive 21 new measures. In addition, five new measures are derived from the two goals that are the aims of SCM solutions. The procedure results in 55 measures in 18 objectives. The identified relationship between the measures and SCM solutions can be used to evaluate the impact of SCM solutions (Mahmood, 1993; Shin, 2001).

We found that the consideration of product characteristics does not require new measures, but the importance of the measures is affected by product characteristics. To explore this phenomenon, we conducted a comparative case study of two distinctive companies: one from the food products manufacturing industry, and the other from cosmetic and healthcare products manufacturing.

The paper is organized as follows: The next section reviews the literature on the BSC, SCM performance measurements, SCM solutions with their goals, and the impact of product characteristics on the BSC. Based on the literature review, we undertake a step-by-step study to derive the measures of the BSCS, starting with those of the BSC. By considering the measures in the SCM literature, the BSC is extended to create an SCMaugmented BSC. Then the measures are expanded by considering the goals of SCM solutions. As a final step, we analyze the impact of considering product characteristics, and describe the results of a comparative case study. We conclude with a summary of the findings and discuss their limitations and implications.

## Literature review on performance measurement

To properly conduct research on constructing the BSCS, we need to survey the literature on the BSC, SCM measures, SCM solutions (Lee, 2002; Lee & Turban, 2002), and the impact of product characteristics on the BSC. This section reviews each of these topics and discusses its relationship with the BSCS.

## Balanced scorecard

Kaplan & Norton (1996) proposed the BSC concept, which claims that performance evaluation criteria should include non-financial perspectives such as customer, internal business process, and learning and growth perspectives, as well as the financial perspective. They also proposed a layer structure for each of the four perspectives, with the three layers comprised of: mission, objectives, and measures.

The BSC has been applied in many industries such as banking (Beechey & Garlick, 1999), healthcare (Stewart & Bestor, 2000; Zelman et al., 2003), and hotels (Denton & White, 2000). Small companies’ characteristics are considered by Chow et al. (1997). The BSC has also been applied in various business processes such as product development (Curtis & Ellis, 1997), public relations management (Fleisher & Mahaffy, 1997), internal auditing processes (Ziegenfuss, 2000), and information technology management (van der Zee & De Jong, 1999).

The notion of the BSC has been applied to various business activities such as IS activities, e-commerce, Customer Relationship Management (CRM), and SCM (Jones & Hughes, 2001). Martinsons et al. (1999) developed a BSC for information systems that measured and evaluated IS activities from the following perspectives: business value, user orientation, internal process and future readiness. Hasan & Tibbits (2000) proposed a BSC for e-commerce based on Martinsons’ IS scorecard, the literature on management of e-commerce, and their own case study. Kim et al. (2003) suggested a BSC for evaluating the effectiveness of CRM solutions which consisted of four perspectives: customer value, customer satisfaction, customer interaction, and customer knowledge. Brewer & Speh (2000) used the BSC to measure supply chain performance, and showed a fraction of the possible measures that can be developed.

Although the performance measurement of SCM may be studied as a BSC from the purely SCM perspective, this approach is not effective for corporate-level evaluation because many measures will also be influenced by other business activities. Therefore, this study attempts to reconcile the SCM measures with the corporate-level BSC by merging and identifying its relationship with SCM activities. This approach must be extended to other business activities such as CRM. Ultimately, using the BSC framework in SEM will allow companies to trace the key business activities that influence performance measures.

## Performance measurement of supply chain management

Supply chain design and its performance have been studied in the supply chain research area. Beamon (1999) suggested three performance measure types: resources, output, and flexibility. He also proposed a goal and several measures for each type, and established a foundation toward the development of a framework for the selection of performance measures for supply chain systems. Gunasekaran et al. (2001) suggested a framework for measuring the performance of a supply chain that consisted of three levels: strategic, tactical, and operational. Some industry experts and consulting groups have also proposed a framework for SCM measurements (Lapide, 2000). Most studies simply suggest a common framework for supply chain measures, with the need to consider the company’s unique circumstances for a more balanced perspective between financial and non-financial factors.

As mentioned in the Introduction, to take SCM into account, the BSC must be extended in two ways:

\- The internal business process perspective in the BSC must be expanded to include the inter-organizational process for the communication and collaboration of SCM with suppliers and customers. Therefore, in the BSCS, we will change the internal business process perspective to the business process perspective to incorporate both internal and external processes.

\- There are many process measures related to the customer, particularly with regard to the demand chain side of SCM. In this study, we include measures on the customer related processes in the Customer Relationship objective within the customer perspective. The other internal and supplier related processes are included in the business process perspective.

## Supply chain solutions

To build effective SCM systems, particularly in the B2B e-commerce context, there are many solutions that fulfill various goals. Typical ones include EDI, CPFR, reverse auctions and tendering, desktop purchasing, SRM, and PLM. These solutions have been developed to meet the goals that must be measured, as described in the following:

Electronic data interchange EDI is the computer-tocomputer exchange of business data in standard formats. In EDI, information is organized according to a specified format set by both parties, allowing a computer transaction without human intervention (DISA, 2005). EDI provides a number of potential operational benefits (Massetti & Zmud, 1996). It reduces both cycle time and cost by improving the quality and speed through standard document exchanges. According to the Data Interchange Standard Association (DISA), the potential goals of EDI include reduced cycle time, better inventory management, increased productivity, reduced costs, improved accuracy, improved business relationships, enhanced customer service, increased sales, minimized paper use, and increased cash flow. The potential benefits of EDI are various, but many of these benefits are influenced not only by EDI but also by many other business activities. Hence, in this study, we mainly focus on the direct effect of EDI on order processing as the unique goal of EDI and select the Percentage of online purchase order processing as a new measure.

Collaborative planning, forecasting, and replenishment CPFR is Web-based coordination between trading partners on various activities such as production and purchase planning, demand forecasting, and inventory replenishment (Fliedner, 2003). Its objective is to reduce uncertainty by sharing mutually agreed-upon internal information between partners. Fliedner (2003) indicates potential benefits of CPFR for manufacturers, such as higher order fill rates, lower product inventories and faster cycle time. The issue of sharing of order, inventory, forecast information, and trust with partners is described in SCM literature (Brewer & Speh, 2000; Lapide, 2000). Since the inventory, stock-out, and order fill rate are of concern both to satisfy the customer and to evaluate the suppliers, we include these three measures in both the customer perspective and business process perspective. Two unique goals – Adherence to schedule and Forecast accuracy – of CPFR are selected as additional measures of the business process.

Reverse auction and tendering In electronic commerce, reverse auctions have received keen attention. In the reverse auction, the buyer calls for bids, and sellers bid without sealing their bids. The prices offered by the sellers decline until the closing time, theoretically reaching a rational market price.

Many companies such as Quaker Oats and SmithKlineBeecham report millions of dollars of savings by adopting reverse auctions (Smeltzer & Carr, 2003). The primary goal of reverse auctions is to reduce the purchase prices. Price reductions (5–12%) are commonly reported in reverse auctions, reducing the time to process RFQ by 25–35%.

Despite its benefits, a large portion of direct material purchase is not viable in a reverse auction because the time to attain the successful bid is long and the risk of making an unsuccessful bid exists. So the reverse auction is more adequate for the purchase of Maintenance, Repair, and Operations (MRO). A reverse auction reduces the time to process RFQ, but sometimes may take more time to attain a successful bid. In this study, we consider not only Price of purchased goods but also Time for successful bids and Percentage of successful bids as additional measures from the goals of reverse auction.

Desktop purchasing Desktop purchasing systems are designed to support non-purchasing professional and casual end-users, who can pick items from an electronic catalogue and order without the intervention of a procurement department or a rigid approval process by supervisors. A desktop purchasing service may be provided by an internal database or external e-marketplaces. Since this approach aims to save administrative costs, it is effective for frequently purchased small items. Therefore, desktop purchasing is used primarily for the procurement of indirect (secondary) items and commodities from preapproved vendors, such as office supplies, computer equipment, or MRO goods. Desktop purchasing solutions automate and support purchasing operations such as product and supplier selection, requisitioning, catalogue search, approval process, purchase order processing, catalogue update and content management, and reporting of spending patterns and supplier performance (Segev & Gebauer, 2001). Since reducing the Procurement administration cost is the primary goal of desktop purchasing, we added this goal as a new measure of the business process perspective.

Supplier relationship management Definitions of SRM differ widely from vendor to vendor. One group of vendors uses the term SRM to describe the extended procurement process; others describe a collaborative portal-like layer that sits above existing SCM applications; and still others describe it in the context of supply chain analytics and decision support. Barling (2002) defines SRM by focusing on the procurement processes, such as sourcing analytics, sourcing execution, procurement execution, payment and settlement, and supplier scoring and its performance monitoring. In this study, we adopt the definition of Barling, thus regard the unique benefits of SRM as the high quality of supplied products with on-time delivery. Accordingly, the Quality of purchased goods, Material return rate, and Supplier’s on-time delivery have been selected as new measures of the external business process perspective.

Product lifecycle management Recent PLM solutions are very diverse and largely immature. Burkett et al. (2002)

confined the scope of PLM to the processes most relevant to new product development. According to Burkett et al., the functional requirements of PLM contain the five major process flows: Product Portfolio Management, Customer Needs Management, Direct Materials Sourcing, Product Data Management, and Collaborative Product Design. From this definition, we derive the unique goal of PLM as increasing the speed and agility of product development while reducing risk. Therefore, the New product time to market is selected as the unique measure for PLM.

Since many of the goals and measures from SCM solutions are not included in the measures of the BSC and SCM, we must include them in order to assess the impact of SCM solutions on SCM performance.

## Impact of product characteristics on the BSC

Different market situations, products, manufacturing strategies, and logistics strategies require different scorecards to reflect their particular mission and strategy. In this study, we focus on the impact of product type.

Fisher (1997) argued that each different product type requires a distinctly different supply chain. He classified products into two types: functional and innovative. Functional products have stable, predictable demand with a long lifecycle. They include products like groceries and gas. On the contrary, innovative products have an unpredictable demand with a short lifecycle because of their newness. Fashion and technology goods are typical innovative products. Because of their different natures, the supply chains of different product type require different performance measurements.

While conducting two case studies, one from each type of product, we noticed that perishable products require a different performance measure than simply the length of the product lifecycle. Therefore, in this study we adopt three factors to identify the characteristics of products: Product lifecycle (long or short), demand predictability (predictable or unpredictable), and perishability (perishable or non-perishable).

## Framework for developing the BSCS

To measure the performance of SCM, we adopt the BSC framework, which is well-recognized and has been used to evaluate the integrated domain of business and technology in many industries.

## Process of deriving the BSCS

Figure 1 shows the process of building the BSCS and the outcomes for each step. First, we extracted the 11 objectives and 29 measures from the corporate BSC as Kaplan & Norton (1996, 2000, 2004) have proposed. To augment the BSC with SCM, we reviewed the SCM performance studies and derived six objectives and 45 measures from the literature. For the literature survey, we have used 10 papers including Bower & Hout (1988), Lee & Billington (1992), Stewart (1995), Levy (1997), Beamon (1999), Lapide (2000), Brewer & Speh (2000), Gunasekaran et al. (2001), Morgan (2004), and SCC (2005). In all, 24 measures overlap with those of the BSC, and 21 new measures are added. In this step, we change the perspective called internal business process to business process to include both internal and external processes. Through this phase, we generate an SCMaugmented BSC.

In the final step, we analyzed the impact of product characteristics on the BSCS. Although many factors may affect the design of the BSCS, we focus on the product characteristics in this study. We consider three factors as characteristics of products – product lifecycle, demand predictability, and perishability, and find that the goals associated with these factors already exist in the measures derived in the earlier steps. However, the factors determine the relative importance of measures, as validated through the case study in the following section.

## Business process perspectives

The traditional BSC mainly covered the internal business process, but the external business process is very important in SCM (Morgan, 2004). So the BSCS extends the scope of measures accordingly and groups them into two: internal business process and external business process.

Internal business process perspective The internal business process starts with the receipt of a customer order and finishes with delivery of the product or service to the customer (Kaplan & Norton, 1996). We define five strategic objectives in terms of Manufacturing Process, Inventory Management, Delivery, Flexibility and New Product Development. Measurements of internal business processes have been developed extensively in the past. These measurements are likely to be included as critical performance measures in any organization’s SCM internal business perspectives. Seven measures are inherited from the BSC and reconfirmed by the SCM literature, and nine measures are newly added from the SCM literature as summarized in Table 4. The new features from SCM literature are Perished inventory, Obsolete inventory, Delivery flexibility, Truck cube unitization, Responsiveness to urgent order, Adherence to schedule, Forecast accuracy, Volume flexibility, and Mix flexibility. Material inventory, Material stock-out, Forecast accuracy and Adherence to schedule are related to CPFR and New product time to market is related to the PLM solution.

External business process perspective The increased outsourcing of manufacturing companies results in more focus on the external business process and many SCM solutions are developed to support this. Hence, the BSCS is expanded to include the external process with three objectives: Improve Sourcing Leadership, Improve Collaboration with Partners and Improve Purchase Order Transaction Efficiency. Thirteen measures are listed in Table 5.

Recently, many SCM solutions have focused on improving a firm’s sourcing leadership. SRM mainly targets improving Quality of purchased goods, Material return rate, and Supplier on-time delivery. Desktop purchasing endeavors to decrease Procurement administration cost and reverse auction is designed to decrease Price of purchased goods. To evaluate the effectiveness of reverse auction, we also need to measure Time for successful bids and Percentage of successful bids. Some of these measures are also identified from the SCM literature.

## Measures in learning and growth perspective

Kaplan & Norton (2004) identified three measures of intangible assets essential for implementing a strategy in the learning and growth perspective:

\- Human capital: the skills, talent, and knowledge that a company’s employees possess.

\- Information capital: the company’s databases, information systems, networks, and technology infrastructure.

\- Organizational capital: the company’s culture, its leadership, how aligned its people are with its strategic goals, and employees’ ability to share knowledge.

These intangible assets are the result of highly aggregated efforts, including the SCM activities. Nevertheless, they are not unique measures to SCM. Therefore, the BSCS merely inherits the measures Human capital, Information capital and Organizational capital from the BSC (Table 6).

## Effect of product characteristics on the BSCS

We have derived a common framework for the BSCS regardless of the company’s characteristics. Through the relationship rules between the BSCS measures and SCM solution activities, we can identify the importance of goals that are influenced by the SCM solutions. However, the importance of measures will also significantly depend upon product characteristics.

We adopt three factors to identify the characteristics of products: Product lifecycle (long or short), Demand predictability (predictable or unpredictable), and perishability (perishable or non-perishable). We derived these three factors from a comparative case study we developed that examined one company from the food manufacturing industry and a second company from cosmetics manufacturing. To analyze the effect of product characteristics based on these three factors, we postulate a set of propositions and validate them exploratively through the case study.

Products with a long lifecycle tend to have stable demand for a long period. However, their long and stable demand invites competition, which often leads to low profit margins (Fisher, 1997). The low profit margins make a company focus on maximizing production efficiency and minimizing inventory cost. In contrast, products with a short lifecycle can enable a company to achieve higher profit margins at a higher risk. Owing to the short lifecycle, sales in the early stage of the lifecycle are critical and companies must respond to customers quickly and minimize Obsolete inventory. We determine that the relative difference in each measure’s importance implies the effect of the product characteristics. Based on this rationale, we postulate two propositions on product lifecycle:

Proposition 1: Yield, Cost per unit produced, and Inventory carrying cost are relatively more important for long lifecycle products.

Proposition 2: Customer response time, setup/change-over cost, new product time-to-market, percentage of sales from new product, and obsolete inventory are relatively more important for short lifecycle products.

If the demand is unpredictable, the risk of shortage and excess supply increases. Thus, a company with products of unpredictable demand will be concerned more with forecast accuracy, finished goods inventory, finished goods stock-out, and volume flexibility. Mix flexibility between products will also be a major concern if the products are substitutable. On the other hand, if the demand is predictable, the company will be concerned less with the above issues, but must consider its order fill rate and on-time delivery. These goals are important to any company, but their relative importance will vary depending upon demand predictability. Based on this rationale, we derive two propositions:

Proposition 3: Order fill rate and On-time delivery are relatively more important for products with predictable demand.

Proposition 4: Forecast accuracy, finished goods inventory, finished goods stock-out, mix flexibility, volume flexibility, and supplier on-time delivery are relatively more important for products with unpredictable demand.

Perishable products like milk have a short use-by date. Thus companies must minimize the perished inventory. This leads to a proposition on perishability:

Proposition 5: Perished inventory is relatively more important for perishable products.

## Comparative case study

To validate the propositions on the effects of product characteristics, we conducted a comparative case study. Two companies were selected whose products are distinctive based on three factors. We call them F Company and I Company.

F Company is a leader in the food manufacturing industry in Korea. Its major products include bean curd, fresh vegetables, and frozen foods. Its products have a long lifecycle with predictable demand; the products’ use-by date is relatively very short. It has only about 50 first-tier suppliers, some of which, such as those selling vegetables, do not depend on second-tier suppliers. Other suppliers that produce finished and half-finished goods may have second and third-tier suppliers.

I Company is a leading company in cosmetics and healthcare products. In contrast to F Company, its products have a short lifecycle with unpredictable demand. It systemically sources from 250 first-tier suppliers, not including suppliers used for spot purchasing. Most of them have second- and third-tier suppliers. Compared to F Company, it has many suppliers because of its vast product variety and shorter life cycle.

According to the classification category by Fisher (1997), we analyzed two companies’ product characteristics. Table 7 shows each company’s product characteristics based on the categories. Each company has similar features to those Fisher suggested from each category. Therefore, the products of F Company are functional, while the products of I Company are innovative.

Since F Company’s products have long lifecycle with stable demand, F Company has a relatively smaller number of items with lower margins in comparison with those of I Company. The average margin of error and average forced end-of-season markdown as a percentage of the full price is higher for I Company because of its products’ short lifecycle with less predictable demand.

## Survey and data analysis

To survey perceptions about the importance of the measures in the propositions, we conducted a structured interview with a questionnaire. The questionnaire covered the full list of measures, although this paper describes only the measures associated with the product characteristics. We selected three SCM-related managers from each company. The three managers had work experience of more than 7 years and their divisions were manufacturing, logistics, and SCM planning, respectively. Since we believe that the opinion of experts is more meaningful, we invited only experts instead of asking too many regular employees. ## Conclusions

We have derived the BSCS by merging the measures from the corporate level BSC with measures derived from SCM literature and solutions. To derive the measures of the BSCS, we start with the 11 objectives and 29 measures in the BSC proposed by Kaplan & Norton (1996, 2000, 2004). We develop a total of 18 objectives and 55 measures for the BSCS by adding 21 measures from the SCM literature and five from the SCM solutions. The most significant changes occur in the business process perspective.

In the BSCS, the internal business process perspective from the BSC was renamed the business process perspective to include the external business process as well, since the recent SCM depends heavily on B2B integration. The external business process brings three new objectives: Sourcing Leadership, Collaboration, and Purchase Order Transaction. Since the meaning of operational efficiency in the BSC is too broad, it is split into four objectives: Manufacturing Process, Inventory Management, Logistics, and Flexibility. In all, 17 new measures derived from SCM literature are added to the internal and external business process, and five new measures derived from SCM solutions are related to the external business process. The measures of the performance of the demand chain with regard to customers are placed in the customer perspective, while the other internal processes and interactions with suppliers are placed in the business process perspective.

To study the impact of company characteristics on the measurement, we explored the impact of three aspects of products: product lifecycle, demand predictability, and perishability. We found that we do not need to add new measures because the measures in the BSCS already capture the goals the company characteristics require. However, company characteristics have an impact on the measures. To validate this assumption, we postulated five sets of propositions and conducted a comparative case study with two companies that have distinctive characteristics. The exploratory study supports our propositions strongly. However, we need to conduct the study with a larger number of companies to make this conclusion more general.

The results of this study have interesting implications for the design of SEM systems. By providing meta-data on the relationship between the BSCS in SEM with the business activities such as SCM, companies can identify the impact of the business activities on the BSCS. This feature will be useful in evaluating the impact of developing SCM solutions, and tracing the origin of contributors for the achieved measures. A rule-based system will enable this analysis.
