# Assessing the robustness of meta-analytic results in information systems: publication bias and outliers

Sven Kepes and Manoj A. Thomas

School of Business, Virginia Commonwealth University, Richmond, VA, USA

## ABSTRACT

Meta-analytic studies serve to generate cumulative knowledge and guide evidence-based practice. However, publication bias and outliers threaten the accuracy and robustness of metaanalytic results. Unfortunately, most meta-analytic studies in information systems (IS) research do not assess the presence of these phenomena. Furthermore, some methods commonly used for the detection of publication bias are now recognised as inappropriate. We conduct a comprehensive assessment of four previously published meta-analytic studies in IS. We use multiple methods to assess the efects of publication bias and outliers on the meta-analytic results. Our findings indicate that publication bias and/or outliers have afected the results of three of the four meta-analytic studies. Some methods indicate that select meta-analytic means were misestimated by potentially more than 100%. Our analyses ofer methodological exemplars that can be followed to assess the potential adverse efects of publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results, including their combined efects. We make additional contributions to scientific knowledge by evaluating the performance of diferent publication bias assessment methods used across scientific disciplines. In brief, we highlight the importance of a rigorous assessment of publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results to improve the trustworthiness of our cumulative knowledge and evidence-based practice.

Decades ago, the generation of cumulative knowledge was viewed as impossible in the social and organisational sciences (Schmidt, 1992). Meta-analysis, a method that summarises research literatures by empirically synthesising the efect sizes found in a particular research area, has changed this (DeGeest & Schmidt, 2010; Schmidt, 1992). Today, meta-analysis is the primary way of systematically summarising quantitative research findings, generating cumulative knowledge (Borenstein, Hedges, Higgins, & Rothstein, 2009; Kepes, McDaniel, Brannick, & Banks, 2013; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015) and guiding evidence-based practice (Banks & McDaniel, 2011; Kepes, Bennett, & McDaniel, 2014b; Rousseau & McCarthy, 2007). Testament to its importance is evident from the increase in the number of meta-analyses over the past years across many areas of science (e.g., Aguinis, Dalton, Bosco, Pierce, & Dalton, 2011; Aytug, Rothstein, Zhou, & Kern, 2012; Egger, Smith, & O’Rourke, 2001), including information systems (IS) (Hwang & Schmidt, 2011; King & Jun, 2005). Unfortunately, the accuracy and robustness of meta-analytic results has recently come under scrutiny (e.g., Banks, Kepes, & McDaniel, 2012b; Hartshorne & Schachner, 2012; Kepes & McDaniel, 2013;

Rothstein, Sutton, & Borenstein, 2005a). Although several factors and phenomena may afect meta-analytic results (Aguinis et al., 2011; Aytug et al., 2012; Geyskens, Krishnan, Steenkamp, & Cunha, 2009; Kepes et al., 2013), two have received considerable attention in recent years: publication bias and outliers (e.g., Ada, Sharman, & Balkundi, 2012; Kepes, Banks, McDaniel, & Whetzel, 2012).

Typically, meta-analytic studies summarise and synthesise the publically available quantitative literature on a relation of interest. Yet, scientific evidence suggests that the publically available literature may not be representative of the population of completed studies. This phenomenon is referred to as publication bias, which indicates a situation in which the available literature on a particular relation of interest is systematically diferent from the population of completed studies on that relation (Banks, Kepes, & McDaniel, 2015; Kepes et al., 2012). This bias typically occurs when small sample studies with statistically insignificant results on a relation of interest tend to be missing from the publically available literature, which, in turn, can lead to misestimated meta-analytic results. Recently, research in the social and behavioural sciences, including publications in Science, have started to examine the selective reporting of scientific findings (i.e., publication bias; e.g., Franco, Malhotra, & Simonovits, 2014; Mervis, 2014). As a result of this phenomenon and its potentially distorting efects, even the popular press has asked whether there “is something wrong with the scientific method” in the social sciences (Lehrer, 2010, p. 52; see also, e.g., Belluz, 2015; Yong, 2012).

In addition to publication bias, outliers, which can be defined as observations that “deviate markedly from other members of the sample in which it occurs” (Grubbs, 1969, p. 1), may also have a distorting efect on meta-analytic results (Ada et al., 2012; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015; Schmidt et al., 1993; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). Specifically, such data points tend to inflate the amount of residual heterogeneity in the meta-analytic distribution, leading to biased mean efect size estimates (Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). Because heterogeneity due to outliers can be mistakenly attributed to publication bias, a comprehensive assessment of the influence of publication bias should also include an assessment of outliers (Kepes, Bushman, & Anderson, 2017; Kepes & McDaniel, 2015). In other words, to obtain precise and robust estimates regarding the presence of publication bias, one should account for outliers when conducting publication bias analyses.

In our study, we build on prior IS research that has highlighted the importance of meta-analysis as a statistical technique and discuss diferent statistical approaches to improve the accuracy and robustness of our meta-analytic results (e.g., Ada et al., 2012; Hwang, 1996; Hwang & Schmidt, 2011; Sharma & Yetton, 2011). For example, Ada et al. (2012) as well as Sharma and Yetton (2011) noted the importance of outlier analyses. More generally, Hwang and Schmidt (2011) pointed out that new meta-analytic techniques should be applied to existing meta-analytic data-sets to provide an update of the literature. The reasons for this are obvious. Misleading meta-analytic results are problematic as they not only distort current scientific knowledge but also inhibit future scientific progress (Hwang, 1996; McDaniel, Rothstein, & Whetzel, 2006). Such erroneous results also widen the research-practice gap, negatively afecting evidence-based practice (Kepes et al., 2014b; Rousseau & McCarthy, 2007). The concern is so pivotal that disciplines in the medical sciences require the use of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of meta-analytic results (e.g., Higgins & Green, 2011). Sensitivity analyses involve comparing meta-analytic results using diferent techniques, assumptions, or decisions to determine the degree to which the results are influenced by publication bias, outliers or other phenomena (Greenhouse & Iyengar, 2009). To the extent that the results do not meaningfully change across analyses, they can be considered robust.

We acknowledge that factors other than outliers and publication bias may influence the accuracy and robustness of meta-analytic results. They range from decisions around the procedural steps in the meta-analytic review process to the methodological choices associated with the statistical analyses (e.g., Aytug et al., 2012; Geyskens et al., 2009; Kepes et al., 2013; Wanous, Sullivan, & Malinak, 1989). Examples of procedural decisions include determining inclusion criteria for studies (e.g., sample characteristics such as age and occupation), data extraction criteria (e.g., all data versus a subset of the data) and the coding for characteristics related to the reported efect sizes (e.g., research design-related features). Judgement calls related to the statistical analysis include the selection of a general meta-analytic approach (e.g., Hedges and Olkin or Hunter and Schmidt approach), the selection of the estimation model (e.g., fixed- or random-efects model), the potential use of psychometric corrections (e.g., no corrections or corrections for measurement error and/ or range restriction) and the use of sensitivity analyses (e.g., analyses for outliers, publication bias, missing data imputations; Kepes et al., 2013). In the Supplementary materials (Table A1), we summarise studies that have addressed some of the key procedural and methodological decisions that researchers should take into consideration to ensure the transparency of meta-analytic reviews as well as the reproducibility and accuracy of the reported results.

Our main objective in this paper is to assess whether publication bias is likely to be present in meta-analytic studies in IS, and whether this phenomenon and outliers have led to misleading meta-analytic results. We do so by reanalysing four published IS meta-analytic studies. To achieve our objective, we introduce and apply several publication bias assessment and outlier detection methods. To the best of our knowledge, IS meta-analytic studies have not addressed the phenomenon of publication bias in a comprehensive manner and, in particular, the combined influence of publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results. If we want our cumulative IS knowledge to be trustworthy and provide sound recommendations to practitioners, the efect of publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results should be assessed.

Given our objective, we focus on sensitivity analyses for publication bias and outliers, and not other issues. First, the procedural choices during the meta-analytic review process are under the control of the researchers conducting the meta-analysis. Given that we will be reanalysing four published IS meta-analytic studies, we have virtually no control over those decisions; the original meta-analysts made them. Second, efects on meta-analytic results due to many of the methodological choices (e.g., Hedges and Olkin vs. Hunter and Schmidt approach; fixed- vs. random-efects estimation model) have been answered conclusively (e.g., Kepes et al., 2013; Schulze, 2004). Third, and most importantly, sensitivity analyses, especially for publication bias, are today viewed as essential to any meta-analytic study as it presents “perhaps the greatest threat to the validity” of meta-analytic results (Rothstein et al., 2005a, p. 2) and the trustworthiness of our cumulative knowledge and evidence-based practice (Kepes et al., 2014b). That is partly why even the popular press has reported about publication bias and some of its potential causes (e.g., Lehrer, 2010). Because of this potentially very serious problem for the scientific community, professional organisations such as the American Psychological Association and the influential Cochrane Collaboration in the medical sciences recommend or require the empirical assessment of publication bias (American Psychological Association, 2008; Higgins & Green, 2011). Despite this, we found that the potential presence of this bias has mostly been ignored in the IS literature that we reviewed (see Table A2 and Figure A1 in the Supplementary materials).

The contribution of this research is two-fold. First, by conducting comprehensive sensitivity analyses, we examine the efects of publication bias and outliers, including their combined efects, on four meta-analytic studies in IS. Our reanalysis of four studies serves as an illustrative example to demonstrate why conducting a comprehensive sensitivity analysis is important, and to show how such an analysis is performed using up-to-date methods. Our study thus makes a contribution that goes beyond the field of IS. Second, by evaluating the performance of diferent publication bias assessment methods, we contribute to the wider body of scientific knowledge across scientific disciplines. As more meta-analytic studies are conducted, our study will enable researchers to be better informed about the selection, application, and performance of sensitivity analyses, especially publication bias and outlier analyses.

Before conducting our study, we provide brief background information on the history, impact, and likely direction of meta-analytic studies in IS. This serves to illustrate the importance of our study. Next, we describe the phenomena of publication bias and outliers, and their potential efects on meta-analytic results. We then describe the meta-analytic samples included in our study. Finally, we provide our results and discuss their implications.

## 1. Background

## 1.1. Meta-analysis in IS research

Just as in any maturing scientific field, primary studies in IS tend to report inconsistent and often conflicting results regarding the same variables and relations of interest (King & Jun, 2005). A meta-analysis helps to make sense of such results by summarising and synthesising efect sizes, which are statistics that measure the magnitude of a relation or phenomenon (e.g., correlations, mean diferences, odds ratios), from primary studies (Borenstein et al., 2009; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). Although meta-analysis as a statistical tool is underutilised in IS (Hwang, 1996; Palvia, En Mao, Salam, & Soliman, 2003), there have been important and influential meta-analytic studies. In what may have been the first use of meta-analytic techniques in an IS-related area, Tornatzky and Klein (1982) assessed the relation between innovation characteristics and innovation adoption implementation. Their study had a profound and lasting efect on the literature. More recent meta-analyses have used better statistical meta-analytical approaches and could become equally influential (e.g., Wu & Lederer, 2009).

As in other scientific disciplines, meta-analyses in IS have helped to assess the validity of important theories and phenomena. For instance, Wu and Lederer’s (2009) meta-analytic study has helped to better understand the validity of the technology acceptance model (TAM). Given that IS has matured to the stage of a reference discipline (Baskerville & Myers, 2002), the importance of meta-analysis in IS will only rise (King & Jun, 2005). Figure A2 in the Supplementary materials illustrates the increase in meta-analytic studies in IS over the past decades and we expect that this number will continue to rise. However, we also note that many literature areas in IS lack multiple meta-analyses (see Table A3 in the Supplementary materials). Because meta-analytic studies are today’s standard for cumulating knowledge (Schmidt, 1992), they are typically among the most highly cited studies in mature scientific disciplines (Aguinis et al., 2011). There is thus a need for more meta-analytic studies in IS to build cumulative knowledge and assess the robustness of their results and thus the trustworthiness of our cumulative knowledge.

## 1.2. Publication bias

Publication bias occurs when the publically available literature is not representative of all studies on a relation of interest (Banks et al., 2015; Kepes et al., 2012). This bias stems from the tendency to submit and/or publish studies with statistically significant results, rather than basing submission and publication decisions on the quality of the research (Greenwald, 1975; Orlitzky, 2012; Rosenthal, 1979). Unfortunately, evidence suggests that only between 18% (Aytug et al., 2012) and 31% (Banks et al., 2012b) of meta-analytic studies in the social sciences assess publication bias. Yet, the “true” number is likely to be substantially smaller in magnitude as these estimates include inappropriate publication bias assessment methods, such as the fail-safe N analysis (Banks et al., 2015; Becker, 2005). In fact, once inappropriate and limited methods are excluded, only around 4% of meta-analytic reviews in the social sciences assess publication bias (Banks et al., 2012b). This small number should be of concern to researchers as well as practitioners.

Evidence indicates that publication bias exists in literature areas across scientific disciplines, from the medical sciences (Dwan et al., 2008; Sutton, 2005) and engineering (Jørgensen, Dybå, Liestøl, & Sjøberg, 2016) to the social sciences (Banks et al., 2015), including psychol ogy (Benjamin, Kepes, & Bushman, in press; Ferguson & Brannick, 2012; Kepes et al., 2017), human resource management and organisational behaviour (Kepes, Banks, & Oh, 2014a), entrepreneurship (O’Boyle, Rutherford, & Banks, 2014), strategy (Harrison, Banks, Pollack, O’Boyle, & Short, 2017), marketing, (Eisend & Tarrahi, 2014), education (Banks, Kepes, & Banks, 2012a), economics (Doucouliagos & Stanley, 2009), sociology (Gerber & Malhotra, 2008b) and the political sciences (Gerber & Malhotra, 2008a). In the medical sciences, Chalmers (1990, p. 1406) concluded that publication bias exists in most published meta-analyses, and that distorted meta-analytic results lead, at the very least, to the “inappropriate deployment of limited resources; at worst it may have resulted in the continued use of a harmful policy” (see also Sutton, 2005). Given its relatively wide-spread existence across the social and medical sciences, it seems reasonable to assume that there is the potential that this bias also exists in some IS literature areas. However, even if this bias exists, its efect on meta-analytic results can be minimal or negligible (Kepes et al., 2012). Therefore, it should not only be important to assess the mere presence of publication bias in the IS literature but also whether, if present, it has noticeable adverse efects on published meta-analytic results.

## 1.3. Outliers

Outlier detection has a long history (Huber, 1980). In general, outliers in efect size data are data points that are inconsistent with the bulk of the data. Outliers in efect sizes may be due to random sampling error, particularly for efect sizes drawn from small samples. Outliers may also be due to errors in analysis or transcription, or they may reflect some unusual characteristic of the study. Furthermore, one might have an outlier with respect to sample size in the meta-analytic data-set. Sample sizes can be directly used as study weights or can influence precision-based weights through their estimation of population means and variances, and, as such, may have a substantial influence on meta-analytic results (e.g., Ada et al., 2012; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). Unfortunately, less than 3% of all meta-analyses report assessment of outlier analyses in the organisational sciences (Aguinis et al., 2011). Given that outliers can distort meta-analytic results (e.g., Ada et al., 2012; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), this miniscule number should be of concern.

Our review of meta-analytic studies in IS revealed that the published literature has yet to address publication bias and outliers in a comprehensive manner. Of the 49 meta-analytic studies published in IS between 1981 and 2012 that we reviewed, the vast majority did not conduct a comprehensive sensitivity analysis to assess the efects of publication bias and/or outliers on their results (see

Figure A1 in the Supplementary materials). As meta-analytic studies become more prevalent in IS, it is important that researchers, including editors and reviewers, are aware of these phenomena and understand how to assess their potential influence on meta-analytic results. Furthermore, some publication bias assessment methods may perform better or worse when outliers are present in a meta-analytic data-set (Kepes & McDaniel, 2015; Kepes et al., 2012; Terrin, Schmid, Lau, & Olkin, 2003; Vevea & Woods, 2005). We are unaware of research in IS or other areas in the social sciences that has examined this possibility. By assessing the combined influence of publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results, we triangulate meta-analytic results and evaluate their robustness. Triangulation refers to the use of “multiple reference points to locate an object’s exact position” (Jick, 1979, p. 602). In the social and organisational sciences, it refers to the use of diferent study designs, settings, samples and methods when assessing a relation of interest (Sackett & Larson, 1990). In a meta-analytic review, it typically denotes the use of multiple methodological approaches when estimating a mean efect (Kepes et al., 2012, 2014a). This allows for an assessment of the robustness of the meta-analytic mean estimate, where robustness denotes the degree to which results and conclusions of research studies remain stable when conditions of the data or the analysis change (Greenhouse & Iyengar, 2009).

## 2. Current study

We conducted a series of publication bias and outlier analyses on data from previously published meta-analytic studies in IS. We searched scholarly databases, including EBSCOHost’s Academic Search Complete and Business Source Complete, all databases within Proquest (e.g., ABI/INFORM Complete), and all databases within the American Psychological Association’s PsycNet (e.g., PsycINFO), for meta-analytic studies in IS using terms such as “meta-analysis”, “metaanalysis”, “meta analysis” in the title, abstract and keywords fields. This search yielded 49 actual meta-analytic studies (see the Supplementary materials for a description of the search process and Tables A2 and A3 for an overview of the 49 meta-analytic studies). To be included in our study, a meta-analytic study had to fulfil three criteria. First, it had to provide a clear description of the methods and meta-analytic distributions. Second, it had to include the raw data necessary for our reanalysis as well as several meta-analytic distributions with at least 10 primary samples (Kepes et al., 2012; Sterne et al., 2011). These two criteria were set so that we could perform our analyses and determine whether or not publication bias and/or outliers have afected previously published results (the majority of the identified IS meta-analytic studies did not include their data). Finally, to be somewhat illustrative of IS research topics, the included meta-analyses had to address diferent research questions within IS, come from distinct journals, and be published in diferent years. Of the identified 49 meta-analytic studies, we randomly selected studies until we identified four that fit our criteria. However, we want to emphasise that the meta-analytic studies in our analyses are unlikely to be representative of all such studies in IS. Still, they fulfilled our requirements and serve as illustrative examples of meta-analytic studies in IS.

The first meta-analytic study is from Lin, Standing, and Liu (2008); it aimed to identify social and task-related factors that influence the efectiveness of virtual teams. The authors did not examine the efects of publication bias or outliers on their results. The second meta-ana lytic data-set comes from Wu and Lederer (2009). Their meta-analysis examined the efect of environment-based voluntariness on the relations among TAM constructs. Although they excluded two primary studies from their final analyses (i.e., the two largest samples) and used the funnel plot and the fail-safe N to address issues related to publication bias, they did not conduct comprehensive publication bias or outlier analyses. The third meta-analysis is from Hwang (1998), who assessed the efect of task type on group support systems (GSS) efectiveness. This meta-analysis does not contain publication bias or outlier analyses.

The final meta-analytic study is from Rains (2005), who examined the aggregate impact of GSS use on member influence behaviours, and whether GSS use fosters more democratic group processes than face-to-face meetings. To account for the efects of potential outliers on the meta-analytic results, Rains excluded efect sizes from primary studies that were ±3.29 standard deviations from the average efect, or outside the 99.9% confidence interval. In addition, he used the fail-safe N analysis to determine the number of primary studies with an efect size of zero that must exist in order to nullify his obtained results.

## 2.1. Statistical approach and sensitivity analysis techniques

We followed the Hedges and Olkin (1985; Hedges & Vevea, 1998) meta-analytic approach, which allows for the implementation of comprehensive sensitivity analyses (Kepes et al., 2013). We used the R programing language and the metafor (Viechtbauer, 2010, 2015) and meta (Schwarzer, 2015) packages to conduct our anal yses. Following recommendations from prior research (e.g., Kepes & McDaniel, 2015; Kepes et al., 2012; Rothstein, Sutton, & Borenstein, 2005b; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), we used contour-enhanced funnel plots (Peters, Sutton, Jones, Abrams, & Rushton, 2008), trim and fill (Duval, 2005), cumulative meta-analysis (Kepes et al., 2012), selection models (Vevea & Woods, 2005), PET-PEESE (precision-efect test, precision efect estimate with standard error; Stanley & Doucouliagos,

2014) with a one-tailed conditional significance test, the one-sample removed analysis (Kepes et al., 2013), and Viechtbauer and Cheung’s (2010; Viechtbauer, 2015) outlier and influence diagnostics as part of our sensitivity analyses.

The fail-safe N was not used because it is a flawed method to assess publication bias and should be “abandoned in favour of other […] methods” (Becker, 2005, p. 111; see also, e.g., Banks et al., 2015; Becker, 1994; Evans, 1996; Iyengar & Greenhouse, 1988). Kepes et al. (2012) provide an excellent overview of most publication bias assessment methods (see also Banks et al., 2015; Rothstein et al., 2005b). A brief summary of the methods used is provided in the Supplementary materials (Table A4). We note that we follow recommendations for trim and fill (Kepes et al., 2012), cumulative meta-analysis (samples sorted by precision; Kepes et al., 2012), and selection models with moderate and severe assumptions of publication bias (Vevea & Woods, 2005). Aligned with prior research (Kepes et al., 2017; Stanley, Jarrell, & Doucouliagos, 2010), we also present the cumulative meta-analytic mean of the five studies with the largest sample sizes for each analysed distribution.

## 2.2. Analysis approach and decision rules

We first conducted a naïve meta-analysis and applied a comprehensive battery of publication bias analyses to each meta-analytic distribution. Next, aligned with prior recommendations, we assessed the potential presence of outliers using (Viechtbauer, 2015; Viechtbauer & Cheung’s 2010) multifaceted influence diagnostics. Because outliers can introduce heterogeneity to the meta-analytic distribution (Schmidt & Hunter, 2015), which can afect the results of our analyses (Peters, Sutton, Jones, Abrams, & Rushton, 2007; Terrin et al., 2003; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), we then deleted any identified outlier(s) from the meta-analytic distribution and reran all analyses. Thus, all meta-analytic and publication bias analyses were performed with and without identified outliers. We did this to determine the possible range of efect size estimates rather than relying on a single one (Kepes et al., 2012), which is aligned with the advantages of the triangulation approach (Orlitzky, 2012) and customer-centric science (Aguinis et al., 2010).

We used Kepes et al.’s (2012; see also Kepes & McDaniel, 2015) decision rules to assess the degree to which publication bias and/or outliers afect the meta-analytic mean estimate: a relative change of less than 20% in the mean estimate indicates that bias is “negligible”, a change between 20 and 40% can be interpreted as “moderate”, and a change of more than 40% should be considered “large” or “severe”. We only assess the robustness of the meta-analytic results in distributions of at least 10 samples $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , k \geq 1 0 )$ . Results from smaller meta-analytic distributions are questionable due to the lack of statistical power and second-order sampling error (Schmidt & Hunter, 2015; Sterne et al., 2011). Given that we follow the Hedges and Olkin (1985; Borenstein et al., 2009) meta-analytic approach, our results may difer slightly from meta-analyses following the psychometric meta-analytic approach (Schmidt & Hunter, 2015), and thus from the results reported in the original meta-analytic studies (for a discussion of the diferences and similarities between the two approaches see Kepes et al., 2013).

The cumulative point estimate of the five most precise samples (cumulative sample size $[ N _ { _ { c u m } } ] = 2 , 8 2 6 ,$ , cumulative number of samples $[ k _ { c u m } ] = 5 )$ is .16, drifts to .36 with the addition of the next five samples $( N _ { c u m } = 3 , 8 1 0 ; k _ { c u m } = 1 0 )$ , and to .53 once the remaining samples are added $( N _ { c u m } = 4 , 2 5 9 ; k _ { c u m } = 1 8 )$ The cumulative mean estimate of the five most precise samples (.16) is substantially smaller in magnitude than the naïve meta-analytic mean $( . 5 3 ; \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = - . 3 7 ; 7 0 \% )$ . We thus conclude that there is evidence consistent with an inference of noticeable, potentially “severe” publication bias.

Although the severe selection model supports the conclusion of a noticeable degree of publication bias (sm $, \bar { r } _ { o } = . 4 1 ; \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = - . 1 2 ; 2 3 \% )$ , the moderate selection model suggests that the efect of the bias is negligible $( \mathrm { s m } _ { \mathrm { m } } \bar { r } _ { o } = . 4 9 ; \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = - . 0 4 ; 8 \% )$ . Furthermore, the PET-PEESE analysis suggests that the naïve meta-analytic mean is underestimated instead of overestimated (PP $\bar { r } _ { o } = . 8 0 ; \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = . 2 7 ; 5 1 \% )$ . Taken together, our publication bias results are not consistent. Most results indicate that the naïve meta-analytic mean estimate is “moderately” to “severely” overestimated. However, the moderate selection model indicates that the naïve mean estimate is fairly robust and the PET-PEESE analysis suggests that it is actually underestimated. Consequently, we used Viechtbauer and Cheung’s (2010; Viechtbauer, 2015) procedure to empirically identify potential outliers, deleted them from the distribution, and re-ran all analyses. Taken together, we can conclude that the true mean for the CR-PF relation is likely to be noticeably smaller than originally estimated, and that publication bias had a distorting influence on the results from the full distribution (k = 18).

For the other meta-analytic distributions, our results tend to suggest that the respective naïve meta-analytic mean estimates are not necessarily robust to the influence of outliers, publication bias, or both (see Table 1). We can conclude that the naïve meta-analytic mean estimates regarding the efect of social and task-related factors on the efectiveness of virtual teams presented by Lin et al. (2008) are unlikely to be robust. Consequently, recommendations for future research and practice based on Lin et al.’s (2008) results may not be trustworthy. Most likely, the “true” mean efect size estimates are smaller in magnitude, likely to a “moderate” or even “severe” degree, than originally reported by Lin et al. (2008).

## 3.2. Wu and Lederer’s (2009) data on the technology acceptance model

Table 2 contains the results of our analyses of the Wu and Lederer (2009) data. In addition to performing our analyses on the complete data-set (before the removal of outliers) and after the removal of outliers identified by Viechtbauer and Cheung’s (2010; Viechtbauer, 2015) procedure, we also followed Wu and Lederer (2009) and analysed some of the distributions without the two largest samples. For the relation between usefulness and behavioural intention $\left( k = 5 2 \right)$ , our initial analyses suggest that the naïve meta-analytic mean estimate (.61) does not necessarily seem robust to most sensitivity analyses. First, the $Q$ statistic and the $I ^ { 2 }$ index suggest that the data are very heterogeneous, which explains the very wide 95% PI ([.33, .79]), and indicates that the meta-analytic mean estimates may not be very robust. In support of this notion, the trim and fill analysis showed that 26 imputed samples were necessary on the right side of the efect size distribution in order to achieve symmetry. The diference between the trim and fill adjusted estimate (.77) and the naïve mean estimate is fairly sub stantial $( \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = . 1 6 ; 2 6 \% )$ . This is inconsistent with an inference of the typical case of publication bias (in the typical case of publication bias, missing samples tend to be imputed in the area of statistical insignificance on the left-hand side of the funnel plot distribution; Kepes et al., 2012). However, the cumulative meta-analysis by precision supports this inference: the mean estimate of the five most precise samples is $. 7 0 \left( N _ { c u m } = 6 0 , 3 5 0 ; k _ { c u m } = 5 \right)$ yielding a .09 diference to the naïve meta-analytic mean of .61 $( \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = . 0 9 ; 1 5 \% )$ . The PET-PEESE analysis even indicates that the naïve mean estimate could be underestimated by almost a third $\left( \mathrm { P P } \hat { r } _ { o } = . 8 0 ; \Delta \hat { r } _ { o } = . 1 9 ; 3 1 \% \right)$ Together, these results suggest that the naïve meta-an alytic mean (.61) could be noticeably underestimated. By contrast, the results from the moderate and severe selection models (.60 and .60, respectively) suggest that the naïve meta-analytic mean is likely to be robust. As indicated above, these conflicting results could be partly driven by the large amount of heterogeneity in the data.

Not surprisingly, after the removal of the two largest samples, the degree of heterogeneity in the data decreased noticeably. The meta-analytic mean changed minimally (from .61 to .60). Also, the results of most publication bias analyses converged; they yielded very similar results. For instance, trim and fill and both selection models yielded mean estimates (.58, .60, .59, respectively) that are very close to the original naïve meta-analytic mean estimate of .61. However, the cumulative meta-analysis by precision suggested that the original mean estimate could be overestimated $( p r \bar { r } _ { o } = . 5 2 ;$ $\Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = - . 0 9 ; 1 5 \% )$ and the PET-PEESE indicated that it could be underestimated (PP $\bar { r } _ { o } = . 7 2 ; \Delta \bar { r } _ { o } = . 1 1 ; 1 8 \% $ Yet, these are just negligible diferences. In the final step, we used Viechtbauer and Cheung’s (2010; Viechtbauer, 2015) procedure to identify potential outliers. However, this procedure did not identify any such cases. Therefore, we can conclude that Wu and Lederer’s (2009) naïve mean estimate for the relation between usefulness and behavioural intention (k  =  52) is fairly robust to the influence of publication bias and outliers.

Our results for the other distributions are similar (see Table 2). Overall, we can thus conclude that Wu and Lederer’s (2009) data on the impact of environment-based voluntariness on TAM are unlikely to be noticeably afected by publication bias or outliers. Therefore, we can have confidence in Wu and Lederer’s meta-analytic results and their associated conclusions.

Hwang (1998) examined the efects of task type on the efectiveness of GSS. His data contain standardised mean diferences and Table 3 shows our results. ## 3.4. Rains (2005) data on the efect of GSS on member influence behaviours

Rains (2005) meta-analysis examined the aggregate impact of GSS use on member influence behaviours. Our results, shown in Table 4, indicate that the naïve meta-analytic standardised mean diferences were not robust, typically overestimated, for all distributions for which the meta-analytic sample size was large enough $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , k \geq 1 0 )$ . Both outliers and publication bias seem to have distorted the original naïve meta-analytic results. As an example, removing one outlier from the “production of unique ideas” distribution yielded relatively consistent results across most analyses. Specifically, the osr analysis indicates that outliers may have a substantial influence on the meta-analytic standardised mean diference of 1.10. More importantly, all publication bias assessment methods suggest that $\bar { \boldsymbol { d } } _ { o }$ is noticeably overestimated, albeit to varying degrees. After the deletion of one identified outlier, three publication bias assessment methods yield very similar adjusted mean estimates, .62 (t&f $\bar { \boldsymbol { d } } _ { o } )$ , .66 $( p r \bar { d } _ { o } )$ , and $. 6 9 \ ( \mathrm { s m } _ { \mathrm { s } } \ \bar { r } _ { o } )$ . In addition, the estimate of the moderate selection model is relatively close to these adjusted estimates. Only the PET-PEESE estimate did not converge with the results of the other publication bias assessment methods. We can thus conclude that publication bias and the one outlier had a sizable distorting influence on the naïve mean estimate of 1.10, and that this estimate is likely to be noticeably smaller in magnitude (e.g., it may be in the .60s rather than the originally estimated 1.10).

Our results for the other distributions are similar. Publication bias and outliers appear to have a noticeable efect on the originally estimated standardised mean diferences. Thus, our results question the accuracy and robustness of the originally reported meta-analytic findings. We therefore conclude that the mean estimate for decision shifts is non-robust and that additional research is needed to determine the aggregate impact of GSS on member decision shifts.

## 3.4. Summary of results

To summarise the degree of any observed bias and thus the robustness of the meta-analytic mean estimate (e.g., $\bar { r } _ { o } )$ , we followed the procedure outlined by Kepes and McDaniel (2015). We started by calculating the baseline range estimates (BREs) and maximum range estimates (MREs). The BRE was defined as the absolute diference between the naïve mean estimate $( \mathrm { e . g . , } \bar { r } _ { o } )$ the meta-analytic mean before the removal of outliers) and the mean estimate from any sensitivity analysis $( \mathrm { e . g . , } \bar { r } _ { o }$ after the removal of outliers as well as osr $\bar { r } _ { o } , \mathrm { t } \& \mathrm { f } \bar { r } _ { o } , \mathrm { s m } _ { \mathrm { m } } \bar { r } _ { o }$ , sm ${ } _ { s } \bar { r } _ { o } ,$ and ${ p r } \bar { r } _ { o }$ before and after the removal of outliers) that is farthest away from the naïve meta-analytic mean. We note that we excluded the results from PET-PEESE from these calculations because they did not converge well with the results of the other, more established methods. The lack of convergence could be due to the degree of heterogeneity in the data as PET-PEESE may yield incorrect estimates if the data are too heterogeneous (Moreno et al., 2009).

The MRE was defined as the absolute diference between the lowest and the highest value from any of the results of our sensitivity analyses. We then calculated the absolute and relative diferences for both range estimates. In calculating the relative diference of the range estimates, we used the potentially “best” mean estimate, the naïve meta-analytic mean $( \mathrm { e . g . , } \bar { r } _ { o }$ before the removal of outliers), as the base (i.e., as 100%). To infer the magnitude of the robustness of this estimate, we followed recommendations by Kepes et al. (2012) and Kepes and McDaniel (2015). Thus, non-robustness was denoted as “negligible” if the relative range (BRE or MRE) was less than 20%, “moderate” if it was between 20 and 40%, and “large” if it was greater than 40%. To assess the overall degree of non-robustness, we averaged the practical diferences from BRE and MRE.

We note that in the few instances where the severe selection model (sm ) provided nonsensical estimates, likely due to extreme variance estimates caused by heterogeneity in the respective distribution (Kepes et al., 2012; Vevea & Woods, 2005), the resulting range estimates may not be directly comparable to range estimates of distributions that did provide a such an estimate. Similarly, if a distribution after the removal of outliers contained less than 10 samples (i.e., it did not meet our threshold to conduct publication bias analyses), the range estimates were calculated based on less results when compared to distributions that met our threshold. Therefore, the resulting range estimates may not be directly comparable to range estimates of distributions that did allow us to conduct all sensitivity analyses after the removal of outliers.

As an example, for the relation between coordination and performance (CR-PF), the naïve meta-analytic mean estimate before the removal of outliers $( \bar { r } _ { o } = . 5 3 )$ was not robust to the influence of publication bias. The adjusted mean estimates for the five most precise samples before and after the removal of outliers (.16) are the lowest mean estimates and 70% smaller in magnitude than $\bar { r } _ { o } ,$ indicating that the naïve meta-analytic mean is likely an overestimate. All but one of our sensitivity analyses agree with this general conclusion. Only one of the osr $\bar { r } _ { o }$ estimates (.56) suggests that $\bar { r } _ { o }$ is underestimated by 6%. Therefore, the BRE is .37 or 70% while the MRE is .40 or 75%. Such range magnitudes are clearly “large” and, therefore, the meta-analytic mean estimate for the CR-PF relation is likely to be non-robust.

The results for the other distributions are similar. The adjusted mean estimates of all publication bias assessment methods tend to suggest that the naïve meta-analytic mean estimates appear noticeably overestimated (see Table 1). Furthermore, although outliers were detected in all but one of Lin et al.’s (2008) distributions, they typically did not have a noticeable adverse efect on the original naïve meta-analytic mean estimate. Therefore, we can conclude that the observed non-robustness is primarily due to publication bias and not outliers. Finally, our obtained results indicate that the result of the PET-PEESE analysis before the removal of the outlier were adversely afected by outlier induced heterogeneity (Moreno et al., 2009), giving credence to our decision to omit the PET-PEESE estimates from our range estimate calculations and conclusions regarding the robustness of the original meta-analytic results. In sum, our assessment of Lin et al.’s (2008) data indicates that their naïve meta-analytic mean estimates are not robust. The degree of non-robustness tends to be “moderate” to “large” and primarily due to publication bias (and not outliers). Based on the sum of evidence, we therefore conclude that additional research is required to identify the efects of social and task-related factors on the efectiveness of virtual teams.

Table 6 displays our conclusions regarding the robustness of Wu and Lederer’s (2009) meta-analytic results. As can be seen, for the relations involving usefulness, behavioural intention, and ease of use, the practical diferences for the BREs were “moderate” and for the MREs “large”. For the relations involving usefulness, usage and ease of use, the practical diferences were smaller in magnitude; typically “negligible” for both the BRE and the MRE. Hence, the naïve meta-analytic mean estimates for some relations appear to be non-robust, for other relations, the respective naïve meta-analytic mean estimate seems robust. However, it should be noted that the “highest value”, especially for relations involving usefulness, behavioural intention, and ease of use, comes typically from the trim and fill analysis before the removal of outliers. Once we removed the outlier(s) or otherwise influential data points (e.g., the two largest samples), the trim and fill results did not diverge much from the naïve meta-analytic mean (or the results from the other publication bias assessment methods). In fact, the practical diferences were “negligible” to “moderate” after the removal of outliers. Therefore, we can conclude that some naïve meta-analytic mean estimates from Wu and Lederer (2009) tend to be relatively robust.

Table 7 summarises the results of Hwang’s (1998) data. Of the 13 distributions analysed, the results indicate that the vast majority of the naïve meta-analytic mean estimates were greatly misestimated, typically overestimated. The BREs ranged from 26% to well over 100%, and the MREs ranged from 44% to well over 100%. Only two practical diferences for the BRE were “moderate” (i.e., quality [choice] and participation [negotiation]), the remaining ones were “large”. All the practical diferences for all MREs were “large.” These practical diferences suggest that Hwang’s (1998) naïve meta-analytic mean estimates are not robust. In addition, we can conclude that the degree of non-robustness tends to be “large” and attributable to the influence of publication bias and outliers.

Finally, our conclusions regarding the robustness of Rains’s (2005) meta-analytic results are summarised in Table 8. As with many of our reanalysed distributions, the practical diferences for BREs and MREs tend to be noticeable; typically “large” in magnitude. Hence, we can conclude that the naïve meta-analytic mean estimates of Rains’s (2005) data are likely to be non-robust, and that the observed non-robustness seems to be due to publication bias and outliers.

## 4. Discussion

Publication bias and outliers are serious threats to the accuracy and robustness of meta-analytic results (Ada et al., 2012; Banks et al., 2015; Kepes & McDaniel, 2013;

Kepes et al., 2017; Rothstein et al., 2005b; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010) and, consequently, the trustworthiness of our cumulative knowledge and evidence-based practice (Banks & McDaniel, 2011; Briner & Rousseau, 2011; Kepes et al., 2014b). Publication bias and outliers can thus adversely afect future scientific progress and widen the gap between research and practice. Unfortunately, the meta-analytic studies in IS that we reviewed have not conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses to assess the potential adverse efects of these phenomena.

The purpose of our study was to assess the possibility that publication bias and/or outliers have adversely afected the accuracy and robustness of meta-analytic results in IS using recommended techniques (e.g., Kepes et al., 2013; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). Following the triangulation approach (Jick, 1979; Orlitzky, 2012), we examined the potential influence of publication bias and/or outliers on the results of four previously published IS meta-analytic studies. A finding indicating that the results of the previously published meta-analytic results are robust and not afected by either phenomena increases our confidence in the initially published results. As Kerr (1975) put it so eloquently decades ago, we, the academic community, especially our journals, seem to be rewarding A (e.g., finding statistically significant results), while hoping for B (e.g., reporting robust, accurate trustworthy results).

## 4.1. Implications for research and practice

First and foremost, we recommend that all meta-analytic studies in IS conduct comprehensive sensitivity analyses. Specifically, given that our results indicated that outliers can have distorting efects not only on meta-analytic results but also on the results of publication bias analyses, we suggest that outlier and publication bias analyses be conducted. If we want the cumulative IS knowledge to be accurate and provide sound recommendations to practitioners, all meta-analyses should conduct sensitivity analyses to assess the potential influence of both publication bias and outliers on meta-analytic results.

Regarding the performance of the diferent publication bias assessment methods, our results suggest that selection models may perform better than any other method because outliers appear to have a less adverse efects on their performance (e.g., see our results for Wu & Lederer’s [2009] data in Table 2). This provides confirmatory evidence for Vevea and Woods (2005) assertion that selection models are relatively robust to heterogeneous influences. By contrast, trim and fill and cumulative meta-analysis by precision seem to be less robust to the influence of outliers, especially when the meta-analytic distribution is small. For trim and fill, our findings are aligned with prior suggestions (Kepes et al., 2012; Terrin et al., 2003). For cumulative meta-analysis by prevision, our observation seems counter to some prior suggestions (Kepes et al., 2012). Similarly, contrary to the results by Kepes and McDaniel (2015), for the data-sets we reanalysed, PET-PEESE appeared to perform noticeably worse than any other publication bias method. In addition, our results indicated that outliers may afect the performance of PET-PEESE more adversely than any other method. However, in the Rains’s (2005) data-set, the meta-analytic sample sizes were relatively small and all methods performed about equally, although the selection models may have performed slightly better than trim and fill and the cumulative meta-analysis by precision. In sum, there seem to be no consistent theme regarding the performance of the individual publication bias methods across meta-analytic data-sets.

We therefore caution against generalising from the results we obtained to other meta-analytic data-sets. As suggested previously (Kepes et al., 2012), simulation studies are needed to explore the conditions under which each method performs well. It is likely that each method’s performance is dependent upon factors such as k, N, I<sup>2</sup>, and the magnitude of the meta-analytic efect size. Until then, we echo previous advice and recommend the use of multiple publication bias assessment methods in assessing the robustness of naïve meta-analytic mean estimates (Banks et al., 2015; Kepes et al., 2012). This approach is consistent with the concept of triangulation (Jick, 1979; Orlitzky, 2012) and customer-centric reporting of scientific results (Aguinis et al., 2010).

Although methodological advances for the assessment of publication bias exist and should be used, there are ways to minimise the occurrence of this bias (for overviews, see Banks et al., 2015; Kepes et al., 2012; Sutton, 2009; Templier & Paré, 2015). For instance, the literature search process is often limited to a few electronic databases that typically contain only published journal articles. Consequently, relevant research from the grey literature (e.g., dissertations, conference presentations, technical reports, white papers; Rothstein & Hopewell, 2009) and unpublished data-sets tend to be excluded from many meta-analytic reviews. Such a situation increases the risk of publication bias (Kepes et al., 2012; Rothstein, 2012). To minimise this threat, Wu and Lederer (2009), for instance, included samples from the grey literature (e.g., doctoral dissertations) and posted inquiries for unpublished data on the AISWorld mailing list. We endorse this practice and recommend that researchers follow guidelines for a systematic and comprehensive search and review of the literature (for a detailed review, see Rothstein, 2012). Other recommendations to minimise the threat of publication bias include the establishment of research registries, the provision of supplementary information by publishers, the use of a two-stage review process, and better data sharing requirements (e.g., Banks et al., 2015; Kepes & McDaniel, 2013).

Finally, we would like to note that other scientific disciplines, such as medicine (Egger et al., 2001) and some areas in the social sciences (e.g., management, psychology; Aguinis et al., 2011), publish substantially more meta-analytic studies than IS. As a result, these scientific disciplines could have a better understanding of their cumulative knowledge. Although the number of meta-analytic studies in IS is increasing, we recommend that more such studies are conducted and published. As Table A3 in the Supplementary materials illustrates, there are several literature areas in IS that are devoid of any meta-analysis. Meta-analytic studies could help to better understand the validity of theories and phenomena in these areas.

Our search procedure of the IS meta-analytic literature yielded multiple articles that contained the term “meta-analysis” in their title, abstract, and/or keywords although the articles did not conduct an actual meta-analysis (e.g., Heine, Grover, & Malhotra, 2003; Palvia, Jain Palvia, & Whitworth, 2002). Meta-analysis is a clearly defined statistical technique (e.g., Borenstein et al., 2009; Hedges & Olkin, 1985; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015) and authors of articles that do not conduct such an analysis may want to refrain from using “meta-analysis”

or related terms in their title, abstract, and/or keywords. As the field of IS matures further, it becomes imperative that knowledge is easily identifiable, trustworthy and accessible to the academic and practitioner communities (Rousseau & McCarthy, 2007; Shapiro, Kirkman, & Courtney, 2007). Thus, we should make sure that terms we use to identify our journal articles are appropriate and accurate.

## 4.2. Limitations

Given our objectives, our study was limited to an assessment of the efects of publication bias and outliers on naïve meta-analytic mean estimates in four meta-analytic studies in IS. Certainly, other decisions and judg ment calls during the meta-analytic review process, be they procedural or methodological in nature, are likely to afect meta-analytic results as well (Aytug et al., 2012; Cooper, Hedges, & Valentine, 2009; Geyskens et al., 2009; Kepes et al., 2013; Wanous et al., 1989). Yet, we had no control over these decisions and judgment calls; the authors of the original meta-analytic data-sets made them. Importantly, due to the potential severity of the threat to meta-analytic results from publication bias (e.g., Kepes et al., 2012; Rothstein et al., 2005a) coupled with the fact that publication bias has not been comprehensively investigated in IS, we focused on this particular phenomenon in our study. Furthermore, because outliers can afect the accuracy of meta-analytic and publication bias results (e.g., Terrin et al., 2003; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010), we also assessed the potential influence of outliers. Still, other factors are likely to have adverse efects on meta-analytic results and we encourage future research to assess their influence in IS research.

With regard to our methodological approach, all sensitivity analyses have shortcomings and limitations, just as all statistical techniques. Some of the methods we used (e.g., trim and fill) rely on the degree of asymmetry in the funnel plot to detect publication bias. In addition to publication bias, funnel plot asymmetry can be caused by heterogeneous influences such as “true” diferences between small and large samples (i.e., the small sample bias), moderators, and outliers (Kepes et al., 2012; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). PET-PEESE is also dependent upon the funnel plot distribution and, as our results indicated, seems susceptible to heterogeneity as well (Moreno et al., 2009). To mitigate concerns related to these and related factors, we used contour-enhanced funnel plots to distinguish publication bias from other causes of funnel plot asymmetry, especially the small sample bias (Kepes et al., 2012; Peters et al., 2008). Furthermore, given the literature areas of the re-analysed meta-analytic data-sets, we echo Kepes and McDaniel’s (2015) argument regarding the very small likelihood that the observed bias is due to reasons other than the suppression of small efects sizes from the publically accessible literature (i.e., publication bias).

Regarding other causes of heterogeneity (e.g., moderators and outliers), we formed subgroup distributions based on the theoretically derived moderators by the original authors of the meta-analytic studies that we reanalysed. This allowed us to minimise moderator-induced heterogeneity to the best of our ability and to assess whether the influence of publication bias and/or outliers varied across sub-distributions. Moreover, by empirically identifying outliers and reanalysing the data after we removed them from each individual distribution, we ensured that heterogeneity due to outliers did not have an undue influence on our obtained results and conclusions. Therefore, we are confident that our publication bias assessment results are due to the suppression of empirical findings from the publically accessible literature and not due to heterogeneous influences such as outliers, moderators, and the small sample bias.

In addition, some of the methods we used (e.g., selection models) are less afected by heterogeneity (Vevea & Woods, 2005). Therefore, such methods are likely to provide accurate and robust results even in the presence of heterogeneity. Given that the results from these methods tended to converge with the results of the trim and fill and other publication bias analyses, especially after the removal of outliers, we feel confident in the accuracy and validity of our results and conclusions. Our approach is aligned with the idea of triangulation and may help social sciences to elevate their methodological rigour and accuracy by discontinuing reliance on a single point estimate and, instead, using a range of results to determine the verisimilitude of empirical results (Kepes et al., 2012; Orlitzky, 2012).

Finally, we would like to address the PET-PEESE method. Given that the results of this method did not converge well with the results of the other, more established methods, especially when the analysed distribution included outliers, its inclusion in our study may be viewed as a limitation. Although we agree that, in retrospect, the PET-PEESE method may not be appropriate for the data-sets we reanalysed, we view its inclusion as a strength, not as a limitation of our study. PET-PEESE is a relatively new method that has not been used with any frequency in the social sciences. We thus argue that it is informative to apply PET-PEESE to our data-sets in order to examine the extent to which its results converge with the results of the other, more established methods. Recently, Kepes and McDaniel (2015) included it in a reanalysis of data on the relation between conscientiousness and performance. Their PET-PEESE results tended to converge quite well with the results of the trim and fill method as well as selection models. Thus, they concluded that PET-PEESE performed reasonably well. However, Kepes and McDaniel (2015) also noted that future research should investigate the appropriateness of PET-PEESE for data-sets that include noticeable degrees of heterogeneity. Our findings emphasise Kepes and

McDaniel’s (2015) concerns. Therefore, we encourage future research to assess the performance of PET-PEESE in more detail.

In sum, each statistical technique, including any sensitivity analysis, has some limitations, which is why we used multiple methods with diferent underlying statistical assumptions, ran multiple analyses and sought convergence across methods. In addition, we minimised many of the potential shortcomings by, for instance, accounting for known sources of heterogeneity. Given that the results of most methods tended to converge quite well, with the exception of PET-PEESE, we are confident in the obtained results and our conclusions. Based on the theoretical and empirical evidence, the diferences in the originally obtained naïve meta-analytic mean estimates and the results of our sensitivity analyses are best attributed to publication bias and/or outliers. Most importantly, our objective was to assess the robustness of four previously published meta-analytic studies in IS. Thus, the reason for non-robust meta-analytic results should not be of primary concern. We conclude that many of the originally published meta-analytic results of the studies we reanalysed are likely to be non-robust. This can have adverse efects on future research, the trustworthiness of the cumulative IS knowledge, and evidence-based practice.

As indicated before, our study was limited in that we only conducted an electronic search of 137 journals (see Description A1 in the Supplementary materials). Because we did not conduct a corresponding manual search, it is likely that we missed some meta-analytic studies. However, it was deemed impractical to conduct a manual search of 137 journals. In addition, it is highly unlikely that this limitation introduced a systematic bias that would alter our results and the associated conclusions regarding the prevalence of publication bias and outlier assessments in IS.

Also, our analyses were limited to four published meta-analytic studies in IS. Given that most meta-analytic studies in IS do not include their data, a reanalysis of all (or most) published results is virtually impossible. This limited the number and literature areas of meta-analytic reviews where we could perform the required analyses. Furthermore, many of the meta-analytic distributions we reanalysed were relatively small in size. In fact, except for Wu and Lederer’s (2009) meta-analytic study, all other studies contained distributions with relatively small meta-analytic sample sizes. A small meta-analytic sample size can have an adverse efect on publication bias assessment methods. To safeguard against such concerns, we followed Sterne et al.’s (2011) suggestion and only analysed meta-analytic distributions with at least 10 samples. Nevertheless, caution must be exercised when interpreting results for which the analysed meta-analytic distributions are small. Such caution applies not only to the results of our publication bias assessment methods, but also to the results of the naïve meta-analysis (Kepes et al., 2014a; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). Because the meta-analytic data-sets we analysed were originally published with results stemming from distributions that were even smaller than our cut-of (k ≥ 10), they were judged to be suficiently large to perform such analyses by the authors of the original studies (and the journal in which the studies were published). Furthermore, using multiple methods to triangulate our results, we demonstrate the robustness of our findings by showing convergence in results across diferent methods (Kepes et al., 2012; Orlitzky, 2012).

## 5. Conclusion

Meta-analyses synthesise the results of individual research studies to generate cumulative knowledge (Borenstein et al., 2009; Schmidt & Hunter, 2015). They are thus important tools for the advancement of science and evidence-based practice (Briner & Rousseau, 2011; Kepes et al., 2014b; Le, Oh, Shafer, & Schmidt, 2007). Yet, publication bias and outliers can threaten the robustness of meta-analytic results and may harm the generation of trustworthy cumulative knowledge (Kepes et al., 2012, 2013; Rothstein et al., 2005a; Schmidt et al., 1993; Viechtbauer & Cheung, 2010). Using a comprehensive battery of sensitivity analyses, we assessed the efects of publication bias and outliers on four previously published meta-analytic studies in IS. Our results indicated that both phenomena were present in the reanalysed data-sets, although the degree of their distorting influence on meta-analytic results varied considerably. In cases where their distorting influence was moderate to severe, caution should be exercised with regard to the trustworthiness of past findings. To establish greater confidence in our cumulative knowledge, publication bias analyses should be performed in conjunction with an assessment of the efect of outliers on all meta-analytic results. Guidelines and standards established in medical sciences, management, and psychology should guide future meta-analytic studies in IS to ensure the trustworthiness of their results and the progress of our scientific knowledge. The guidance and illustrations we provided on how to conduct a comprehensive sensitivity analysis may not only assist researchers in the selection and application of publication bias assessment and outlier detection methods, but also help disseminate sound and trustworthy knowledge to researchers and practitioners.
