## a b s t r a c t

In response to the catastrophic outcome of the 2008–2009 Financial Crisis we report on a qualitative study of decision maker surprise in the banking industry. Banks use a remarkably sophisticated ensemble of information technologies for supporting their management control systems and enabling oversight by government regulators and industry watchdogs. Banks depend on a global network of data processing and information systems to provide their core banking services and to manage the complex financial and macroeconomic elements of their environment. They are also subject to federal and/or state oversight, which includes on-site examinations and quarterly financial data monitoring, to reaffirm their safety and soundness. Yet, the financial crisis caught them unawares. To get behind the headlines of the crisis, we opted to not study crisis decisions overtly, but rather to explore bankers' general ability to interpret the data that they were receiving via their information technologies and observations and to follow a number of crisis and non-crisis decisions that had surprise outcomes for them.

Our focus is on understanding the context, process and patterns of decisions that resulted in surprise outcomes for bankers. We interviewed 23 senior executives from banks in the southeast who recounted fifty-one post-decision surprises that had occurred between 2008 and 2010. From analyzing those interviews, we found that they attributed surprise outcomes of their decisions to specific behaviors, including their complacency and over-confidence, their over-trusting of others, their deviation from protocol, their habitual information reporting and decisioning efforts, and their deficient detection of warning signals. In addition they tended to rationalize surprise outcomes by diverting blame to others. Combined, these symptoms reveal a chronic organizational and cultural susceptibility for being surprised. It was evident from our analysis that the bankers exhibited a narrow focus of attention and a reduced sense of inquiry, refrained from calibrating their mindfulness with the complexity of their decisions, limited their sense of accountability and experienced rigidity in procedures from bank routines and information standardization. Together, these elements nurtured less-mindfu behavior and triggered surprise outcomes. Given the important role banks play in our financial world and the ineffectiveness of their elaborate information support systems for reliable management contro the results are disturbing.

## 1. Introduction

Managers do not deliberately set out to hamper firm success by accepting excessive risk or by making decisions with negative outcomes. Yet, the banking industry has recently been challenged by the consequences of just such actions. This is amply demonstrated by the way that the banking industry was caught unawares, ill-equipped and unprepared for the global 2008–2009 Financial Crisis. The gravity of the crisis is clear from the decline in banking industry earnings as reported by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (“FDIC”). Their data for 2006 showed banking industry net earnings to be \$145 billion, dropping to a dismal \$4.5 billion in 2009 with 32% of the 8025 U.S. banks reporting financial losses. These results are surprising because banks are considered to be operationally progressive, technologically advanced and managerially responsive to their operating environment.

## 1.1. Research question

Our study asks a deceptively simple question.

How do bankers experience the sensing of problems or opportunities, the inquiring about those problems or opportunities, and the making of decisions concerning them, that result in surprise outcomes?

We address this question to bankers primarily from leading community banks, which are categorized as having assets under \$1 billion and include the vast majority (90%) of commercial banks located in the USA. Our sample banks had an average \$350 million in assets, which is in line with the industry average.

## 1.2. Bank information and control systems

Even though our banks are small in asset size and perhaps less advanced in proprietary technologies than mega money-center banks such as Chase or Citi, when considered from a “core operating routines” perspective (e.g. deposit gathering, check clearing and branch servicing — what bankers would term the “fundamentals”), these regional community banks are technologically sophisticated and highly trusted organizations. The industry-wide adoption of information technologies<sup>2</sup> and advances in global data networks have been important enablers of bank core operating routines. In 2013 ACH electronic payments volume reached som \$39 trillion dollars, credit/debit card payment volume for Visa, MasterCard and American Express reached \$6 trillion dollars; and bank loans totaled \$8 trillion dollars attesting to the banks remarkably efficient computer based collection and payment systems.<sup>3</sup> In addition, ERP systems have been embraced by banks to replace piecemeal software. Overall, banks are characterized by exceptionally high volumes of transactions processed with minimal errors in a globally distributed network of shared functions with other banks. The magnitude and reliability of their information technology is undeniable and is taken for granted.

For strategic and operational decisions, the importance of fit between IT capability and organizational strategy is well accepted (Drnevich & Crosen, 2013; Hitt, Keats, & DeMarie, 1998; Sambamurthy, Bharadwaj, & Grover, 2003 and Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011) and is considered a vital building block for organization reliability (Butler & Gray, 2006). In this respect, banks have thoroughly embraced the IT/strategy connection. For example: there are comprehensive customer information systems (CIS and CRS) to monitor and analyze customer behaviors and needs (Chen & Popovich, 2003) and product systems that drive analytica modeling for tracking revenue, risk and usage patterns of nancials, loans, securities, hedging and collections to name just a few. Banks use complex mathematical models for measuring volatility of earnings, and also to conduct stress and scenario tests for detecting operating and portfolio risks. Banks also employ IT based value at risk (VaR) and capital at risk (CaR) measurement tools for identifying risk boundaries, as well as asset and liability management systems (ALM) to monitor and control earnings dynamics. An excellent overview of the breadth of IT tools used in bank management and control systems is provided by Wernz (2014).

The management information and control systems of banks are an integral part of their key executive committees which engage human expertise in a systematic, periodic analysis of key information to monitor bank performance in its dynamically changing environments. Two of the major key executive committees found in all banks are:

1) Asset/Liability Management Committee (ALCO). This is a strategy-level committee for enhancing the market value of the bank. It seeks the optimal structure of the balance sheet and the bank's net interest margin (return on earning assets) by developing the bank's borrowing, lending and funds acquisition strategies (Gup, 2011). Members of a bank's ALCO are responsible for mitigating liquidity risk, interest rate volatility, and credit default risk as well as for advising shifts in deposit and loan maturity term structures.

2) The Credit Management Committee. This committee approves loans, sets credit underwriting policies and practices and evaluates the quality of the bank's loan portfolios, making adjustments as needed (Gup, 2011).

With these and other management control systems described below, banks have a full arsenal of practices, structures and oversight processing mechanisms that utilize sophisticated information technologies to mon itor and evaluate performance and provide business intelligence. In this way, a bank's information systems are intentionally designed to enable a heightened awareness of the ongoing status of the bank.

Like any significant commercial enterprise, banks have the additional internal source of control and gov ernance provided by a board of directors, with an audit committee and operating review committee. Beyond the board, there are additional institutionalized layers of managerial oversight, including the Federal and State banking agencies that are in place to ensure the on-going confidence in and stability of the financial system<sup>4</sup> itself. This is accomplished through on-site bank examinations and the monitoring of a bank's financial condition and a hands-on review of loan portfolios to ensure accurate risk levels are being reported. Each quarte banks must provide detailed financial reports to regulators. This UBPR (uniform bank performance report) is readily available on-line (fdic.gov) for access by all banks. This enables each bank to compare key financial statistics with peer institutions and allows for a basis of comparison to other bank's best practices. Government oversight proposes standards for and offers guidance on so-called “best-practices" in order to minimize sys: temic risk and to establish a “level playing eld” (Geithner, 2014). This is part of what Meyer and Rowen (1977) characterized as institutional structures enacted as a public display of acceptability. If a bank deviates from standard behavior they can become less legitimate than banks which more closely mimic the behaviors of peer institutions (Deephouse, 1996). Thus, bank legitimacy is constructed by government regulators and public opinion, with the intended purpose of encouraging banks to act prudently.

Despite all the sophisticated operational capabilities, information technologies, management control tools, and regulatory oversight, the recent financial crisis revealed the fragility of the banking system and underscored banks' false display of security. In part, as our data will show, the crisis was driven by a “never going to end” success mentality, but the real story goes much deeper than that, and concerns the ability of bank management to make good use of data from their information systems to sense emerging problems and take action to avoid or mitigate their effect. Although routines for collecting and analyzing data are helpful when they provide awareness of issues and options for responding to them. they are detrimental when they hinder detection of changes affecting their task performance or environment (Butler & Gray, 2006:214). It is the reduced detection capability role of our banks' socio-technical IT systems and banker's decision-making weakness that are revealed in this study.

## 1.3. Motivation, structure and early realizations of our study

The dismal record of banks in the financial crisis sparked our interest in conducting this study, but we did not want to do a retrospective study of the crisis itself.⁵ We were concerned that if we proceeded along that line of inquiry it would raise banker suspicion, prompt a defensive stance and encourage subjects to repeat well-rehearsed rationalizations. Therefore, we decided to focus on gaining insight into the adequacy of these banker's ability to conduct inquiry on changing conditions and make appropriate decisions without focusing on the scal crisis, per se. Accordingly, we conducted a qualitative study of top-level banking executives' experiences in making decisions that yielded surprise outcomes. By surprise outcome, we mean that results differed from expectations, with the degree of surprise as the difference between outcome and expectation (Harrison & March, 1984). Because it is the occurrence of the unforeseen, a surprise “is what contradicts and ruins expectation in its very structure” (Dastur, 2000:185). Surprise can be either positive or negative, but generally organizations treat surprise as an unwelcomed event that represents a “disconfirmed expectation” (e.g., Harrison & March, 1984; Meyer, Reisenzein, & Schutzwold, 1997; Ortony & Partridge, 1987; Teigen & Keren, 2002, 2003) or, as Cunha and colleagues suggest, management as being “taken unawares” (Cunha, Clegg, & Kamoche, 2006: 318).

We present our research method in detail later, but in overview, we interviewed 23 senior executives from 23 regional community banks, with average assets of \$350 million, which corresponds to the national average. Using a semi-structured interview guide we asked them to recall decisions they had made in the recent past that had fallen short of expectations, and to describe the process from beginning to end. The intent was to develop an empirical understanding of the context and process of decisions that resulted in surprise outcomes for them, in order to give us insights into their problem sensing and decision making behaviors during the period of the financial crisis.

Initially we anticipated that the surprise decision outcomes they reported would be shaped primarily by external forces beyond the banker's control, such as external competitive constraints, or adverse economic conditions or distractions from internal factors such as implementation difficulties or from the demands of other higher priority projects. However, we were astounded at what the interviews revealed. The decisions that resulted in surprise were not complex or esoteric. It seemed to us that anyone with minimal experience in business and with an everyday knowledge of bank's operating environment could frame the “problem” they reported in the situation they faced far more accurately and usefully than they did. It became evident that surprise outcomes were not the result of “mistakes” in making their decision. There was a fundamentally defective process of inquiry about the situation they were in and the choices to be made that was creating surprise.

By applying the constant comparative analysis technique of grounded theory to the bankers' interview transcripts as we conducted them, we saw early on that the post-decision surprise was the result of operating in a less than mindful manner. It was the bankers' way of knowing — the way that they understood the situation they were in and framed the decision to be made that led to decisions with surprise consequences. Within their framing of the situation, they may have acted sensibly. But their framing did not reflect an adequate inquiry into the nature of the situation they were in. The framing of the situations that they faced was conventional, habitual and taken for granted. Using Ellen Langer's term, they displayed a kind of mindlessness in that they were “oblivious to alternate ways of knowing” (Langer, 2000:220) and thus chronically suscepti ble to being surprised. We found that the top bank executives that we interviewed did not use their information systems capabilities to the extent necessary for developing a deeper understanding of their decision domain. Instead. they were inattentive and remained unaware of key elements of the situation that would influence decision-outcomes and reduce their organization's reliability. Their reports of experiencing surprise indicated their need for an “unfreezing” of their ingrained thinking (Bazerman & Moore, 2009) and also suggested that the very information technologies and management control systems they had adopted were adding to their apparent mindlessness

The rest of our paper is organized into three main sections. First, we review the existing research on the relationship between information and strategy, between decision-making and surprise, between mindfulness and mindlessness, and between routine-based reliability and institutionally driven unreliability. Second, we present our interview-based qualitative study of bank executives' post-decision surprises and discuss the key findings, including the role of individual and collective mindlessness on bankers' problem sensing and decision making. Finally, we draw conclusions and provide guidance for minimizing the types of surprise occurrences these bankers' reported.

## 2. Literature review and theoretical framework

## 2.1. The relationship between information systems and organization strategy

The financial services industry relies heavily on relevant information to compete and thrive in the highly dynamic U.S. economy. Flexible access to timely and accurate financial and market information, as provided by ‘data warehousing’ or more recently, ‘big data’, enables management to make better-informed business decisions and to repeatedly adapt and re-align competitive strategies to meet emerging customer expectations, enable multi-delivery channel options, or improve service levels and back-of ce operations. In other words information technology (IT) is the key mechanism through which banks create and capture value (Croteau & Bergeron, 2001). It is also integral to a bank's strategy-making process (Drnevich & Crosen, 2013; Melville, Gurbaxani, & Kraemer, 2004) because it can provide high reliability collection and analysis of data for business intelligence. The ability to make fast decisions (Eisenhardt, 1989) to “detect and respond to opportunities and threats with ease, speed, and dexterity is a key business imperative” (Tallon & Pinsonneault, 2011:464). This need for ‘corporate agility’ or the ability to cope and thrive in dynamic environments is generally enabled by information systems' capabilities (Galliers, 2007; Hitt et al., 1998) including their information quality and system design as well as competent, sophisticated use by managers.

However, researchers have also noted that IT may hinder and sometimes even impede organizational agility (Overby, Bharadwaj, & Sambamurthy, 2006; Weill, Subramani, & Broadbent, 2002). For example, the organizational setting can create collective “blind-spots” to problems (Wissema, 2002; Zajac & Bazerman, 1991) This is exemplified by JP Morgan's London Whale surprise in 2013 and their subsequent announcement to spend \$1 billion on improving internal controls (Bloomberg.com/London Whale). Moreover, facing increasing amounts of data, it can become difficult to detect relevant facts and it can even carry the risk of omitting crucial facts needed for decision making, thereby limiting managers' awareness of the discriminatory detai (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001). This is especially true as individuals and organizations are significantly less likely to detect stimuli for which they are unprepared. (Bazerman & Watkins 2008:161) noted that organizations often possess all the information they need to recognize emerging problems, but fail to “connect the dots”.

Banks are heavily regulated and are controlled by information and control systems designed to promote error-free transaction activity, and to produce the information required to enable organizational strategy and change. It follows that having a high degree of corporate agility, aligning strategy with flexible IT, should help managers to make preemptive decisions and experience fewer surprises.

## 2.2. Decision-making and surprise

It is well accepted that important decisions require a careful inquiry process (Cray, Mallory, Butler, Hickson, & Wilson, 1991; Mintzberg, Raisinghani, & Theoret, 1976; Hammond, Keeney, & Raiffa, 1998;), which should include all major factors that can influence outcomes (Bazerman & Moore, 2009; Roese,

1999; Simon, 1991) and pave the way for implementation. Sound decision making requires relevant information, innovative thinking and cognitive skills of the decision maker (Nutt, 1989).

There is no simple prescriptive model for achieving success with decision-making (Brownstein, 2003). This is often attributed to the growing complexities associated with many decision problems (Cooksey, 2000; Einhorn & Hogarth, 1986; Kahneman & Tversky, 1974, 1979; Lindblom, 1959; Russo & Schoemaker, 1989; Simon, 1979) to the biases of the decision-maker (Bazerman & Moore, 2009) and to the difficulties of defining future preferences (Luce & Raiffa, 1957; Nutt, 2001). In spite of these obstacles, a willingness to move forward with a decision emerges in the organization based on the expectation of achieving success.

Many organizations enjoy success and their decisions meet expectations. But not all decisions turn out as anticipated. In an extended series of studies, Nutt (1999) showed that failed decisions are far more prevalent than we realize and despite the importance of their occurrence (Cunha et al., 2006), our knowledge is rather limited when it comes to understanding the sources and significance of them. To date, attention to how managerial behaviors result in surprise has been minimal. The focus of the research has instead been on surprise prevention (LaPorte, 1996; Weick & Roberts, 1993; Weick, Sutcliffe, & Obstfeld, 1999), surprise response (Bechky & Okhuysen, 2011), weak signal response (Ansoff, 1975; Haeckel, 2004; Roux- Dufort, 2009), and decision maker affect, (e.g., Cozier, 1981; Harrison, 1992; Hartley, 2011; and Huxham & Dando, 1981; Mintzberg et al., 1976; Nutt, 1989; Rodrigues & Hickson, 1995; Trull, 1966, and Whyte, 1991). Other lines of research have addressed surprise detection (Lampel & Shapira, 2001), surprise avoidance (Watkins & Bazerman, 2003) and surprise predictability (Bazerman & Watkins. 2008). Surprise has also been studied in relation to learning (Adler, 2008; Sitkin, 1992), creativity (Filipowicz, 2006) and judgment (McGuire, McGuire, & Keane, 2011). Some scholars have discussed the sources of surprise, and attributed it to internal operating complexity (Roux- Dufort, 2009) or external market volatility (Daft & Weick, 1984; Mitroff & Kilmann, 1984; Pauchant & Mitroff, 1992). Despite this range of valuable analysis and understanding, surprise remains elusive and ambiguous for scholars and managers alike. This is in part because it is a topic that is uncomfortable — a reflection of managerial mistakes, ill-advised decisions (Nutt, 2002) or slowness in recognizing emerging problems (Hartley, 2011).

When a high degree of con dence is built into a pre-decision prediction and the resultant outcome contradicts those expectations, the magnitude of the surprise will likely be greater, especially if the outcome has signi cant negative consequences. In other words, if we can operationalize unexpectedness in terms of subjective probability, we can link the surprise outcome to low probability events (McGuire et al., 2011:184). Conversely, when a high degree of uncertainty exists about the anticipated outcome of a decision, due to limited information, incomplete analysis or substantial risk, unexpected outcomes are not necessarily considered surprising, but rather, may simply be considered as a disappointment. Surprise therefore is relative to one's expectations, and its feedback mechanisms.

Feedback depends on recognition: meaning that management will see what they want to see and won't see what they don't (Maturana & Varela, 1992). Feedback also depends on significance: is management action required or not for resolving the surprise outcome and moving the organization forward? If the surprise is more of an aggravation than a mandate for correction, ignoring the surprise outcome is often considered practical. To an extent surprise depends on the knowledge context in which the initial decision was made (Adler, 2008). Management, by attributing the decision surprise to an external factor can justify failed outcomes and avoid blame (Elster, 1999; Gilbert, Morewedge, Risen, & Wilson, 2004). Furthermore, because individuals have a tendency to seek confirmatory information for what they think is true, and to avoid searching for alternative or dis-confirmatory evidence (Bazerman & Moore, 2009:41) it is no wonder surprises persists.

## 2.3. Individual mindfulness

Information absorption is central to decision-making, so we include a brief review of Ellen Langer's work on individual “mindfulness" (1989a and 1989b). She defined mindfulness as a state of alertness, with active awareness and open information processing, characterized by a “continuous creation of new categories” and distinctions and an “implicit awareness of multiple perspectives”. Mindfulness, then, is a flexible cognitive state of inquiry in which we are actively engaged in seeking greater awareness of the present moment (Kashdan et al., 2009), noticing new things and being sensitive to context (Langer, 2000). Being mindful allows one to notice differences, nuances, discrepancies, and outliers that would otherwise pass unnoticed (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2006). It follows that being more mindful should lead managers to make decisions that have fewer surprises.

## 2.4 . Organizational mindfulness

Langer's mindfulness concept was raised to the organizational level by Weick and Roberts (1993), Weick et al. (1999) and Weick and Sutcliffe (2001, 2007). The later authors observed the prevalence of mindfulness among members of high reliability organizations (HROs), and argued that the practices of HROs are transferrable to mainstream organizations. The literature on mindfulness in organizations is accumulating. Ray, Baker, and Plowman, (2011) extended its value (Vogus & Sutcliffe, 2012) and also confirmed that the five factor cognitive process proposed by Weick and Sutcliffe (2001) was sound. The five cognitive processes that constitute and sustain collective mindfulness are as follows:

▪ Anticipation (describes how to detect errors before their actual occurrence)

1. preoccupation with failure (rather than successes)

2. reluctance to simplify interpretations (maintaining divergent viewpoints)

3. sensitivity to operations (seeing the larger context)

▪ Containment (how the organization responds to an error after it has occurred)

1. commitment to resilience (ability to bounce back from the unexpected)

2. deference to expertise (fluid location of decision making)

Mindfulness therefore is not as much about what firms' know as about how they interrogate their situation and how managers strive to know what they don't know (Roberts & Bea, 2001).

## 2.5. Organizational mindlessness

It follows that organizations are likely to be operating in a “mindless” state when they regularly experience decision surprise. This regularity indicates that decision-makers might be stuck in a single, rigid perspective with behavior dominated by distinctions drawn from the past. They are not actively engaged in the present, not noticing new things, and not being sensitive to context (Gersick & Hackman, 1990; Langer, 2000; Weick, 1993). Instead, they are on “auto pilot” (Langer, 1997: 4) and follow predetermined mental pathways without challenge, curiosity or conscious effort (Chanowitz & Langer, 1981). Thus, mindless behavior can complicate the search for workable solutions, especially when key assumptions are taken for granted and are based on habit and convenience (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991).

Although researchers sometimes characterize mindful and mindless as a dualism and managers as being one or the other, with positive outcomes attributed to the former and negative results to the latter (e.g., Swanson & Ramiller, 2004; Vogus & Welbourne, 2003), in practice they form a duality (Giddens, 1984) with both being evident in most situations.. Rerup (2004) has noted that all organizations occasionally operate with less-than-mindful behavior, and that doing so usually has no adverse results. As such, being less mindful should not be thought of as necessarily detrimental (Bargh & Chartrand, 1999; March, 1994; Bigley and Roberts 2001). Carlo, Lyytinen, and Boland (2012: 1105) suggest “collective mindfulness is only possible through a dialectic process of collective minding, in which organizational actors simultaneously exhibit elements of being both mindful and mindless emerging in a process of becoming as a continuous struggle with contradictions of organizing”. We might expect decision outcomes to be particularly sensitive to decision complexity and to the appropriate level of available mindfulness. Understanding the need for harnessing more-mindful behavior or perhaps less-mindless behavior, when called for, is an essential ingredient for sur prise avoidance.

Underlying less-mindful behavior is the notion that the decision process, even though it may be complex, is likely to be routine-based and replicated from previous or similar successful responses and thus not requiring original or innovative thought. Langer (2000:220) has proposed that “when we are mindless, our behavior is rule and routine governed, when we are mindful, rules and routines may guide our behavior rather than predetermine it”. If decision outcomes surprise management, it is reasonable to speculate that a “more-mindful” decision process was needed

## 2.6. Rigidity of organizational routines

Routines are not fixed. stable obiects but instead exhibit dynamic qualities (Feldman & Pentland. 2003: Pentland & Feldman, 2005) and can be considered to be emergent structures (Giddens, 1984) evolving to appropriately address opportunities (Levitt & March, 1988) and hopefully avoid surprises. However, even the best of routines can sometimes fall awry. For instance, if the existing routines are institutionalized and overly habitual, they can become a source of rigidity and inflexibility (Adler, Goldoftas, & Levine, 1999). As in Giddens' notion of structuration (Giddens, 1984), routines shape peoples' practices, but at the same time, peoples' practices produce and reproduce routines. Hence, routines should be constantly modified as conditions change (Feldman, 2000). Behaviors that are based on habit and convenience can inhibit the search for efficient solutions (DiMaggio & Powell, 1991) becoming a source of surprise. Turner (1976: 388) suggests that a lack of foresight and “rigidity in perception and belief” are major causes of surprise. Rijpma (2003: 40) describes this as the “belief that things won't go wrong.” This is exemplified by the sub-prime led housing bubble, when financial institutions behaved as if it was “business as usual” and fueled the 2008 financial crisis bank failures.

## 3. Research methods

## 3.1. Qualitative methodology

A qualitative approach fit our research objective, since we were in search of first person accounts of surprise outcomes and the nuances of their managerial perception, inquiry and decision-making. We adopted a Grounded Theory approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) for data collection and analysis, using a semi-structured interview guide (see Appendix A), asking open-ended questions about concrete examples of surprises of their choosing, and encouraging respondents to narrate those lived experiences (Glaser & Strauss, 2008).

## 3.2. Sample

Twenty-three senior banking executives, each from a different community banking institution located in the southeast USA, participated in the study. All of the banks in the sample were identified from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation membership directory (www.FDIC.com) and on-line annual reports. The banks ranged in asset size from \$50 million to \$60 billion, with the average size bank having assets of approximately \$350 million, which is similar to the national average. These banks are operationally complex and are required to employ advanced management practices. Since respondents needed to hold key interpretational roles (Bennis & Nannus, 1985), we only interviewed senior personnel (Chairman, President, CEO, CFO or Senior Vice-president) of each bank. These managers operate at an organizational level where details of important surprise outcomes having corporate implications are channeled and readily known. Of the 23 executives interviewed twenty-two were male and one was female; all had lengthy careers re ecting thei experience and title.

## 3.3. Data collection

Each semi-structured interview took approximately ninety minutes to complete. Twenty were conducted in-person and three by phone. All interviews were recorded with consent of the interviewee and transcribed by a professional service. Following Corbin and Strauss (2008) the interviews focused on respondent's actua experiences. The questions prompted managers to share their specific recollection of decisions they had participated in that produced unanticipated results. Our goal was to channel their complete attention to a decision experience in a nonjudgmental and accepting way.

Interestingly, the interviewees did not initially recognize the category of “surprise” events — being surprised was not a customary part of their vocabulary. However, by using phrases like “a decision you participated in that did not have the expected outcome”, the conversation flowed nicely and we obtained reports of their experience of 51 important decisions with surprise outcomes. The surprises, included financial (19), human resource (15), product (5) and organizational (12) issues. Representative examples of these four types of surprise are shown in Appendix B.

## 3.4. Data analysis

Our analysis began with open-coding, a process of non-iudgmental reading of the transcript to identify textual fragments of potential significance. This initial coding process resulted in the identification of 1200 potentially important portions of text, which were categorized and labeled. In subsequent rounds of coding, cat egories were continually reexamined, refined and relabeled, in an effort to “encompass all nuances in the data” (Isabella, 1990: 13). This analysis was conducted with a constant comparative approach concurrent with conducting the interviews. In the second (axial) phase of coding, relationships noticed among categories and the constant comparison of them across interviews resulted in the emergence of themes and concepts that required a review of new, relevant literature. It was at this stage that the relevance of Langer's work on mindfulness and mindlessness began to emerge for us. During this second coding process, we reduced our focus to 250 codes. In the final (selective) phase of coding the focus was further refined, concentrating on 11 key themes from which the “accumulating interrelations formed an integrated theoretical framework” (Glaser & Strauss, 2008: 40). The data were systematically reanalyzed again with a constant comparative technique, to test our con dence in the credibility and plausibility of the themes and their synthesis into our three findings.

## 4. Findings

Our findings are grouped into two major categories of behaviors: 1) flawed key operating behaviors and 2) weak collective mindfulness behaviors. The two categories of behaviors are discussed separately below:

## 4.1. Finding 1: flawed key operating behaviors drive surprise outcomes

Banking executives attributed the surprise outcome of their decisions to specific behaviors, including their complacency and over-confidence, their over-trusting of others, their deviation from protocol, their habitual information reporting and decisioning efforts, and their deficient detection of warning signals. In addition they tended to rationalize surprise outcomes by diverting blame to others. Details follow:

## 4.1.1. Managerial complacency and over-confidence drives surprise outcomes

Eighteen of the twenty-three managers revealed that their flawed interpretations of the contexts surrounding a decision resulted in surprise (Table 1). They admitted, in retrospect, to overconfidence in their abilities to succeed. Remarks such as “things were good,” and “we thought the good times would never end” described how respondents interpreted the prevailing environment in which they made decisions. The interview data provided examples of managers living in the moment with solid expectations of achieve ment. As one bank president commented: “you get lax when things are good.”

## 4.1.2. Deviation from protocol increases the risk of unexpected results

Revelations by sixteen out of twenty-three executives suggest deviation from sound banking principles is linked to surprise outcomes (Table 2). Managers identified careless loan approval, restrained communication, chasing unprofitable growth, asset over-concentrations, and undisciplined due-diligence as typical practices resulting in failed deals, underperforming loans, and substantial bank losses. Neglecting protocol led one CEO to comment, “we call it violating the principles” and another banker attested that principles are “easy to deviate from when you're chasing earnings” while another echoed, “it's tough to have the discipline to maintain standards” when stretching for loan growth. A preoccupation with profits contributed to bankers ignoring the potential downside effects of their decisions.

## 4.1.3. Bankers' over-reliance on “trust” facilitates surprise outcomes

Nineteen out of 23 respondents admitted that unwarranted trust, described in retrospect as unsubstanti ated belief in another's integrity (honesty and truthfulness) and competency (knowledge and skills) precip itated surprise outcomes (Table 3). In defense of decisions that yielded negative outcomes, bankers explained, “…but…he had been at the bank since the 90s”, and “I like to give people the benefit of the doubt.”

## 4.1.4. Habitual information reporting and decisioning fosters surprise

Surprise outcomes were associated with an over-reliance on past experience and a dependence on organizational habits (we have “always done things a certain way”) by fifteen of our twenty- three respondents resulting in formulaic decisions insensitive to non-routine environmental conditions (Table 4). This was especially evident in regard to the financial crisis where important “managerial routines” such as ALCO and Credit and Management Committees appeared to be overly standardized and suffered from inflexibility in banker's mental models, which combined to produce mindless patterns of decision making (Feldman, 2004). This tendency toward information standardization and fixed perspectives lowers decision quality, especially with their mechanical risk management models (ALCO) which became important and distinctive drivers of bank failures (Honohan, 2008). An over-dependence on standard information systems without attempts to simulate the effect of their habitual decision making routines in the changing conditions they faced, was a major contributing factor to the banking crisis surprise. ## 4.2.1. Little evidence of preoccupation with failure

Weick et al. (1999) suggest that concern with potential failures (based on a belief that the system may be flawed) encourages mindfulness. Contrary to these expectations we found bankers were more preoccupied with success than worried about failure. For example, banks produced robust earnings during the run-up to the 2008–2009 Financial Crisis which our data suggests triggered a “never going to end” mentality that inhibited the detection of weak signals – or the denial of them if sensed. They reported near misses as successes – rationalizing outcomes as being “out of their control” and “not (really) mistakes.” They were not “preoccupied” with mistakes and instead recognizing them as sunk costs to be moved beyond, a normal condition of the industry.

## 4.2.2. Little reluctance to simplify

Our findings indicate that bankers' embraced rather than eschewed simplification, in effect lowering their interpretive competence. Managers reported relying on their management teams for exposure to divergent views and access to a broad set of assumptions. On its surface, this could be “mindful” behavior. ## 4.2.3. Low sensitivity to operations

High reliability organizations (HROs) adopt interpretive practices and pay close attention to detail. Instead, our bankers reported a tendency to explain variations as normal rather than as potential problems. As Weick and Sutcliffe (2001) suggests, they practiced a “normalization of abnormalities”. One CEO, for example, reported “looking at statistics, historically calculated but never tracked” and another admitted that “we didn't use to look (at reports) that closely.” In other words, our bankers failed to pay close attention to detail. For example, one banker observed “we did not make the condo-loans (now in foreclosure) but our customers obtained them from other banks and as a result, we are experiencing collateral damage.” The bank was deficient in maintaining current knowledge of their customers' overall creditworthiness and financia exposure. The old axiom of “know your customer” is an edict to understand the detail.

Our research also showed that some embedded routines and rules were changed (e.g. loan origination criteria and standards), producing desired and valuable short term outcomes that made sense to bankers at the time. Unfortunately, they produced negative consequences in the longer term. As one CEO commented, “we were driven by the frenzy of not wanting to miss the window” of opportunity.

## 4.2.4. Low commitment to resilience

“The signature of a high reliability organization (HRO) is not that it is error-free but that errors don't disable it.” (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001: 14). HROs show resilience by responding to surprises as they occur — they are able to bounce back through improvisation (Weick, 1998), to minimize escalating consequences, and to regain a dynamic stable state (Bigley & Roberts. 2001: Roberts. 1990: Weick et al., 1999). Our bankers instead made a “non-computational, routine-based response” (Perrow, 1970) as evidenced by some CEOs intentions to “earn their way through” their surprise situations, and their belief that “good performance will eventually return”. As another banker explained “weathering the storm is our way of retaining stability” reinforcing a business-as-usual response in lieu of some flexible strategic action, or as Levinthal and Rerup, (2006: 509) put it, using, “…novelty to respond to changing and possibly unique circumstances”. It is rare that unexpected events conform to preexisting solution sets (Weick & Roberts, 1993). In response to the Financial Crisis of 2008–2009, our bankers confessed to uncertainty (“I don't know what the future holds”), increasingly cautious behavior (“[we're] trying to be more conservative”) and recognition of the need to change (“we are going to pull ourselves together”). Taken as a whole, these responses become a mechanism for maintaining continuity of existing operating routines “to preserve existing accumulated experience” (Levinthal & Rerup, 2006: 509) and thereby promote a less-mindful perspective. It leads to inaction, and an inability to transform events into new experiences (Tsoukis, 1994) that could mitigate and rectify future unanticipated surprises.

## 4.2.5. Minimal deference to expertise

Deference to expertise means that decision-making migrates to individuals in the organization who have the greatest expertise and are closest to the problem (Rerup, 2004; Roberts & Libuser, 1993). Our data suggests that the formal hierarchical structures of banks inhibit the migration of decision-making based on expertise and proximity. It also suggests that in small to mid-sized banks, decision-making sits heavily on the shoulders of the CEO. So long as clear distinctions and habitual ways of acting were sufficient to meet the situation, there was minimal reflective thought on their parts because there was little need for it.

We conclude that bankers can readily recall vibrant examples of decisions they had made that resulted in surprise — yet rationalized them, in retrospect, as one-time unexpected events. Surprises were considered to “exist in the moment” and accorded no significance beyond having “happened”. Consequently, we found no evidence of accountability for their surprise results. Further, no evidence of institutionalization of learning from surprise or of Weick's five principles for collective mindfulness was observed.

## 5. Discussion

Our qualitative study set out to understand bank managers' experience of making decisions with surprise outcomes. Our findings revealed a deeply flawed process of collecting, analyzing and interpreting information that produced a chronic organizational and cultural susceptibility toward making decisions that resulted in surprises with negative outcomes.

Our analysis of the bankers' narratives of decision surprise contributes to a greater clarity and better understanding of post-decision ‘negative’ surprises in three ways. First, we highlight surprise as a predictable ‘business process’ that results from tolerating imperfections in decision making practices and subsequently impedes organizational performance. Second, we contribute to the literature on organization reliability and related collective and individual mindfulness constructs by demonstrating that a less-than-mindful behavioi exists in our community banks. Third, we identify some of the reasons behind the pervasiveness of surprise by revealing how surprises are managed in organizations. We showed the influence of several variables including: the organization's mindset (its lack of commitment to eliminate surprise); management's routinized decision behavior (their susceptibility to rigid inquiry from inappropriate standardization); the organization's information systems and learning capability (its inability to translate information into useful knowledge); management's predictive surprise capabilities (their inability to identify emerging threats); and management's outcome accountability (their inability to learn from mistakes). We next discuss each contribution in detail.

## 5.1. Surprise as a predictable business process

It is well documented that decisions are the result of a sequence of behaviors (Cyert & March, 1963). A negative surprise outcome therefore reflects some vulnerability in the overall decision process. Our study shed light on this vulnerability by showing how decisions relied heavily on highlysocialized, analytical traditions with limited concern for considering alternate problem formulations or action choices. Although our bankers professed the need for both sound analysis and reliable data, we found instead a more rule-based practice that they employed with conscious bias toward preconceived expectations, consistently rationalizing surprises as one time uncontrollable events and accorded them no significance beyond having happened. They were not seen as a predictable outcome of management's inadequate inquiry and closed minds. In addition, the interviews contained no indication of learning from surprises or of considering any of Weick's five principles of collective mindfulness. They may have intended their decisions to be rational, but their decisions were less than mindfully made and in that sense, surprise is a predictable outcome. Based on our findings, bankers' decision processes and the possibilities for surprise reduction are ripe for improvement.

## 5.2. Surprise as a measure of organization reliability

We adopted the 5-factor measure of organizational mindfulness for better understanding potential threats to organizational reliability because it is best used “where ugly surprises are most likely to show up” (Weick et al., 1999: 90). We conclude that deficiencies in anticipating and containing surprises reflected a low level of mindfulness being displayed by our bankers, exposed a built-in bias toward unreliability, and revealed a weakness in the organizational culture, in that average performance became an accepted norm, and rich discriminatory thinking and a capacity for action (Weick et al., 1999: 80; Ray et al., 2011: 199) was not encouraged. Our findings support the usefulness of organizational mindfulness construct as an indicator of firm reliability. We have shown that each of the five principles, if not closely attended to, can lead to failure in becoming a more mindful organization and can have serious negative consequences for obtaining improved performance.

Our study also adds meaning to the individual mindfulness construct by identifying the need for a relent less inquiry into the appropriate level of mindfulness to apply to the details in a given situation. Our bankers, in contrast, displayed a uniform, habitual way of attending to the making of their operating and review deci sions. Thus they “essentially make a commitment to a single way of understanding” (Langer, 2000: 220), which may occasion behavior mindless of relevant details (Langer. Blank, & Chanowitz. 1978: 641). thus pro moting mediocrity in decision-making. Without the active search for multiple viewpoints, causal understandings, and interpretations, the bankers' decision-making was less-than-mindful and produced undesirable outcomes. Adopting a more-mindful approach would require our bankers to consciously stretch beyond their current business practices. Perhaps Langer (2000: 200) declared it best: “one has to be mindful to know if they are mindless”.

Several streams of research have covered the continuum between mindful and mindless activity (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2006: 522), the related dialectic tensions that exists between the two extremes (Levinthal & Rerup, 2006) and the struggle to engage collective minding (Carlo et al., 2012: 1102). Unfortunately, these understandings of mindful versus mindless behaviors are not evident in our data. It is important to remember that the surprises that our bankers reported were not related to overly complex or esoteric situations. But, they do operate in a highly volatile environment of financial and technological changes and they have survived the financial crisis, suggesting that they are capable of making adequately mindful decisions. This dichotomy suggests there is a misalignment between managers' sense of the level of mindfulness required for certain decisions versus the level which is actually required. By not aligning their level of displayed mindfulness with what is required by a particular situation, they create a propensity for surprise outcomes.

Rerup (2004) noted that organizations occasionally operate with less-than-mindful behavior, and that doing so usually has no adverse results. But, in the case of our bankers their decision narratives show them repeatedly anchoring their assumptions in past experience and adopting a narrow focus in defining the situation that shaped their less-mindful behavior, delivering a restricted use of multiple perspectives and a closed rather than an open process of inquiry. In the end, without thoughtful analysis and sound judgment, decisions are destined for the surprise bin. The challenge therefore, is in “calibrating mindfulness” to align the actual versus apparent requirement for mindfulness of the task at hand. In calibrating the required mindfulness, our bankers suffered from a series of biases that made under-estimating the required mindfulness more likely, almost becoming a part of their standard operating procedures. We discuss these biases below as components of their capacity for surprise management.

## 5.3. Surprise management

In unraveling the reasons behind our banker's decision surprises we have observed that their closed managerial mindset, their rigidity of inquiry from standardization, their information systems lack of management value, their lack of predictive capabilities and their absence of accountability each bring its own dynamics to the surprise outcome. Details follow:

## 5.3.1. Organization's closed mindset

Our study highlights the importance of top managers' mindsets (or mental models) in understanding the pattern of their company's behavior and the triggers and enablers that drive the decision process (Senge, 1990; Weick, 1987). We have learned that surprises are made more likely by the way the banks conduct their normal business activity, essentially with a business-as-usual mindset, deeply embedded in the bank's management traditions and conceptualized as a source of stability. Given the dynamic nature of banking, adopting such a tacit model that exists below a required level of awareness only leads to counterproductive actions (Senge, 1990). This was made clearly evident as bankers processed surprises as unexpected outcomes and contrary to expectations were somewhat inattentive and dismissive to the relevant detail layered within them. They did not sense anything was pressing for effecting immediate change, and accepted that nothing could be done other than simply taking losses and moving on, which was seen as an established industry practice. When we ignore perspective and how information looks differently depending on how it is viewed, Langer suggests “we confuse stability of our mind-sets with the stability of the underlying phenomena” (Langer, 2000:221). It was apparent there was a need for having greater cognitive flexibility (Spiro, Vispoel, Schmitz, Samarapungavan, & Boerger, 1987) and for rejecting normative approaches (Klein, 1993). We anticipate that these bankers are destined to experience further surprise outcomes by virtue of their “normalization of deviance” as they tolerated minor failures and defects, ignored the early warning signals and opted to ‘carry-on instead of restructuring their mental representation of the decision environment.

## 5.3.2. Rigidity of inquiry through standardization

Our study supports the literature on organizational routines and how routines can be a source of inertia and inflexibility (Feldman & Pentland, 2003). Adherence to overly-formalized standardized management processes, which was evident at our banks, narrows the bank's attention and limits its capacity to act mindfully. Proponents of overly formalized practices (e.g., bank regulators and bank management) encourage attentiveness to standards that might otherwise be neglected. More specifically, when a bank displays ‘normal’ (average) behaviors, which of course is not necessarily the same as desirable behaviors, it can lead to confirmation of their managerial capability. By conforming to the performance of others, which thei information technologies for comparing each bank to peer averages encourages they receive institutional legitimacy as competent actors in the banking community.

Recall that bankers provide financial data to the FDIC each quarter where it is collated, analyzed and transformed into the UBPR, which is made available to banks for the purpose of comparing their performance with rivals. Banks, by default and perhaps by moral suasion, mimic strategies used by other banks. In doing so, they become recognized as more reliable than a bank that deviates from this behavioral norm (Deephouse, 1996), but this is a rather low standard when so many banks share the same propensity for less-mindful behavior. Within this self-sealing framework, the regulator's requirements regarding “best practices” forces a movement toward conformity that creates self-induced limits, restricts mindful behavior and encourages rigidity of action.

A repeated example, which was directly related to the financial crisis, was a loss in decision quality due to the extreme confidence management had (Fischhoff, Slovic & Lichtenstein, 1977) with their mechanical use of risk management models (ALCO), which became important and distinctive drivers of bank failures (Honohan, 2008). In using the ALCO model, bankers traditionally simulated the effect of a shock to the financial base by going up 200 basis points and going down 200 basis points to reveal what the potential impact would be on the bank's financial performance. But the federal funds interest rate fell 500 basis points, catching management unprepared. This was a devastating blow to their profitability. It is this built-in bias for less-mindful patterns of decision making, together with the search for certainty or rigidity from standardized procedures that can cloud judgment and produce generators of undetected change (Feldman, 2004).

In so doing, management reinforced the tactical nature of their decisions instead of providing a meaningful prescription for the future. Thus, they adopted a more narrow focus, which ultimately reinforced less-mindful behavior. One thing remains undeniable, constant reliance on routine for routine's sake can be detrimental to performance. As Langer stated “there is power in uncertainty, yet most of us seek certainty” (Langer, 2000: 220). It is this striving for certainty and use of standardized information reporting for making normative judgments of ‘best practices’ that traps banks in a cycle of limited patterns of management control, making it hard for them to be highly inventive, hence, hindering their strategic decision processes.

## 5.3.3. Organization's information system and learning

Our study shed light on the importance of information technology as a vital building block for organization reliability (Butler & Gray, 2006) and its contribution to organizational performance (Brynjolfsson & Hitt, 1998; Melville et al., 2004; Tallon, 2008). It seems intuitive that embracing surprise reduction should be associated with greater attention to information, novelty and uncertainty. In banking, change is frequent and ofter disruptive and “flexible IT has become a primary differentiating factor in banking success” (Tallon, 2008: 2) At the core of effective decision-making is an integrated process involving IT and measurement reporting systems for tracking performance and focusing management attention on operating activities in ways that should prevent unreliability. Even though the importance of integrating organizational knowledge and management processes in decision making was stated as being of prime importance to our bankers, what we saw was that their IT system's information was often rigid and inconsistent with their changing environment, leading to faulty decisions. One executive stressed “in retrospect, we should have probed more on the issue and got more information” or how one bank “recorded debt/income and loan/value ratios into the loan system but never historically tracked them” or how another “didn't fully understand the extent they had got themselves into some large development loans”. The IT system collected and stored the information but it was not used for business intelligence reporting. A positive outcome of the financial crisis is, as one CEO explained “we've got a whole laundry list of new measures that we evaluate, reports that we look at, concentrations, etc., to ensure it doesn't happen again”. It would be interesting to follow their behaviors going forward and test if such is evident.

## 5.3.4. Surprise predictability

Recognizing the dynamics of how firms either identify or miss the “early” warning signals is the domain of managing the unexpected and calls for deliberative mindfulness (Weick & Sutcliffe, 2001). As Langer (1989b: 134) noted, “to catch the early warnings of trouble, we must be alert to new information, to subtle deviations from the way things typically go”. Our research evidenced the inability of our banks to recognize emerging threats and deliver appropriate responses. It uncovered a weak link in recognizing fluctuations in perfor mance that stemmed either from managerial denial or inadequate triggering mechanisms (i.e. early warning systems). For example, possessing all the information did not necessarily guarantee success, as management failed to “connect the dots” (Bazerman & Watkins, 2008: 161) forcing one CEO's denial to a problem “in about 95% of the cases where we've had problems (bad loans) at this bank, there'd been very clear warning signs that perhaps we were taking another bank's problems and we shouldn't have done the deals”. In terms of recognition, the inability to identify environmental cues led to surprise outcomes. A common missed signal related to the financial crisis was expressed best by one CEO:

“In retrospect, I think all of us (referring to bankers) would say you know if you look at where home prices were relative to trend, obviously that was so far away from history and trend that you would think that probably you would anticipate there being an issue there. I think we get so used to anticipating a normal range of outcomes”

Banks' sourced internal information through their regular standard reports for normal business management. The extent to which data reporting recognized emerging threats was not a focus of managerial attention nor was “disciplined post-problem learning” (Bazerman & Watkins, 2008: 11) from them a priority. Thus, adequately sourced information for identifying surprise outcomes was a retrospective process and was hampered by institutionalized normative behaviors.

## 5.3.5. Outcome accountability

We realized during our study that the implications of post-decision surprise must come to terms with failure or more appropriately, avoiding failure. Our bankers transferred blame: denying failure and avoiding accountability for decisions (Lerner & Tetlock, 1999; Tetlock, 1985) and therefore restricted learning. Accountability is simply the need to “justify ones views or actions to another” (Day & Klein, 1987; Tetlock, 1992a,b) and while it is a complex construct it is widely used for holding management answerable for achieving specific goals. What was evident from this study was an absence of “outcome" accountability in that management rationalized prior causes of action (in some cases, accepting any explanation) or directed accountability more to the “process” (Conlon & Wolf, 1980; Fox & Staw, 1979), or attributed it to external forces and events to justify failed outcomes and avoid blame (Elster, 1999; Gilbert et al., 2004). “It was not our fault but rather it was the market that prevented our success” was a common response of our executives. This factual restructuring (Svenson & Benthorn, 1992) togeth er with hindsight bias (Fischoff, 1975) drives self-justification and reinforces their managerial legitimacy. We are not suggesting that accountability is absent in banking, any CEO can provide plenty of evidence to the contrary (e.g., meeting monthly financial and strategic objectives and satisfying Board members). We are suggesting that accountability for surprise outcomes was inadeguate and it can ad vance learning and furnish a tool for future surprise avoidance.

Overall, our bankers did not foster creative and adaptive learning (Senge, 1990) or exhibit any curiosity (Harvey, Novicevic, Leonard, & Payne, 2007). As one CEO commented, “we are looking at the short term…we can't be worried about the next (surprise)”, suggesting an operating mode focused on the moment. Such a myopic perspective can lead to oversimplification in strategy and incomplete decision-making (Miller, 1993). Therefore, success may depend on the bank's ability to manage its environment by creating a new sense of urgency about gaining better predictive capabilities and fostering a culture of accountability, where detail and expertise matter.

## 6. Limitations

Several distinct limitations of this study should be noted. Our sample, while considered adequate for a grounded theory inquiry (n = 23, with theoretical saturation reached after 18 interviews) was regionally limited and restricted primarily to community banks. We did not include large U.S. money-center, or superregional banks which are generally more sophisticated and provide different and more complex array of services to a broader range of clients from those in our sample. Our sample did, however, capture a coherent, representative cross-section of the remaining bank population, suggesting reliable generalization to that subsector.

We also recognize that our research required subjects to recollect their past experiences and we recognize that their narratives may have been influenced by the effect of time on memory. To mitigate this affect, we invited respondents to self-select the experiences they narrated, assuming the choice of memorable and impactful events. We did not address the validity of the decision expectations. These CEOs possess significant resources in formulating their decisions but this is no guarantee decisions were optimal choices. Thus decision quality may have faced many behavioral. cognitive and epistemic influences resulting in less than optimal decisions, but this specific aspect was not our research goal. Keep in mind that the expectation in all decisions was that they would prove to be successful. To this end, we concentrated on post-decision outcomes that varied from the subject's expectation. It is important to realize in interpreting the findings that these banks were addressing negative surprises and not their overall managerial successes (i.e. events that did not surprise).

## 7. Implications for additional research

The exploratory nature of our study makes us conclude that more empirical research is needed on organizational mindfulness, especially in relation to surprise outcomes at a wider variety of financial institutions, as well as firms in other industries. It is important to have a better understanding of the early warning signals and how they can be sensed, interpreted and addressed (or not) by managers. We looked at surprise outcomes of relatively significant events, but research should also address all surprise outcomes especially small surprise events that when combined can magnify the overall impact. There is also a research opportunity of investigating the relationship between collective mindfulness, firm productivity and firm performance: and a complementary research opportunity to understand the dynamic of interaction between individual, team and organizational level mindfulness.

Future research should address managerial decision-making deficiencies and their relationship to the fundamental causes of surprises. We also suggest there is a need for careful analysis of the nature of banker identity. Many of our bankers did not see themselves as being responsible for decisions because such actions were inconsistent with their preferred identity of being an esteemed influential membe of the community they serve. We also need to reevaluate the safety-net that regulators try to create as it obviously has surprise implications and limitations, evidenced by the regular occurrence of financial crises. Although the work of bankers is rather complex, we must also evaluate how banks can better overcome the rigidities of their standardization and better relate their organizational systems to the dynamics of their environment and not over-focus on internal issues if they are to reduce the occurrence of surprise and its consequences.

## 8. Conclusion

We propose that surprise transactions are deeply rooted in almost all banks. We must not forget that hidden within all these surprises is an opportunity cost, defined as the return that might have been earned if the surprise was eliminated. This failure to recognize a surprise or if a firm is susceptible to surprise, slow to recognize their mistakes of omission (Hartley, 2011) and blind to the “unintended consequences of purposive actions" (Merton. 1949). it has the distinct potential for damaging the organization's financial performance (Aldrich, 1979; Child, 1972; Harrison & March, 1984; Hrebiniak & Joyce, 1985; Nutt, 2002). Thus, surprise can have a detrimental effect upon the bank's long-range success. This is especially true if surprise events shift from episodic to continuous, consume management efforts and further divert their focus and attentiveness.

We consider this paper a fundamental step in a longer-term exploratory study into understanding the experience of decision surprise and its avoidance. In the process, we have offered a sense of how these CEOs have faced surprise outcomes, and touched on the attributes of information technology, organizational practices and environmental constraints, and behavioral decision-making, concluding that only those who mindfully appreciate the potential for surprise can truly aspire to be successful decision-makers.
