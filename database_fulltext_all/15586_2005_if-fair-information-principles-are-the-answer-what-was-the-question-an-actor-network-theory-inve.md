# If fair information principles are the answer, what was the question? An actor-network theory investigation of the modern constitution of privacy

William Bonner <sup>a,\*</sup>, Mike Chiasson <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Faculty of Business Administration, University of Regina, Education Building, Regina Saskatchewan S4S 0A4, United States

<sup>b</sup> Haskayne School of Business, University of Calgary, 2500 University Drive NW, Calgary, Alta., Canada T2N 1N4

Received 29 January 2004; received in revised form 9 January 2005; accepted 14 March 2005

## Abstract

Privacy concerns are increasing, despite considerable government legislation and organizational resources expended in addressing them. In this paper, we explore the historical and contemporary influences on privacy legislation that redirect privacy eforts towards data, and data sharing. Particularly, we use actor-network theory (ANT) to discover and trace the development, translation and subsequent spread of the influence of international privacy principles (fair information principles, FIP) into de facto standards for addressing privacy concerns. Despite the acceptance of the ‘‘black box’’ of FIP, by oficial actants across 30 years, its enlistment has enhanced rather than prevented, the sharing of data. Our investigation ofers insight into the complex interaction among various social, political and technical elements that have influenced the framing and discourse on privacy: from the early conflict among potential possibilities envisioned in emerging information and communication technologies and diverse concerns about those same potentials, to the development of FIP and how through enlistment, drifts, happenstance, convenience and expediency, its influence has been extended to shape the constitution of privacy across time and space.  2005 Elsevier Ltd. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Fair information principles; Actor-network theory; Privacy; Privacy legislation; Canada; Balance; Black boxes; Action from a distance; The organisation for economic and co-operation and development; Historical analysis

## 1. Introduction

Concerns about privacy are increasing, despite the considerable governmental and business eforts to address these concerns (Equifax/Harris, 1994, 1996; Federal Trade Commission, 1998; Roy Morgan Research, 2001). Privacy discussions suggest that the individual should have control over the information that is known about them, in order to balance their desire for privacy against other objectives. Westin, perhaps the most cited authority on privacy, emphasizes the role of the individual in this process.

‘‘Each individual is continually engaged in a personal adjustment process in which he balances the desire for privacy with the desire for disclosure and communication of himself to others, in light of the environmental conditions and social norms set by the society in which he lives’’ (Westin, 1967, p. 7)

In North America, Europe, and Asia, governments have passed privacy legislation, based on fair information principles or practices (FIP), which serve as surrogates for the ‘‘personal adjustment process’’ and ‘‘social norms’’. Businesses have also recognized the need to address privacy, and have directly employed FIP in assuring individuals that their privacy is respected and protected, through privacy policies and on-line privacy assurance seals such as TRUSTe and BBBonline. The Canadian government has sought to ofer further assurances through the latest privacy legislation aimed at corporate businesses (e.g., PIPEDA in Canada<sup>1</sup>). Both government legislation and business initiatives are increasingly being employed as surrogates for the individual adjustment process.

The importance of privacy is reflected in the stream of IS research stemming from Masons (1986) call for more privacy research; a call taken up by others (Culnan, 1993; Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Culnan & Bries, 2003; Smith, 1993; Smith, Milberg, & Burke, 1996; Stewart & Segars, 2002). Mason considers the linking of facts, across the spectrum of ones life, a threat to privacy. He also feels that our field must pay more attention to the social costs of inevitable data errors (Mason, 1986, p. 7). Subsequent research has taken an increasingly narrow conception of privacy, largely within the context of individual organizations. Culnan (1993) focuses on ‘‘personal information’’, Smith (1993) focuses on ‘‘personal data’’, and Smith, Milberg &

Burke focus on ‘‘information privacy’’. Stewart and Segars (2002) narrow it further in linking individual concerns about privacy to computer anxiety. A central theme of this body of research is the importance of control over the subsequent use of personal information and the notion of seeking a balance between privacy and programs deemed to require the use of personal information (Culnan, 1993; Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Culnan & Bries, 2003). FIP plays multiple roles in this research, as a means of assuring individual control over information, as an objective standard for assessing individual concerns about privacy (Culnan, 1993; Culnan & Armstrong, 1999; Smith et al., 1996), and as an ethical standard for corporate behaviour (Culnan, 1993; Culnan & Bries, 2003).

Despite these numerous attempts to address privacy in research, government legislation and commercial privacy policies, concerns about privacy continue to increase. This is counter-intuitive. However, our research shows, through a detailed historical analysis of the constitution of privacy legislation, that the FIP that underlies such legislation paradoxically leads towards reducing privacy, rather than protecting it.

While aware of the increased attention that privacy as an issue was attracting, we observed a particular local conflict, where privacy was raised as an important issue and yet existing government data sharing practices were preserved and privacy issues and privacy legislation that was supposed to protect privacy were by-passed. This was puzzling. In an efort to understand the influences on this outcome, we reviewed the privacy research, government legislation and commercial eforts to address privacy. We were struck by the common discourse in these literatures – privacy is important, the computer is an important cause of privacy issues, and government legislation and business practices are progressively addressing the issues through international standards, such as FIP. The literature was unable to explain how, if privacy was so important, this local issue and legislation supposedly protecting privacy could be so easily dismissed.

Earlier, we had become interested in actor-network theory (ANT), particularly its focus on the ‘‘How?’’ question with respect to the construction of ontological status. Viewing the ‘‘givens’’ of the discourse as possessing such status, we decided that we needed to understand their construction, their actual elements, in order to understand how they come together to shape events. We decided to employ ANT, as it allows (even requires) the researcher to look beyond the ‘‘givens’’ and explore the underlying influences that create and substantiate them.

We will briefly describe the local issue and then discuss how ANT directed our view and investigation.

## 2. A local event and surprising outcome

The Alberta Ofice of the Information and Privacy Commissioner conducted a privacy audit on the governments own Motor Vehicle Registry (MVR), in 1996–1997. The MVR gathers the names and addresses of registered motor vehicle owners as well as their date of birth, eye and hair color, height and weight. In requesting the audit, the Deputy Minister responsible for the MVR expressed concern about existing MVR practices with respect to privacy,

‘‘The disclosure of this information [personal information in data banks], and in particular the selling of it, has been raised a number of times with the Minister, with myself and other department oficials. As a result, ensuring adequate privacy practices are adopted – especially as they relate to the FOIP [Freedom of Information and Privacy Protection] Act – is important to us’’ (Ofice of the Information and Privacy Commissioner and the Auditor General Alberta, 1998, p. 3)<sup>2</sup>

The audit produced 12 recommendations. Seven were accepted and implemented. The remaining five recommendations were accepted in principle but action was delayed for further study. The first of these five recommendations referred to the absence of legislative authority for the MVR to sell collected information to ‘‘public bodies, municipalities, federal government bodies, hospitals, post-secondary institutions, parking companies and private sectors businesses’’ as well as ‘‘law firms, private investigators, collection agencies, small businesses, private parking companies, etc’’. The second recommendation was that the MVR should not be exempt from the provinces privacy legislation (FOIP), despite a direct exemption for it in the act. If a change to FOIP legislation to eliminate this exemption was impossible, the audit recommended that the MVR adopt fair information principles (FIP) as an adequate alternative. The other three recommendations dealt with the release of drivers abstracts in circumstances that exceeded specific legislative authority, the lack of oversight by the government on the use of the information released to MVR clients, and a lack of control over the issuance and expiry of search accounts issued to MVR clients (Ofice of the Information and Privacy Commissioner and the Auditor General Alberta, 1998).

Later in the same year, the ministry responsible for the MVR undertook consultations with, ‘‘. . . the people who use the information for business purposes’’ and Albertans in order to ‘‘. . . strike the right balance . . .’’ (Alberta Registries, 1998, p. 2, italics added). Part of the goal of these consultations was to define the issues arising from the five accepted but unresolved privacy audit recommendations, and to provide recommendations to the ministry. In 1999, before this process was completed, a committee of the Alberta Legislature, reviewing the FOIP Act<sup>3</sup>, cited ‘‘historical purposes and practices’’ related to the MVR as justification for its continuing exemption from FOIP legislation (Select Special Freedom of Information & Protection of Privacy Act Review Committee, 1999). The resolution that allowed the MVR to continue sharing information with outside clients, in the face of the stated importance of privacy, was an important motivation for this research.

This local event contains within it many of the same discursive elements that tend to emerge in privacy discussions. First, in requesting the audit, the government explicitly stated its belief in the importance of privacy. Second, there was an appeal to independent standards such as FOIP or FIP as representatives of the interests of privacy. Third, there was an explicitly stated desire to seek balance. In this microcosm, we see the employment of what actor-network theory (ANT) refers to as black boxes; FOIP and FIP are employed as oficial surrogates for privacy on the scale that balances privacy concerns against MVR practices.<sup>4</sup> From an ANT perspective, however, we cannot derive roles-in-practice from their titles or their use in action, without running the risk of granting them a substance they may not possess. Instead, we adopted a naı¨ve stance, consistent with ANT, in order to understand the resolution of this case, which was inconsistent with the stated objectives. We also felt that the boxes, so efortlessly employed and referenced in oficial discourse, were actually quite complex and required historical understanding.

We undertook the task of recreating the genealogy of these black boxes in order to understand their construction, and from this, their substantive roles-in-practice. In seeking to understand the contradictory outcomes in this local case, we asked two questions.

1. What is FOIP and how did the MVR come to be exempt from it?

2. What is FIP, and how did it achieve its status as a surrogate for privacy?

Our investigation reveals assumptions about privacy legislation and its foundation in FIP and FOIP that challenge commonly held beliefs about their role in the protection of privacy. FIP, despite its widespread use as the foundation of privacy legislation and organizational action, actually enhances the movement of personal data by substituting questions of privacy and broader ethical questions with minimal rules that, if satisfied, legitimize the movement of personal data.

We now turn to the ANT methodology employed in addressing these questions. This is followed by the findings, discussion, and conclusions and future research.

## 3. Methodology

To understand the role played by FIP in local events as privacys representative, we need to understand its genesis and the taken-for-granted assumptions and arguments embedded within FIP, that manifest themselves in the local setting.

We selected ANT as the lens for the reasons stated earlier. The potential value of the ANT lens for ICT research has been explored on a theoretical level by IS scholars (Walsham, 1997; Braa & Vidgen, 1999; Doolin & Lowe, 2002) as well as in management studies (Calas & Smirchich, 1999). Arguments for the applicability of ANT for

ICT research include understanding the trajectories of sociotechnical networks (Braa & Vidgen, 1999), as a means of recognizing the historical contingency of the ‘‘taken for granted’’ (Doolin & Lowe, 2002; Calas & Smirchich, 1999) and as a form of critique through revealing the contingent nature of the taken-for-granted (Doolin & Lowe, 2002). ANT has also been used in empirical studies and we refer to those in the discussion below.

We focused on three key features of ANT that guided our investigation. First, the role of the researcher and the focus on the ‘‘how’’ question directed us to trace the construction of the present by discovering and following the understandings, actions and interactions of those involved in the construction of present events, without imposing our own rationales. Second, ANT focuses attention on the black boxes employed by actants, those who act or cause others to act. Black boxes, once enlisted, become actants themselves, inscribing and shaping the behavior of those employing them. Third, ANT recognizes the influence of distant forces and actants on local events in the concepts of networks and action at a distance (Latour, 1987; Latour, 1996).

The researchers role is to articulate, through description, the understanding possessed by actants, in their specific time and place (Kendall & Wickham, 1999). The researcher must be open to the idea that local times, places and understandings will most likely reshape ideas or concepts that, on the surface, may appear to be very similar. Scott and Wagner (2003) capture this in tracing the users perceptions of time across stages of the implementation of an ERP system in a major university. McGrath (2002) describes similar translations of meaning over time with respect to organizational change and technology programs employed in the London Ambulance Service. Walsham and Sahay (1999) explore the translation of meaning and understanding with a GIS system imported from North America for use in India. Researchers must leave themselves open to discover how the meaning of distant objects, ideas or techniques, are translated locally across time and space, and yet appear to remain linked (networked) to these distant ideas/objects/techniques, despite translations and local diferences in understanding.

ANT focuses on the construction of the present (ideas, practices, objects, beliefs – black boxes), by tracing the past and current actions and interactions of human and non-human actants that have collectively constructed and continually reconstruct this present. Local actants interact and work through aligned networksthat, if successful and sustainable, come to traverse space and time (Latour, 1993). Hanseth and Monteiro (1997) describe such an event in tracing the interplay and shifting positions of various distant and local actors and interests in the eventual shaping of standards employed in the health information infrastructure in Norway. The resulting black box, the infrastructure standard, subsequently inscribed behavior on current and future actants in Norways health sector by shaping their role and options. Conversely, Holmstrom and Stadler (2001) ascribe the failure of a sponsors (a bank) IT innovation to the unwillingness of the sponsor to accommodate alternative visions and interests (or drifts from the sponsors firm conception of the system) in its trial implementation. As a consequence of this failure to enlist other critical actants (merchants and customers), the innovation died a lonely death. This case underscores

ANT belief that macro actants have no ontological reality; the system sponsor and its innovation remained unsuccessful local actants through a failure to enlist and enroll other actants. For ANT, having ideas or programs adopted by others is a practical accomplishment, an ongoing pattern of local enactment and re-enactment, which requires explanation (Latour, 1990b, p. 170; Latour, 1992a, p. 286).

One of the critical distinguishing features of ANT is that it keeps both the local and the distant visible at the same time, avoiding shortcuts and appeals to abstract concepts that transcend the local. In our case, the local enactment of balance is bound by the standards of FOIP and FIP; black boxes purported to represent privacy that, in being employed, construct and shape the local privacy-data sharing debate. These objects have unique local and distant meanings and rationales that we must keep visible in order to determine how these objects survive translations across time and space while, at the same time, they retain enough apparent commonality that local actants enroll or are willingly aligned in networks.

Understanding how networks emerge requires a return to a point in time where many possibilities existed, before the black boxes emerged and their influence spread (Latour, 1987; Latour, 1992a, p. 287). At such moments various possibilities compete in a symmetrical fashion, and diverse actants and their local actions, interactions, understandings and rationales, are visible. Over time, actants align themselves around particular orientations, in networks, and an asymmetry develops, reducing the scope of other possibilities. ANT focuses on describing the construction of this asymmetrical outcome and how the black box comes to assume the status of a taken-for-granted truth where its label replaces its contents (Callon, 1991, p. 152; Latour, 1987, p. 139; Latour, 1992b).

Going back in time and tracing the threads forward requires the investigator to render visible the threads that link the past and present. Latour and Serres use the example of a volcano to illustrate the need to focus on what connects rather than what severs (Latour, 1990a, pp. 55–56; Serres, 1990; pp. 130–149). A volcanic eruption is an awe-inspiring event, which unavoidably draws our attention. To understand volcanoes, though, our focus should be on the forces that produced the eruption, the shifting tectonic plates and underground streams of molten lava, as they shape the event and are poised to again shape future events. In our case we came to realize that a focus on the computer might be creating such a divide – between precomputer and post-computer privacy eras. As a result of this severing of time, privacy becomes redefined as dealing with data generally and electronic data specifically.

ANTs apparently simple but methodologically dificult principle is to follow the actants. In our case, we started our investigation by examining the privacy audit in detail, analyzing the documents produced and following the subsequent statements and actions of those directly involved in the conflict. We then interviewed key actants in the Ofice of the Privacy Commissioner as well as within the MVR system. Through this we became aware of other potential influential actants and events, which led to more interviews and the gathering and analysis of additional documents. Over thirty interviews were conducted and transcripts were produced. Documents were sourced and gathered from the provincial archives, the Legislature of

Alberta, published Canadian government documents as well as publications by influential actants, including the Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development (OECD) in Europe. Where actants act or influence others to act, these network efects were pursued and this process continued until no new actants emerged. At that point events and actants were grouped, their actions, linkages and translations across time and space were identified and traced, ultimately reconstructing the trajectories through which they shaped the present. The gathering of information and the reconstruction of events were not separate events. In the process of reconstruction, gaps were identified and more information, either interview or document based, was gathered. This research took place over a period of 2 years and the entire research project spanned 90 years of history.<sup>5</sup> Because of the scope of the full research project, we are focusing here on the last 40 years with specific reference to the modern constitution of privacy. Even this is ambitious, within the page constraints of a journal paper, and we strive to present a reasonable foundation for our findings.

Starting from the local issue, our investigation quickly expanded in two directions: back into time and across space (beyond Alberta to Canada, and internationally). What we discovered helped us understand the local enactment that resulted in the preservation of data sharing at the MVR, how FIP and privacy legislation are the complex product of other and earlier balancing acts that are predisposed towards this preservation, and why privacy concerns continue to exist and escalate despite considerable government, business and academic efort to address them.

## 4. Findings

Tracing the genesis of FOIP in Alberta, through oficial documents and interviews, revealed a web of influences that spanned time and space, to include other parts of Canada and Europe, and time periods dating back to the 1960s. Alberta was the second to last Canadian province to develop FOIP (1994), and its construction was heavily influenced by earlier acts passed in two Canadian provinces, British Columbia (1993) and Saskatchewan (1991). These two acts were influenced by an earlier act passed in the province of Ontario (1987); which in turn was influenced by Canadian federal legislation passed in 1982. In tracing the development of the federal legislation, we discovered the influence of the OECD in its creation of FIP, as FIP forms the foundation of Canadian privacy legislation. Tracing the OECDs development of FIP led us back to conflict and contentions around privacy, which appeared in the 1960s and 1970s.

FIP first came to our attention in the second of the five outstanding audit recommendations; that it be adopted as an adequate substitute for FOIP for the MVR. Here, FIP was referred to as ‘‘the generally accepted principles known as -Fair information practices forming the basis of the privacy sections of the FOIP Act’’<sup>6</sup> (Ofice of the Information & Privacy Commissioner & the Auditor General Alberta, 1998, p. 22). The 1999 annual report of the Privacy Commissioner of Alberta, cites the OECD as the source of FIP (Ofice of the Information & Privacy Commissioner, 1999; p. 1). The 1998 report also mentions the important influence of the OECD and FIP on worldwide privacy legislation (Ofice of the Information and Privacy Commissioner, 1998; p. 1).

Further evidence of the influence of the OECD and FIP on FOIP legislation in Canada and Alberta, came from an Industry Canada report: ‘‘Much of the public sector privacy legislation in Canada is based on the principles in the OECD guidelines’’ (Akay Information Consulting Inc., 1995, p. 9). This was echoed in an earlier review of the development of federal privacy legislation, ‘‘These international standards generated privacy principles that had a considerable impact on the current provisions of the Privacy Act’’ [Canadian federal act] (Gillis, 1987, p. 124). Further research revealed that the scope of the OECDs influence was not limited to Canada. Most European legislation, developed after 1981 reflects, ‘‘The spirit if not the text of the OECD guidelines’’ (Mayer-Schonberger, 1997, p. 220) and the Privacy Commissioner of New Zealand suggested that FIP ‘‘Forms the basis of privacy laws in New Zealand Australia, Canada, Hong Kong, among others’’ (Slane, 2000, p. 3). Authors in the United States often credit the US Department of Health Education and Welfare (1973) for the development of FIP but as one U.S. author concluded, ‘‘Virtually all American and European privacy legislation is based on a regulatory regime called Fair Information Practices’’ (Laudon, 1996, p. 96). Thus FIP has become a widereaching force in shaping privacy legislation – influencing events from a distance. As FIP is embedded in the Canadian FOIP, it was important for us to understand its genesis and how it came to attain its influence, in order to understand the role that it played in maintaining the data-sharing practices of the MVR.

## 4.1. Fair information principles

The standard reference for FIP is a document produced by the OECD, in 1980, entitled, Guidelines: On the Protection of Privacy and Transborder of Personal Data (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980).<sup>7</sup> The eight principles of FIP, contained in the OECD document, are reproduced in Table 1.

To understand the development and influence of FIP, network threads were traced back to events in the 1960s and the 1970s. During this period, the growing use of increasingly powerful centralized computers (non-human actants using ANT) by government and commercial organizations to process personal information and build dossiers on individuals, generated negative public reactions in Europe. ‘‘In the 1960s and early 1970s, as information technology emerged as a tool for public administration, large computer projects encountered a groundswell of resistance’’ (Davies, 1997, p. 145). In the United Sates, at around the same time, proposed plans to build a National Data Center, linking files in diferent government agencies into one massive citizen record, led to the ‘‘[d]evelopment of substantial public concern with respect to the privacy issue’’ (Commission on Freedom of Information & Individual Privacy Vol. 3, 1980, p. 505). In Canada the potential construction of similar databases also raised public concerns. ‘‘The widespread development of highly eficient computerized databanks has given rise to increasing concerns about their potential use for invasions of personal privacy’’ (Task Force on Privacy & Computers, 1972, p. 1). This was also reflected in Alberta. In 1970, a Special Select Committee of the Legislature produced a report entitled, A Report to the Alberta Legislature of the Special Committee on Invasion of Privacy, in which the potential threat of computers to privacy occupied a third of the document (Simpson et al., 1970). Thus, while computers had been around for some time before this period, its increased use and the perception of its potential for gathering and using personal data generated public concern that came to be labeled as ‘‘privacy’’.

In response to these concerns, actants aligned with the OECD adopted the concept of balance, as reflected in a 1971 OECD Informatics Studies report entitled, Digital Information and the Privacy Problem. ‘‘We have seen that the storage of digital information confers a great many advantages, but, balanced against this, there has been a growing fear in recent years that the increasing use of computers for storing and processing information will lead to unacceptable invasions of individual privacy’’ (Niblett, 1971, p. 16, italics added). In this statement we can see the existence and nature of an emerging conflict, a point in time when various possibilities and potentials existed.

From here we can trace the asymmetrical actions of the OECD that eventually produced the black box of FIP. Actants within and aligned with the OECD, government mandarins and commercial organizations, were concerned that individual governments, reacting to public pressure, might develop and pass unique pieces of national legislation that would impede the flow of data across borders. ‘‘Restrictions of those flows [data flows across borders] could cause serious disruption in important sectors of the economy, such as banking and insurance’’ (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980; p. 5).

In pursuit of balance the OECD, as well as the European Commission and the Council of Europe, worked towards the creation of, ‘‘A group of principles [that] slowly evolved to form a basis of law’’ (Davies, 1997, p. 156). These principles (FIP) emerged from a symposium attended by more than ‘‘270 experts from 23 countries and 16 international organizations’’ (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980, p. 37). The focus of that symposium was:

‘‘to gather facts and opinions from representatives of governments, public authorities, industrial and commercial interests (e.g., banking and insurance), trade associations, service bureaux, information and telecommunication services, on: current and future developments in the data processing industry and in user requirements; economic, social and legal aspects and their international dimensions’’

## From the same document:

‘‘The papers and discussions showed the need for international co-operation on common principles for the protection of personal data crossing national frontiers, without at the same time creating unnecessary barriers to all kinds of international data flows’’ (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980 publication list and description in the Appendix).

‘‘Unnecessary barriers’’ would arise if individual countries (actants acting alone) passed substantially diferent privacy legislation, in response to public concerns. Thus, FIP was derived predominantly by a network of actants, large enterprises capable of acquiring and putting computer technology to use, who had a collective interest in avoiding such restrictions in order to protect the varied envisioned potentials seen in evolving ICT and the ‘‘freedom of information’’.

‘‘Certain problems require particularly urgent attention, e.g., those relating to emerging international data networks, and to the need of balancing competing interests of privacy on the one hand and the freedom of information on the other, in order to allow the full exploitation of the potentialities of modern data processing technologies in so far as is desirable (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980; p.16, italics added).

The concept of balance implies a symmetrical means of addressing questions of pri vacy and opposing interests, but its enactment in the development of FIP was decidedly asymmetrical. FIP emerged from behind the closed doors of the OECD. Rule, McAdam, Stearns, and Uglow (1980), writing at the time of this controversy commented, ‘‘In the case of privacy, as with many other issues, the fateful turning point came not in the heat of public debate, but at the unnoticed point where key assumptions were taken for granted’’ (Rule et al., 1980, p. 41). While not explicitly employing an ANT perspective, the authors were commenting on the shift towards an extremely narrow conception of privacy and its protection, the -protection of data, that they saw as a ‘‘fateful turning point’’ (or fateful translation in ANT terminology) emerging in the oficial discourse.

The beginnings of the translation of privacy into the much narrower concern with data or data protection are reflected in FIP itself (Table 1). The word data appears in all eight principles while the word privacy remains present only in the documents title, Guidelines on the Protection of Privacy and the Transborder Flow of Information.<sup>8</sup> Despite the recommendation that these principles ‘‘be regarded as minimum standards which are capable of being supplemented’’ (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980, p. 10), they in fact become the maximum standard in practice, by discouraging any supplements. Member countries, in developing legislation, are advised to, ‘‘Avoid undue interference with the flows of personal data between Member countries’’ (Organisation for Economic Co-Operation and Development, 1980, p. 22). As FIP evolved into a widely accepted black box for government legislation, shaping the focus and language of privacy discourse decades later, the scope of privacy discussions has narrowed to this focus on data and data rules that, once met, permit its movement and use.

As a result, the existence of the black box of FIP became a convenient tool for legislators and others to address growing privacy concerns that were emerging in the Canadian context. In traveling across distance and willingly enlisted in local Canadian settings, FIP increased its status as a tool to address privacy and, in the process of local enlistment and translation, shed the messy details of its origins in conflict and its emphasis on data sharing. As a result, the contents of the black box slowly disappeared from view.

We appreciate that, in our necessarily abridged presentation, the events described may appear linear and to have been easily orchestrated by the OECD and its network of actants. We want to emphasize that the widespread adoption of FIP was very contingent. In 1980, the OECD and those involved in the development of FIP were only local actants, each with their own agendas, possessing only a local set of ideas on paper in the form of the Guidelines. For these ideas to travel beyond the local, others must adopt the program, self-enlist for their own reasons, or those ideas would forever remain local (Latour, 1986). Self-enlistment is a contingent local undertaking, out of the OECDs control. The local nature of a seemingly powerful actant is reflected in Holmstrom and Stadlers (2001) recounting of an innovation failure when merchant and customers refused to self-enlist in the banks proposed program. This is also why Scott and Wagners (2003) articulation of actant-subjective time is so important. As we will discuss, local actants (Canadian) saw the appeal of FIP as a ready-made tool, which they were able and willing to import in order to address local needs and circumstances. In doing so, these actants aided in converting FIP into a black box where the surface understanding of FIP, as a privacy tool, replaced the details of its construction and contents. This process was also aided by the convenience factor, an existing tool that could be easily enlisted to address public rumblings about privacy and other concerns of critical actants, as will be discussed. In this light, the extent of the OECDs influence was not a foregone conclusion and was not something that the OECD could centrally orchestrate. That was the work of others.

In the next section we will speak to how the influence of FIP spread and how its adoption and translation in Canada produced an influential local actant and obligatory reference point that has shaped subsequent discourse on privacy.

## 4.2. The development of FOIP in Canada

In Canada, the origins of concerns about privacy were ‘‘Generally the same as elsewhere, computerization and universal identifiers’’ (Bennett, 1990, p. 555). In the early 1980s, Canadian governments faced both internal and external pressure to develop -privacy legislation. Internally, Canadian governments were perceived as being behind other jurisdictions, such as Europe and the United States, where legislation had already been passed (Rankin, 1984, p. 328; Bennett, 1990, p. 556). Externally, Canada faced potential isolation in the flow of data if it failed to produce legislation consistent with the OECD Guidelines – a pressure that Canada still faces today<sup>9</sup> (Akay Information Consulting Inc., 1995, p. 7). At the same, the federal government came under increasing domestic pressure to develop and pass access to government information legislation – an issue that drew the attention of many diverse actants. The media, opposition parties and special interest groups were pressuring reluctant governments to disclose internal information. The federal government could not easily resist these requests, because it would be seen as a sign of ‘‘something to hide’’. However, the level and means of access to government information provided a fertile ground for public debate and became a struggle of ideas, a conflict. The issue of privacy emerged as a by-product of this struggle, because personal information might be contained in released government documents and therefore something was required to protect and sever this information (Gillis, 1987, p. 125). The Canadian government addressed this issue in a local (Canadian) fashion by creating a single piece of legislation that addressed both access to government information and protection of personal information possessed by the government (Gillis, 1987, p. 121).

This mixing of the two issues is significant because it afected various actants attention. A review of the Canadian federal parliamentary debate showed that the focus of legislators was almost exclusively on the access to information provisions of the FOIP legislation, with only one afternoon devoted to the privacy sections of the legislation (Flaherty, 1989, p. 245)<sup>10</sup> A detailed reading of all of the debates that took place in the Alberta Legislature, over a decade later, reveals a similar focus.<sup>11</sup> Thus, the debate focused on access to government information and the tradeof between access to government information and the suppression of personal information about individuals that might be contained in it (Gillis, 1987). The local adoption of FIP was an easy solution to the privacy ‘‘problem’’, as it emerged in this access to information debate, and its enlistment was seen as a quick remedy to increasing public perception that the government was inactive on issues of privacy. Enlisting FIP also relieved the threat of restricted information flow with jurisdictions that had already or were in the process of adopting FIP-based privacy legislation. As a consequence, Canadian -privacy legislation and its constitution rested on the convenient importation and adoption of FIP, a Latourian black-box whose contents were never explicitly debated, examined or challenged.

Another uniquely Canadian action was to change the title of the -privacy components of the legislation to protection of privacy, replacing the term in the title of similar European legislation, data protection. This simple action profoundly altered the implied scope of Canadian legislation, as does the title of the OECD document containing FIP. Privacy in its broadest context might include privacy of information (of which digital data is but a small subset), privacy of space (Schwartz, 1968, p. 747; Shapiro, 1998), privacy of thought (Finestone, 1997, p. 6) and privacy of the body in terms of its integrity, sovereignty and knowledge about it (Cavoukian & Tapscott, 1995). Protection of privacy might also be thought to include protection of freedoms; freedom from the noise and amusement of others, freedom to develop ideas with the option of discarding them after pausing for private reflection, without having to justify having these ideas and thoughts in the first place (Westin, 1967; Marcuse, 1968, p. 244–245). An expanded view of privacy might define it as a necessary precondition for the development of autonomy, trust and accountability, which can only emerge from the private space where the opposite possibility exists and has been faced (Introna, 1997, p. 274). However, despite its label, FOIP legislation in Canada addressed none of these, as it was founded on FIP principles that speak only to data. As a result, the label protection of privacy creates the impression that the legislation is addressing something more substantial than is warranted by its actual contents.

This federal legislation was used as a model by provincial jurisdictions. In local provincial enlistments of FOIP, the scale and scope of the network of actants employing FIP was extended and, in the process, so too was the influence of the original OECD actants. However, one of the operational diferences between federal and provincial legislation lies in the types of public bodies covered by the respective acts. In Canada, the provinces have jurisdiction over the operation and registration of motor vehicles. This leads us back to Alberta and the exemption of the MVR from the Alberta FOIP Act, when the act was constructed.

## 4.3. The exemption of the MVR from FOIP

The exemption of the MVR from the FOIP act of 1994 occurs in Section 5. This section exempts a record made from information in the ofice of the Registrar of Motor Vehicle Services (Section 5(1)(h)(ii)). Thus, in attempting to understand the local enactment of balance, given the Deputy Ministers statement of the importance of privacy, we encounter an exemption for MVR information that was written into the legislation.

How did this exemption come to exist? In 1992–1993, the drafters of the Alberta legislation gathered copies of similar legislation existing in other provinces, and each contained an exemption for MVR data. A review of the history of the sale of this information suggested that it has been sold by a number of provinces since the 1920s.<sup>12</sup> Government revenue from the sale of this information has grown from less than \$8000 a year in the 1960s to \$4–5 million dollars today. In addition to the governments interest in protecting revenue steams, there were numerous actants already accessing this data and interested in preserving its flow.<sup>13</sup> We were unable to trace the degree of behind the scenes influences or actions taken, through documentary evidence, but it was very clear to the drafters of the legislation that they were to create an exemption for the MVR.

The drafters of the Alberta FOIP legislation consulted individuals responsible for the MVR on the sections of the proposed legislation that afected them. The focus of these individuals was on creating a similar exemption for the MVR that existed in other provinces, in part, to formally sanction the ongoing sale of MVR data and to avoid being ensnared in the intricacies of FOIP legislation.<sup>14</sup>

The drafters of Section 5 cut bits and pieces of existing provincial privacy legislation and pasted them into the Alberta draft legislation, making adjustments that they felt were necessary to recognize the privatized motor vehicle registry system in Alberta.<sup>15</sup> Those responsible for the drafting of this section monitored public hearings and debate in the Legislature to see if this section attracted attention. The exemption was never discussed.<sup>16</sup> To the drafters, it appeared that their intentions had been realized; the sale of this information was protected by the exemption in FOIP.

However, little time passed (1994 until the audit in 1996–1997) before it became apparent that these intentions were not realized. Although the privacy audit acknowledged that the MVR was exempt from the FOIP Act, it pointed out that it was not exempt from the MVRs own legislation, the Motor Vehicle Act, which did not authorize the sale of this information. This absence of legislative authority, forgotten or ignored in the flurry of activity surrounding the development of FOIP legislation and attempts to avoid its snare, had been an on-going and unresolved issue for decades. In the drafting of FOIP legislation, actants attempted to use FOIP to sever longstanding historical tensions around the release and sale of MVR data only to have those unresolved tensions surface again in the privacy audit.

It is beyond the scope of this paper to review that history in depth, but its relevance here is in the Latourian caution against imposing divides or breaks that sever underlying currents and continuities that link and transcend punctuated events. FOIP and FIP are such markers, distracting attention from historical continuities.<sup>17</sup> The issues that arose in the outstanding privacy audit recommendations (1996–1997) are mirrored in a report almost 20 years earlier, with the exception of references to FOIP and FIP because neither existed at the time. In 1979, an incomplete government study indicated that, ‘‘Oficials of the department have spied defects in the practice of imparting information to whomever requests it merely upon payment of the proscribed fee’’ (Solicitor Generals Department, 1979; p. 4). The ‘‘spied defects’’ included: the lack of legislative authority for selling the information; vague grounds for initially establishing search accounts; a lack of audit on subsequent uses of the information; and little or no attempt to assess the continued need for individual accounts. The attempt by drafters of the legislation to sever the past through the insertion of FOIP, failed in the 1996–1997 privacy audit. With the committees declaration that the status quo be maintained, due to ‘‘historical purposes and practices’’, data sharing does continue (with some friction), and the privacy debate has temporarily abated.

## 5. Discussion

In pursuing an understanding of our counter-intuitive observation, that actions to address privacy fail to appease concerns about it, we find instead a paradox; that FIP, the purported protector of privacy, in-fact reduces it. To explain how this occurs, three key points need to be explored. First, FIP has come to assume multiple and even contradictory meanings, despite its narrow constitution. Demonstrating and explaining this helps us understand how privacy concerns and data-sharing are increased by FIP. Second, early ICT has played a critical role in the construction of the privacy problem and its solution, while the evolving constitution has invalidated many of the assumed protective features of FIP. Finally, we must address the asymmetrical concept of balance embedded in the dominant discourse, in practice. FIP is the product of an earlier balancing act disposed towards preserving and increasing the flow of data.

It has been argued that the widespread adoption of FIP into privacy legislation has redirected public debate about privacy to data sharing and administrative eficiency (Clarke, 1988, p. 500; Finestone, 1997, p. 33; Davies, 1997, p. 143; Raab & Bennett, 1998, pp. 265–266). This redirection could be characterized as the product of an economic, technological or administrative imperative, if one accepts that these labels have an independent influence on others actions. From an ANT perspective such labels have little meaning as they are retrospectively applied to the consequence (from a distance), without ofering any insight into how and why local actants construct and continually reconstruct this outcome.<sup>18</sup> At the same time, FIP is seen as an adequate protector of privacy, as indicated in the Minsters request for the audit and the Privacy Commissioners audit recommendations. So, how do we reconcile these contradictory interpretations of FIP? For the moment, let us hold both sets of labels to one side and focus on the local enlistment of FIP and its incorporation into FOIP in Canada, which extended the scale and scope of the influence of FIP, beyond its OECD origins. By understanding the production of these network efects, we will be able to shed light on the apparent fit of these very diferent labels.

In examining FIP we have seen that its contents represent an attempt to balance both desires and concerns about the potential increased use of evolving ICTs, some decades ago. Critical actants emerged and worked together, to mitigate concerns while seeking to preserve the perceived potential of ICT to share data. In Canada, two socio-political twists aided in the enlistment and translation of FIP through a primary focus on increased access to government information and the prevention of potential trade barriers. In other words, the development of Canadian federal and provincial privacy legislation emerged from an unusual mixture of freedom of access to government information and the protection of economic trade. As a result, the -privacy segments of Canadian legislation were largely imported rather than developed. The provinces modeled their legislation on the federal legislation, with local translations, thus extending the network evolving around FIP.

The extension of the influence of FIP did not occur because of overt coercive actions by the OECD to force local compliance, or through heated local public debate. It spread through a translation of FIP into privacy protection standards and through the mundane actions of ‘‘cut and paste’’ into government legislation. The enlistment of FIP in Canadian legislation was the complex product of political processes involving diverse local actants, each with their own interests and actions (media, opposition parties, interest groups, the freedom of information, computer technology and envisioned potential of the same technology, those concerned about trade, legislation writers and buyers of MVR information). It was diverse local eforts and rationales that contingently fused together around FIP.

The contradictory nature of FIP can be understood as diferences between the oficial label of the black box (FOIP) and its contents (FIP). The label ‘‘protection of privacy’’ has replaced FIPs contents, which are essentially rules permitting the movement of data. Over time and because of repeated use in other local settings, it has now become an obligatory point of passage for oficial actants to speak about privacy. For these actants, the adoption of FOIP and its language is obligatory in that privacy discourse and practices, local and distant, are now constructed by it. Legislators pass ‘‘privacy’’ legislation based on FIP, businesses base privacy policies on FIP, researchers employ FIP as a privacy protection standard, policy makers define the privacy problem in terms of FIP, as was the case with the Deputy Minister in requesting the privacy audit, ‘‘Ensuring adequate privacy practices are adopted, especially as the relate to FOIP [FIP] is important to us.’’ Privacy commissioners are created and bound by legislation based on FIP. Consultants and lawyers build practices based on their expertise with FIP and its by-products. For many, the thought there might be other ways and methods to address privacy is foreign, dificult and, for all practical purposes, irrelevant. Why reinvent the wheel when FIP is so handy and widely employed? Even those who feel that they have experienced a privacy violation will have the legitimacy of their case leveled and rendered appropriate or inappropriate through FIP. Ones ability to participate is inscribed by the discourse that constructs it. The discourse and its inscriptions set parameters on issues of privacy, reducing its potential scope to a broad set of principles that can be translated into operational rules. In fact, we would argue that the widespread enlistment of FIP is based, in part, on its weak inscription efects through creating minimal obligations on governments and organizations. Its adoption places very few restrictions on organizational practices and goals, while creating the desired appearance of positive action, and is much easier to deal with than engaging in the quandary of vague ethical and philosophical questions that might otherwise arise. The label attached to the black box of FIP has, over time and repeated use, come to replace its contents and substance.

In actual content and despite the label, FIP are broadly defined principles that promote rather than prevent the movement of data. That was the general goal of the varied actants involved in the original construction of FIP, to protect the potential of ICT. Once these broad principles are translated into operational rules and these rules are satisfied, data movement is possible and legitimated. The executive version of FIP, the eight principles that have been widely adopted by legislators, policy makers, consultants and organizations alike, are a set of minimal standards that are, in practice, treated as the maximum. In many cases, these minimums have been further eroded in the enlistment by local actants, through the creation of exemptions from the legislation, as was the case with the MVR. In an interview, a former provincial cabinet minister and privacy commissioner ofered the following observation on how this shift from minimum to maximum takes place, in practice.

‘‘That happens so often. The minimum becomes the maximum. . . . When you get bureaucrats wanting to do something or being asked to do something, they go and interpret the law. Thats what they have to do. Any thought of more protection is pretty foreign. Quite often theyre being pressured to [discover], How are we going to get around this?’’

The truncation of content and history is compounded by the passage of time whereby new actants inherit and build on existing black boxes, long since sealed, that constrain discourse and bound understandings. From an ANT perspective, there is nothing inherently wrong with black boxes. Like a standardized word processing packages, there is little practical need for understanding how all of the software components work and interact with the hardware. Instead, we rely upon them to do the job, until something breaks down. In this sense we all employ existing black boxes for convenience. So do new actants dealing with issues of privacy in their oficial capacities. They appeal to existing standards and tools to frame the issue. FOIP and FIP are such tools that have become part of the oficial vernacular that address and shape privacy. The constitution and construction of these is perhaps unimportant for current actants dealing with current issues. For them, the tools are given and available for quick use in dealing with the issue.

As a result, 20 plus years after its development, FIP has come to constrain and shape debate on privacy, setting limits on the discourse and participation. The topic has drifted from being a vague and broad public concern to one narrowly concerned with rules about data and, in the process, has been transferred to the domain of experts who have mastered that discourse.<sup>19</sup> Policy discussions and legislation focus on FIP. Public discussions on issues of privacy are translated into issues of FIP, as is the case with the MVR. IS research has adopted FIP as a standard for ethical behavior for personal data handling (Culnan & Armstrong, 1999) or used the readiness of individuals to pursue opt-out options, a practical implementation of the Use Limitation Principle (Principle 4, Table 1), as a way to measure individual concern about privacy (Smith et al., 1996; Stewart & Segars, 2002). Commercial endeavors have come to appeal to FIP as a standard of good corporate behavior.<sup>20</sup> Those responsible for information systems development have come to see FIP, as it resides in FOIP, as a data-handling blueprint, defining the absolute limits of what is possible with respect to the use of personal information (Herriges & Hudson, 2002).<sup>21</sup> Even complaints about potential invasions of privacy have their legitimacy tested against the standard of FIP.

In addition to restricting the debate, FIP contains within it assumptions about the future shape of ICT, centralized computing, that have been invalidated by the subsequent evolution of distributed ICT.<sup>22</sup> The state of technology and the underlying assumptions are reflected in the following comment, ‘‘Because computerization of files normally leads to centralization, it is easier to institute and police controls on the handling of computerized personal information’’ (Task Force on Privacy & Computers, 1972, p. 20). Changes in ICT since FIPs construction have led to decentralized computing resources and increased the mobility and portability of data. These newer developments make particular assumptions embedded in FIP, such as individual empowerment to control the use of their information and the supposed ease of policing, dubious. For example, the Openness Principle (Principle 6) assumes that individuals will be aware of the location and possession of data about them. With widespread decentralized computing, individuals have little way of knowing where such information might reside, and if it even exists. This limits the ability of individuals to exercise control over subsequent use of the information (Principle 4), their ability to see and contest data (Principle 7), their knowledge of who is accountable (Principle 8), and their knowledge of the accuracy and security of the data (Principles 5 and 2).

Our findings suggest that FIPs role, as the ‘‘answer’’ to the question of how to protect individual privacy is questionable, given its contents. In Latourian terms, the concept of balance, the label, is substantively very diferent from its contents. The label alone implies fairness and neutrality. Its contents, though, reflect the enactment of an earlier balancing act, a tradeof between the protection of privacy and the protection of the flow of information.<sup>23</sup> That enactment was symmetrically constructed by the OECD actor-networks, whose collective interests were in ensuring the movement of data. It is then questionable how FIP can reasonably represent privacy, in subsequent balancing acts. If our conclusions are reasonable, this would point to a growing schism between the dominant discourse that masks the underlying standardization of data sharing and the existence of diverse alternative constructions, including a role for individuals, that may exist but are silenced by the din and constraints of that discourse. Evidence of their existence may be reflected in the counter-intuitive observation that motivated this research where more time and efort expended on privacy is failing to diminish concerns about it, which may be based on the paradox that the protector of privacy, FIP, has, in practice, the opposite efect.

It remains an open historical question as to how one might alternatively describe and characterize the public concerns raised in the 1960s and 1970s that led to the construction of FIP. Escalating concerns about privacy today suggest that undercurrents that led to the surface expression of what came to be labeled as privacy concerns, in the 1960s and 1970s, may have been severed and unaddressed by discourse derived from a focus on the modern computer. It might be prudent to shift focus from the volcano (the computer) and look for unacknowledged undercurrents that were and still are at play today.

The role of ICTs in creating this paradox is multiple and ongoing. It was potentials envisioned in the enlistment of ICTs that created the initial conflict and started the trajectory of subsequent events, described in this study. The Internet is another potential ICT actor as organizations create an online presence advertising their personal data tracking products or services (or being caught in the act of developing such products). The open nature of this technology may be shedding unwanted light not only on current practices but also past practices, as organizations use the technology to extend past of-line practices that had largely been invisible/unknown to outsiders (the public). The role of technology change and its impact on the practical ability of FIP to meet its minimum mandate has already been discussed. Technological change has also come to haunt commercial organizations in Canada, with the passage of private sector privacy legislation, as they attempt to comply with a requirement to disclose to individuals the personal information they possess about them, if requested. Personal data is scattered among a host of legacy systems making locating, gathering and making it available onerous. It is interesting to observe that what was acceptable to actants representing this group 20–30 years ago (FIP was an acceptable limitation) is seen as a burden on similarly situated actants today (FIP is technologically dificult, onerous and expensive). At the same time, an adherence to FIP has also failed to diminish privacy concerns.

## 6. Conclusions and future research

In this paper, we used ANT to conduct an historical investigation, to understand the present through the past. This was motivated by a paradox – that more and more efort is expended on the privacy issue and yet privacy concerns continue to increase. One particular example, the maintenance of the MVRs status quo, after the privacy audit, provided an important local and contingent event to explore this question.

ANT enabled us to shed light on threads of influence and action that come together in the present, illuminating what is obscured by the black boxes and inscription effects of FIP, FOIP, balance and ‘‘historical purposes and practices’’. Starting from a position of naive exploration, the ANT lens permitted insights into the resolution of the MVR conflict through tracing the development and subsequent production of the modern constitution of privacy.

We feel that this paper makes a number of important contributions to the understanding of the privacy issue, as well as the value of and extension of the use of the ANT lens in ICT research. With respect to privacy, the paradox is based in part on the implicit removal of the individual from the balancing act and the substitution of FIP as an appropriate surrogate by legislators, policy makers and organizations. The success of this substitution (and artifacts based on them, such as legislation or privacy policies and a host of privacy experts) depends on the enlistment or willing enrollment of individuals. Their physical presence may be unnecessary, but their acquiescence, their willingness to be enrolled, is necessary. We have to believe, given the failure of oficial eforts to diminish the issue, that this enrolment has not been entirely successful. One reason may lie in the dual character of the inscriptions efects of FIP. On the one hand, it appears to impose minimal inscription consequences on those adopting it and its enlistment and use replaces the messier questions of ethics, trust and moral responsibility. Over time, though, its enlistment has inscribed strict restrictions on the discourse about privacy, at least in the field of oficials and experts that have arisen around privacy. This is a consequence for these actants, but it tells us little about what individuals as actants are doing or believing and whether this discourse is relevant or being ignored. This raises interesting research challenges.

In this paper, we have also answered five specific calls by ICT researchers, with respect to ANT. Firstly, Walsham (1997) observed that most studies employing ANT have used it to interpret data already gathered. That was also the case in Walsham and Sahays later study, where ANT was employed with Structuration Theory to interpret gathered data (Walsham & Sahay, 1999). In this research, ANT informed the research from the start, from framing the questions to the gathering and interpretation of the data and findings. Secondly, this study has contributed to the call for more recognition of the importance and consequence of diferences in understandings across time (Scott & Wagner, 2003). Understandings of FIP are local and depend on the actants vantage point. In the field, consultants selling their privacy compliance services refer to FIP without understanding its history or origins. The positive view of FIP with commercial organizations, during FIPs construction the 1980, is not currently held by Canadian actants holding similar positions, a quarter of a century later. Thirdly, we hope to have demonstrated the value of resisting the acceptance of the obvious, in order to gain insights into the forces at play (McGrath, 2002). That suspension of belief, in resisting the accepted substance of privacy legislation by its title, led us to this study and its findings. Fourthly, ANT has traditionally been employed to study how scientists and engineers shape society (Kaghan & Bowker, 2001). We have extended that idea to reflect on the role of experts (those involved in the creation of FIP, legislators and legislation writers, policy interpreters, consultants, researchers, etc.) and the construction and acceptance of expertise in influencing the shape of important issues in society. Finally, we have engaged in a form of critique through revelation (Doolin & Lowe, 2002).<sup>24</sup>

We admit that there are practical dificulties in employing ANT. The most obvious is trying to capture in a short space the complexities and messiness of the subject investigated. We hope that we have managed to condense the material without straining the credibility of the reader. The forward presentation of events in the telling of the story masks the dificult, time-consuming and unexpected twists and turns that were encountered in following threads backwards, that made the forward telling possible. At the very least, we hope that we have been organized and convincing enough to provoke and generate new ideas and ways of looking at privacy and perhaps other areas, and the potential of ANT in investigating them.

We feel that much more research is needed in the area of privacy to better understand its complexity. We have already suggested the existence of unrecognized undercurrents that may be producing the paradox. We also see a disturbing absence of empirical investigations into the other side of the scale in the act of balancing. For instance, many claims are put forward in the name of law enforcement, security, eficiency, ‘‘improved service’’ and the economy that appear to be granted considerable substance, despite the absence of empirical evidence and critical evaluation of the constitution of that substance. In fact, it is our impression that the metaphor of a scale balancing privacy against other benefits sufers from three essential shortcomings. First, privacy is underrepresented, based on our findings. Calling it privacy is itself problematic as its focus in practice is on data, which ignores other potential aspects of privacy such as privacy of space, the body, information, thoughts, communication and as well as its links to freedom which is reflected in the title of Westins book, Privacy and Freedom (Westin, 1967). Furthermore, there are interaction efects between these facets of privacy, and it is unclear how data can be artificially separated these other forms of privacy. Secondly, the substance placed on the side of the scale opposite privacy is rarely if ever articulated or examined. This might be explained by the lack of need for substantiation, given privacys representative. One has to wonder, though, about the failure to meaningfully substantiate such claims on organizational trust and credibility in the minds of the broader public. There are few studies that examine this side of the scale. A few industry studies exist, but, to the best of our knowledge, no peer-reviewed studies exist. Finally, given the first two points, it is dificult to accept that any meaningful sense of balance is actually taking place. We have to conclude that the balance metaphor, as currently constituted in practice, is illusionary.

ANTs recognition of the co-construction of both human and non-human actants opens up a broad vista of IS research possibilities. For example, difusion concepts of ‘‘compatibility’’ and ‘‘relative advantage’’ would be productively investigated using concepts from ANT, such as ‘‘immutable mobiles’’ and the translation and transformation of diverse interest around an innovation or class of innovations. Use of ANT to investigate such concepts would complement Swansons (2002) research eforts, in tracing the life cycle of innovations and the construction of networks around popular IS and IT concepts, such as Enterprise Resource Planning systems and Customer Relationship Management systems. Such investigations would bring to the foreground a comprehensive ensemble picture of the interplay of near and distant actants and networks that produce and perpetuate these innovations, enabling IS researchers and practitioners to examine the construction of the underlying logic of these innovations. In addition, a call has already been made for more ‘‘ensemble research’’ examining ‘‘systems development’’, ‘‘production networks’’, ‘‘embedded’’ and ‘‘structure’’ perspectives (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). These goals would benefit from ANT-based investigations that focus on the interaction and redefinition of human and non-human actants in local contextual settings.

Bill Bonner received his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Calgary, and is now an assistant professor and CMA Faculty Fellow in MIS at the University of Regina. His research focus is on understanding privacy, the interaction of ICT potentials and diverse interests, understandings and actions in shaping and creating ideas and technologies, historical insights that shed light on active but obscured tensions, and understanding the formation of public policy with respect to ICT adoption, development and deployment.

Mike Chiasson received his Ph.D. in information systems and his postdoctoral in health promotion from the University of British Columbia, and is now an assistant professor at the Haskyane School of Business, University of Calgary. His research is increasingly focused on agency-structure issues in the development and implementation of information systems, in both traditional and non-traditional (e.g., entrepreneurial, professional and legal) contexts. His specific IT areas include web-based systems for e-commerce, and knowledge management systems. He is currently investigating the efect of emerging IS on entrepreneurial organizations, criminal and civil cases involving e-commerce and software development, and knowledge management systems in professional organizations.
