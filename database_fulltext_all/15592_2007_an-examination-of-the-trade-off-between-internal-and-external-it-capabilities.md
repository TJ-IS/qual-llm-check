# An examination of the trade-of between internal and external IT capabilities

Saggi Nevo <sup>\*</sup>, Michael R. Wade, Wade D. Cook

Schulich School of Business York University, Operations Management and Information Systems, #S337D – SSB, 4700 Keele Street, Toronto, Ont., Canada M3J 1P3

Received 25 March 2006; revised 7 September 2006; accepted 16 October 2006 Available online 27 November 2006

## Abstract

The business case for hiring external IT consultants is compelling. Consultants can represent a rich source of valuable, short-term capabilities. From a resource-based perspective, however, the fungible nature of these capabilities argues against their long-term strategic value. Furthermore, IT consultants may be at odds with existing internal capabilities. Institutional theory suggests that external consultants may not share the same norms and beliefs held by the internal staf and consequently their eforts in achieving organizational goals may be diluted or compromised. This paper explores the tension between internal and external IT capabilities on the realization of enhanced IT productivity. Drawing on telephone survey data on the adoption of Internet business solutions, the results of the study show that firms see tangible benefits from using external IT consultants, but these benefits are moderated by the level of existing internal IT capabilities. 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: IT consultants; IT capabilities; Data envelopment analysis; Resource-based view; Institutional theory; Transaction cost economics; Microeconomic theory; Complementarities

## 1. Introduction

While large IT outsourcing projects have dominated the headlines recently, outsourcing on a much smaller scale, through the use of external IT consultants, has been going on for decades. The efectiveness of using external IT consultants, however, varies greatly. When a firm’s internal IT capabilities are lacking, external IT capabilities (in the form of consultants) represent a very efective tool to pursue organizational goals (Massey and Walker, 1999; Thong et al., 1996). However, in some cases, external IT capabilities add no value beyond what can be provided by internal IT capabilities. It is also conceivable that conflicts may arise between internal and external IT capabilities that serve to diminish the overall productivity of organizational IT. Indeed, some research has uncovered great dissatisfaction with IT consultants (James, 1998; Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Violino, 2005).

This study is motivated by the mix of positive and negative experiences with IT consultants and is conducted with the goal of providing an explanation for the contradictory evidence. Surprisingly, little research has been directed toward these important issues and since diferent IT outsourcing configurations require diferent management practices (Cullen et al., 2005) it may not be efective to apply wisdom learned from other outsourcing configurations. Hence, in order to shed some light on this area, this study examines the relationship between internal IT capabilities, external IT capabilities, and IT productivity.

The impact of external IT capabilities, in the form of consultants, on IT productivity is important for at least four reasons. The first reason relates to scope. Many firms use external IT capabilities at some point in their lifecycles. In many cases, external IT capabilities form a larger component of overall spending than internal IT capabilities – a trend that may be increasing (IDC, 2005). The second reason is financial. A project involving 10 consultants lasting 6 months can cost more than one and half million dollars (Violino, 2005). Anecdotal evidence suggests that projects involving IT consultants often run over-time and over-budget, and at times conflicts arise between the consultants and the internal IT staf resulting in lower performance (Bettencourt et al., 2002). A third reason is learning and growth. Firms must balance the need for immediate help gained from outside resources, against the longer-term development of internal capabilities. A question faced by many firms is when to look for help outside the firm, and when to rely on internal capabilities. The fourth reason is the link between productivity and firm performance. Recent literature on the business value of IT has argued that improvements in IT productivity often lead to improvements in firm performance (Barua et al., 2004). Therefore, decisions on whether or not to utilize external IT capabilities could have an impact on firm performance. In this study, we explore salient factors that can help managers assess the potential contribution of external IT capabilities on the productivity of IT.

## 2. Previous work on IT consultants

The line between IT consulting and IT outsourcing has been blurred in recent years with firms like Accenture and IBM Global Services providing short-term and long-term contract work, project management and consultation, and application service provision (ASP). In this study we focus on short-term consulting services provided by external IT specialists for the purpose of implementing an IT solution at the contracting firm. Hence, we exclude large scale outsourcing projects, long-term (multi-year) service agreements, and on-demand technical services.

As IT projects become more strategic and complex, firms are increasingly drawing on external help from IT consultants (Beaumont and Costa, 2002; Gable, 2006; Lee et al., 2004; Yoon and Suh, 2004). This trend is particularly relevant in the case of e-business applications (Kishore et al., 2004–2005; Watjatrakul, 2005). However, there is a lack of agreement in the literature about the responsibilities of IT consultants. For example, Barthelemy (2001) claims that vendors’ sole job is to provide their clients with cutting-edge skills whereas Bettencourt et al. (2002) and Lacity et al. (1996) propose the need for a more inclusive view of organizational IT. When clients fail to collaborate with the vendor, they may contribute to project failure because much of the knowledge needed for a successful completion of a project is embedded in the minds of the in-house staf (Bettencourt et al., 2002). Moreover, the internal IT staf may play the role of spoiler if it feels threatened by an outside vendor or a knowledgeable internal IT workforce may be unwilling to internalize knowledge transferred from the vendor (Bettencourt et al., 2002). In rare cases, the internal IT staf may sabotage the work of external contractors. This tension between internal and external parties has been under-explored in the IT literature.

Firms hire IT consultants for a variety of reasons. They may not have suficient knowledge or expertise in-house, or they may be under time or budget pressures (Barthelemy, 2001; Gantz, 1990; Sengupta and Zviran, 1997). In tight labor markets, some firms hire IT consultants as an alternative to hard-to-find full time IT staf (Sengupta and Zviran 1997). IT consultants are often hired for non-routine functions, such as software and Web application development, project management, or benchmarking. Firms may also draw on the accumulated expertise of IT consultants in the hope that they transfer this knowledge to internal IT staf (Barthelemy, 2001; Koh et al., 2004; Willcocks et al., 2004).

Much of the work on external IT capabilities has focused on the measurement of perception-based client satisfaction from vendor services (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Sengupta and Zviran, 1997; Yoon and Suh, 2004), or the development of best practices (Barthelemy, 2001; Gantz, 1990). According to these studies, IT consultants have received mixed reviews as clients cite high costs of projects, low service quality, and lack of knowledge transfer as antecedents of discontent (James, 1998; Lacity and Willcocks, 1998; Yoon and Suh, 2004). In another study, Beaumont and Costa (2002), raised questions pertaining to situations where the use of IT consultants is not efective. The lack of consistency in the research findings is unfortunate considering the ever-increasing use of IT consultants to develop and manage IT projects. Consequently, Lee et al. (2004) and others have called for additional research on the relationship between outsourcing practices and productivity.

Studies on contract workers, such as consultants, raise several issues that could hinder the realization of benefits from using ‘‘professional strangers’’ (Pfefer, 1994). The extent to which IT consultants are successful in their eforts depends, to a large degree, on the commitment of the client to the project, on the skills of the consultants, on the collaboration between the client and the vendor, and on the level of information sharing between the client and the vendor (Bettencourt et al., 2002; George and Chattopadhyay, 2005; Slaughter and Ang, 1996). Researchers also list mutual trust, open and honest communication, the availability of in-house dedicated staf, and contract management as necessary for the successful execution of outsourcing projects (George and Chattopadhyay, 2005). The existence of a cultural fit between the two organizations and the understanding of the client’s business goals, as well as the appointment of a liaison person to serve as an interface between the client and the vendor, are all considered conducive to the realization of mutual benefits (Kern and Willcocks, 2002; Willcocks et al., 2004).

Kishore et al., 2004–2005 argue that since e-business applications are typically more complex than traditional IT applications, it is important to study the efects of using IT consultants for developing and managing such applications. Among the reasons cited for the increased complexity of e-commerce applications are the fact that they serve as an interface between the organization and its clients, and because they often involve multiple stakeholders with potentially conflicting agendas (Lacity et al., 1996).

## 3. Theory and hypotheses

The extent to which firms should utilize external IT capabilities rather then rely on internal capabilities has theoretical relevance. This study draws on five theoretical perspectives to derive conflicting hypotheses regarding the balance that firms should strike between internal and external IT capabilities. The five theories are the resource-based view of the firm (RBV), microeconomic theory (MET), transaction cost economics (TCE), institutional theory (INT), and identification theory (IDT).

The resource-based view states that firms can earn sustainable, above-normal returns to the extent that they possess rare and valuable resources, and that isolating mechanisms exist to prevent the dissemination of those resources to competing firms (Barney, 1986; Barney, 1991; Wernerfelt, 1984). Thus, the theory acknowledges that some firms can outperform others even in eficient and competitive markets.

Microeconomic theory studies the distribution of production and income among firms and industries, and the economic behavior of consumers. The theory assumes that organizational decision makers are rational in their pursuit of eficient organizational designs, and further assumes that organizations operate in perfectly competitive environments. Unlike the RBV, MET assumes that above-normal economic returns will be competed away by rivals or new entrants. Production decisions – i.e., the conversion of inputs, like IT, into outputs – focus on what goods to produce, how to produce them, and how to optimize the mix of resource inputs used in production. This production information is combined with market information to determine the optimal quantity of products and their prices (Varian, 1992; Williamson, 1981).

Transaction cost economics (Coase, 1937; Williamson, 1981) explores factors leading to the formation of firms and is mostly interested in identifying situations in which an internal structure outperforms market-based interactions among buyers and sellers. The theory is based on the notion that in the absence of complete information about a given asset, such as an IT product or a service, a firm might be better of producing that asset itself and maintaining full control over it. This conclusion is based on the idea that firms seek to minimize costs associated with economic transactions. Transactions costs are the result of asset specificity, transaction frequency, and behavioral and environmental uncertainties (Watjatrakul, 2005). Empirical evidence suggests that in the case of IT, asset specificity – i.e., the contextualized value of the asset – dominates transaction frequency and uncertainty in terms of its impact on the transaction cost (Watjatrakul, 2005). The commodity-like nature of IT makes it an ideal candidate for market-based solutions (Aubert et al., 1996; Ngwenyama and Bryson, 1999).

Institutional theory rejects the premise that organizational phenomena are the products of rational choices. Instead, it focuses on organizational routines and on pressures to achieve and maintain legitimacy. As with MET, INT acknowledges that organizations seek to maximize eficiency, but unlike MET, it explicitly recognizes cognitive and institutional constraints (Baum and Oliver, 1992; Burns and Wholey, 1993; Roberts and Greenwood, 1997). INT argues that the success of IT projects depends not only on technical factors, but also on factors such as trust, relationships, and personal beliefs and goals, and can enhance our understanding of IT-related phenomena (Cousins and Robey, 2005; Lewis et al., 2003; Teo et al., 2003).

Identification theory explains the processes that cause individuals to identify with, and attach themselves to a group. The theory explores how an individual derives values and emotional meaning from group membership. According to IDT, individuals define and categorize themselves and at the same time align their values in relation to the characteristics and actions of the group. Enacting a set of activities that are congruent with an individual’s identity strengthens his or her alignment with the group and builds attachment (Peteraf and Shanley, 1997). Group identification has been found to play a role in determining the outcome of IT projects (Schwarz and Watson, 2005).

These five theories employ very diferent interpretations of the way individuals, groups, and organizations behave and compete. At times they agree, while at other times they predict diferent outcomes (see Table 1). Since the efect of IT consultants on organizations is multi-faceted, with outcomes at diferent levels, it was felt that choosing a single conceptual foundation would be unduly restrictive. Thus, rather than select one theory for this study, all five theories were employed to provide (sometimes competing) views of the underlying phenomena. By employing more than one conceptual perspective, this study explores the tension between diferent theories.

The resource-based view suggests that firms can achieve positive organizational outcomes to the extent that they possess rare, valuable, and appropriable capabilities (Barney, 1991). Further, those gains can be sustained if firm capabilities resist imitation and substitution (Wade and Hulland, 2004). Individual components of IT infrastructure may be valuable, but they are unlikely to be rare. By contrast, internal IT capabilities in the form of accumulated, firm-specific IT knowledge and experience, is likely to be both valuable and rare. Due to the inherent complexity of these elements, internal IT capabilities may be hard to imitate or substitute, at least over the short term. In summary, everything else being equal, we expect to find a positive association between strong internal IT capabilities and IT productivity. Thus,

Hypothesis 1 (H1). The productivity of IT is positively associated with higher levels of internal IT capabilities.

Some IS researchers argue that IT skills and knowledge are not firm-specific assets and can be acquired from the market. For example, according to (Mata et al. (1995), p. 498), ‘‘[t]he know-how needed to build IT applications using the available technology and to operate them to make products or provide services. . . are usually not heterogeneously distributed across firms. . . (or) are highly mobile. . . (Therefore) firms without the required analysis, design, and programming skills required to make IT investments can hire technical consultants and contractors’’.

According to TCE, non-specific assets do not pose a hold-up problem and, consequently, are associated with lower transactions costs (Watjatrakul, 2005). Thus, IT capabilities become a good candidate for outsourcing. Additionally, since TCE suggests that outsourcers often exhibit economies of scale (Jayatilaka et al., 2003), and due to important characteristics of IT such as high availability and afordability (Clemons and Row, 1991), the transaction cost associated with an internal solution for an IT project would surpass that of a market-based solution. Consequently, economies of scale of IT outsourcers are expected to lead to an increase in IT productivity because an equal or better IT capability is obtained at an equal or lesser cost. Hence,

Hypothesis 2 (H2). The productivity of IT is positively associated with a market-based solution for IT capabilities.

Microeconomic, institutional, and identification theories disagree with the above claim and argue that the strength of the internal IT capability should be taken into account when organizations contemplate hiring external IT capabilities.

To the extent that internal IT capabilities are high (i.e., above average) and high quality external IT capabilities are used, MET argues that these two factors of production are substitutes, and would thus provide no net benefit. However, other research suggests that the usefulness of external know-how depends on the ability of the organization to put it to use (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Zahra and George, 2002). If external know-how provided by external IT consultants adds little to internal knowledge, then it may be of little use and result in failure to create synergies (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). In other words, if the organization hires external IT consultants when its IT staf possesses relevant knowledge and expertise, it will not realize additional benefits because the two groups provide similar and redundant functionality. Thus,

Hypothesis 3a (H3a). Given high internal IT capabilities, the productivity of IT will b unafected in a firm that utilizes external IT capabilities.

It is conceivable, however, that the combination of high or strong internal IT capabilities coupled with external IT capabilities may produce a negative outcome. According to INT, the efectiveness of organizational activities depends on the legitimacy assigned to them by relevant constituents. Activities that lack legitimacy may face active or passive resistance and are therefore less likely to achieve their intended goals. If a firm with strong internal IT capabilities (say an experienced IT staf) hires external capabilities (say a group of IT consultants), resentment and conflict may arise. The external IT capabilities may lack legitimacy and will not be efectively used by the firm. Thus, synergies with internal IT capabilities may not accrue as expected.

Further, identification theory suggests that internal IT staf may perceive external IT consultants as a threat, or a signal that its own legitimacy is in jeopardy. Under such circumstances, the collaboration between internal and external IT capabilities will not be efective in achieving the goals of the organization, i.e., improvements in organizational performance (Ashforth and Mael, 1989; Currie and Seltsikas, 2001; Hussin et al., 2002; Massey and Walker, 1999; Newman and Sabherwal, 1996; Suchman, 1995). Moreover, a more experienced in-house staf may be less flexible and may even exhibit certain behav iors that are not conducive to project success (Bettencourt et al., 2002). Thus,

Hypothesis 3b (H3b). Given high internal IT capabilities, the productivity of IT will be lower in a firm that utilizes external IT capabilities.

## 4. Research methodology

The study uses data drawn from the Net Impact Study (Varian et al., 2002). The purpose of this study is to collect data on past and expected impacts of Internet business solutions (IBS) at the firm level. Since IBS often span departments within the organization or even across organization boundaries, the Net Impact Study presents an opportunity to test our hypotheses on some of the most complex and sophisticated forms of organizational IT (Kishore et al., 2004–2005).

The selection of the sample frame was done in a manner conducive to increasing the accuracy of the data. In particular, a respondent had to be a senior decision-maker with a formal job responsibility for designing, purchasing, or implementing and IBS for his or her organization. A respondent was also required to be familiar with the measuremen tools (e.g., financial metrics) used to track the impact of IBS on the organization.

Data on 2065 American firms were collected by means of a telephone questionnaire. The questionnaire was designed and pre-tested by the researchers, and administered b a professional research firm. The questionnaire took approximately 25 min to complete. For the purposes of the study, a sample of 111 firms was selected from the full sample, for which complete information about all the inputs used and all the outputs produced in the process were available. To avoid the possibility of large-scale confounding efects, the sample contained only firms that owned and operated their IBS internally. Thus, firms that outsourced their IT entirely were not included in the sample.

## 5. Variables and measures

Information technology is ‘‘[h]ardware, software, telecommunications, and the services of IT personnel, whether employed in-house or purchased’’. (Oz, 2005, p. 790). Hence, five input variables were used in the analysis, each measured by their dollar investment amount. These variables were ‘‘communication and networking hardware purchases’’, ‘‘communication services purchases’’, ‘‘computing hardware purchases’’, ‘‘total software purchases’’, and ‘‘other personnel and consulting purchases’’. The three output variables were measured as a percentage change. They were ‘‘revenues’’, ‘‘costs of goods sold (COGS)’’, and ‘‘sales, general and administrative costs (SG&A)’’. The input variables were converted into percentages to enable a scale-free measurement. Number of employees, as a proxy for firm size, was included in the analysis as a control variable.

In accordance with recent literature on external capabilities (Vogus and Welbourne, 2003) the decision to use or not to use external IT capabilities was operationalized as a dummy variable with a value of one (1) being assigned to firms who decide in favor of using external IT consultants and a value of zero (0) to those who choose to rely on internal IT capabilities only.

Internal IT capability is a multi-faceted construct. For example, IT infrastructure (Byrd and Turner, 2001; Zhu, 2004), IT change management (Sherer et al., 2003), and IT planning (Zhuang and Lederer, 2004) are some of its dimensions. In this study we focused on a dimension of internal IT capability that taps into the organization’s knowledge of, and familiarity with, its own IT infrastructure.

Accordingly, two variables were used to operationalize internal IT capabilities: the years that have passed since the organization’s first experience with IBS (time-passedsince-first-IBS-experience), and the number of IBS (# of IBS) owned by the organization. These variables were employed as proxies for levels of IBS-related knowledge and expertise – hence, stronger internal IT capabilities. The first of these two measures is similar to the one used by Grover et al. (1999) to measure IT knowledge and experience. The second measure is comparable to other measures of IT adoption, which were used to operationalize IT capability (e.g., Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997).

The efect of internal IT capabilities on external IT capabilities (i.e., IT consultants) was measured through an interaction efect. Operationally, this was done through a multiplication of each of the two proxies for internal IT capabilities by the use of IT consultants.

While the organizational IBS could have been partially or completely developed by a 3rd-party, IBS-related knowledge would still be accumulated from on-going activities such as operation, use, and maintenance (Sabherwal and Sabherwal, 2005).

## 6. Results

The data analysis was conducted in two distinct stages. The first stage utilized data envelopment analysis (DEA), a nonparametric tool, to identify the firms with the highest IT productivity. Because DEA does not pre-assume any functional form that prescribes the way in which inputs are turned into output, it frees us from the need to conjecture about the theoretical production function. DEA can be used to rank-order firms based on their IT productivity.

This methodology, developed by Charnes et al. (1978), is a mathematical programming model for evaluating the eficiencies of a set of n decision making units (DMUs), relative to one another. The method, which has been used to investigate productivity-related ques tions in the IS field only recently, has been used to assess input productivity in many different environments, including sets of bank branches, hospitals, schools, maintenance units, software, and so on.<sup>1</sup> (Banker and Slaughter, 1997; Cook et al., 2000; Herrero and Salmeron, 2005; Lee and Menon, 2000; Seiford and Zhu, 1999; Shao and Lin, 2002).

The original model of Charnes et al. (1978), assumed a constant returns to scale (CRS) situation prevailed. In order to accommodate situations where variable sizes of DMUs are present, this model was later extended by (Banker et al. (1984)) to encompass variable returns to scale (VRS).

In the typical problem setting, each DMU is characterized by a vector of outputs $Y _ { j } = ( y _ { r j } )$ , and a vector of inputs $X _ { j } = ( x _ { i j } )$ . One measure of eficiency $e _ { \mathrm { o } }$ of any DMU $" \boldsymbol { 0 } ^ { \flat }$ is given by the solution to a particular non-linear, non-convex fractional programming problem. Specifically, let $\nu = \left( \nu _ { i } \right)$ be vector of multipliers to be applied to the vector of inputs. Then the aggregate or weighted total input available to DMU ‘‘o’’ is given by the inner product $\nu X _ { 0 } = \textstyle \sum _ { i } \nu _ { i } x _ { i }$ . Similarly, letting $\mu = \left( \mu _ { r } \right)$ denote a vector of multipliers of the outputs, the aggregate output is then given by $\mu Y _ { \mathrm { o } }$ . Charnes et al. then argue that a reasonable measure of the eficiency or productivity of DMU ‘‘o’’ is given by the ratio of the aggregate output to aggregate input $\mu Y _ { \mathrm { o } } / \nu X _ { \mathrm { o } }$ . This measure is analogous to the benefit to cost ratio created by the DMU. More precisely, Charnes et al. argue that the most optimistic expression of the overall eficiency of the DMU ‘‘o’’ is created by choosing the set of multipliers that maximizes this ratio for the DMU in question. This provides for what is termed the CRS measure of technical eficiency. Banker et al. (1984) have extended this idea to create the VRS model of eficiency, by adding one additional scalar variable x. In formal terms, the VRS model of Banker et al. is given as the solution to the set of fractional programming problems:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max \frac {u Y _ {\mathrm{o}} + \omega}{v X _ {\mathrm{o}}}, \\ \text { subject   to: } \\ \frac {u Y _ {j} + \omega}{v X _ {j}} \leqslant 1, \quad j = 1, \ldots , n, \\ u, v \geqslant 0, \quad \omega \text { unrestricted. } \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The restrictions or constraints on the model require that any multipliers used must be such that this ratio does not exceed 100% for any of the DMUs. To facilitate solving this non-linear form, Charnes et al. (1978) have shown that (1) can be represented by the more tractable linear programming equivalent:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \max \mu Y _ {\mathrm{o}} + \omega \\ \text { subject   to: } \\ v X _ {\mathrm{o}} = 1, \\ \mu Y _ {j} + \omega - v X _ {j} \leqslant 0, j = 1, \ldots , n, \\ \mu , v \geqslant 0, \quad \omega \text { unrestricted }, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

and its dual formulation

$$
\begin{array}{l} \min \theta \\ \text { subject   to } \\ \theta X _ {\mathrm{o}} - \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {j} X _ {j} \geqslant 0 \\ \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {j} X _ {j} \leqslant Y _ {\mathrm{o}} \\ \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {n} \lambda_ {j} = 1 \\ \lambda_ {j} \geqslant 0, j = 1, \dots , n \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

In (3) h is the eficiency score. If for example, $\theta = . 8 0$ , then the DMU in question would be seen as ineficient. Furthermore, a $2 0 \% ( 1 - . 8 0 )$ reduction in each input would be required to render them DMU eficient (bring it to the eficient frontier). The $\lambda _ { j }$ are the multipliers applied to the various DMUs. If, for example, $\lambda _ { 1 }$ and $\lambda _ { 2 }$ are both positive (say $\lambda _ { 1 } = 7 5 \%$ and $\lambda _ { 2 } = 2 5 \% )$ then the DMU in question is being compared to two peers, DMU #1 &

DMU #2, and is ‘‘more’’ like DMU #1. Alternatively, one can view the $\lambda _ { j }$ as saying that the ‘‘eficient’’ version of the DMU in question would be in the convex hull of DMUs 1 and 2.

The goal of the DEA analysis is to compare the diferent units on the basis of their ability to transform inputs into outputs. The basis for this comparison is the score given by the objective function value of (2) or (3).

One outcome of the DEA analysis is a rank ordering of the DMUs from the data set, based on their relative eficiencies in converting inputs to outputs. The scores obtained provide an understanding as to which firms are more productive than others are, without having to pre scribe a predetermined parametric relationship between the input and the output variables.

From the dataset, a subset of 111 firms was chosen for which complete data were avail able on all five inputs and three outputs. Because the firms represent diferent industries, we chose the VRS model for the best-practice frontier. Additionally, because the dataset contained a small number of not-for-profit organizations, it was decided to proceed using an input-oriented model. The input-oriented model is more appropriate for situations where organizations attempt to minimize costs rather than maximize profits. The results for both models are generally very similar.

Executing the DEA Solver (Zhu, 2002) on the set of firms resulted in a rank ordering of firms based on their relative IT productivity. A higher-ranking firm was able to convert hardware, software, telecommunication technologies, and IT professionals’ knowledge and efort into higher revenues and/or lower costs of good and sales and lower administration expenses relative to a low-score firm.

The firms were then divided into three equally large groups based on their relative scores. Group one contained the lowest ranking 37 firms; group two contained the middle firms, while the third group contained the 37 best performers according to the results of the DEA analysis. The hypotheses were then tested by a logistic regression analysis.

The logistic model examined the probability of being classified as a best performer (high IT productivity) vs. a worst performer (low IT productivity). The middle group was excluded to make the diferences between the remaining two groups more evident, thus assisting in the identification of the discriminating factors. Table 4 presents three measures of goodness of fit of the model to the data. The various goodness of fit measures indicate that the model is adequate.

The results of the logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 5. As expected, IT productivity was positively associated with the number of IBS systems. Clearly, having prior experience with IBS implementation helps a firm implement new IBS more efectively. The second measure of IT capabilities – time-since-first-using-IBS – was not significant (although its coeficient was positive as expected). Thus, hypothesis 1 was partially supported by the data.

The direct efect of IT consultants on IT productivity was positive. This is in accordance with hypothesis 2 that postulated a positive relationship between external IT capabilities and high IT productivity.

The interaction efect between external IT capabilities and internal IT capabilities was significant. Hypothesis 3a (H3a) proposed that external IT consultants would have no efect on IT productivity in the presence of strong internal IT capabilities. The data did not support this hypothesis. To see this, note the negative sign of the interaction efect between IT Consultants and # of IBS (a measure of internal IT capability). The negative interaction efect means that if a firm decides to hire IT consultants (i.e., an external IT capability) when its internal IT capability is suficiently high it will experience a decline in overall IT productivity.

## 7. Discussion

The findings of this study support the contention that external IT capabilities can increase IT productivity. This efect appears to be especially pronounced in cases where internal IT capabilities are weak. This result supports transaction cost economics by providing evidence showing that turning to factor markets – in the form of IT consultants – is an economical solution for firms with weak internal IT capabilities. When the internal IT capability is weak, developing and implementing an IT solution is likely to cost more compared with hiring external IT consultants to do the same job. One cautious interpretation of this finding suggests that by turning to external IT capabilities, the firm can benefit from the economies of scale associated with the knowledge and expertise of the IT consultants.

The findings for firms with strong internal IT capabilities were more interesting and complex. The data support resource-based arguments that internal IT capabilities can lead to positive IT-based productivity benefits, as long as those capabilities are valuable and rare. However, this positive afect can be confounded by the existence of external IT capabilities (Watjatrakul, 2005).

Microeconomic theory considers strong internal IT capabilities and external IT capabil ities, in the form of consultants, as substitutes. According to this view, using external IT consultants when internal IT capabilities are strong would add no net benefit to the firm as the two groups provide redundant functionality. Interestingly, the results did not support the view that given strong internal IT capabilities, external IT capabilities would have no efect on IT productivity

Instead, the data appeared to favor institutional and identity-based concepts. Institutional theory suggests that external IT consultants will not receive the legitimacy they require if they are perceived as a threat to the incumbent IT staf, or if their knowledge and expertise do not difer from that possessed by the in-house IT team. Under these circumstances, external IT consultants’ impact on IT productivity is expected to be lower. The data showed that if a firm decided to hire external IT capabilities for the purpose of implementing IBS when they already had suficient, relevant expertise internally, then the outcome would be sub-optimal (i.e., these firms were not among the most productive according to the DEA analysis). This result could be interpreted as a reflection of low legitimacy, an issue considered by institutional and identification theory to be an important component of efective activity accomplishment.

## 8. A further exploration of the trade-of between strong internal and external IT capabilities

When the relationship between internal and external IT capabilities is plotted on a graph (see Fig. 2), it is possible to observe an interesting moderating efect. From Fig. 2 it is clear that when a firm has a small number of IBS systems, that is, when its internal IT capabilities (IITC) are low, hiring IT consultants will have a net positive efect on IT productivity (measured as the vertical distance between the dashed line and the upward sloping line to the left of $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 } )$ . However, if the firm has already integrated many similar systems and has presumably developed adequate knowledge, the firm would be better of not hiring IT consultants and instead relying on its internal IT capabilities. The net (negative) efect of resorting to external expertise rather then relying on internal capabilities is measured as the vertical distance between the dashed line and the upward sloping line (to the right of $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 } )$ . Our exploratory analysis shows that for high levels of internal IT capabilities, the net efect on IT productivity following the decision to hire external IT consultants is negative.

Our results suggest that the optimal decision strategy is to hire external IT consultants when internal IT capabilities are low and to rely solely on the knowledge and expertise of the internal IT staf when these are of moderate or better quality. From Fig. 2 it is apparent that to the left of $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 } .$ , the decision should be to rely on external as well as internal IT capabilities, whereas to the right of $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 } .$ , the firm should rely solely on its internal IT staf. $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 }$ – the level of internal IT capabilities above which the firm should not hire IT consultants is expected to vary from firm to firm. This cut-of level is a function of the firm’s IT capabilities, its ability to legitimize the consultant team, and to foster healthy collaboration between the external and internal IT groups. Firms are advised to evaluate their internal IT knowledge before hiring external IT consultants. Failure to do so may in fact hurt the firm instead of helping it.

Further analysis of our data reveals that the cut-of point beyond which firms may not wish to use IT consultants is four $( \mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 } = 4 )$ IBS systems. While we recognize that this number may be an artifact of this study’s sample, it is nevertheless interesting to note that it is also significantly smaller than the average number of IBS implemented per firm – six. Thus, on average, firms in our sample appeared to be drawing on the expertise of IT consultants beyond the point where it is eficient to do so. After a certain point, IT consultants failed to provide any positive spillover benefits to these firms. The data suggest that a moderate quality of internal IT capabilities may be suficient to ofset advantages accruing from the (perhaps) superior knowledge of external IT consultants.

## 9. Conclusions

No study is without limitations, and this one is no exception. First, DEA provides only relative productivity measures thus allowing a comparison among firms but not an estimation of compared to a theoretical maximum. Second, because DEA is a non-parametric technique, statistical tests of goodness-of-fit are not available. Third, as with any cross-sectional study, establishing causality is rather limited and results should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, the operationalization of the internal IT capability construct was measured using only two variables, namely, number of IBS implemented and the time that has passed since the first IBS was implemented. Fifth, the selection of the sample of 111 firms used in the analysis was drawn from a larger sample of 2065 firms based on the completeness of data, rather than on a probabilistic approach. Finally, we were not able to examine the efect of IT capabilities on intermediate-level output variables, and had to instead rely on firm-level measures.

Despite these limitations, this study makes several contributions to both practice and research. First, this study has demonstrated that external IT capabilities, in the form of consultants, can provide skills and expertise that are not in the possession of a firm, thus enabling it to increase its IT productivity. This increase could help a firm achieve superior performance. If, however, a firm already possesses proficient internal IT capabilities, the benefits of external capabilities may not be realized. Based on our preliminary findings, even moderate internal experience with IT is suficient to outweigh the benefits provided by external IT consultants. These findings support propositions advanced by other researchers (Willcocks et al., 2004). These researchers suggested that clients repeatedly underestimate the capabilities of in-house teams and often overlook their ability to understand unique organizational aspects not recognized by external professionals. Further, the internal IT staf may perceive the external IT consultants as a threat, resulting in conflict and ultimately lower IT productivity. Because the productivity of IT depends on complementary relationships between various resources and factors of production, such as IT professionals (Lee et al., 2004), firms should evaluate their internal IT capabilities before making a decision to hire external IT capabilities. If the in-house IT staf is capable of implementing the IT solution on its own and if the potential for conflict is high, this study suggests that the firm is better of relying solely on internal IT capabilities.

This study makes an additional contribution by demonstrating that internal IT capabilities need not possess cutting-edge skills to match, or even outperform, external IT capabilities. Due to the intimate knowledge of organization’s culture and goals, as well as the efects of IT applications and infrastructure on diferent business units can, an internal IT staf is able to compensate for its inexperience. This outcome is consistent with the systemic view of organizations that takes into account synergies from complementary interactions among their various components (Cullen et al., 2005). In other words, the co-evolution of internal IT staf and other organizational resources create synergies that external IT consultants, who for the most part provide a commodity-like service, cannot match.

Researchers have suggested that firms can benefit by outsourcing its IT selectively – i.e., diversify it outsourcing portfolio across multiple suppliers (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). More recently, the notion of selectivity was extended to include dynamic outsourcing configurations (Cullen et al., 2005). This study adds to this important research stream by arguing that selectivity should be a temporal issue as well. That is, that firms should continuously assess their internal IT capabilities and consider in-sourcing when these capabilities are suficiently strong to of-set any advantages associated with the market-based solution. Hence a further contribution of this study is in extending the contingency theory of management of IT outsourcing (Cullen et al., 2005).

Another contribution of this study is associated with its empirical nature. A recent article (Watjatrakul, 2005) cited the lack of empirical research in the IT outsourcing as an important gap in our understanding of the topic and in our ability to prescribe conditions under which outsourcing makes a sound business practice. We believe that this study makes a step in the right direction by informing academics and practitioners alike about the appropriateness of one type of IT outsourcing configuration – namely, hiring IT consultants.

## 10. Implications for managers

In this paper, we identify an important factor that can help managers assess the potential contribution of external IT capabilities to organizational performance. Managers should assess their internal IT capabilities before considering using external IT consultants. If the internal IT capabilities are strong, or even moderate, there may not be a need to bring in additional IT personnel. Instead, the firm would be better of spending its limited resources on a continued upgrade of its internal IT staf capabilities. External IT consultants should only be hired when it is clear that without them the IT project would not be completed on time and within budget and when the internal IT staf has much to learn from the consultants. Conversely, if the in-house IT team could complete the project on its own and has similar knowledge and expertise to those possessed by the external consultants, the efectiveness of the consultants will diminish and may even result in negative outcomes for the firm. Based on the analysis, we find that the cutof point beyond which firms are better of relying solely on $\mathrm { I I T C } _ { 0 }$ is about four IBS systems. Apparently, the experience gained from implementing four IBS solutions in the past provides organizations with a strong enough IT capability that renders the external know-how superfluous.

It is important to note that among the many reasons firms cite for using IT consultants is the novelty of the technology coupled with the lack of suficient in-house knowledge to put the technology into proper use. Managers should be aware that projects involving the outsourcing of new and complex technologies might result in lower satisfaction because it is highly possible that the consultants themselves do not have suficient expertise with the technology (Willcocks and Plant, 2003). Consequently, managers are advised not to settle for an assessment of the capabilities of internal staf, but also to conduct a similar assessment of the capabilities of external IT consultants. This study provides a decision-supporting mechanism that can be used for a comparison between the internal and external IT capabilities and ultimately to make a better IT sourcing decision.

In summary, top managers who subscribe to the concepts of the RBV and TCE and justify outsourcing decisions by citing either the non-strategic nature or the non-specificity of IT could benefit by considering the many complex relationships among IT and other organizational resources and capabilities. While IT, in and of itself, may not be strategic or specific, it may form a composite asset (e.g., with internal IT capabilities) that possess a high strategic value or that is highly specific. In cases such as this, an outsourcing configuration may not be the most viable solution.

## 11. Future directions

Although the results of this study are based on strong statistical analyses, the tension between internal and external IT capabilities should be examined by developing appropriate scales for measuring the quality of the two constructs. The IS community would benefit further from qualitative studies in which a small number of organizations that hire IT consultants are studied in order to gain better understandings into the mindsets of both internal and external IT professionals and the outcomes of their interactions. Finally, we encourage researchers to devise sophisticated ways for measuring resource complementarities and organizational synergies in an efort to establish the value of internal IT capabilities in particular, and of IT in general.
