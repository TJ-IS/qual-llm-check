Keywords: Community governance Community credibility Community vigilance Measurement instrument Content use

## A B S T R A C T

Wiki technology enables organizational members to collectively verify content and increase the accuracy, relevancy, and legitimacy of contributions. This is referred to as community governance in this paper. The goals of this paper are to understand the nature and dimensionality of the perceptions of community governance construct, operationalize it, and examine its role in content use from wikis. Data analyses show that perceptions of community governance can be measured as a second-order construct with two first-order factors: community credibility and community vigilance. Further, perceptions of community governance are positively associated with content usefulness, which explains content use from community-governed wikis.

## 1. Introduction

Recent reports suggest that the number of organizations that use wikis to revitalize knowledge transfer and knowledge use is increasing [19,40]. Wikis are different from traditional electronic repositories used for knowledge management in that wikis help organizations leverage the collective efforts of users to not only generate content but also verify existing content [37]. Content verification, defined as increasing the accuracy, relevancy, or legitimacy of content [28], is important, because content that is not properly verified is less likely to be used by employees, which in turn undermines knowledge management efforts (see Refs. [28,58]). While traditional content verification involves a peerreview process, where a select group of experts check the accuracy, relevancy, and legitimacy of content, wikis use a ‘‘crowd-sourced’’ approach, where content verification can be performed by all wiki users. We refer to the mechanism with which content is verified in wikis as community governance hereafter.

Prior work on wikis suggests that users of wikis have an awareness of the content verification mechanism employed in wikis [27,37]. In other words, wiki users develop perceptions of community governance and use these perceptions as they interact with wikis. However, prior literature does not shed much light on these perceptions. Therefore, the goals of this study are to understand the nature of the perceptions of community governance, and how these perceptions influence content use from wikis. The goals of this paper are motivated by two reasons. First, the concept of community governance is one of the most salient features of wikis that distinguish them from other types of electronic repositories. However, our understanding of users’ perceptions of community governance is very limited as we still do not know how to measure them or incorporate them into existing theoretical frameworks. This is preventing us from unraveling the value propositions of wikis, understanding how wikis, vis-a\`-vis other types of electronic repositories, contribute to knowledge management efforts, and how wikis facilitate the development and reuse of organizational memory [36,37]. Therefore, investigating perceptions of community governance is an initial step toward filling these gaps in the literature.

Second, using content from electronic repositories, including wikis, is critical for many organizations not only for performing day-to-day activities but also for gaining competitive advantage [3,39]. Although organizations are increasingly implementing electronic repositories, and community-governed wikis in particular, scholars and industry reports indicate that knowledge use from these repositories is still a problem [37,54]. Therefore, there are calls for research to increase our understanding of content use from wikis [36]. This study addresses this gap by studying the role of perceptions of community governance in content use from wikis.

The specific research questions of interest to this study are as follows: (1) What are the nature and dimensionality of perceptions of community governance? (2) How can perceptions of community governance be measured? (3) What is the role of perceptions of community governance in content use from community-governed wikis?

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we survey the existing literature on content use. In the following section, we describe the nature and dimensionality of perceptions of community governance by drawing upon earlier work. In the fourth section, we propose the measurement model, and then draw upon the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) to conduct nomological validity. In the fifth section, we discuss the research methods employed in this study. In the sixth section, the results of our analyses are presented. In the final section, we discuss our key findings as well as the theoretical and practical implications of this study.

## 2. Background

In order to see if extant literature considered the perceptions of community governance in explaining content use, we focus on prior work that examines content use from electronic repositories. The most commonly used approach to explaining content use from electronic repositories is the use of dual-process theories such as the ELM. However, studies that use dual-process theories do not lend themselves to comparison, because they focus on a variety of contexts and explain different types of dependent variables. Some of the dependent variables examined in the literature include decision to adopt expert-system recommendations [38], persuasiveness of an expert system [14,15], self-reported repository use for technical support [20], and adoption of information obtained from e-mail communications [57] and discussion forums [60].

Using dual-process theories, prior work explains these dependent variables using two sets of independent variables: the properties of content and the characteristics of content source. Regarding content properties, it has been reported that quality of content [57,60], framing of content [14,15], and content ambiguity [38] determine content use. Specifically, people are more likely to use content if they perceive it as being of high quality [57,60], if it is provided as a rule rather than a write-up [15], and if it concerns a less ambiguous context [38]. Regarding the characteristics of the content source, findings suggest that people are more likely to use content if it is provided by a credible person [38,57,60], and if it is provided by an automated system rather than a human [14,15].

Existing studies also report that the characteristics of the individual who use content play a role in content use. Specifically, content users’ involvement [38,57], expertise [57], use of a focused search strategy [60], cognition [14,15,38], and intellectual demands and risk aversion [20] positively influence content use through main and moderating effects.

Although prior work examines content use through a variety of dependent and independent variables, it neither investigates community-governed wikis nor examines how perceptions of community governance influence content use. Considering that there is a growing interest toward community-governed wikis for internal knowledge sharing, it is important to understand the nature of people’s perceptions of community governance and their effects on content use. In this paper, we intend to extend earlier work on the concept of community governance [26,27] and elaborate on its nature and effects. This paper is different from earlier work in that we propose a multidimensional approach to assessing perceptions of community governance and discuss the role of perceptions of community governance in using content from wikis. Below, we discuss the rationale behind the need for a new conceptualization.

## 3. Community governance

The concept of community governance has enjoyed a rich tradition in the field of sociology and political science. In a general sense, community governance is defined as the mechanism whereby a group of individuals that make up a community work collectively to solve a societal problem within the community [30,31]. The most distinguishing feature of community governance is that, unlike other forms of governance, community members actively participate in solving a problem rather than relying on someone else to solve that problem. For instance, in the case of hierarchical control, which has been the most predominant mode of governance throughout history, a group of people, generally referred to as governors, make decisions on behalf of community members. This may cause tensions between the governors and community members because of the privileges of governors or the obligations imposed on community members [56]. To the contrary, community governance enables a community to solve a problem using the autonomous and voluntary efforts of its members.

Scholars suggest that community governance is preferable especially for diverse, complex, and dynamic contexts, because no single person, group, or organization may have the power, authority, knowledge, or resources to solve problems in such contexts [8,10]. The wide use of community governance in the context of information systems (IS) is a testament to this: most organizations take advantage of community governance for problems that are difficult, if not impossible, for IS professionals to handle. For example, community governance is commonly used on the Web to reduce information overload (as in identifying the most helpful review of a product through the use of rating systems) or to moderate content (as in removing profanity-laden comments on a news site through the use of flags).

Within the scope of this study, we define community governance as the mechanism with which content is verified in wikis. Although verification is defined as increasing the accuracy, relevancy, and legitimacy of content [28], one can increase the scope of this definition using other qualities of information identified in the DeLone and McLean [12] information quality framework. It is also important to note that within the boundaries of this paper, we use the term community to refer to the users of an electronic repository.

Community members can verify the contributions of a repository in various ways, some of which include editing, reviewing (by providing comments to), or rating them. Although these are necessary to verify content in wiki technologies, they are not sufficient as users who perform these activities are also expected to have a certain level of expertise to ensure the accuracy, relevancy, or legitimacy of content. This implies that community governance should be multidimensional and consist of community members’ expertise as well as their actions. These two dimensions are referred to as community credibility and community vigilance, respectively, hereafter. Recent work on wikis provides support not only for the existence of these dimensions but also for their salience. For example, it has been suggested that users of wikis make assessments not only about the knowledge of wiki users but also the frequency with which users edit Wiki contributions and the ways in which users organize content or remove redundancies [27,37]. In fact, the essence of the two dimensions can be traced back to earlier research on transactional memory systems and knowledge sharing through electronic media, which suggest that employees make assessments about the expertise of their colleagues from which they retrieve knowledge as well as their behaviors and the extent to which their colleagues contribute to knowledge sharing efforts [23,24,33,41]. Therefore, the dimensions we propose in this study are rooted in earlier work but novel for research that examines community-based wikis. Below, we elaborate on these two dimensions.

## 3.1. Community credibility

In the context of this paper, community credibility refers to the expertise and trustworthiness of community members who perform the verification tasks in a community-governed repository. Here, we consider credibility as a characteristic of an entity rather than a characteristic of a person. This is consistent with prior conceptualizations as entities such as firms or websites have been suggested to have credibility (see Refs. [11,43]).

Extant work suggests that users’ perceptions of the knowledge, expertise, and trustworthiness of community members who verify wiki articles play a role in forming attitudes toward those articles [27,37]. Accordingly, users form favorable attitudes toward a wiki article if community members who perform the verification tasks are perceived as being credible. This is not surprising for two reasons. First, the verification process used in wikis blurs the boundaries of content authorship and helps generate content through collective effort [22]. As prior literature shows that people form favorable attitudes toward information provided by credible individuals [45,49], it is likely that users of a wiki form favorable attitudes toward collectively generated content if community members are credible. Second, users of a wiki may consider community members who perform the verification tasks as reviewers and develop favorable attitudes toward an article if they perceive these reviewers as being credible. This argument is supported by prior work on peerreviewed content: readers have more favorable attitudes toward peer-reviewed content if reviewers are perceived as being credible [17].

A pertinent question at this point is: how do users of a community-governed wiki develop perceptions of community credibility? Prior research on credibility suggests that there are three ways in which people develop perceptions of a target individual’s credibility: (1) through prior familiarity with the individual, (2) by looking at the credentials of the individual, or (3) by assessing the quality of the messages conveyed or actions taken by the individual [55]. Perceptions of community credibility can be formed in similar ways. For example, if people personally know community members, they may already have perceptions of community credibility. Otherwise, people may infer community credibility either by looking at community members’ online biographic profiles (if available) or through their assessments of the quality or usefulness of content generated by them.

## 3.2. Community vigilance

Community credibility is necessary but not sufficient for people to assess community governance. Prior research indicates that wiki users develop perceptions of other users’ behaviors such as the frequency with which they edit contributions, or the extent to which they remove redundant content [37]. Further, prior interviews suggest that wiki users pay attention to actions taken by other users intended for content verification [27]. Therefore, community members’ actions should be a part of the perceptions of community governance. Accordingly, community members’ actions toward verification of wiki contributions should induce the development of attitudes toward community governance. This is intuitive, because if community members take no action, community governance will not function even if members are perceived as being credible. Therefore, community members’ actions should be as important as community credibility to assess community governance, even though they may be related to and, in certain cases, indicative of community credibility.

In the context of this paper, we refer to community members’ taking action to verify content as community vigilance. The set of actions available to community members may differ from one community-governed repository to the next. For a discussion forum or the comments section of a news story, community vigilance may involve flagging inappropriate entries. On the other hand, community vigilance may involve adding, changing, removing, rating, or flagging content for a wiki technology.

There are at least two ways in which people can form judgments about community vigilance. First, users can observe community members’ reactions to their own contributions in the community-governed repository. For instance, if community members edit an article, the author of that article may form stronger perceptions of community vigilance compared to another person who may not have contributed an article or whose article has not been edited yet. This is why people who submit an article to Wikipedia have strong perceptions of the vigilance of Wikipedia users (see Refs. [29,52]). Second, people can form judgments about community vigilance using various cues provided in the repository. One such cue can be the document history feature in wikis. If the wiki technology keeps track of the history of an article, people may observe the evolution of articles and the changes made by users over a period of time. Other types of cues include ratings, number of raters, comments, or warning messages depending on the type of repository used.

## 3.3. Community governance as a second-order construct

Based on the above discussion, we propose that perceptions of community governance constitute a second-order construct with two dimensions: community credibility and community vigilance. Our decision to model perceptions of community governance as a second-order construct is motivated by several reasons. First, the review of literature shows that there are two distinct dimensions that are salient for knowledge transfer through electronic media: expertise of people from whom knowledge is obtained and people’s behaviors geared toward facilitation of knowledge transfer [23,24,33,41]. The dimensions of community governance described above extend these dimensions to the context of wikis: expertise is conceptualized using community credibility, and behaviors using community vigilance. It is plausible to consider these two dimensions as two separate first-order constructs, but doing so may eliminate the interplay between the dimensions. For example, users of a wiki can be experts in their domains, but if they do not take any action to verify contributions, there would be no community governance. Therefore, the salience of the two dimensions can be better accounted for using a second-order construct. Further, the second-order construct provides a better abstraction, if the two dimensions can be viewed as the different facets of community governance. It has long been argued that a second-order construct is needed when researchers want to take the different facets of a construct into consideration [25,44]. As it is not possible to conceptualize community governance in the absence of one of these facets, the secondorder construct serves the conceptualization of community governance better.

Second, there are benefits to proposing a second-order construct: it allows for more parsimony and reduces theoretical complexity [34]. Conceptualizing the dimensions as separate constructs not only increases the number of theoretical relationships but also increases the complexity of inferences that can be derived from the constructs [32]. Therefore, the absence of the second-order community governance construct would induce future research to propose the main and interaction effects of community credibility and community vigilance separately, which in turn would introduce unwarranted complexity to theoretical frameworks.

## 4. Research model for nomological validity

In an effort to test the validity of the proposed second-order construct and assess its utility in explaining content use from wikis, we propose the research model presented in Fig. 1 by drawing on the ELM (see Refs. [46,47]). The use of the ELM as the theoretical framework is appropriate, because the ELM explains attitude change toward target objects or behaviors, and the subsequent adoption of these objects or behaviors (e.g., Refs. [4,6]). Therefore, it provides insights into adoption of information from electronic repositories (see Refs. [14,15,38,57]). In our model, we extend the ELM’s dependent variable from attitude to perceived content usefulness, which in turn explains content use. This is consistent with prior work (see Ref. [57]). In the sections that follow, we discuss the hypotheses in the research model.

## 4.1. Effect of content usefulness on content use

The association between perceived content usefulness and content use has its roots in the technology acceptance model, which is based on the theory of planned behavior [2]. Although the theory of planned behavior does not suggest a direct relationship between usefulness and adoption, research on technology adoption supports this link by suggesting that if individuals expect performance improvements from the use of a technology, they become more likely to adopt that technology [1]. We expect the same relationship to hold in our context and posit that people who perceive content as being useful are more likely to use that content. This conceptualization is consistent with prior research on content use (e.g., Ref. [57]). Therefore,

H1. Perceived content usefulness is positively associated with content use in a community-governed wiki.

## 4.2. Effect of content quality on content usefulness

The ELM suggests that there are two ‘‘routes’’ to attitude formation: central and peripheral. The central route is an effortful process, in which people scrutinize the merits of arguments provided for a target object to form attitudes about that object. The central route requires a certain degree of cognitive resources as people need to engage in critical thinking to make an assessment about the arguments. It is this assessment that determines someone’s attitude about the target object [46,48,49]. In the context of content use, the central route is usually operationalized using the perceived content quality [57,60]. Therefore, it has been shown that if people have favorable perceptions of content quality, they perceive content as being more useful [57]. This relationship should also hold in our context. Therefore,

H2. Perceived content quality is positively associated with perceived content usefulness in a community-governed wiki.

## 4.3. Effect of community governance on content usefulness

The second route of the ELM is the peripheral route, which operates unconsciously and requires less cognitive effort. People who rely on the peripheral route do not devote any cognitive effort to arguments provided for a target object, and therefore use simple decision rules or cues. Prior research has operationalized the peripheral route using various constructs such as source attractiveness, availability of celebrity endorsements, source charisma, identification with the source, source trustworthiness, and source credibility [4,21,48,51]. Therefore, it has been shown that people’s perceptions of objects or behaviors are more favorable if these objects or behaviors are promoted by those that are attractive, famous, charismatic, trustworthy, or credible [46]. Research that investigates content use from electronic repositories predominantly operationalizes the peripheral route using source credibility (e.g., Refs. [57,60]). Therefore, it has been shown that people have favorable perceptions of content usefulness if content providers are perceived as credible [57].

In the context of this study, we operationalize the peripheral route using perceptions of community governance instead of source credibility, and argue that people’s perceptions of content usefulness should depend on their perceptions of community governance. The use of perceptions of community governance instead of source credibility is reasonable due to several reasons. First, perceptions of community governance involve community members’ actions, such as commenting on, rating, or editing content. The importance of these in evaluating content usefulness has long been established in prior work [42,50]. Therefore, it is plausible for users of community-governed repositories to use their perceptions of information about or conveyed through comments, ratings, or edits (which are subsumed under community vigilance) as peripheral cues and decision rules to form judgments about content usefulness.

Second, the content verification process of wikis not only blurs the boundaries of content authorship but also transforms articles from being static to dynamic [22]. Therefore, a wiki article has many authors and, more importantly, has no final version. Wiki content continuously evolves through the collective efforts of many wiki users. Therefore, it can be difficult to associate a wiki article with a single contributor at any given point in time. Therefore, people’s overall perceptions of content contributors, which are subsumed under perceptions of community governance, should be more influential than their perceptions of an individual contributor. As a result, perceptions of community governance should become a simple decision rule or cue to assess content usefulness. Therefore, users who believe that community members consist of credible people who take action to ensure the accuracy, legitimacy, and relevancy of content should make more favorable assessments about content usefulness. Therefore,

H3. Perceptions of community governance are positively associated with perceived content usefulness in a community-governed wiki.

## 4.4. Moderating effects of elaboration likelihood

The ELM posits that people’s reliance on the central and peripheral routes depends on their elaboration likelihood, which is defined as individuals’ ability and motivation to elaborate [45]. People who are in a higher state of elaboration likelihood are more likely to rely on the central route, and those who are in a lower state are more likely to rely on the peripheral route [46,47]. This is because people in the high elaboration likelihood state have the ability and motivation to spend the cognitive resources required for the central route, whereas those in the low elaboration likelihood state lack the ability and motivation to elaborate, therefore forming attitudes with the help of simple decision rules or cues.

Based on this reasoning, elaboration likelihood should positively moderate the effect of perceived content quality on perceived content usefulness and negatively moderate the effect of perceptions of community governance on perceived content usefulness. This is because users who have the ability and motivation to elaborate on content retrieved from wikis should rely on their assessment of content quality, whereas those who do not have such an ability or motivation should rely on their assessment of credibility and vigilance of community members (and thus perceptions of community governance) to determine content usefulness. Both of these relationships are consistent with earlier works that examine content use from electronic repositories (e.g., Refs. [57,60]). Therefore,

H4. Elaboration likelihood negatively moderates the relationship between perceptions of community governance and perceived content usefulness (such that the relationship is stronger for people with low elaboration likelihood).

H5. Elaboration likelihood positively moderates the relationship between perceived content quality and perceived content usefulness (such that the relationship is stronger for people with high elaboration likelihood).

## 5. Methods

## 5.1. Research setting and data collection

In order to validate the two proposed dimensions of community governance and test the nomological validity, we collected data from an organization in Turkey. The organization (referred to as ABC hereafter to maintain anonymity) implemented a wiki technology in 2009 to create an organizational memory. ABC provides consulting services in various areas, some of which include organizational strategy, total quality management, organizational restructuring, and recruitment. It has 10 internal divisions to serve the needs of customers in various industries that offer goods as well as services. At the time of the data collection, these divisions employed a total of 213 members in various offices of the organization. Each division used the same wiki technology; however, each division had their own dedicated area in the repository to share insights and best practices.

When ABC first implemented the wiki technology, many organizational members were unwilling to make contributions, because they feared that they would be vulnerable to being replaced by novice professionals. This was exacerbated by the global economic worries at the time, resulting in little to no contributions to the repository. The management acted swiftly and added wiki participation into employees’ performance evaluations. Employees could make either new contributions or improve existing ones in their domain of expertise. At the end of each quarter, division managers received reports about their subordinates’ contributions and made necessary assessments for performance evaluations. This increased the extent of contributions and enabled ABC to capture crucial information necessary for building an organizational memory.

The survey used for data collection employed the critical incident technique [16] by asking participants to recall the last contribution they retrieved from the organizational wiki and answer the questions that followed. To eliminate any misunderstandings, the survey was conducted in the native language of participants. To ensure validity, a back-translation approach was used: people outside the research team translated the questionnaire back and forth while the research team made adjustments in the translated questionnaire. The final version of the questionnaire was administered through the Web. The link was e-mailed internally to all organizational members and a \$100 gift card from a local electronics retailer was raffled off among organizational members for their completion of the survey. The data collection lasted 2 weeks and a total of 163 usable responses were obtained, making the participation rate 77%.

## 5.2. Measures

Measurement items were adapted from earlier studies where applicable (see Appendix A for all items and their anchors). We measured community credibility using four five-point Likertscaled items adapted from Sussman and Siegal [57]. The first two items concerned the overall expertise of community members, and the last two concerned their overall trustworthiness.

We developed new items to measure community vigilance. To do this, we used the ‘‘maintenance’’ tasks performed by Wikipedia users at the time of data collection on articles published in Wikipedia. We categorized these tasks into five categories: deleting inappropriate content, editing content, reverting vandalized content, checking the accuracy of content, and warning users about content. An independent validation of these categories was not performed before data collection. To address this limitation, we conducted a sorting study after data collection and provided the definitions of the categories and the individual items to three other individuals who were not familiar with the research study. We asked the participants to categorize the items using the predefined categories or create new categories. The initial agreement was 82%. Clarifying the definitions increased the level of agreement to 94%.

Drawing upon earlier work, we operationalized elaboration likelihood using two constructs: expertise and involvement [6,57]. Expertise accounts for individuals’ ability to elaborate, while involvement accounts for individuals’ motivation to elaborate [6]. Both of these constructs were measured using semantic differential scales adapted from Zaichkowsky [59]. Content usefulness was measured using a five-point semantic differential scale adapted from Sussman and Siegal [57] and Bhattacherjee and Sanford [6]. The individual items concerned whether participants found the content they retrieved from the repository valuable, informative, helpful, and persuasive. Content quality was also adopted from Sussman and Siegal [57] and a five point sematic differential scale was used. The items for the dependent variable, content use, were adapted from Sussman and Siegal [57] and Zhang and Watts [60]. They concerned whether the participant used, followed, and applied content retrieved from the repository.

As prior research uses the source credibility construct to operationalize the peripheral route of the ELM (e.g., Refs. [57,60]), we added Sussman and Siegal’s [57] source credibility measure as a control variable in explaining perceived content usefulness. Further, in order to control the variation in content use due to personal characteristics, we measured each participant’s gender, age category, work experience, work experience in the current organization, frequency of wiki visits in a typical day, and duration of wiki visits in a typical day as additional control variables.

The descriptive statistics of the participants are presented in Table B1 of Appendix B. Sixty-four percent of the participants were male. ## 6. Results

## 6.1. Perceptions of community governance as a second-order construct

In order to assess the validity of the second-order perceptions of community governance construct, we used 50% of the responses (81 observations) chosen randomly from our data set and conducted a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The remaining observations were saved as a holdout sample to test our hypotheses (discussed later).

The specification of the measurement model and the associated model fit statistics are presented in Fig. 2. As shown in the figure, community governance was conceptualized as a second-order construct, consisting of two reflective dimensions (community credibility and community vigilance). The adequacy of the individual items was assessed using convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was determined based on three suggestions by Fornell and Larcker [18]: (1) all factor loadings should be significant and >0.70; (2)

composite reliability (r ) of each construct should exceed 0.80, and (3) the average variance extracted (AVE) for each construct should exceed 0.50. Per Fornell and Larcker’s [18] suggestion, we assessed the discriminant validity by comparing the square root of AVE of each construct with the bivariate correlations between that and all other constructs.

The means, standard deviations, and the factor loadings of items are presented in Table C1 of Appendix C. All items, except the third item of community vigilance, had a factor loading higher than the 0.70 threshold. The third item of the community vigilance did not load properly, because it concerned vandalism in wikis, which is less likely to be observed in organizational repositories. As we had four more items to measure community vigilance, we excluded this item from the rest of the analysis.

The composite reliability scores, presented in Table C2 of Appendix C, exceeded the 0.80 acceptance norm (with the community vigilance construct having the lowest score of 0.94). Furthermore, the square root of AVE values (diagonal elements in Table C2) were >0.71 (the lowest being 0.89 for community vigilance). Therefore, all three conditions of convergent validity were met.

Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the square root of AVE of each construct with the correlation of that construct with other constructs. The correlation matrix, shown in Table C2 of Appendix C, shows that the lowest square root of AVE was 0.89, which was >0.56 (the correlation between community credibility and community vigilance). These suggested that the discriminant validity criterion was also satisfied.

We also assessed the loadings of community credibility and community vigilance as reflective constructs. As shown in Table C3 of Appendix C, the loadings were >0.70 and significant at $p < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Overall, the second order construct seems a reasonable representation of users’ perceptions of community governance in community-governed wikis.

## 6.3. Post hoc analysis

In the preceding analysis, we conceptualized perceptions of community governance using two reflective constructs. Reflective constructs are purported to cause their indicators, so the arrows point toward indicators [44]. One of the decision rules that help identify reflective constructs is the ‘‘interchangeability’’ of indicators [25]. It has been suggested that indicators that are interchangeable are reflective, because they measure the same phenomenon, have a common theme, and thus are caused by the corresponding construct [44]. If researchers have reasons to believe that their indicators are not interchangeable, they can conceptualize constructs as formative. Formative constructs are purported to be caused by their corresponding indicators, so the arrows point toward constructs [44]. Therefore, indicators define constructs, concern their different aspects, and thus cause changes in them.

Based on the above discussion, the measurement instrument used in this study (presented in Table A1 of Appendix A) may create concerns about the use of reflective constructs. Therefore, we conducted a post hoc analysis to see if perceptions of community governance can be conceptualized using formative constructs. A closer look at the measurement instrument shows that two of the indicators that measure perceptions of community credibility concern the expertise of community members, while the other two concern the trustworthiness of community members. As these indicators are interchangeable only within themselves, one can argue that perceptions of community credibility can be a second-order formative construct consisting of two reflective constructs. In a similar vein, one can also argue that perceptions of community vigilance can be a formative construct as its indicators are not interchangeable, but measure the different ways in which community members verify contributions. This leads to the measurement model presented in Fig. 3.

When the measurement model consists of formative con structs, the construct validity criteria used for reflective constructs cannot be used to assess validity [44]. Therefore, researchers suggest that the first step is to ensure that all formative constructs have content validity [13]. Content validity can be assessed using a literature review or Q-sorting [7,44]. In this study, both community credibility and community vigilance have content validity, because the community credibility construct was developed based on our review of the literature, and the community vigilance construct was developed by a sorting activity. Therefore, the content validity criterion is satisfied.

Researchers suggest that the second step is to ensure that the paths between indicators and formative constructs are significant [44]. However, one of the challenges in testing path significance is the pairwise correlations between indicators. While correlations between indicators should be high for reflective constructs, they should be low for formative constructs as they undermine path significance [13]. If the indicators are highly correlated, researchers are advised to either drop each indicator one by one until significance is achieved or conduct a principal components analysis to eliminate correlations [13,44]. Principal components analysis is preferable over eliminating indicators, because each eliminated indicator reduces content validity [13]. Our analysis of the first half of the data (that were used earlier for CFA) revealed that the indicators of the community vigilance construct were highly correlated (with the highest pairwise correlation being 0.83), so we conducted a principal components analysis for this construct.

The results of the principal component analysis suggested that there was only one component whose eigenvalue was >1. This component concerned four indicators (the first and last two), and accounted for a cumulative variance of 68%. The second principal component had an eigenvalue lower than (but close to) 1, and only concerned the third indicator. In an effort to maintain content validity, we decided to keep the second principal component as well. The component coefficients, eigenvalues, and cumulative variances of the principal components are presented in Table 3.

In order to test the path significance, we employed PLS. The results are presented in Fig. E1 of Appendix E. All paths, except the one from the second principal component to community vigilance, were significant. The nonsignificant link between the second principal component and community vigilance was expected, because this principal component concerned vandalism of wiki content, which did not exist in the study context. As a result of the path analysis, we can confirm the validity of the formative measurement model.

We also checked the convergent and discriminant validity of the two reflective constructs, community expertise and community trustworthiness, using Fornell and Larcker’s [18] suggestions. The findings presented in Tables E1 and E2 of Appendix E show that the requirements of both convergent and discriminant validity were satisfied.

Following measurement validity, we used the holdout sample to test the validity of the nomological network using formative constructs. Due to high pairwise correlations between the indicators of the community vigilance construct, we used principal components once again. Similar to the measurement model, we included two principal components to the analysis. The first concerned the first and last two indicators, while the second concerned only the third indicator of community vigilance. ## 7. Discussion

## 7.1. Key findings

The goals of this study were threefold: (1) understanding the nature and dimensionality of perceptions of community governance, (2) measuring perceptions of community governance, and (3) examining the role of perceptions of community governance in content use from community-governed wikis. In order to achieve these goals, we used the scale development procedure provided by MacKenzie et al. [35]. As the first step of the procedure, we defined perceptions of community governance as the mechanism with which content is verified in wikis, and we modeled it using two dimensions. We defined the first dimension, community credibility, as the expertise and trustworthiness of users who performed verification tasks, and the second dimension, community vigilance, as community members taking action to verify content.

Following conceptual development, we generated items to measure the dimensions of perceptions of community governance (step 2 of the scale development procedure). We ensured the content validity of these items by conducting a review of earlier work (and using previously generated items where applicable) and using a sorting task for new items (step 3 of the procedure). Then, we specified the measurement model and validated it using data collected from the employees of an organization (steps 4–6 of the procedure). Our analysis showed that the items proposed to measure perceptions of community governance satisfied both convergent and discriminant validity (except one of the items of the community vigilance construct, which was not applicable in the study context).

Following the validation of the measurement model, we drew upon the ELM and used the holdout data to conduct nomological validity (steps 7–9 of the procedure). The analysis provided support for the measurement model as well as the validity of the proposed hypotheses. In summary, our approach to measuring perceptions of community governance satisfied the requirements of the scale development procedure proposed by MacKenzie et al. [35].

## 7.3. Theoretical implications

There are two major theoretical implications of this study. First, we develop an instrument to measure individuals’ perceptions of community governance for the wiki technology. In today’s world, more organizations are implementing the wiki technology to support organizational knowledge management [19,40]. The wiki technology is different from other electronic repositories used for knowledge management in that it leverages the collective efforts of wiki users to verify content and thus institutes community governance. Therefore, if the concept of community governance, a unique feature of wikis, is not taken into account, the wiki technology becomes indistinguishable from other types of electronic repositories used for knowledge management. This hinders theory development efforts and prevents researchers from identifying the value propositions of wikis vis-a\`-vis other electronic repositories. This study provides an initial step toward tackling this problem by discussing the nature of community governance, its dimensions, and the proper operationalization of the dimensions. This is an important step toward the development of new theories concerning wikis – a much-needed effort in this field [36]. As a result of this study, the concept of community governance can be used in future research to study the role of wikis in the development as well as the use of organizational memory by employees. For example, the community governance concept can help researchers investigate whether contributing to wikis is similar to or different from contributing to other types of electronic repositories, and how perceptions of community governance – along with other salient features of the wiki technology – influence contribution behaviors.

The second theoretical implication of this study is that this study contributes to the literature on content use from wikis by extending the ELM. Despite the growing interest in wikis, recent studies as well as reports indicate that knowledge use from wikis, and electronic repositories in general, is still not understood [37,54]. Extant work that draws upon the ELM to investigate content use focuses on individual contributors and suggests that the use of content is contingent upon the credibility of a contributor and the quality of content he/she provides. This study takes these suggestions one step further for wikis and adds to earlier findings that content use from wikis is contingent upon users’ perceptions of the wiki community as well. Specifically, users take other users’ credibility and actions into consideration as they use content from a wiki technology. This not only extends the use of the ELM in explaining content use but also provides new insights into understanding content use from wikis.

## 7.4. Practical implications

The most important practical implication of this study is that this study shows that wiki users develop perceptions of community governance by making judgments about the credibility and vigilance of other users. These perceptions are then used to assess content usefulness, which in turn determines content use. Therefore, organizations that strive to increase content use from community-governed repositories, especially wikis, should assure users that the community members are credible and vigilant. In order to ensure the credibility of community members, the profiles of community members can be made available to users. This way, the credentials or experience of individuals can be visible to everyone and help users make more informed decisions about community credibility. The availability of member profiles can also reduce any second-guessing or ambiguity about community credibility, which may hinder content use in community-governed repositories.

In order to ensure community vigilance, community-governed repositories can be designed such that they inform users of the actions taken by other users to verify contributions. This can be done by providing metadata about each contribution. For example, repositories can provide information about the creation date of each contribution, its last change date, the total number of edits performed on that contribution, the number of unique individuals who edited the contribution, and so on. Such information can demonstrate to users that community members are actively engaged in the verification of that contribution, and thus the contribution is accurate, relevant, and legitimate.

In summary, electronic repositories that employ community governance should be designed and implemented such that retrieving content from these repositories should not mean only the retrieval of contributions. Instead, these repositories should provide users with additional information about how repository contents are verified and by whom. Only then can organizations motivate users to consult the repository more in performing their responsibilities.
