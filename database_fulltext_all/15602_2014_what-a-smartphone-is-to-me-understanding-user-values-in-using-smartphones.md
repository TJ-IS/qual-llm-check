# What a smartphone is to me: understanding user values in using smartphones

Yoonhyuk Jung\*

\*School of Technology Management, Ulsan National Institute of Science and Technology (UNIST), Ulsan, South Korea, email: yjung@unist.ac.kr

Abstract. The objective of this study is twofold. First, it aims to investigate the various values users achieve with smartphones, which is a form of user-empowering information technology (IT). The other objective is to introduce a means-end chain approach into IT-user studies. An important attraction of smartphones is their personalized environment, which is mainly provided by varied applications. The user personalization ability implies that users achieve diverse bene<sup>fi</sup>ts with smartphones; that is, users decide what a smartphone is to them rather than adopt a given product. Thus, investigating what values users pursue with a smartphone (i.e. a value-oriented approach) will give insights into understanding the users. To investigate user values in using smartphones, we conducted a laddering interview with 54 smartphone users and analyzed the data by using a means-end chain approach to understand consumers hierarchical value structure. This study contributes to value-oriented research on user-empowering IT by revelling how users bene<sup>fi</sup>t from smartphones. Furthermore, the study advances value-oriented research by showing what users actually do with smartphones, from concrete activities to abstract values. In addition, a means-end chain approach introduced in the study can be another angle for the investigation of user adoption of technology, in that it can describe IT use contexts and practices, which become an important object of analysis in the information systems research.

Keywords: mobile computing, smartphone, means-end chain analysis, laddering interview, user empowering, value-oriented approach

## INTRODUCTION

A mobile phone is regarded as an essential component of modern life. The continued evolution of mobile phone technologies has profoundly changed the world. About 6 billion people corresponding to over four-<sup>fi</sup>fths of the global population, had subscribed to a mobile phone service by the end of 2011 (ITU, 2012), and it is expected that by 2015 more Americans will access the internet through advanced mobile devices, such as smartphones, than desktop computers (IDC, 2011). Also, mobile devices have become more embedded in everyday life, as they have evolved from simple communication tools to versatile portable computers, enabling various mobile services. The recent proliferation of smartphones allows us to predict that smartphones will overtake traditional mobile phones in the near future.

Unlike traditional mobile phones, smartphones offer more functions, including diverse internet content and multimedia players. Particularly, one innovative aspect of smartphones is that users can download various kinds of mobile applications, or ‘apps’, onto their smartphones. The appearance of an app market has transformed the mobile phone industry. For example, website providers (e.g. portal sites) may have more visitors through mobile apps connecting users to websites, and individual developers may have new revenue sources. The most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant change is that users are able to shape their own mobile devices and services by installing the mobile apps they want, implying that users have more control over the mobile territory (Verkasalo et al., 2010).

By using the various functions of smartphones and having the apps they want, users can have very individualized smartphones and services. Personalization has been a trend in Web services, and in particular, user-driven personalization (e.g. iGoogle) allows users to tailor the contents of information technology (IT) services to match their preference (Tam & Ho, 2006). Because a mobile phone is linked to an individual user, mobile services have been assumed to achieve greater personalization than Web-based services (Jarvenpaa & Tomak, 2003). In particular, smartphone users can have highly adapted mobile services, which they are capable of con<sup>fi</sup>gurating their own apps in their devices (Tossell et al., 2012). The highly personalized nature of smartphones means that users can pursue a variety of goals with smartphones. We can therefore say that users decide what a smartphone is for themselves, rather than just adopting a given product. Like smartphones, emerging are providing users with an expanding array of capabilities and experiences, and this user-empowering characteristic of IT makes scholars and practitioners interested in understanding the diversity of goals that users seek through their use of IT. Thus, whether or not users adopt smartphones may become a less signi<sup>fi</sup>cant focus of discussion; instead, what users actually do with IT can be a main topic (Yoo, 2010).

Gaining insight into the goals that underlie the ‘why’ of user behaviour has been a core topic in the information systems (IS) research, and this has especially been the case in technology adoption research based on the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989). In the research stream, factors related to the motivations or goals of users are key elements to explain important outcomes, such as usage intention, satisfaction and intention to return (Koufaris, 2002; Bhattacherjee & Premkumar, 2004; van der Heijden, 2004). In spite of its successfu application in user adoption of IT, traditional adoption research has taken little interest in the diverse values users pursue. This may be one reason that the traditional models assume IT users are passive, and just respond to a technology, rather than active agents who decide what the technology is (Bagozzi, 2007). The limited view of traditional adoption models may hinder comprehensive understanding of the adoption of user-empowering IT. In the context of smartphones, because users create their own devices by choosing apps themselves, the passive-user perspective has a limitation in explaining users’ IT behaviour. In other words, because it misses the diverse user values of smartphones, the traditional adoption framework provides little explanation for what smartphones mean to their users. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the various types of goals that users seek through their smartphones as an illustration of user-empowering IT. For this, the study employs a means-end chain (MEC) approach (Olson & Reynolds, 1983), which can be an alternative approach to study user values in IT.

## THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## Value-oriented perspective on adoption of information technology

The traditional perspective on technology adoption is challenged by the trend of adaptable and user-tailorable technologies (Germonprez et al., 2007). User-tailorable devices and systems provide users with a wide range of options for con<sup>fi</sup>guration, modes of use, and mix-and-match integration of functions. What distinguishes these applications is the high degree of personalization afforded the user in determining the ways in which they provide diverse values. In the typical paradigm of technology adoption, users are regarded as passive subjects who simply respond to the technology as given. For example, the underpinning tenet of traditional adoption models, founded on the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989), is that users’ perception of the given technology predetermined affects their intention to use it. In other words, these perspectives have focused on users’ perception, which is limited by the boundaries of the prescribed technology. Those models have been fruitfully employed to explain adoption of traditional technologies; however, emerging user-empowering IT, characterized by a rich user experience, user self-con<sup>fi</sup>guration and openness (e.g. weblogs, virtual worlds and mobile services), are not adequately explained by the traditional adoption framework. Although personalization, which is a core feature of user-empowering IT, is a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant phenomenon in IT services, prior information systems research has little interest in the phenomenon (Tam & Ho, 2006). By providing a wide diversity of goals or by supporting goals in new ways, these newer IT environments make users empowered. In these environments, the user is no longer a passive actor who simply responds to stimuli (i.e. properties of the technology), but rather determines what the technology is to them through their participation with it. For instance, some bloggers may use their blogs as a channel for sharing their private lives; others may operate blogs for online business or social networking. These technologies are designed to provide users with the opportunity to pursue their personal goals/values and create meanings of the technology beyond a mechanical response that is externally de<sup>fi</sup>ned (Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). As emerging IT become more personalized and versatile, research focusing on the diversity of user values will be required (Hong et al., 2008).

The diffusion of user-empowering IT changes the focus of technology adoption research from the effect of users' perception of given artefacts to the diverse goals or values which users seek The usage of technology today may enable a much broader set of user goals and values than models of adoption currently re<sup>fl</sup>ect (Yoo, 2010). Thus, traditional technology adoption research may not be robust or nuanced enough to help us understand the emerging user-empowering IT. In order to develop a more comprehensive understanding of technology adoption, it may be necessary to move beyond the traditional adoption approach. To better understand user adoption of this type of IT, in this paper, we employ a value-oriented approach, which focuses on goals or values that users pursue with a technology, rather than investigate the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of some perceptual factors on an outcome variable.

In media research, the value-oriented approach has been widely adopted to explain users choice and use of media, particularly based on uses and grati<sup>fi</sup>cations theory (Katz et al., 1974). The major premise of the theory is that individuals’ use of media is motivated by their psychological needs. The theory assumes that media users are active communicators and their choice of media is a goal-directed activity (Rubin, 2002). For example, it is reported that people use the internet to achieve some key motivations including information, convenience, entertainment and social interaction (Papacharissi & Rubin, 2000); social network sites for coordination, immediate access, affection and leisure (Xu et al., 2012); Short Message Service for immediate access, relaxation, entertainment, information seeking, socialization and status (Grellhesl & Punyanunt-Cater, 2012). In user-tailored IT settings, users not only become more active but also have more opportunities to achieve their varied goals and values than in a traditional media context. The value-oriented perspective, therefore, can be considered a useful framework for explaining users of such kinds of IT. Furthermore, the value-oriented perspective is particularly welcomed in the context of mobile services. Mobile technologies generate value for consumers by radically transforming the structure of everyday life, and this mechanism leads to highly personalized services, differentiated from Web-based services (Jarvenpaa & Loebbecke, 2009). Thus, the value-oriented view is an appropriate framework for exploring diverse values derived from personalized mobile services.

## Research on mobile computing from the perspective of the user

Even though the mobile computing phenomenon has been studied in various ways, user adoption is regarded as the most popular research theme and has been increasingly investigated (Ladd et al., 2010). From the framework of user adoption, researchers have investigated a wide range of mobile environments including Short Message Service (Lu et al., 2010), multimedia messaging services (Chang & Pan, 2011), mobile banking (Püschel et al., 2010), mobile transaction (Wang et al., 2006) and mobile commerce (Pedersen, 2005). Such studies focus on how users perception of the traits of the mobile technology affects their adoption of the technology. For example, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived enjoyment have been widely examined for understanding user adoption of mobile services (Lu et al., 2010; Verkasalo et al., 2010). Also, some researchers have studied the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of personal traits, such as resistance to change (Sanford & Oh, 2010), self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Wang et al., 2006), subjective norms (Kim et al., 2011a, 2011b), personality (Zhou & Lu, 2011) and demographic factors (Kumar & Lim, 2008), on the adoption of mobile services. The prior adoption research has concentrated on factors affecting use adoption, but has overlooked what users actually obtain, that is, the goals or values which users seek with mobile services. Because mobile phones are not only possessed by the single user but also go everywhere with that user, their usage is inclined to be more personalized and more comprehensive than other IT services (Hong et al., 2008). Especially, considering that the enhanced user-driven personalization characteristic of smartphones may lead to a wide range of personalized activities (Tossell et al., 2012), prior research cannot provide a comprehensive understanding of the relationship between users and mobile services.

There are a few studies of mobile services from value-driven approaches. Kim et al. (2007) developed a value-based adoption model to examine the adoption of mobile internet services.

In this model, users’ perception of the overall value, which is based on a balance between bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs, is a main predictor of adoption intention. Other studies (e.g. Hong & Tam, 2006; Lee et al., 2007) also demonstrated that users’ assessment of monetary value has an important role in their adoption of mobile services. Despite a focus on user values, those studies have mainly leaned to the overall value and the monetary value. In other words. there has been little attention paid to what kinds of values are achieved by using mobile services, and furthermore, how those values are related.

One the other hand, a small number of studies have tried to break down the overall value of mobile services into several subvalues. Turel et al. (2007) examined the impact of perceived value, consisting of four subvalues (Performance, Emotional, Money and Social), on the adoption of Short Message Service. Turel et al. (2010) re-examined their value-based model to include a new type of value (Visual/musical appeal). Also, Kim & Han (2009) investigated the adoption of mobile services from a three-value perspective: utilitarian, hedonic and socia values. They found that whereas users’ perceptions of utilitarian and social values have signi<sup>fi</sup>cant in<sup>fl</sup>uence on the adoption of mobile services, the perceived hedonic value has a weaker impact than the other values. Recent studies examine analogous adoption models, which selectively include those three values in the context of mobile services (Chun et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2012; Park et al., 2013). Such studies contribute to the richness of value-driven adoption approaches, in that they take one more step forward into user values. Still, they do not comprehensively explore a variety of values nor the relations among values. By using the MEC approach that is introduced in the following section, we try to remedy the weaknesses of the current value-driven approaches to mobile service research.

## Means-end chain approach

An MEC approach has been popularly used to discover hierarchical structures of consumption values. The MEC approach claims that attributes of the product or service have a role in the means by which consumers achieve goals or values, which is subsequently the means to realize another higher goal or value (Gutman, 1982; Olson & Reynolds, 1983). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the approach assumes that consumer knowledge is hierarchically organized into three levels of abstraction: attributes, consequences and values. Attributes are the means to realize consequences, which are also the means to achieve values (Olson & Reynolds, 1983). Attributes indicate ‘perceived qualities or features of products or services’ (Reynolds & Olson, 2001, p. 92) and can be physica or abstract product characteristics. Consequences are ‘personal outcomes or results derived from usage or consumption’ (Reynolds & Olson, 2001, p. 92). Values are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

The beliefs that people hold about themselves, and desirable values are goals that represen governing drives and motivations. [Reynolds & Olson, 2001, p. 93]

For example, the crispy/crunchy attribute of a snack produces the consequence of refreshment, which might lead to the value of happiness. Another example is that the role-playing attribute of online games generates the consequence of absorption in the role, which ultimately leads to users’ psychological state of avoiding reality (i.e. the value of escapism).

An attribute–consequence–value chain is usually expressed by a hierarchical map, which includes the nodes and relationships among them. The MEC approach has been comprehensively used in consumer research that tries to understand consumers’ preferences toward products or services (e.g. Reynolds & Rochon, 2001; Klenosky, 2002), and organization research, which elucidates an organization’s strategic values and decision-making structures (e.g. Peffers et al., 2003; van Rekom et al., 2006). Recently, this approach has been introduced into information systems academia, where it has been applied to the exploration of user requirements for a system (Chiu, 2005), and users’ goals in virtual worlds (Jung & Kang, 2010).

The MEC analysis is an inductive approach, which is an alternative to consumption value research using prede<sup>fi</sup>ned taxonomies of values, such as Rokeach’s (1973) Value Survey or a list of values. MEC researchers should develop a setting in which respondents speak by themselves without using cognitive categories (i.e.,value inventory) (Grunert & Grunert, 1995). Thus, the approach is proper for this study, which attempts to explore the variety of goals or values users pursue with multi-functional IT.

The MEC approach typically depends on a laddering interview technique. As it continues to ask subsequent questions to require deeper re<sup>fl</sup>ection from respondents, the technique shows the way in which the respondent sees the world (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988). A laddering procedure typically includes three questions: the attribute question (What attribute makes the product (or service) attractive to you?), the consequence question (Why is the attribute important or desirable to you?) and the value question (Why is your response important?). In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase, the respondent is asked to supply the attributes of a product that affect his or her consumption decision. The respondent is then asked to explain what bene<sup>fi</sup>ts he or she attains from the attributes. Finally, in the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation stage, the respondent is asked to offer the reasons why those bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are important to him or her. Based on the responses, a hierarchica map is produced by the level of abstraction: attributes → consequences → values.

Some studies employ an alternative way to decide the level of abstraction instead of adhering to a rigid classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of elements into three abstract levels (i.e. attribute, consequence and value). Depending on network theory (see Scott, 1991), those studies generate the abstractness of each element and use it to determine the position of the element in a hierarchical map, instead of a strict speci<sup>fi</sup>cation of three levels of abstraction (Bagozzi & Dabholkar, 1994: Pieters et al.. 1995: Capozza et al., 2003). This revised method allows researchers to know the relationships of the elements without having to conduct additional work to classify the elements into three abstraction levels. One critique of the laddering technique is that an answer frequently does not correspond to the level targeted by the question (e.g. a consequence or value answer to the attribute question). The alternative technique reduces this limitation because each element has a level according to its abstractness, without any labe (e.g. attribute).

## METHODOLOGY

We took an inductive methodological approach, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally the MEC approach, which helps diverse user goals or values to emerge in an inductive manner. After introducing our participants, we explain our MEC approach consisting of three stages: laddering interviews, coding and drawing a hierarchical value map, which are essential components of an MEC analysis (Reynolds & Olson, 2001).

## Participants

The participants were 54 undergraduate students at a university in South Korea, who were recruited by email invitation. All participants were in their early 20s. Seventy-eight per cent of them majored in Engineering, and the other 12% did Business Administration. Over 83% of the participants made fewer than 30 min of voice calls per day through a smartphone; 57% of them used a smartphone for more than 2 h per day with the purpose of accessing the internet or using smartphone applications. This <sup>fi</sup>nding shows that the participants use a smartphone as a device more than as a mobile phone. Information on their demographics and smartphone usage is sum marized in Table 1.

## Laddering interviews

Laddering interview refers to an in-depth and step-by-step interviewing method used to understand how individuals translate the attributes of objects into meaningful associations (Gutman, 1982). All interviews were performed in a comfortable place (the author’s of<sup>fi</sup>ce) where the interview would not be disturbed by others. The participants were <sup>fi</sup>rst asked to complete a questionnaire including the demographic and supplemental questions. This was followed by the interview, during which the interviewees wrote their responses on the laddering interview paper by themselves after answering the interviewer’s questions. Although an interview is a typical method to collect data in studies employing the MEC approach, some researchers use a paper–pencil version (i.e. laddering questionnaire) for cost-effective data gathering (e.g. Pieters et al., 1995; Botschen et al., 1999; Bagozzi & Bergami, 2003). Whereas a face-to-face laddering interview provides the interviewer with a chance to clarify questions during the interview, the paper–pencil technique enables interviewees to re<sup>fl</sup>ect upon their answers (Pieters et al., 1995; Jung & Kang, 2010). We combined the laddering interview with the paper–pencil version. During interviews, the interviewer took notes. The interviews took 30 min on average. The interview basically consisted of three questions to elicit the goal structures of the users. Based on the adapted laddering interview style described by Pieters et al. (1995), we asked open-ended questions to elicit the users’ goals for smartphone use, as follows: (1) What functions do you most frequently use on a smartphone? Please, write three examples; (2) Why do you use the function? What do you obtain by using the function?; (3) Why is this reason (answer of the prior question) important? For example, an interviewee might answer ‘mobile messenger, map mobile application, and email’ for the <sup>fi</sup>rst question. Then, for each response we asked the second question (e.g. Why do you use mobile messenger? What do you obtain by using mobile messenger?). The interviewee may answer

## Coding

The coding procedure is summarized in Table 2. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the data analysis was to categorize the responses to the interview questions, which interviewees wrote on the laddering questionnaire by themselves. The <sup>fi</sup>rst coder classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the data into the set of categories, each of which is a word or phrase representing a group of the responses. The coder also reviewed <sup>fi</sup>eld notes, which were taken by the interviewer during the interviews, for more rigorous coding and classi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the data. The coder classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the data using an open coding procedure in which codes and categories were not predetermined but rather emerged from the data. When choosing words or phrases for codes, we attempted to use expressions which participants had used in interviews in order to minimize the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the coders and allow the nuances to show through their words (Table 3).

Next, in order to mitigate bias of the initial classi<sup>fi</sup>cation conducted by the <sup>fi</sup>rst coder, coding facilitators participated in reclassifying the data and deciding on the category words/phrases. Two facilitators regrouped the data using the set of categories identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the <sup>fi</sup>rst coder, and then the codes and categories were elaborated through discussion. In the result, six functions/characteristics (entertainment, mobile commerce, communication, management of schedule and information, information search and choice of apps wanted) and 13 conse quences/values, which are generated by those functions/characteristics, were extracted. Based on the elaborated set, the <sup>fi</sup>rst coder classi<sup>fi</sup>ed the data again, and a second coder independently recategorized the data using the elaborated set of categories. The two raters were in agreement on 289 of the 314 categorizations assigned (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.91), indicating an acceptable level of inter-rater reliability (Fleiss, 1981). Inter-rater disagreements were then reconciled through discussion.

## Generating the hierarchical goal structure

The next step in the analysis was to generate the goal hierarchy for using smartphones. The coded responses to questions generated a means-end chain, or a ladder of meanings; answers to the <sup>fi</sup>rst question became a starting point, and answers to the second question corresponded to the means to attain the goal or value re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the response to the follow-up ‘why’ question. For example, if the coded responses were mobile messenger (<sup>fi</sup>rst question), makes constan communication possible (second question), makes closer friends (third question) and pleasant (last question), then a hierarchical chain is created: communication function → Improve communication → Socialization → Amusement. All relations were summarized in an implication matrix which depicts the number of times one code led to each other code in the responses (Klenosky, 2002) (Table 4). For instance, C12 (acquire information) led to C6 (productive daily life) 15 times and to C10 (sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence) 10 times.

The current study employed an alternative method for analyzing laddering interviews proposed by Bagozzi & Dabholkar (1994) and Pieters et al. (1995). This approach produces a hierarchical structure by comparing the number of times each element is mentioned as the means to the number of times it is mentioned as the end. The approach uses out-degrees and in-degrees in order to estimate the abstractness level of each element. The out-degree of a particular element refers to the number of times the topic serves as the source or origin (means) of linkages to other elements (i.e. the row sum of the element in an implication matrix); whereas the in-degree of the element indicates the number of times the topic serves as the object or end of linkages with other elements (i.e. the column sum of the element in an implication matrix) (Pieters et al., 1995).

Abstractness of an element is the ratio of in-degree divided by in-degree plus out-degree of the element, and ranges from 0 to l (Pieters et al., 1995). Elements with high abstractness scores are regarded mainly as ends, whereas ones with low abstractness scores are thought of primarily as means. Based on this alternative approach, we generated the abstractness score for each topic (Table 4). For informative analysis, we also generated the centrality of each element, which represents the degree to which the element has a central role in the structure (Knoke & Burt, 1982). Centrality is calculated by dividing total degree (in-degree plus out-degree) of a particular topic by the sum of all active cells (no-blank cells) in the implication matrix; an active cell indicates a cell in which a relation between two elements appears. The sum of all active cells was 165 in the current study, as shown in Table 4.

Next, we produced the hierarchical value map according to the information in the implication matrix. Based on their level of abstractness, elements were placed in the map, and then were connected with the other elements according to their means-end relations. In this stage, the important point was to determine what linkages were to be included in the map. Because inclusion of all linkages can decrease a map’s usefulness and informativeness, we did not embrace all linkages and decided to employ a cutoff level (Reynolds & Gutman, 1988). Following Bagozzi & Dabholkar’s (1994) method, we built Table 5 to choose the cut-off level. The selected cutoff level was three; only the relations with three or more frequencies from the implication matrix were included on the map. This cut-off level represented 33.3% of the active cells, and 71.5% of the active linkages, which corresponds to a measure of variance (Gengler & Reynolds, 1995). As a guideline, one recommendation is that selected linkages should correspond to more than two-thirds of all linkages of an implication matrix (Gengler & Reynolds, 1995), and our cut-off level satis<sup>fi</sup>ed the recommendation. According to the cut-off criterion, C4 (use more multimedia content), C5 (thrill of new applications) and C7 (habitual use) were excluded from the map because they had no linkages with a value of 3 or greater. ## RESULTS

The hierarchical map in Figure 1 reveals what goals users try to pursue through a smartphone and how these goals are related. The map shows that by using a smartphone, users attempt to sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence amusement sense of comfort restorative, which are placed in the highly abstract <sup>fi</sup>eld of the map. In terms of centrality of the codes, socialization, productive daily life, improve communication and acquire information have a predominant role in the means-end goal structure. These four elements are connected to 62.1% of all linkages in the implication matrix. Functions most frequently used on a smartphone include the communication function (e.g. mobile messengers and social networking service apps) and the information search function (e.g. map and dictionary), which jointly correspond to 69.5% of functions mentioned in the implication matrix. The detailed <sup>fi</sup>ndings are as follows.

Improve communication is the most signi<sup>fi</sup>cant means in that it leads to several of the highly centralized goals such as socialization, productive daily life and acquire information. A better communication environment (improve communication) can be achieved by the communication function. For example, a mobile messenger provides users with another constant communication channel in addition to voice communication, and applications for social networking services (SNSs) also enable users to more easily communicate with SNS peers. In turn, the better communication environment facilitates socialization, which

Improve communication leads to sense of comfort mediated by socialization. Sense of comfort can be de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the state of ease and peaceful contentment’ (Kolcaba & Kolcaba, 1991, p. 1302), and individuals can reach this psychological state by having positive social relations (Kolcaba & DiMarco, 2005). For instance, mobile applications for social network sites can make smartphone users feel a constant connection to their peers, and this sense of social connection can help them to reach a state of comfort.

Sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, one of the highly abstract goals of smartphones, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as self-assurance in one’s abilities to execute a desired behaviour (Feltz, 1988). Sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence is achieved in diverse ways. First of all, productive daily life is a critical means for users to develop sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence. Productive daily life is led mainly by acquisition of information, which is achieved through improve communication and the information search function. Also, by reaping bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from schedule applications on smartphones (management of schedule & information function), users can achieve effective management of daily life or work. In turn, with the positive experiences derived from productive daily life, users develop an improved sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence. Such experiences arouse positive psychological states, which promote the sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence. This <sup>fi</sup>nding re<sup>fl</sup>ects the argument that individuals’ con<sup>fi</sup>dence depends on their own assessment of their physiological states (Wood & Bandura, 1989). Therefore, when users feel they manage their daily life well, they may have increased con<sup>fi</sup>dence about their life.

A stronger sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence stems directly from acquire information, as users may consider timely information obtained by a smartphone as a resource. Con<sup>fi</sup>dence can be built on personal assessment of the availability of resources that may affect performance (Gist & Mitchell, 1992). Such availability of timely information may help users to develop con<sup>fi</sup>dence. Personalized device environment also leads to sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, because applications downloaded on a smartphone, which generate personalized environments, can be considered available functions which can affect users’ performance.

There is a strong relationship between hanging out and restorative. When users have some spare time, they often use a smartphone with the purpose of hanging out, indicating that they kill time gaming, chatting or reading newspaper gossip. The <sup>fi</sup>nding implies that users seek restorative through entertaining themselves with a smartphone rather than simply aiming fo entertainment itself.

## DISCUSSION

In this study, we explored what goals users pursue through smartphone use from a value-oriented perspective. The MEC analysis was used to trace user goals and values. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that users try to achieve diverse goals and values, which are associated with one another, through a smartphone. The study has several theoretical and practical implications, which are introduced in the following section

## Implications for research

The present study has a theoretical implication, in that it suggests a new angle on user acceptance of IT. The principal theoretical contribution of the study is an examination of the value-oriented approach to user acceptance of technology. The value-oriented approach offers an alternative way to understand users’ acceptance of user-empowering IT beyond the deterministic paradigm of user adoption. In the current user-centric paradigm, users are active subjects who pursue their own goals with the technology rather than passive individuals who are assumed to respond to the given technology as in the traditional research on user adoption of IT. The conceptual shift of users calls attention to what users actually do with the technology or to what goals or values users acquire with the technology (i.e. value-oriented perspective), rather than which factors affect user adoption of a given technology (i.e. the user adoption perspective). Through the exploration of goals and values which users obtain in using a smartphone, the current study proposes that there is a need for more focus on value-oriented research on users’ IT behaviour.

Furthermore, this study contributes to advance the prior value-oriented research. The study is different from prior value-oriented research in that the prior research delves into highly abstract values (e.g. utilitarian, hedonic and monetary); by encompassing a spectrum from concrete use behaviours to aimed values, the study offers more vivid explanation o smartphone practices. For example, although a utilitarian value is simply used as an antecedent of perceived value or intention in the previous value-oriented research (e.g. Kim & Han (2009)); Kim et al. (2007); Turel et al. (2007)); our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that the value is not only achieved through various use behaviours, such as information search, use of enhanced communication functions and scheduling apps, but is also an intermediate goal which ultimately allows users to get con<sup>fi</sup>dence in themselves. The study encompassing detailed descriptions of IT practices is also the same line with increasing interests in use contexts in research on mobile environments (e.g. Lee et al. (2005); Mallat et al. (2009); Yang et al. (2012)). Thus, by investigating the entanglement of the concrete and the abstract of IT practices, this study provides an alternative approach for value-oriented research on IT behaviour.

Compared with the prior value-oriented research, this study has another advantage. The prior studies employ a few types of values, such as utilitarian value, hedonic value or social value, to investigate values, which IT users pursue (e.g. Turel et al., 2007; Kim & Han, 2009; Choi et al., 2011). Although those values or goals can be hierarchically organized (Maslow, 1954; Pervin, 1989), the prior value-oriented research does not explore a value hierarchy and relationships among various types of values. The overlooking of the value hierarchy also appears in the use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation approach (Katz et al., 1974) which has been extensively used to explore individuals’ use of media technologies. The use and grati<sup>fi</sup>cation approach relies on a value-oriented perspective in that its emphasis is on individuals’ needs or goals in using media; many studies employing the approach list severa goals without their relations (e.g. Kim et al., 2011a, 2011b; Grellhesl & Punyanunt-Cater, 2012) or examine their in<sup>fl</sup>uence on result variables, like intention to use (e.g., Cheung et al., 2011). Investigation of relations among goals can provide a richer picture of why and how individuals use media technologies. As our results show, a utilitarian goal (i.e. improve communication) is a subordinate goal for a social goal (i.e. socialization) and intrinsic goals (i.e. amusement, sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence). Accordingly, this study contributes to drawing a full picture of IT use values by elucidating relationships among values in addition to extracting important user values in using smartphones.

Future research needs to consider the relationships between values, when investigating user values in IT, and furthermore may compare <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study with <sup>fi</sup>ndings in different IT contexts. An examination of the relationship between a social factor (socialization) and a hedonic factor (amusement) can be a promising topic for future research. In prior literature, the relationship between two factors has rarely been investigated. Although consumption values have been extensively examined in consumer research, two factors are separately listed as values, which consumers seek through consumption, with little interest in the relationship between them (e.g. Sheth et al., 1991; Ledden et al., 2007). In the IS research area, the relationship has also seldom been reported. Some studies just examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of each of two factors on an outcome variable (e.g. Kim & Han, 2009; Turel et al., 2010). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, however, show that a social factor can be an antecedent for a hedonic factor in the smartphone context. Recent research also supports this relationship by demonstrating that positive self-image, which is formed by positively maintaining social relations by using smartphones, signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly leads to pleasure (Chun et al., 2012). The causal relationship from the social to the hedonic re<sup>fl</sup>ects a socioemotional aspect of information and communication technologies. The socioemotional process involves the formation of emotions through interaction with others (Santrock, 1997). In contemporary IT environments, where users easily interact with others by using advanced mobile technologies and social network sites, the socioemotional characteristic of IT becomes a more essential element in understanding user behaviour. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings propose that IS researchers should not only have more interest in the relationship between the social and the hedonic but also examine this relationship in various IT contexts

This study also reveals the importance of perception of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in understanding smartphone users. Sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence is the destination at which several other goals or values ultimately arrive in the hierarchical map. This result re<sup>fl</sup>ects the nature of userempowering IT through which users are able to execute diverse tasks and develop con<sup>fi</sup>dence. Thus, a sense of confidence can be thought of as a central outcome of using smartphones, and this study showed that a sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence is an important factor in studying smartphones and other user-empowering IT environments.

Despite its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance for comprehending users’ behaviour, sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence has not been examined in prior research on users’ behaviour in user-empowering IT, including smartphones. Instead of perception of con<sup>fi</sup>dence, the previous research has widely used self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy, which is a similar concept. Although both have been used to explain one’s perceived capability to execute a task, they have differences. While self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy indicates a situationally speci<sup>fi</sup>c con<sup>fi</sup>dence (Bandura, 1977), such as exercise self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (McAuley et al., 1993), computer self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Compeau & Higgins, 1995) and learning self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy (Pajares, 1996), sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence in this study is regarded as a more comprehensive belief in one’s judgement or capabilities (Feltz, 1988). Also, self-ef<sup>fi</sup>cacy has been mainly used as a determinant of perceived ease of use in research on end-use IT adoption (e.g. Yi & Hwang,

2003; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008); sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence is regarded as an ultimate goal or a result variable according to our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Given the continuing diffusion of user-empowering characteristics into the IT area, sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence can be considered a critical outcome variable. Thus, this study proposes that future research elucidates a theoretical meaning of sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence and empirically demonstrates its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance in research on user-empowering IT environments. One possible model is that researchers could use sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence as a result variable and investigate which factors affect sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence (e.g. information quality, access quality and personalized environment). Another potential application is its role as a mediator between users’ beliefs toward a technology and satisfaction with the technology. We may be able to demonstrate the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of sense of con<sup>fi</sup>dence by examining its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on satisfaction.

Finally, from the methodological aspect, this study makes a contribution by introducing the MEC analysis in the IS discipline. Given that the analysis has been successfully used to investigate consumers’ goals for a product or service in the marketing <sup>fi</sup>eld, it can be useful in exploring users’ goals or motivations for using IT. Despite its potential, the approach has been rarely considered and applied in IS research. There are a couple of recent studies employing this approach to explore users’ goals (e.g. Jung & Kang (2010); Jung & Pawlowski (2009)). Still, there is a lack of research applying this approach in investigating users of emerging IT, and this study <sup>fi</sup>lls an empirical research gap. Moreover, this study elaborates the coding procedure and detailedly presents the approach in phases. Accordingly, the study helps IS researchers to apply it in different IT settings.

## Implications for practice

This study further offers practical insights for mobile industry players. The <sup>fi</sup>rst practica contribution of the study is its generation of baseline knowledge about what goals and values users achieve with a smartphone. Prior research regarding usage or adoption of mobile services has extensively examined factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing user intention and failed to inform about what users actually do through mobile services. This study provides concrete information by <sup>fi</sup>nding diverse goals and values and elucidating their relations. Such knowledge is essentia for service providers and device vendors to develop marketing and design strategies.

By helping marketers understand consumers’ purchase decisions and use behaviour, the means-end chain approach offers guidance for the development of marketing strategies (Reynolds & Olson, 2001). Marketers can apply our <sup>fi</sup>ndings in advertising smartphones. For example, marketers can attract customers by advertising that smartphone users are able to acquire information anywhere at any moment, and this merit allows users to have more con<sup>fi</sup>dence in life. Another possible scenario for advertising is to suggest that a smartphone makes users feel comfort, because a better communication environment supported by mobile messengers and SNS mobile applications offers constant connection with friends. In this manner, marketers can utilize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings in developing marketing strategies.

Another managerial implication is that companies need to offer opportunities for employees to proactively use smartphones at work. As shown in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, performance improvement (e.g.

acquire information and productive daily life) is an important goal associated with smartphones. Many businesses have also recognized the potential of smartphones for increasing work ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and have encouraged their employees to purchase smartphones (Gruman, 2010). However, they have left how to exploit the smartphone entirely up to employees. For example, employees keep up with new apps, which can improve their ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, either from lists of popular apps or through word of mouth. Companies need to provide chances to share and create useful apps for work. Managers may not only create an online board where employees can share information of those useful apps but also provide support for employees to develop apps for work. The organizationa commitment can extract the worth of smartphones for improving work ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency beyond private use.

Our results also con<sup>fi</sup>rmed that smartphone applications are the crux of smartphone use. It can be assumed that apps, which satisfy a variety of goals and values not only enhance users loyalty to a smartphone but also attract potential users to purchase one. However, restrictions of app portals, which individual developers have to stick to. may impede inspired production of apps, and users may want a ‘jailbreak’ process to obtain more peculiar apps (Holzer & Ondrus, 2011). Device vendors and service providers, therefore, should invest in a better environment which attracts individual developers to participate in producing various smartphone apps. Additionally, app portal providers can use the long tail strategy (Anderson, 2006), which generates revenues by selling a large number of unpopular but distinctive apps. Providers need to encourage individual developers to develop creative apps, which enable users to use smartphones in diverse ways or for varied goals, though those apps are downloaded by a small number of users. Because the total number of sales of unpopular, but creative, apps is comparable with revenues from selling a few popular apps, providers may need to have regard for production and distribution of these kinds of apps (Holzer & Ondrus, 2011).

## Limitations and future research

This study has the following limitations. Our research subjects are all university students in South Korea. The sampling may limit generalizability of <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the study. However, university students are IT-savvy users who can lead the diffusion of new IT. Also, South Korea is a leading country in the domain of mobile infrastructure and services (Forge & Bohlin, 2008). Therefore, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be useful in obtaining insight for future smartphone users.

The second limitation is that we did not take into account possible differences between values aimed at by experienced users and values aimed at by novice users. Goals and values which users pursue with a smartphone can change over time. Because of the small sample size, the current study has a restraint in group comparison. A future research strategy for this is the comparison of values, which are pursued between long-term users and new users.

Another potential weakness is that the small number of participants in the coding process may limit the validity of the results. However, we tried to mitigate this limitation through the iterative procedure of coding content (i.e. continuous independent codings and discussions by two coders and two coding facilitators). Despite the incomplete procedure, our efforts enhance the validity of the results. For more rigorous coding, researchers may be able to increase result validity by having more people participate in the coding procedure. Another way is that researchers could conduct a more acceptable coding process by rigorously referring codes and concepts, which are commonly used in relevant literature.

The fourth limitation is that analyses of some codes (i.e. use more multimedia content, thril of new things (apps) and habitual use) were excluded in this study, because they had little relation with the other goals in our data. Such weak relations could have been caused by our data size, and these codes could have more salient connections with others as the data size increases. Therefore, the exclusion does not necessarily indicate that those codes are unimportant. Rather, they can offer some insights into understanding user values in a smartphone. For example, Thrill of new things (apps) implies that smartphone users pursue epistemic value, indicating the perceived utility derived from curiosity or novelty (Sheth et al., 1991). Accordingly, future research may need to pay attention to elements, which were excluded in this study.

One possible limitation of this study is that it included only positive gains derived from using a smartphone; there may also be negative gains. For example, some interviewees mentioned that entertainment applications, such as games, cause them to waste spare time, and they then feel regret. The exclusion of negative gains may be caused by the assumption of the meansend chain approach: people use a means to achieve a (positive) goal. The weak relations of habitual use with the other elements can be also explained from that perspective. Because habitual use may lead users to smartphone addiction, which can have a negative impact on their life, it could not be connected to the other positive elements. Recently, a negative outcome of IT use has begun to receive attention from IS researchers (e.g. internet addiction in Young (2007), technostress in Tarafdar et al. (2007)). Considering that a negative impact of IT use can distort individuals’ IT behaviour, research on the negative side of IT use should be included in IS research (Turel et al., 2011). Therefore, future research may need to revise the traditional means-end chain approach to explore the negative gains and the values.

Lastly, because of the methodological design (i.e. interviews probing personal values rather than social meanings), this study did not encompass what smartphones mean for society. Mobile phones have radically changed our daily life and moreover, have transformed culture, social structures or values. For example, by facilitating communication among diverse groups of people, mobile phones contribute to decreasing gender barriers and further accelerating democratic progress in India (Doron & Jeffrey, 2013). The focus of this study on personal values excluded discussion on the social meanings of smartphones, which are critical in understanding the impact of smartphones on human beings. Future research is required to investigate socia meanings or social values of smartphones and to examine how personal values are interpreted in terms of society.

## CONCLUSION

Even though we are witnessing the rapid dissemination of smartphones into human life, numerous questions about this phenomenon are still unanswered. In order to offer insight, we explored the meaning of a smartphone from the users’ standpoint. The results revealed that users’ diverse goals and values are associated with smartphone use. We hope the study will not only be a starting point for future research on smartphone users but also encourage value-oriented approaches, which focus on the values users seek with a technology, rather than depending on the framework of traditional technology adoption.
