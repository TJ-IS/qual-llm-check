# Profile of IS research published in the European Journal of Information Systems

Yogesh K. Dwivedi<sup>1</sup> and Jasna Kuljis<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

This paper profiles the types of research activity that have been published in EJIS from 1997 to 2007. Our analysis includes variables such as the most productive authors, citation analysis, universities associated with the most research publications, geographic diversity, authors’ background, subject areas most often investigated, unit of analysis and research methodologies. The classification of the topics and methodologies used by the most highly published authors will help prospective authors gauge whether their paper is suitable for EJIS. The major geographical source of information system (IS) research published in EJIS is from AIS region 2 (Europe, the Middle East and Africa), but with a substantial AIS region 1 (American-based researchers and universities) and AIS region 3 (Asia–Pacific) contribution. The most common research method used is the case study approach, with other methods such as surveys and library research also used frequently. IS management and IS development are the two most researched IS topics published in EJIS. This research and results reported in this paper are comparable with a previous paper published about the Information Systems Journal. Any further such studies will thus be able to make similar comparisons between these journals and any others that have subsequently been covered in this way. The paper concludes with the need for more substantive research on the topic if journal comparisons are to achieve their potential.

## Introduction

The Association for Information Systems (AIS) recently declared six journals as ‘A’ Class IS journals, listed alphabetically as European Journal of Information Systems (EJIS), Information Systems Journal (ISJ), Information Systems Research (ISR), Journal of AIS (JAIS), Journal of MIS (JMIS) and MIS Quarterly (MISQ) (AIS, 2007). The Australian Council of Professors and Heads of Information Systems has also included EJIS within its list of nine A þ journals which includes all the above six journals along with Decision Support Systems (DSS), Information & Management (I&M) and the Journal of Information Technology (JIT) (Fisher et al., 2007, 2008). These journals have now reached a state of maturity in terms of both quantity and quality of publications to make worthwhile to conduct research on classifying, categorising and profiling the papers published in various dimensions such as authors and institutional productivity, geographical diversity, theoretical/methodological diversity, etc. Such research could be the basis for forming a new research agenda, or could provide some frequently called for guidelines and suggestions on how to publish in highly rated journals (Barki et al., 1993; Avgerou et al., 1999; Claver et al., 2000; Avison et al., 2001, 2008; Galliers & Whitley, 2002, 2007; Vessey et al., 2002; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Huang & Hsu, 2005; Xu & Chau, 2006; Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich, 2007; Galliers et al., 2007; Irani et al., 2007; Lyytinen et al., 2007; Mingers & Harzing, 2007; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007; Vidgen et al., 2007).

This paper presents a study of published papers in EJIS in the period 1997–2007. Our study deliberately emulates similar studies published in the Information Systems Journal (Avison et al., 2008) and Information & Management (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). We believe that our study and other such studies can help scholars determine which journals have a readership that would be most receptive to the completed research that the scholar wishes to write a paper about. An informed choice of an appropriate journal for disseminating research can potentially increase the chances of paper acceptance, reduce the number of review circles, and hence transmit new knowledge quickly and most effectively and efficiently to those readers in the field with a determined interest in similar studies.

As a by-product of our research, we are able to contribute to the increasing debate about differences between North American and European IS research (Avgerou et al., 1999; Katerttanakul & Han, 2003; Lyytinen et al., 2007; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). For example, one input to the debate was by profiling the research published in the European Conference on Information Systems (Galliers & Whitley, 2002, 2007; Galliers et al., 2007; Vidgen et al., 2007). Similar efforts have been made profiling ISJ research, another leading European IS journal (Avison et al., 2001, 2008). Although EJIS has recognised and supported such scholarly research (Avgerou et al., 1999; Galliers & Whitley, 2002, 2007; Vessey et al., 2002; Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich, 2007; Galliers et al., 2007; Irani et al., 2007; Lyytinen et al., 2007; Mingers & Harzing, 2007; Vidgen et al., 2007), this is the first time its own publications are profiled and reviewed.

As already mentioned, we undertook our analysis bearing in mind a desire to make the results comparable if possible with those published in the Information Systems Journal (Avison et al., 2008) and Information & Management (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). To do this, we established that we needed to seek the following to match the approaches taken by these two papers (set out in more detail in the next section):

1. to identify the most productive authors;

2. to determine the research impact of the most productive authors;

3. to determine the geographic location of contributing authors based on the AIS classification;

4. to identify authors’ background, that is academic or practitioner;

5. to identify the universities associated with the most research publications;

6. to determine the topics most investigated, and analyse their trends;

7. to identify the various units of analysis commonly used in EJIS publications;

8. to classify EJIS publications according to the research paradigm; on the basis of their use of primary research data (empirical and non-empirical); on the basis of the nature of primary research data (quantitative and qualitative); and according to the research methods used; and

9. to discuss the subject areas and methodologies preference of the most productive authors.

To realise these objectives, a systematic and comprehensive review of the 305 articles published in EJIS during the period 1997–2007 was conducted. This review is covered in the remainder of this paper, which is structured as follows. The next sections provide a discussion of the method used in the analysis of the published EJIS research. The main findings are presented in the section that follows and are then put in the context of the similar studies conducted on other European IS journals in the Discussion section. We end the paper with a summary of our findings before concluding with how the potential benefits of studies like this can be made to benefit the IS community.

## Research method

To create a profile of research topics, research methodologies, universities and authors, the study thoroughly examined the EJIS papers published between the years 1997 and 2007. Following Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich’s (2007) suggestions this study excluded editorials, guest editorials and book reviews from the analysis. As it was not possible to obtain detailed information by only reviewing the abstract, a more in-depth literature review was undertaken on all 305 articles. A full version of each article was carefully examined to capture the data related to topics and methodology. This required the articles to be (laboriously) coded, which took the first of the co-authors a period of 4 months part-time.

Various items were recorded for each article including the productivity of authors, citations of selected articles, geographic regions, authors’ background, research topics and the research methodology used by the author. As in previous studies (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007; Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich, 2007; Galliers & Whitley, 2007), in order to record authors’ productivity data, a normal count approach was used. A normal count approach means that each publication counted as one for all authors, regardless of the number of co-authors (Gallivan & Benbunan-Fich, 2007). The impact of the research by the most productive authors was assessed by counting Google’s scholar citation counts; the ISI citation counts; total citation counts; and h-index values. Institutional contributions/productivity was examined by using both the normal count approach, where each publication counted as one for all authors regardless of the number of co-authors from the same university (Avison et al., 2008), and an adjusted count approach in which only one count was allocated to one or more authors from the same institution (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). Both the background of authors and geographic location variables were adapted from Avison et al. (2001, 2008); however, geographic regions were divided according to the AIS guidelines.

For capturing the data about the research topics, we adapted Palvia & Pinjani’s (2007) classification scheme that consists of 33 research areas. The reason for choosing this scheme over Barki et al.’s (1993) classification was simply to provide researchers with comparative data. The categories for recording the research methodologiesrelated aspects were adapted from various studies (Avison et al., 2001, 2008; Chen & Hirschheim, 2004; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). We decided to follow Palvia & Pinjani (2007). Our coding allowed for up to two research methods per article as some articles generally use more than one methodology.

It is important to emphasise at this point that like previous profiling studies (Claver et al., 2000; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007) the findings of this study, in terms of most productive authors and universities with the most contributors, should be regarded as ‘indicative and not an authoritative declaration’ (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). This is because such profiling analysis may ‘exclude important researchers because they might not have published’ (Claver et al., 2000; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007) in EJIS within the duration of this analysis or many authors simply may have expertise and skills to publish in other preferred outlets than EJIS. Similarly, it is possible that ‘several universities have niches of research expertise that are not reflected in our results’ (Claver et al., 2000; Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). Therefore, as suggested by previous such studies: ‘caution should be exercised in interpreting these results’.

## The first eight findings

The first eight findings of this study are presented in sequence in the following subsections.

## Productive authors

We started our analysis by identifying the authors who have published the most during the period 1997–2007 in EJIS. As mentioned above, we adopted the approach of Palvia & Pinjani (2007) for assessing research productivity, where all publications naming the researcher are counted equally (e.g. an article with three co-authors would provide one count for each). This approach results in the combined count of all authors being greater than the total number of articles.

For presenting the findings this study included only those authors in the list who have published three or more articles during the period studied. Table 1 lists the 18 most productive authors, along with their current affiliation and AIS region, sorted by the number of publications as well as alphabetically for authors sharing the same number of publications. Irani tops the list with eight publications, followed by Lyytinen with six, then Love and Powell with five publications each, and then by Lycett and Wei with four publications each. The remaining 12 authors in Table 1 each contributed to three publications. Sixty authors contributed to two articles, and finally, the largest number of authors (477) contributed to just the one article.

## Citation analysis for most productive authors

A citation analysis was conducted to determine the research impact of the most productive authors based on EJIS publications (aggregated in Table 2). The findings suggest that the highest number of ISI citations is for articles contributed by Irani (60) followed by Love (54) and Kern (42). However, a total citations count does not produce comparative data as authors with a large number of articles may have a larger citations count. ISI provides an analysis tool, the h-index, which claims to eliminate such problems. According to ISI: ‘The h-index is indicated by a green (sic) horizontal line. The number of items above this line, which is $h ,$ has at least h citations. For example, an h-index of 20 means that there are 20 items that have 20 citations or more’. The h-index values presented in Table 2 are only for EJIS publications; articles published in other journals for these authors were eliminated while estimating the values. Findings show that the largest h-index value of 4 is associated with Irani followed by six authors with an h-index value of $^ { 3 , }$ nine authors with an h-index value of 2 and two authors associated with an h-index value of 1 each. If the h-index is more comparable as claimed, then this suggests that there is not much difference between 15 or so authors.

## Authors’ geographic location – according to AIS regions and publication years

In terms of the number of authors/contributors from different geographical regions suggested by AIS, the largest number of EJIS contributors is 379 (57.3%,) located in AIS region 2 (Europe, the Middle East and Africa), followed by the AIS region 1 (America) with 183 (27.7%) of the authors/contributors. The third and final large category was formed by the AIS region 3 (the Asia and Pacific region) with 99 (15.0%) of the contributors (Table 3).

The findings shown in Table 3 illustrate that although there is no immediate clear pattern, the contributors from the AIS region 2 were generally the most numerous whereas the number of contributors from AIS region 3 was generally the lowest. The only anomaly was the year 2005 in which the number of contributors from both AIS regions 1 and 3 outnumbered those from AIS region 2. One partial explanation for this increase of contributors from the Asia and Pacific regions in 2005 is a special issue (Volume 14, Number 4, December 2005) covering the Pacific Asia Conference on Information Systems 2004, which had 12 authors from the region publishing in it.

## Authors’ background – academic/practitioner

In terms of the number of authors/contributors from academia or industry, the largest number of contributors (605 or 91.5%) were from academia and only a very small proportion of authors were based in industry (56 or 8.5%) (see Table 4). There are no discernible trends.

## Leading research universities

This section describes and illustrates the number of authors/contributors affiliated with a particular organisation/university (see Table 5). Authors/contributors from

292 organisations/universities contributed to one or more articles in EJIS between 1997 and 2007. The breakdown of the frequency of contributors/authors affiliated to a particular organisation or university is illustrated in Table 5 (columns 1–3). From the 292 universities/organisations Brunel University ranked first with the largest number of contributions (38), followed by the National University of Singapore with 22 contributions in second place, and the University of Salford with 16 contributions in third place. Other most productive institutions with six or more contributions are listed in Table 5 (columns 1–3). From the remaining numbers, four universities feature with five contri butions, 18 universities with four contributions, 25 universities with three contributions, 53 universities with two contributions and finally, the largest number of universities (119) with just one contribution from each. However, it is important to mention that the affiliations of 51 authors were either unknown or they were from industry.

In Table 5, in columns 1–3 findings counted all authors of each paper in order to determine the contribution of universities. However, Palvia & Pinjani (2007) argued that ‘it is typical to have several researchers from the same university co-authoring an article, a raw count by the author’s affiliation would result in multiple counts’. Therefore, they suggested that adjustments should be made ‘so that a university having two or more authors on a single publication was counted only once’ (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007). We present a modified listing in data in columns 4–6 of Table 5. Table 5 (columns 4–6) presents the top 22 universities having four or more articles published in EJIS during the period 1997–2007.

The top place in the adjusted list remains occupied by Brunel University, with 25 contributions. However, the London School of Economics, occupies second place with 14 contributions in the modified list pushing the National University of Singapore (13 contributions) into third place. Two leading U.K. universities, namely, the University of Warwick and the University of Salford, each with nine contributions hold fourth place. The schools/ departments and their academics/researchers from the universities mentioned above have strong reputations in IS research. Although the table clearly shows a domination of U.K. universities, of a total of 22 universities included in the list, six are U.S.A. based and four are from the Asia–Pacific. However, European Universities (other than the U.K.) are underrepresented; as there are only two institutions (Copenhagen Business School and Erasmus University – Rotterdam) in the top 22 (see Table 5).

## Discussion

The findings presented above explored a number of dimensions of the EJIS publications from 1997 to 2007. The findings suggest that the most productive authors in terms of number of papers published in the past 11 years were Irani, Lyytinen, Love and Powell. ISI citation analysis clearly suggests that Irani has had the highest research impact followed by a number of authors with the second highest impact. However, Google Scholar’s total citation counts identifies Baskerville as having the largest number of citations followed by Irani in second place. In terms of geographical locations of contributors, a larger number of authors were located in AIS region 2 than in the other two AIS regions. Clearly, a very small proportion of the authors were from industry in comparison to academic authors. A larger number of contributions came from U.K.-based universities than universities from other countries; the largest number of contributors and publications were associated with Brunel University and the London School of Economics. Situated outside of the U.K., the National University of Singapore was also a dominant institution in terms of number of contributions. IS management, IS development and organisation-related topics were reported as being the three most frequently researched topics. Organisational context/unit of analysis was the most frequently researched context. The approaches used most often in publications were interpretive, empirical, qualitative and case study methods.

We wanted to establish how our findings compare with the findings from similar studies recently conducted for other IS journals as well as reporting on any other interesting findings. So, we first performed a comparison of our study with Avison’s et al. (2008) study of ISJ publication. Where possible we extended the comparison to the other European journals. We found that this provides limited results. We then compared EJIS with I&M (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007).

## Comparison of EJIS and ISJ

The ISJ study (Avison’s et al., 2008) covers the first 17 years of the journal (1991–2007) and understandably prominence is given to the content of work published in the journal without much reference to profiles of other journals. Unlike our study, no importance was given to identifying productive authors. However, the author’s gender was considered as well as authors’ affiliations and geographical distribution.

Table 12 provides a comparison of the main topics and research methods published in EJIS and ISJ. Please note that ISJ adopted Barki et al. (1993) categories for research topics (nine categories) whereas in EJIS study we adopted Palvia & Pinjani (2007) categories (34 categories), which obviously affects the accuracy of our analysis.

There are some important similarities and differences in the profile of the papers published in these two journals (see Table 12). In both journals the dominant topics are IS management and IS development. In EJIS in third place are papers covering topics that deal with organisational issues and design/BPR/workflow systems (7.7%). However. in ISI. the third dominant topic is in the category of IS education and research (13.1%) whereas EJIS papers addressed IS research in 6.1% papers and IS education in only 0.6% papers. The dominant research method in EJIS is case study whereas in ISJ library research/literature analysis/frameworks/conceptual modelling takes the prominent place. ISJ published more action research papers (7.3%) than EJIS (2.1%) and EJIS published more papers (17.6%) using interview method than ISJ (5.1%). Both journals publish mainly interpretative and empirical research. The biggest difference between EJIS and ISJ is to do with research methods; EJIS mainly published papers that used qualitative methods (64.3%) whereas ISJ published papers using either descriptive/theoretical/meta analysis (40%) and qualitative (36%) method.

## Summary

A quick summary of the more interesting conclusions drawn out in the above discussion is as follows:

 The European IS journals have little geographical disparity among authors.

 There is marked geographical disparity of authors between European and American IS journals.

 The latter disparity may be a native language problem.

 The geographical diversity of EJIS authors indicates that there are a substantial number of contributors belonging to all three AIS regions.

 The most often published topics in EJIS were IS management-related followed by IS development.

 The most widely investigated unit of analysis in EJIS was the organisation/firm.

 The most commonly used approaches in EJIS were interpretive, empirical and qualitative.

 The most dominant method used in EJIS was case study, followed by interview and survey.

As indicated above in the list of most important points, when considering research in terms of the research paradigm, the interpretive approach is currently used to a much greater extent than both the positivist and critical approaches. This suggests a clear indication of EJIS researchers under-utilising other paradigms that may have implications for editors, reviewers and authors. Similarly, the use of empirical and qualitative techniques and case study research methods appears to have been much preferred over other available alternatives. Therefore, there are clear messages for authors to make greater use of the theoretical and methodological varieties available to them, and for journal reviewers and editors to support the use of such alternative approaches.

## Conclusions

We compared two European journals ISJ (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007; Avison et al., 2008) and EJIS as reported in this paper. We found that because of the different approaches taken by the studies, in particular ISJ vs I&M and ElIS. this makes it more difficult to conduct a thorough comparison. If the journals used the same framework to report the analyses of these journals the similarities and differences among these journals would be much more transparent and hence easier to identify. The question is which framework to adopt, ISJ or I&M? We found the ISJ study (Avison et al., 2008) much more editor oriented as it provides an overview of the journal from a limited historical perspective. This is not to say that the study does not provide valuable information to potential authors. However, information about the number of papers per volume is hardly of any interest to the general journal readership. The study of I&M (Palvia & Pinjani, 2007), on the other hand, is heavily oriented to reporting information about the authors published in the journal, especially identifying the most prolific ones. One can argue that this information does not serve any purpose other than vanity. However, this is the kind of overview authors like to see either if they were singled out because of their publication record or to compare oneself to the named authors. It can also serve the purpose of identifying people and places for the purpose of collaboration, etc. Therefore, we find the I&M study more reader oriented. When deciding which of the two to adopt for our study of EJIS, we went possibly for a slight overkill and adopted a majority of the features from each study.

Reflecting back on this paper and the work that went into it, however, leads us to a more substantive recommendation. We thought as we set out on this attempt to profile EJIS and provide for journal comparisons that with several papers in print on the subject, it would be a relatively straight forward task, time consuming and tedious in places, and with care required in writing the paper to avoid it being too dry and boring. We were wrong: all the latter things were true. But we now conclude that the subject of journal comparison, if it is to provide the community with usable information, requires a great deal more thought, investigation, research and testing. A taxonomy of research topics and research methods has to be in place so that like can be compared to like between the journals.
