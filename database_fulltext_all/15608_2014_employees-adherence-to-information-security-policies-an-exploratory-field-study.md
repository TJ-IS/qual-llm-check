## A B S T R A C T

The key threat to information security comes from employees who do not comply with information security policies. We developed a new multi-theory based model that explained employees’ adherence to security policies. The paradigm combines elements from the Protection Motivation Theory, the Theory of Reasoned Action, and the Cognitive Evaluation Theory. We validated the model by using a sample of 669 responses from four corporations in Finland. The SEM-based results showed that perceived severity of potential information security threats, employees’ belief as to whether they can apply and adhere to information security policies, perceived vulnerability to potential security threats, employees’ attitude toward complying with information security policies, and social norms toward complying with these policies had a significant and positive effect on the employees’ intention to comply with information security policies. Intention to comply with information security policies also had a significant impact on actual compliance with these policies. High level managers must warn employees of the importance of information security and why it is necessary to carry out these policies. In addition, employees should be provided with security education and hands on training.

## 1. Introduction

Information security incidents have increased significantly in the last decade. In order to cope with increased threats, not only technical solutions, but IS security policies have been proposed. Employees, however, seldom comply with these policies, placing their organizations’ assets and businesses at risk. In order to address this concern, several information security policies compliance approaches have been proposed. We have decided to build a new multi-theory based model using the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), and the Cognitive Evaluation Theory (CET) and then to validate it empirically using a large sample of practitioners from Finland (N = 669).

## 2. Previous work on information security policy compliance

Recent research studies on the topic of security policy compliance have been divided into three categories: (1) conceptual principles with no underlying theory or empirical evidence; (2) theoretical models with no empirical evidence; and (3) empirical work grounded in theory.

Studies in the conceptual principles area only present practical guidelines and suggestions for improving employees compliance with information security policies as well as allow users to learn of information security countermeasures that can reduce the resulting problems.

Theoretical models without empirical evidence include studies that provide theory-based insights into how employees policy compliance can be enhanced. These, however, do not offer any evidence to support the insights. These models suggest that both social and technical solutions can be used to reduce computer abuse. Karjalainen and Siponen [8] claimed that that IS security training had some unique characteristics and, therefore, needed its own theory, which, according to the authors, must satisfy four pedagogical requirements: (a) a group oriented approach of teaching and learning, (b) teaching contents based on collective experiences of learners’ contents, (c) collaborative learning based teaching methods that produce collective knowledge, and (d) evaluation of learning needs to emphasize experiential and communication-based methods. They concluded by showing how an IS security training approach can be designed that satisfy these requirements.

Empirical studies grounded in theories present studies that are both theory-based and empirically validated. Aytes and Connolly [1] used the rational choice model to explain why university students engage in risky computing behavior such as opening email attachments without checking for viruses, failing to back up files, and disclosing passwords. Using a sample of 167 students, they found that respondents continued to practice unsafe computing even when they were fairly knowledgeable in safe computing practices. They suggested that providing additional information may not necessarily result in safe behavior and concluded by suggesting that organizations may have to force participants to comply.

D’Arcy, Hovav, and Galletta [3] stated that insider misuse of IS resources was a significant threat to organizations. They used an extended version of the deterrence theory to investigate whether perceived certainty and severity of organizational sanctions were affected by user awareness of IS security countermeasures. They found, using a sample of 269 employees from eight companies, that computer users were aware of the: security policies through the training programs; and observation of computer misuse. They further found that perceived severity of sanctions was more effective in reducing IS misuse than actual sanctions.

Johnston and Warkentin [7] investigated the effect of fearinducing arguments (e.g., fear appeals) on end users’ compliance with information security policies. The authors postulated, based on an extension of the Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), that response efficacy, self-efficacy, and social influence would have a positive effect on employees’ intention to adopt anti-spyware software tools. They hypothesized that the perception of threat severity would negatively affect response efficacy and self-efficacy and also that the perception of threat susceptibility would negatively influence the efficacies. They found support, using a sample of 275 participants, for all hypothesized relationships with the exception of perceptions of threat susceptibility to response efficacy and perceptions of threat susceptibility to self-efficacy.

A recent study by Hovav and D’Arcy [5] used the deterrence theory in an experiment conducted across cultures. Using a sample of 366 MBA and industry respondents from the United States and 360 from South Korea, the authors found that perceived certainty (PC) of sanctions had a stronger influence on IS misuse intention for the Korean sample whereas the influence of perceived severity (PS) of sanctions was stronger for the U.S. sample. Technical counter measures (TCM) had a significant relationship with perceived certainty of measures but not perceived severity for the Korean sample whereas, for the U.S. sample, TCM had a significant relationship with both PC and PS. Age had a significant relationship with IS misuse intention (INT) for the U.S. sample whereas, the Korean sample showed that age was positively associated with INT. The authors surmised, based on their findings, that deterrence theory was not culturally neutral.

Jai-Yeol [6] questioned the efficacy of the results obtained by previous research studies in IS security policy compliance using a general deterrence theory-based model that was embedded in extrinsic motivation. The author postulated that an intrinsic motivation-grounded model could do a better job than an extrinsic motivation-based model. The author used a model that included both extrinsic and intrinsic characteristics of human behavior. Using a PLS-based approach to data analysis on a sample of 602 employees, the author found that constructs in the intrinsic motivation paradigm contributed significantly more to the employees’ security policy compliance variability than constructs embedded in the extrinsic motivation paradigm.

Siponen and Vance [12] felt that IS security researchers had used deterrence theory to examine the violations of IS security policies, whereas neutralization theory could provide a better explanation of IS security violations. They postulated that neutralization positively affect intention to violate IS security policies. The authors also theorized that formal sanctions, informal sanctions, and shame negatively affected intention to violate IS security policies. They found, using a sample of 395 employees from different organizations, that neutralization was a good predictor of employees’ intention to violate IS security policies. The effect of informal sanctions on intention, in the presence of neutralization was not, however, significant. Also, in the presence of neutralization, formal sanctions did not predict IS security violations.

Most recent studies used only one theory. Some authors used a rational choice model to study the risky computing behavior of university students while others used PMT. Combining different theories is termed theory integration; it entails fusing several theories. The hope is that an integrated model will provide more explanatory power than one derived from a single theory. With the exception of Herath and Rao [4], none of the prior studies were based on multiple theories.

## 3. The research model and hypotheses

## 3.1. Theories and the research model

Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), is considered to be the leading theory in the area of health behavior motivation. It involves the appraisal of its two components: threat and coping. Threat appraisal is concerned with the process of evaluating a fear that occurs due to an individual’s perception of how threatened he or she feels due to some situation. There are two threat appraisal factors: perceived vulnerability (how an individual feels that a negative event will take place if no measures are taken to counter the problem) and perceived severity (the degree of physical and psychological harm an illness may seem to cause). In the context of our research, these are measures of how susceptible the employee’s organization is to information security breaches and the potential harms (e.g., financial loss) that may occur because of the breaches. Coping appraisals consists of two dimensions: selfefficacy (individuals’ ability or judgment of their capabilities to carry out the coping response actions) and response efficacy (the effectiveness of the recommended coping response in reducing threat to an individual). Self-efficacy is the most powerful predictor of intention to comply with a behavior and, in the context of our study, refers to employees’ belief that they can apply and adhere to information security policies and procedures.

Response efficacy refers to the employee’s belief in whether the existing information security policies and procedures, when followed, are capable of stopping potential intruders from breaking into the organization’s assets. In recent research on PMT, authors have noted that its four central variables are: perceived severity, perceived vulnerability, response efficacy, and self-efficacy. We used these in our model as the central variables of PMT.

TRA has been found to be very useful in predicting behavior. It suggests that the stronger the intention to engage in a behavior, the greater the likelihood that the behavior will be carried out. In the context of information security policies compliance, an employee’s attitude toward complying with these policies combined with social norms will lead the employee to intend to comply with the security policies; leading to actual compliance with the policies. In our context of information security policies, behavioral intention is a judgment call about how an individual will behave toward complying with information security policies. To make intention and actual behavior more explicit in the context of employees’ adherence to information security policies, we adopted the concepts of intention and actual behavior in our model.

CET was designed to predict detrimental effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation, especially when rewards were tangible (e.g., prizes or awards). According to CET, rewards act negatively when they are interpreted as a tool for controlling behavior (because the recipients feel controlled and their feelings of self-determination and autonomy will decline.

CET also predicts positive effects of rewards, especially verbal rewards ones, on intrinsic motivation. However, verbal rewards must be administered in a non-coercive manner in order to increase employees’ beliefs that they were skilled in completing a task and that their performance was based on evaluation by their supervisor. We expected that such activities would increase employees’ competence and self-determination; thus CET predicts that employees are likely to comply with the policies.

We postulated that CET rewards would be associated with positive behavior (e.g., security policy compliance).

## 3.2. Hypotheses

An increase in perceived vulnerability leads to greater intention to behave in a healthier manner. We postulated (see Fig. 1):

H1: Perceived severity of potential information security threats positively and significantly influence employees’ intention to comply with information security policies.

H2: Perceived vulnerability from potential information security threats positively and significantly influence employees’ intention to comply with information security policies.

Coping appraisals, according to PMT, consist of self- and response-efficacy. Self-efficacy, in our context, referred to employees’ belief in their ability to apply and adhere to information security policies and procedures. We believe self-efficacy to potential information security threats has a positive and significant impact on an employee’s intention to comply with the policies and procedures.

We therefore postulated:

H3: Self-efficacy to potential information security threats positively and significantly influences employees’ intention to comply with information security policies.

Response efficacy, in the context of our research, refers to the employee’s belief in whether the existing information security policies and procedures are capable of stopping potential intruders from breaking into the organization’s assets. We postulated:

H4: Response efficacy to potential information security threats positively and significantly influences employees’ intention to comply with information security policies.

Behavioral intention affects an individual’s actual intention to carry out the behavior. TRA has been used in numerous studies such as shop-bot use, factors affecting IT ethical behavior, user acceptance of IT [11], and adoption of the Internet [14].

In our context, some researchers have reported that peers’ and superiors’ influence may be significant determinants of normative beliefs.

Limayem et al. [10] urged IS scholars to include actual behavior in their research. They further maintained that inclusion of the actual behavior prevented research from reaching wrong conclusions. Thus we hypothesized:

H5: Employees’ attitude toward complying with information security policies positively and significantly influences their intention to comply with these policies.

H7: Employees’ intention to comply with information security policies positively and significantly influences their actual compliance with these policies.

Rewards have been widely used to explain behavior in the context of an organization. For example, using an extensive meta analysis, Cameron et al. [2] found that rewards can be used to increase motivation and performance on tasks that are of low initial interest, but that on high-interest tasks, positive effects are obtained when participants are verbally praised and tangible rewards are offered (explicitly tied to performance standards and success). The only negative effect occurred when tangible rewards signify failure or are only loosely tied to behavior. Thus negative effects of reward can easily be prevented and managers can use rewards effectively to motivate performance and interest.Thus we hypothesized:

H8: Rewards significantly and positively affect employees’ intention to comply with information security policies.

## 4. Study design and research method

We used a sample of 669 employees. Our research model dealt with an employee’s threat appraisal of potential information security breaches and appraisal of policies and procedures that are in place to handle the breaches. The data was collected in 2006 and 2007 from four Finnish corporations that function in different business areas. The security policies in these organizations are communicated similarly through seminars, campaigns, and emails. All of these companies have provided their employers with information security policies and all companies had these policies available on their intranets. These companies have a set of common security rules in their IS security policies including, but not limited to, locking office doors, turning off PCs at the end of the workday, appropriate use of passwords, appropriate handling of confidential information, use of data encryption tools, restrictions on bringing outside materials into the company and taking material out of the company. Since information security compliance was a highly sensitive matter for these corporations, we were unable to provide additional information on the companies. This is a normal practice in the area of information security policies [9].

A total of 2892 respondents from the companies were asked to fill out (anonymously) a web-based instrument. This was physically located at the university who sponsored our research to assure respondents that their companies would not be able to trace any responses. The respondents were geographically spread all over Finland. Taking into consideration the missing data and invalid responses, we had a total of 669 usable responses with a resulting response rate of 23.1%, which is relatively high. Over forty six percent of the respondents (46.6%) have served in their company for more than 10 years (see Table 1).

## 4.1. Constructs and measures

Yang et al. [14], developed and validated an instrument to measure user perceived service quality of web portals using conceptual models from the areas of IS adoption. Siponen and Willison [13] authenticated a five-dimension service quality instrument that was based on the best ISM practices used by managers. We used items in our research that were used and tested in earlier studies (see Table 2). In addition, since the measures had not been tested in the context of IS security policy compliance and especially in the Finnish culture, our analysis first tested the reliability and validity of these measures in our context.

## 4.2. Content validity

The questions included in our instrument were first pre-tested (N = 15). Based on feedback, the readability factor of the questions was improved this helped to establish the content validity of the instrument. The resulting instrument was then pilot tested using 134 employees in a fire department. Reliability and validity tests on the sample provided support for all constructs in the instrument.

## 4.3. Common-method bias

The data was collected on a self-reported questionnaire; we were concerned with their being a measurement error and common-method variance, which could bias the estimates of the relationship among the theoretical constructs. Harman’s single-factor test [11] was used to assess common method variance. All of the items in the study were loaded into an exploratory factor analysis examining the unrotated factor solution using principal-component factor analysis with varimax rotation. The unrotated, principal-component factor analysis did not reveal a dominant factor and analysis revealed the presence of seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. The first factor accounted for 31% of the variance. In all, the seven factors accounted 73% of the variance. Thus, the results confirmed that there was only a small common method variance.

## 4.4. Convergent and discriminant validity

Our data analysis used SPSS 14.0 and AMOS 6.0 SEM software tools applying the maximum likelihood estimation method. A prerequisite for use of the AMOS 6 techniques is that the data must be normally distributed. IBM SPSS 20 was used to test for this normality. According to the test, the sample data was normally distributed.

Convergent validity was ascertained by assessing the factor loadings and by calculating the variance extracted. A single CFA for each of the constructs was performed, since our research is confirmatory in nature (all constructs were based on previously tested and validated items). A practical rule of thumb is used to determine if the factor loading is greater than 0.5 for a construct. In our study, all loadings were above 0.5 and most were above 0.7 (see Appendix A). All model items; with the exception of item #1 in the actual compliance construct, item #2 in the intention to comply construct, item #2 in the attitude construct, item #3 in the perceived severity construct, item #1 in the self-efficacy construct, and item #1 in the rewards construct exceeded the 0.50 threshold level. Items that did not exceed the threshold were dropped from the construct.

The discriminant validity of the items was assessed by computing the correlations between all validated constructs (see Table 3). Correlations between all pairs of constructs were below the recommended threshold value of 0.90. The variance extracted from all the constructs exceeded the threshold of 0.5. The square root of the variance extracted was greater than the correlations of the constructs. Hence the convergent and discriminant validity of the constructs in the model were established.

## 4.5. Reliability

Internal consistency among the items in the sample was assessed by calculating Cronbach alphas (see Appendix A). All of them exceeded the suggested value of 0.60. In addition, we calculated composite reliability [all constructs fulfilled the 0.7 threshold for composite reliability]. These results indicated high internal consistency for each construct in our model.

The fit indices chosen for our study represent two different fit characteristics: the global fit measures and comparative fit measures. The chi-square test $( \chi ^ { 2 } )$ with degrees of freedom is commonly used as the global model fit criteria. The chi-square statistic must, however, be interpreted with caution especially for a large sample size because the hypothesized model may be rejected if the discrepancy is not statistically equal to zero. We used incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), and normed fit index (NFI) to assess the congruence between the hypothesized model and the data. These are generally unaffected by the sample size.

CFI is a non-centrality-based index. Researchers recommend a threshold CFI have a value >0.9 to be most effective. NFI compares the improvement in the minimum discrepancy of the model to the discrepancy for the independence model and is recommended for comparison between the hypothesized and independence models. A well-fitting model is expected to yield an NFI of at least 0.90. IFI is an adjustment to NFI for adjustment due to sample size and degree of freedom; it is relatively stable for the same model in different samples and is therefore a useful complement to the existing fit measures. Some researchers recommend an IFI value of at least 0.9 for a well-fitting model. The final index reported in the analysis is the RMSEA, which considers the error approximation, and addresses the question ‘‘how well does the model fit the population covariance matrix?’’ RMSEA values below 0.08 are considered good and for the hypothesized model it was 0.071. We also included GFI and AGFI values to show how the model satisfied the normal threshold. The fit indices thus indicated that the hypothesized model provided a good fit with the data (see Table 5).

The path from self-efficacy to intention to comply with information security policies $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 7 )$ was significant at the 0.01 level. ## 6. Discussion

We were able to show that perceived severity, vulnerability, self-efficacy, an employee’s attitude toward intention to comply with information security policies, and normative belief of the employee significantly and positively impacted the employee’s intention to comply with information security policy.

Our effort showed that an employee’s self-efficacy to potential information security threats, attitude toward complying with information security policy, and normative beliefs had a highly significant and positive effect on the employee’s intention to comply with information security policies and procedures; it also showed that that the intention to comply with information security policies had a highly positive and significant impact on actual compliance with the policies.

The results of our research did not, however, support the hypothesized positive relationship between response efficacy and potential information security threats and employees’ intention to comply with information security policies and procedures. The situation can possibly be rectified by getting employees more involved in the formulation and implementation of information security policies. Some readers may have concerns about why nonrespondents did not respond. We do not know why these employees did not reply. Over 23 percent of the employees, however, responded to our request which is relatively high for an empirical research. Lastly, our results did not support the hypothesized positive relationship between rewards and an employee’s intention to comply with information security policies and procedures. It is possible that our respondents were not aware of the material rewards. It is also possible that their organization’s culture did not allow supervisors to reward their employees for complying with policies.

## 7. Limitations

Our study was subject to several limitations.

First, it was only conducted in Finnish organizations. However, we used professional employees as our subjects.

Second, our research used a web-based questionnaire, which may have introduced response bias because people outside the target population may fill out the questionnaire, or people in the target population could submit more than one response. Such concerns may be addressed by ensuring that one’s access to the instrument is controlled by one’s PC IP-address: unless a respondent uses a computer with a valid address, he or she will not have access to the survey instrument.

Third, the reliability of self-reporting surveys is sometimes questioned. While biases cannot be totally ruled out, self-reports are perceived as reliable and acceptable in empirical research.

Fourth, the reliability of perception-based data collected in 2007 may be questioned, but over 46% of the respondents had served in their company for more than 10 years and 23% more had a work experience of 5–10 years and this suggests that the data would be reliable.

Fifth, non-respondents bias is always a problem in voluntary surveys. We do not know why the non-respondents did not reply. However, over 23% of the employees responded.

## 8. Managerial implications

Given that perceived vulnerability and perceived severity of information security threats have a significant and positive impact on intention to comply with information security policies and procedures, managers must remind the employees that information security threats are plausible and these threats can cause serious damage to organizational information resources. High level managers should participate in delivering these messages. Departmental meetings and training sessions could also be used to remind employees of the importance of complying with security policies.

We recommend that managers and IT security staff write information security policies in a clear, concise, and easy-tounderstand language. Employees must also be provided with information security education and hands-on training to enhance their confidence in their abilities to comply with security policies. Managers must constantly remind employees that it is important to do so.

## 9. Conclusion and summary

We introduced a new theory-based comprehensive model to explain factors affecting employees’ security policies compliance. It was derived by combining elements from Protection Motivation Theory, the Theory of Reasoned Action, and the Cognitive Evaluation Theory. The model was then empirically validated using data collected from four corporations in Finland. The results showed that an employee’s perceived severity, vulnerability, selfefficacy, normative beliefs, and attitude had a positive and significant impact on the employee’s intention to comply with information security policies and procedures. The employee’s intention to comply with security policies also had a significant impact on his or her actual compliance with these policies. An employee’s response efficacy and rewards provided for complying with security policies, on the other hand, did not have a significant impact on his or her complying with these policies.
