# The Cross-Purposes of Cross-Posting: Boundary Reshaping Behavior in Online Discussion Communities

Brian S. Butler

Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania 15260, bbutler@katz.pitt.edu

Xiaoqing Wang

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, xiaoqingw@gmail.com

ncreasingly, online discussion communities are used to support activities ranging from software development Ito political campaigns. An important feature of an online discussion community is its content boundaries, which are individual perceptions of what materials and discussions are part of the community and what are not, and how that community is related to others within a larger system. Yet in spite of its importance, many community infrastructures allow individual participants to reshape content boundaries by simultaneously associating their contributions with multiple online discussion communities. This reshaping behavior is a controversial aspect of the creation and management of many types of online discussion communities. On one hand, many communities explicitly discourage boundary reshaping behaviors in their frequently asked questions or terms of-use document. On the other hand, community infrastructures continue to allow such reshaping behaviors. To explain this controversy, we theorize how the extent of boundary reshaping in an online discussion community has simultaneously positive and negative effects on its member dynamics and responsiveness. We test predictions about the conflicting effects of reshaping behaviors with 60 months of longitudinal data from 140 USENET newsgroups, focusing on cross-posting activities as a form of reshaping behavior. Empirical results are consistent with the proposed hypotheses that reshaping behaviors within a discussion community affect member dynamics and community responsiveness in both positive and negative ways. Taken together, the findings highlight the boundary-related design challenges faced by managers seeking to support ongoing activity within online discussion communities.

Key words: online communities; virtual communities; boundaries; social computing; design

## Introduction

Online discussion communities are increasingly used to support a wide range of activities. Open source projects rely on online discussion communities to coordinate the production of free open source software (Roberts et al. 2006). Firms sponsor online communities to engage consumers and market products and services to them (Lakhani and Kanji 2008). Political campaigns leverage online communities to engage potential voters and volunteers (Hill and Hughes 1998). Healthcare organizations create online communities to provide information and support for patients (Shaw et al. 2000). Among technology developers and users, talking about the problems and capabilities of information technologies is an important way questions are answered, problems solved, and expertise developed (Lakhani and von Hippel 2003, Moon and Sproull 2008). Across all these domains, online discussion communities have become increasingly important, with 84% of Internet users reporting being involved with at least one online discussion community by 2001 (Pew Internet Report 2001).

At their core, online discussion communities are discussion spaces that consist of arrangements that structure, constrain, and enable particular types of interaction among individuals (Dourish 2006). Underlying these online discussion spaces are technologies that allow messages to be broadcast through passive repositories or active content streams (McAfee 2006). In addition to message broadcasting, online discussion infrastructures may support the creation of bounded content spaces by providing security and access control, archiving, discussion threading, and content filtering. E-mail distribution lists, USENET newsgroups, listservs, Web forums, blogs, social networking sites, and microblogging systems are all technologies that enable bounded many-tomany communication and hence can serve as technical foundations for online discussion communities.

Supporting the distribution of content is a necessary condition for an online discussion community. Yet the significant number of failed or dormant communities suggests that this capability is itself not sufficient (Cummings et al. 2002). An online discussion community must provide both the technical and social infrastructures to support interaction over time (Butler 2001). For instance, prior research has demonstrated the importance of managing identity (Ma and Agarwal 2007), social presence (Dabbish and Kraut 2008), feedback (Moon and Sproull 2008), and reputation (Feller et al. 2008) in online communities. However, a defining aspect of online communities that has received less attention in the literature is content boundaries (Kim 2000). Content boundaries are individuals’ perceptions of what materials and discussions are considered part of a community and what are not. Absolute content boundaries are individuals perceptions about what distinct content is part of the community and what is not. Relative boundaries are individuals’ perceptions about how closely related the content associated with the focal community is to content in other communities. By shaping the identity of an online community, content boundaries influence its future activities and development.

Community content boundaries are significantly affected by the technical infrastructures that support online discussion. A community’s name and address serve as an absolute identifier, defining it as a distinguishable, bounded collection of materials. Within a larger system of communities, directories and hierarchies organize communities, building on absolute identifiers to indicate how communities relate to one another. Technical infrastructures that support the use of absolute identifiers and relative structures enable a community’s founders<sup>1</sup> to affect both its absolute and relative content boundaries.

However, technical infrastructures also allow individual members<sup>2</sup> to reshape these aspects of a community’s content boundaries. Members who associate their contribution solely with one community’s name, address, and location in the larger structure are accepting and reinforcing the absolute and relative content boundaries implied by the community infrastructure. Members who associate content with more than one community (i.e., cross-post) are, if only for the scope of their contribution, asserting different content boundaries by creating an overlap in the content of otherwise distinct communities. The potential impact of these content boundary-reshaping behaviors leads many to believe that they are detrimental to online community functioning and should not be allowed. Some discussion communities, for instance, explicitly include statements in their frequently asked questions or terms-of-use documents that discourage cross-posting. Yet, in spite of these concerns, other infrastructures ranging from old-style listservs and newsgroups to cutting edge social networking and microblogging sites continue to allow it. Examples can be found in social networking sites such as Facebook, LinkedIn, and Twitter, where users can use applications to simultaneously publish status updates and other content to multiple sites. Are these boundary reshaping behaviors really detrimental or are they beneficial? Why do they remain controversial among participants and managers of online communities?

To address these questions, we theorize how the extent of absolute and relative content boundary reshaping in online discussion communities affects two practically significant aspects of a community’s activity: member dynamics and community responsiveness. Building on the resource-based view of online communities (Butler 2001) and prior studies of organizational and group boundaries (Lamont and Molnar 2002), we argue that members’ content boundary-reshaping behaviors will have multiple, conflicting effects on activities within a community. We test the implied hypotheses using 60 months of longitudinal data from a random sample of 140 microsoft.public USENET newsgroups—online discussion groups devoted to questions and discussions about Microsoft and its products. We then discuss the results and their implications for researchers and practitioners interested in the challenges of creating and maintaining online discussion communities.

## Online Community Content Boundaries

Online discussion communities consist of infrastructure, members (who participates), and messages (what is said). A community’s infrastructure is a sociotechnical system that includes not only communication technology but also the norms, policies, and rules that enable it to function (Fulk et al. 1996). Technology platforms and community names, organizing structures, and moderation policies are all infrastructure elements. Although infrastructure provides a framework in which a community can emerge, it is the members and the messages that are the material of its formation. Whether they are community leaders, regular contributors, one-time question askers, or silent readers, members who choose to participate ultimately provide key resources that enable a community to survive (Butler et al. 2007, Preece 2001). Members possess the knowledge, time, and abilities that create the potential for an online discussion community to provide benefits; however, these benefits actually derive from the community’s communication activities. It is in threaded conversations—interrelated chains of messages—that requests are made, support is provided, knowledge is shared, and the work of an online discussion community is accomplished (Smith 2003). Together infrastructure, members, and messages comprise an online discussion community.

Integral to the development of a community, content boundaries are individual perceptions that emerge from the interaction of a community’s infrastructure, members, and messages (Lamont and Molnar 2002). Community owners and designers influence a community’s content boundaries through infrastructure choices, such as community names, discussion space addresses, and the placement of a community within larger organizing structures. Members’ decisions about what to write in a message and where to post it also influence the content boundaries of a community. Together, infrastructure choices and member posting behaviors in the community shape its content boundaries.

At the most basic level, content boundaries are influenced by infrastructure choices. Whether Web or text-based, synchronous or asynchronous, repository or distribution oriented, an online community’s infrastructure always provides a way for individuals to determine and indicate what messages are part of a community and what are not—and in the process affects its absolute content boundary. Infrastructure elements that directly influence absolute content boundaries include addresses and names. Addresses, such as e-mail addresses and URLs, provide technically enforced, unique identifiers for the content collection associated with an online discussion community. Material associated with such an address is linked to the community and is unambiguously distinguished from content that is not. Names also provide a basis for reinforcing absolute content boundaries in the sociotechnical infrastructure of an online discussion community. Typically chosen by the individuals who create the community, names are boundary objects that provide focal points around which an online discussion community can organize its communication activity (Bowker and Star 1999, Wenger 1998). Like computer-readable addresses, community names serve as an identifier for a community’s content collection. Community names influence absolute content boundaries by indicating what “should” be discussed in the community and what content an individual might expect to see in community discussions (Kim 2000). By labeling each community with a unique address and name, community founders influence the absolute content boundary around a community’s content collection, which demarcates it as a meaningful entity distinct from other online materials.

An infrastructure feature that affects the relative content boundary of an online discussion community is the placement of the community within an organizing structure. To help people select a community from among a set of communities, online discussion community infrastructures typically provide structures that organize communities into categories or hierarchies based on the similarity of their discussion content (Smith 2003). By placing a community within a category, or near others in a hierarchy, a community organizing structure indicates how the content collection of a community is related to other communities, affecting individuals’ perceptions of whether content in multiple communities is distinct or similar. Building on the absolute content boundary implied by the community’s names and addresses, a community’s placement in an organizing structure influences its relative content boundary.

A community’s name and placement in an organizing structure may signal the intent of the community creators, but content boundaries are subject to interpretive flexibility (Orlikowski 1992). The decentralized, voluntary nature of online communities means that a community’s content boundaries emerge from the recurring choices of individuals reinterpreting the focus of the community. An infrastructure that enables participants to associate free-form text, graphics, and other content with a community’s name or address gives any member the ability to, either intentionally or not, influence the content boundaries of the community. In mailing lists, USENET groups, discussion forums, blogs, and social networking systems, individuals often have the option to associate the same content with multiple discussion spaces (Kang and Choi 1999). Individuals may cross-post a request for information to multiple mailing lists, so that all subscribers to the mailing lists receive the request at the same time. Some may forward useful information from one discussion forum to another. Others may repost news from another source in their blogs, or cross-post blog entries to their social network feeds. These activities are all examples of content boundary-reshaping behaviors, behaviors that associate the same content with two or more communities and in the process reshape both the absolute and relative content boundaries of a community. When members associate the same content with more than one community name, such behavior blurs the absolute content boundaries implied by the unique names and addresses of those communities. Likewise, although the placement of a community in the organizing structure suggests greater or lesser distinctions between it and other communities, reshaping behaviors may serve to alter the associated relative content boundaries.

The emergent nature of online community content boundaries presents community organizers with a complex design decision regarding the extent of content boundary reshaping to allow. On one hand, community organizers may implement a variety of controlling structures (Thompson 2005), which seek to ensure that the content set of a community reflects the content boundaries they have in mind. These may be strong, technologically or procedurally enforced controls, or more diffuse social controls. Some communities increase the costs of boundaryreshaping behavior by not supporting forwarding, cross-posting, or even copying of content. Other communities use message moderation to explicitly constrain discussions and ensure that the materials associated with the community are clearly defined (Lampe and Resnick 2004, Powazek 2002). To avoid the relatively high cost of moderation, other communities rely on social norms and rules that are maintained through voluntary compliance (Kim 2000). Whatever the mechanism, controlling structures represent a significant investment by the community aimed at ensuring that particular content boundaries are reflected in community discussions.

On the other hand, the practitioner-oriented online community literature (Kim 2000, Powazek 2002) and academic studies of community structures (Thompson 2005) both suggest that the interpretive flexibility of community content boundaries is key to community sustainability. Online discussion communities are viable only when individual members voluntarily choose to participate in the community (Butler 2001). So although a top-down approach may be used to provide community infrastructure that implies a particular content boundary, online discussion communities are only functional when the members also engage in bottom-up activities that reshape and reinterpret the content boundary. As a result, community organizers are faced with the fundamental problem of determining not whether to allow content boundaries reshaping, but rather how much absolute and relative boundary reshaping to enable.

## Content Boundaries, Member Dynamics, and Community Responsiveness

Boundaries play a significant role in the functioning of online discussion communities similar to the one they play in organizations and groups (Lamont and Molnar 2002). Content boundaries affect both who participates and how interaction between individuals takes place—two features that are central to an online community (Kim 2000, Powazek 2002). Members possess the resources that are the foundation for a sustainable community (Butler 2001). Consequently, the ability of an online discussion community to attract and retain members (i.e., its member dynamics), and hence maintain a pool of resources, is critical to its continued survival (Butler 2001). Individuals perceptions regarding the present and future content of the community discussions are central to members’ decisions to participate. Therefore, content boundaries have important implications for the member dynamics of an online discussion community.

Maintaining a pool of members is important, but a community’s ability to generate content in response to members’ efforts to initiate conversation, or community responsiveness, is an indication of its ability to mobilize available resources to provide desired benefits. When posting a message seeking conversation, information, or support, members are using a community’s infrastructure to prompt messages that enable them to benefit from the available resources. Individuals’ willingness to respond to a message is affected by whether the message is perceived as being part of the community discussion: whether it is perceived as being within the community’s content boundary (Arguello et al. 2006). Thus, content boundaries are expected to impact the responsiveness of a discussion community.

Content boundaries are important to community activity; communities vary significantly in how they handle content boundary-reshaping behaviors. Some communities allow members complete freedom to reshape content boundaries by associating messages with other communities, whereas others limit or prohibit these activities. It is our contention that this diversity arises, at least in part, from the complex and somewhat contradictory relationships between the extent of content boundary reshaping and the functioning of online discussion communities. As we shall see below, these relationships create a tension between the support and hindrance of content boundary-reshaping behaviors. In the following section, we develop hypotheses consistent with the argument that the extent of absolute and relative content boundary reshaping has both positive and negative effects on the ability of a community to maintain its membership base and its responsiveness.

## Content Boundary-Reshaping and Member Dynamics

By associating the same content with multiple sources, sites, or communities, individuals can reshape an online discussion community’s absolute and relative content boundaries. First and foremost, reshaping behaviors alter the absolute content boundary. A discussion community’s name or address seeks to identify it as a distinct, bounded content collection. When content boundary-reshaping behaviors associate the same content with multiple communities, they create an overlap between their content collections. Such overlap blurs the absolute boundary implied by a community’s name and potentially reshapes perceptions of what content belongs in the community. When reshaping behaviors result in a large proportion of a community’s content being shared with other communities, the absolute content boundary of the community is altered to a greater extent. That is to say, these behaviors represent a high extent of absolute content boundary reshaping.

The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in a community affects its ability to attract and retain members in two ways. First, the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping enhances the degree to which content is visible to both individuals participating in the community and potential participants who are not regularly involved. Groups and organizations that are too closed often find that they have problems recruiting new members. To recruit new members, the content of a social group must be externally visible (Moreland and Levine 1982). In the extreme, a “secret society” can only survive as long as its longest lived member—for in the absence of at least a minimal flow of information about the group to the “outside” (i.e., individuals who aren’t currently participating), there is no basis for the inflow of new members. Therefore, a group must allow enough information about it and its activities to be visible to potential participants to enable them to be aware of the group and form a positive impression of future involvement.

As with other groups and organizations, online discussion communities need to constantly attract new members (Cummings et al. 2002). Reshaping behaviors are one way that community content can be made more externally visible. In online discussion communities, message content provides a basis for discussions, an indication of activity, and a reason for participation. Particularly in pull-media–based community infrastructures, where users actively visit a community to request messages, content drives future participation by making the resources and capabilities of the community visible. When there is a high extent of absolute content boundary reshaping, the large proportion of the content shared with other communities gives more individuals an opportunity to become aware of the discussions taking place and the benefits available in the community. The shared content provides a glimpse of the community for individuals not currently involved. For potential participants who have never posted in the community, such content creates awareness; for participants who have a history with the community but have been inactive for awhile, it serves as a reminder. An online discussion community that shares a large proportion of its content with other communities is more likely, through repetition and fortuitous timing, to be able to attract the attention of members who otherwise would not participate in the community at that time. Therefore, the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in a community is expected to have a positive effect on attracting new members in later time periods.

## <sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a positive effect on subsequent member attraction.

The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping also affects the creation of a distinct identity within the competitive space of online content. Online environments, and online discussion communities in particular, are subject to significant information overload problems (Jones et al. 2004, Shapiro and Varian 1999). The amount of information in online communities increases at an exponential rate, and individuals often reduce their level of participation or even stop participating to cope with the overload (Jones et al. 2004). Absolute content boundaries distinguish a community from the general information environment and help individuals manage information overload by allowing them to focus their attention and effort (Lee 2002). A distinct identity allows participants to share a common understanding of the community and better focus their discussions around topics consistent with that identity. In addition, a distinct identity increases the likelihood that messages within the community are of interest to participants and allows them to more easily assess whether the community is of interest to them.

Content boundary reshaping behaviors make it more difficult to distinctly identify a community. Content that appears in multiple communities blends the identities of the communities (Roberts 1998), weakening each community’s ability to retain members. An online discussion community with a high extent of absolute content boundary reshaping has a large proportion of its content shared with other communities and is less likely to be perceived as a unique entity. The resulting lack of a distinct identity can make it difficult for existing members to find content of interest to them, evaluate whether it’s appropriate to post a certain message, deal with a large range of topics, and generally justify their continued engagement with the community. Therefore, although H1 suggests that the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will increase the ability of the community to attract new members, it should also have a negative effect on retaining existing members.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a negative effect on subsequent member retention.

The impact of content boundary reshaping behaviors is not limited to its influence on the absolute content boundary of an online discussion community. Reshaping behaviors can also alter relative content boundaries. Just as information overload is an issue with individuals looking for information within an online community (Jones et al. 2004), it also is an issue faced by individuals seeking to find a community to interact with among a set of communities. To help manage this, an infrastructure often provides an organizing structure that places similar communities closer together, allowing individuals to more easily locate similar communities of interest. The placement of a community in the organizing structure, therefore, affects the extent to which its content is perceived as different from others’. When message contributors associate the same content with multiple communities, their behaviors imply that, at least in their view, the communities are similar. When content is shared among communities that are placed close together within the larger structure, it reinforces the perception that those communities are similar, thus reshaping the relative content boundaries to a small extent. When content is shared among communities that are widely separated within the larger organizing structure, the relative content boundaries of those communities are reshaped to a greater extent.

A high extent of relative content boundary reshaping is expected to affect an online discussion community’s member dynamics by making its content more externally visible and its identity less unique. Individuals may easily find similar communities by exploring the organizing structure, because similar communities are often placed near one another. As a result, members are more likely to already be aware of communities with similar content and have already explored or even participated in them. Reshaping behaviors that make only small changes to relative content boundaries are less likely to result in the attraction of many new members, because the adjacent placement of similar communities in the organizing structure already creates some visibility among them. In contrast, individuals will be less likely to find highly differentiated communities and would have little awareness of them. Content boundary-reshaping behaviors that associate communities that are widely separated in the larger organizing structure will significantly broaden the external visibility of the community and attract more new members. Therefore, the extent of relative content boundary reshaping is expected to have a positive effect the attraction of new members.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> The extent of relative content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a positive effect on subsequent member attraction.

The extent to which reshaping behaviors influence relative content boundaries also affects the distinct identity of a community and therefore its ability to retain existing members. The underlying principle of organizing structures is that communities clustered near one another are more alike than those placed in different parts of the structure. When reshaping behaviors associate content with multiple communities that are already closely associated within the organizing structure, they only slightly alter members’ perceptions of how different the content in one community is in relation to another and are unlikely to reduce the identity distinctiveness of the communities. As a result, when the extent of relative content boundary reshaping is low, the community is less likely to lose existing members. In contrast, when reshaping behaviors closely associate communities that are designated as highly dissimilar within the larger organizing structure, they imply that content in those communities is not as distinct as the organizing structure suggests. A greater extent of relative boundary reshaping reduces the perceived distinctiveness of the community, increasing the likelihood of members leaving. Hence it is expected that:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> The extent of relative content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a negative effect on subsequent member retention.

## Content Boundary Reshaping, Member Dynamics, and Community Responsiveness

The role of boundary reshaping behaviors in online discussion communities is further complicated by the importance of community responsiveness in the functioning of these social structures. In addition to needing to attract and retain members to maintain a base of resources, online discussion communities are viable only to the degree that they can convert those resources into benefits by supporting dialogues, i.e., by providing replies to dialog-initiating messages. For active participants, community responses provide benefit in the form of discussions that are tailored to their specific needs and interests. Peripheral participants (i.e., silent readers) also receive benefits from responsive communities through their access to the replies that are broadcast to the entire community (Brown and Duguid 1991, Lave and Wenger 1991). Therefore, online discussion communities are most functional when they are able to provide replies to dialog-initiating messages.

The extent of absolute and relative content boundary reshaping in online communities is expected to directly affect community responsiveness. Content boundaries that distinguish one community from another, even imperfectly, have been shown to reduce free-riding tendencies because they provide the basis for forming even minimalist collective identities (Dawes and Messick 2000). Even if these identities may not be particularly strong, the de-individuating nature of computer-mediated communication magnifies the significance of even minimalist collective identities and boundaries (Postmes et al. 1998). A high extent of either absolute or relative content boundary reshaping in a community results in a less distinct identity, increasing the likelihood that the community will suffer from more free-riding behaviors and become less responsive in the future. Therefore, we expect that:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a negative effect on community responsiveness.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> The extent of relative content boundary reshaping in an online discussion community will have a negative effect on community responsiveness.

The extent of absolute and relative content boundary reshaping can also indirectly influence community responsiveness through their effects on member attraction and retention. New members in an online discussion community often participate because they have a particular need. Whether it is a question about using a Microsoft software product, a desire to share news related to the topics in the community, or simply a need to join a special community, new members often attempt to meet their needs by initiating discussions and, implicitly or explicitly, solicit replies. Because new members are not yet familiar with a community and care more about their own needs than others’, they tend to ask more questions than provide replies to others (Kim 2000).

New members also may bring in different opinions and perspectives, causing conflicts with existing members (Santos and Eisenhardt 2005) and reducing the willingness of existing members to provide replies to them. An increasing number of new members, therefore, is expected to reduce a community’s responsiveness.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>7</sup> <sup>(H7).</sup> Member attraction in an online discussion community will have a negative effect on community responsiveness.

In contrast, returning members to online discussion communities have already learned about and benefited from the community. Their returning to the community indicates that the community has provided benefits valuable to them. The knowledge and experience of returning members allow them to better offer replies and help to others. In addition, ongoing participation is often associated with a sense of commitment with a community, which creates both the motivation and the obligation to contribute back to the community after getting help from others (Preece 2001). Prior research in organizational studies, for instance, found that employees who have been in an organization for a long time are more likely to engage in organizational citizenship behavior (LePine et al. 2002), behavior that is based on the organization’s interests rather than personal ones. Therefore:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>8</sup> <sup>(H8).</sup> Member retention in an online discussion community will have a positive effect on community responsiveness.

Taken together, the hypotheses indicate that the relationship between the extent of content boundary reshaping and subsequent community responsiveness will be partially mediated by member dynamics. ## Data, Methods, and Measures

To test the hypotheses regarding the impact of content boundary-reshaping behaviors on community outcomes, data were collected from a set of USENET newsgroups known as the microsoft.public hierarchy. USENET newsgroups are a form of online discussion community that use a distributed, archivebased infrastructure (Hauben and Hauben 1997). The USENET infrastructure supports tens of thousands of specific communities that vary with respect to topic, governance, discourse structure, language, and user population (Whittaker et al. 2003). Within USENET there is a subset of newsgroups, which are public technical discussion communities for Microsoft products, known as the microsoft.public hierarchy. Though not fully controlled by Microsoft, the approximately 3,889 newsgroups of the microsoft.public hierarchy are recognized and supported by Microsoft employees<sup>3</sup> (Fisher et al. 2006). Thus, these discussion communities provide an example of a population of online discussion communities much like the ones that companies might develop to support their customers or employees.

The Microsoft Netscan project created an archive of USENET activity in the Netscan database (Smith 2003). Through the Netscan project, we have access to the member activity data for all USENET newsgroups from January 2000 to December 2004 and the full message-level header data for 717 microsoft.public newsgroups, a random subset of the 3,889 in USENET. To focus our analysis on a single system of online discussion communities, the 717 newsgroups were carefully screened. Nontechnical, nondiscussion, and foreign language groups were removed. Because of the dynamic nature of the USENET structure, it was also necessary to account for communities at different points in their lifecycle. It may appear to be relatively stable, but the structure of USENET changes as communities are created and abandoned. To focus on established communities, as opposed to those in the very early stages of development or the final stages of decline, newsgroups that started after January 2000 and those that saw fewer than 500 messages during the studied time period were removed from the sample. This cutoff was selected based on examination of the distribution of communities and the nature of typical community activity levels. The final sample contained data from 140 newsgroups.

To examine the effects of content boundaryreshaping behaviors in the 140 newsgroups, we focused on cross-posting activities. Cross-posting allows individuals to designate the names of multiple newsgroups to which a message will be sent, much like when e-mail senders designate multiple recipients to a message. All the target newsgroups will then receive a copy of the message at the same time. For example, a software user in urgent need of an answer to her question or an individual wanting to share technology news with a large audience may cross-post a message to several newsgroups. A reader of the cross-posted message subsequently may either choose to respond to it only within his or her newsgroup or cross-post the response to other newsgroups as well. An individual also may choose to manually add a cross-posting group in a reply, so that the reply, along with the original message, could be broadcast to more newsgroups. Whether deliberate or not, by cross-posting an individual is able to create an overlap between the message sets of two or more newsgroups.

Measures for the extent of content boundary reshaping and community responsiveness were constructed based on the message header data for all messages that appeared in the 140 sampled newsgroups between January 2000 and December 2004. Although the full text of the messages was not available, from message headers it was possible to determine when a message appeared, which newsgroup(s) it was posted in, whether the message was cross-posted and, if so, where it was cross-posted, and whether the message was a reply to a previous message. The member dynamics measures were constructed based on the member activity data for all members that participated in the sampled newsgroups during the study period. These data included members’ participation in all USENET newsgroups, including those outside the sample, allowing us to determine when a member in a sampled newsgroup was actively posting in a cross-posting target and when the member moved from one newsgroup to another.

One concern with USENET data is the potential impact of junk messages (i.e., spam) on the analysis and results. In particular, if spam messages (Templeton 2007) were often cross-posted, it could be hard to tease out the effects of cross-posting activities from the effects of junk messages. To reduce the impact of junk messages on the analysis, all cancelled messages and administrative command messages were removed prior to measure construction, eliminating messages that USENET administrators, protocols, and server software deemed to be spam (FAQ.org 2007). To further assess the impact of spam, an unrelated archive, which included cancelled messages, was used to create a sample of 600 messages (200 from each of three newsgroups randomly selected from the original sample). Coding of these messages indicated that even in the absence of the basic filtering, fewer than 10% of the cross-posted messages were unsolicited advertising and that this proportion was not substantially different than the rate of junk content among non-crossposted messages.

## Measure Construction

The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping was measured by the percentage of cross-posted messages in a newsgroup in a given month, calculated as the number of cross-posted messages divided by the total number of messages. The higher the percentage of cross-posted messages in a newsgroup in a month, the higher the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping in the community. A message was counted as cross-posted if and only if its header referenced more than one newsgroup. If the same text was sent to multiple newsgroups, but in separate messages, it was not counted as cross-posted.

Analysis of the header data of a cross-posted message provides a basis for determining the extent of relative content boundary reshaping, measured by the distance between a focal newsgroup and its crossposting targets in the USENET naming hierarchy. Messages can be cross-posted to newsgroups both inside and outside the Microsoft.public domain. For instance, the Microsoft.public.speech\_tech newsgroup cross-posted with the comp.speech newsgroup in January 2000. The message header shows the names of all the newsgroups that a message was crossposted to, including those that were not in the sample. Aggregating the header information across all messages in a newsgroup in a month allowed us to construct a list of newsgroups that a focal group cross-posted with, as well as the number of messages that the focal group cross-posted with each crossposting target. Because the USENET newsgroups are hierarchically organized, a focal newsgroup’s name and the name of a cross-posting target can be used to calculate their hierarchical distance, i.e., the number of steps that it takes to travel from one group to the other within the USENET organizing hierarchy. Figure 2 illustrates two examples of how this distance is determined. The larger the hierarchical distance between two newsgroups, the more dissimilar the structure implies they are. For instance, a newsgroup named microsoft.access.queries is signaled to be more similar to the microsoft.access.forms newsgroup than the comp.java.lang group. The crossposting distance was then calculated as the weighted average of the distances between the focal newsgroup and all its cross-posting targets in a given month, weighted by the number of messages cross-posted with each target. For example, suppose newsgroup A cross-posted eight messages with newsgroup B and two messages with newsgroup C in a month. If newsgroup A has a hierarchical distance of 1 from B, and a distance of 5 from C, then the weighted average cross-posting distance of newsgroup A would be $( \bar { 1 } * 8 + 5 * 2 ) / ( 8 + 2 ) = 1 . 8 .$ A newsgroup with a large cross-posting distance tends to cross-post with other groups that are far away from it in the organizing hierarchy, and therefore its relative content boundary is reshaped to a great extent. The cross-posting distance measure was only calculated for newsgroups that cross-posted at least one message in a given month; it was missing for the 246 newsgroup-months that did not see a cross-posted message. Therefore, models including this measure had a smaller sample size.

Member attraction was measured as the number of newcomers attracted from cross-posting targets. A newcomer is defined as someone who did not post a message to a newsgroup in the previous month (t − 1) and started to post in the current month (t). Among the newcomers in a month, some were participating in a cross-posting target in the previous month and others were not. If cross-posting attracts members through increasing the external visibility of a community, its impact on member attraction should be mostly on people who were participating in the crossposting targets and were exposed to the cross-posted messages. Therefore, the number of attracted newcomers from a cross-posting target was used as the measure of member attraction. Member retention was measured by the number of returnees who posted in the previous month (t − 1) and continued to post in the current month (t). The number of attracted newcomers and returnees in a month represents the ability of a newsgroup to attract new members and retain existing members in a given time period.

Community responsiveness was assessed by examining the length of the discussion threads in a newsgroup, calculated as the average number of messages in a thread in the newsgroup during a month. The longer the thread length, the more replies each thread-initiating message received. By replying to a thread-initiating seed, a community provides answers to a question or solution to a problem and provides benefits to its members through supporting ongoing dialog in the community. Therefore, the more replies a seed receives in a community, the more responsive the community is and the more capable the community is in providing value-added dialog to its members. Because the thread length measure included the thread-initiating seed, a thread length of 1 indicates that the seed did not receive a response.

Prior research has shown that the overall membership size and message volume in a community could affect the member attraction and retention ability of the community (Butler 2001). To control for the effect of message volume in a newsgroup, we included the total number of messages in a newsgroup in a month as a control. In this sample, the total number of members in a newsgroup was highly correlated with the number of messages at over 0.9, and thus we did not include a control for membership size. We also controlled for the overall USENET size, measured as the total number of members in USENET in a month. To simplify interpretation of the empirical results, the measures of message volume and USENET size were divided by 100. A numerical measure for time was created as a control , with 1 representing January 2000 and 60 representing December 2004. Together, these measures, summarized in Table 1, provide the basis for the empirical analysis.

Measures were constructed for each newsgroup in the sample, resulting in a panel data set for 140 newsgroups over 60 months (N = 81400 group/months). This sample included newsgroups that varied in terms of levels of cross-posting activity and member dynamics (Table 2). In the extreme there were newsgroups such as microsoft.public.word.general that crossposted all of its messages in a month, but on average 27% of the messages posted to a newsgroup were cross-posted to other newsgroups. This reasonably high level of cross-posting activity is consistent with the pattern found in previous research (Jones et al. 2004). On average, a newsgroup attracted 19 newcomers and retained 20 existing members in a month. However, in the extreme, the Microsoft.public.outlook newsgroup attracted 631 newcomers in March 2004 and retained 476 returnees in December 2003, suggesting significant variations in the member dynamics in different newsgroups. The average thread length in the sample was 1.8, suggesting that a seed typically received zero to one reply. But in the extreme, the microsoft.public.greetings.workshop newsgroup provided an average of 13 replies for each seed in April 2000.

## Analysis

The member dynamics and thread length measures were subject to significant nonnormality. Natural log transformation was then applied to them<sup>4</sup> and to the message volume and USENET size controls. Because of the presence of zero values in the data set, a small positive number (0.1) was added to the measures before the log transformations.

Fixed-effect method with robust standard errors was used to estimate the effects of the extent of content boundary reshaping on member dynamics and community responsiveness. Fixed-effect method was chosen to control for unobserved heterogeneity at the newsgroup level that may correlate with the independent variables. The robust standard errors are robust to heteroskedasticity across newsgroups and serial correlation within newsgroups. We performed two additional analyses, one using fixed-effect regressions with AR(1) structure and one using first differencing method, to examine the effect of serial correlation. In both cases, the results remained the same, and thus we only reported the fixed-effect estimates in the paper. To reduce the possibility of reversed causality, we lagged the independent variables by one month. Because of the lagged nature of the data, the first month of data were lost, resulting in a final analysis sample of 8,260 (140 newsgroups ∗ 59 months).

Examination of variable correlations (Table 3) suggested that multicollinearity was not a concern in models predicting member dynamics. Variance inflation factors for all the independent variables in models predicting member dynamics were smaller than 2, well below the generally acceptable threshold (Hair et al. 1998). However, the high correlations between the member dynamics variables and the control variable for message volume suggested that multicollinearity may be a concern in the model predicting community responsiveness. Multicollinearity often inflates the estimated standard errors and causes a statistically significant relationship to be nonsignificant. Therefore, several approaches were taken to examine the robustness of the results. First, message volume was dropped in the community responsiveness analysis to mitigate its high correlation with the member dynamics measures. Second, with message volume dropped, the member attraction and member retention measures were included separately in the model to estimate their individual effects on community responsiveness. Third, a first differencing method was used to test the same models. Because the correlations among the difference scores were lower than 0.2, multicollinearity was not likely a concern in the first differencing models. Across all three approaches, the sign and significance of the coefficients of interest did not change. Given that the largest various inflation factor in the fixed effect models was still smaller than 7 and that multicollinearity did not change the general interpretation of the results, we reported the results of the fixed-effect models including the message volume measure.

Hypothesis 1, which suggests that the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping has a positive impact on attracting new members, was supported. A 10% increase of cross-posted messages in a newsgroup was associated with a 7% increase of the number of newcomers attracted from cross-posting targets in the subsequent month (Model 1), supporting our argument that content boundary-reshaping behaviors attract new members to a community. Hypothesis 2 suggests that the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping has a negative impact on retaining members, which also was supported. A 10% increase of cross-posted messages was associated with a 3.7% decrease of the number of returnees in a newsgroup in the subsequent month (Model 3). Together, these results suggested that the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping was associated with both an increased ability to attract new members and a weaker ability to retain existing members.

However, cross-posting distance was not significant in predicting the number of returnees, providing no support for H4.

## Discussion

The empirical results (Tables 4 and 5) provide support for our theoretical claims that there is an inherent tension in content boundary management and that the relationship between content boundary reshaping behaviors and community activity is complex and subject to significant tradeoffs. First, the empirical findings indicate that the direct effects of content boundary reshaping behaviors in online discussion communities are mixed. Although reshaping behaviors are often thought of as detrimental, introducing spam and reducing the stability of a community, the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping has simultaneously positive and negative effects on member dynamics. On one hand, the extent of absolute boundary reshaping, as reflected in the percentage of cross-posted messages in a month, was associated with a subsequent increase of attracted newcomers in a newsgroup (H1). This is consistent with the argument that absolute content boundary reshaping can be useful in attracting new members because of its effects on the visibility of the community. To the degree that community leaders and managers wish to increase the exposure of an online discussion community and attract new members, promoting, or at least allowing, content boundary-reshaping behaviors appears to be desirable.

On the other hand, enabling reshaping behaviors also has disadvantages. The same messages that attract members can lower a community’s ability to retain members. Having a higher percentage of crossposted messages also was associated with fewer current members staying in the subsequent month (H2), as would be expected if sharing content with other groups reduced the distinctiveness of a community. In USENET and other Web-based forums, the technical infrastructure that allows easy cross-posting and reposting of content may have contributed to the high turnover rate in those communities. This suggests that when retaining existing members is higher priority than attracting new ones, keeping the content of a community unique and discouraging reshaping behaviors will be more desirable. The inherent tension in content boundaries suggests that community leaders’ efforts to manage content boundaries and reshaping behaviors must balance these positive and negative effects.

Our findings also provide some indication that the extent of relative content boundary reshaping occurring within a community matters, at least to potential new members. The cross-posting distance between a newsgroup and its cross-posting targets was positively associated with the number of attracted newcomers (H3), as would be expected if the extent of relative content boundary reshaping has a positive effect on a community’s visibility. However, the results did not show a significant relationship between cross-posting distance and the number of returnees, suggesting that the extent of relative content boundary reshaping does not affect a community’s member retention ability. A possible explanation for the insignificant relationship is that existing members are less sensitive to the relative content boundaries than potential new members. Reshaping behaviors attract individuals who are interested in finding new information, experiences, or people. These individuals are more likely to find new content from less visible, more dissimilar communities than from visible, similar ones. On the other hand, existing members see any content boundary reshaping message as diluting the identity of a community, no matter what other community it is associated with. It also is possible that the extent of relative content boundary reshaping has a more complex effect on a community’s identity than we hypothesized. On one hand, sharing content with distinct communities may diffuse a focal community’s identity to a great extent.

On the other, content from distinct communities may differ enough from that in the focal community that it may not confuse existing members about the purpose, value, and identity of the communities. Future research that examines these more nuanced effects of content boundary-reshaping behaviors is needed to refine the model of how reshaping behaviors interact with formal infrastructure choices in affecting community activity.

Community development issues associated with content boundaries and boundary-reshaping behaviors are further complicated by the fact that online discussion communities seek to both build a base of resources and maintain community responsiveness. The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping was negatively associated with the responsiveness of a community (H5). However, we did not find a significant relationship between the extent of relative content boundary reshaping and community responsiveness (H6). Although unexpected, this finding is consistent with the lack of a relationship between the extent of relative content boundary reshaping and member retention: because the extent of relative content boundary reshaping did not affect community distinctiveness in the eyes of existing members, it did not have an effect on community responsiveness. The extent of absolute content boundary reshaping also indirectly affected community responsiveness through its effects on member dynamics. Returning members who stayed from one month to the next contributed to more responses in a community (H8). This suggests that retaining existing members is important to maintain a community’s ability to provide benefits. Interestingly, our results suggested that the number of newcomers attracted from cross-posting groups also increased the community’s responsiveness (H7). A possible explanation is that the attracted newcomers have already participated in a cross-posting com munity and have been exposed to relevant content, and thus they are willing and able to provide benefits to other members in the community. Together, the results suggested that the extent of absolute content boundary reshaping can both increase and suppress a community’s responsiveness through its positive effect on member attraction and negative effect on member retention, creating an additional layer of tension between content boundary management and community outcomes.

This study suggests particular aggregate results for a population of online discussion communities; it seems likely that community leaders and developers will need to re-examine these tensions and the balance between them continually as they work with their communities in a given context. In some online services, such as Digg or Delicious, the value of the communities comes from the collective filtering of Web content. In these communities, participants draw all content from other sites and sources. In these communities, content boundary-reshaping behaviors create a point of reference that helps participants find other content of interest, and thus it does not make sense to limit reshaping behaviors. In other contexts, communities may be able to adopt alternative strategies for handling content boundary-reshaping behaviors. For example, a community that has the resources to engage in formal advertising or branding activities may be able to attract new members without allowing reshaping behaviors and in the process avoid the negative consequences of these behaviors. Conversely, a community that is able to maintain comprehensive moderation may be able to manage the negative impact of reshaping behaviors. Alternatively, a community may initiate intragroup comparison with the communities that it shares content with to help strengthen existing members’ identification with the community (Bartel 2001), reducing the negative effects of reshaping behaviors.

No matter what strategy is chosen, communities must recognize the true complexity of their design space if they are to avoid unintended consequences. By identifying there relationships and the tensions that exist between them, this work provides a foundation for evaluation and comprehension of the design options that exist regarding content boundary management and reshaping behaviors in online community infrastructures.

## Limitations and Future Directions

As with any study, this work is subject to limitations, each of which suggests directions for future research. Theoretically, this study is constrained because it focuses on one type of boundary (content boundary). In addition to content boundaries, communities also may create and maintain membership boundaries by restricting, either explicitly or implicitly, who is allowed to access or add to the community’s message set. Future studies can expand on this work by considering other types of boundaries (Santos and Eisenhardt 2005) and how these boundaries interact with each other to affect community development. Methodologically, even though we have a longitudinal study and use lagged independent variables, the possibility of common covariates remains, and we cannot claim that the effects are causal. Future work may rule out alternative explanations by controlling for potential exogenous variables that affect both the extent of content boundary reshaping and community outcomes.

This work was conducted at a particular level of analysis (the community) with data from a particular set of online discussion communities (microsoft.public.\*), which are based on a particular infrastructure technology (USENET). As a result, this study only examines one common form of reshaping behaviors in a community: message cross-posting. Other community infrastructures, such as discussion lists, Web-based forums, blogs, listservs, and social network-based community systems, have different mechanisms to handle cross-community activity (Mehler 2007). Future research could consider forms of reshaping behaviors such as hyperlinked content and message forwarding. Each of these forms of reshaping behaviors is likely to affect community member dynamics and responsiveness, but they may do so to greater or lesser degrees. Future empirical and theoretical work examining the impact of different boundary-management mechanisms also would be valuable for both community developers and scholars seeking to explain the role that infrastructure design decisions play in the shaping of online social structures. Message moderation, collection, tagging, and personal filters, for instance, all warrant future examination. Understanding each of these boundarymanagement mechanisms in a variety of contexts would provide a clearer picture of the role of boundary management in online communities and their full implications for community infrastructure design decisions.

These empirical results are based on analysis of a sample of online technical discussion communities, the microsoft.public USENET newsgroups. The arguments regarding the nature of content boundaries and reshaping behaviors should apply to a broad range of online discussion communities and discussion infrastructures, but the specific findings are limited to the context of technical discussion communities. A growing body of work has suggested that different types of communities may behave in different ways (Ridings and Gefen 2004). Future work might consider the degree to which our theory accurately explains the role of content boundary-reshaping behaviors in other types of online communities, such as health support, hobby, and political discussion groups, which are less likely to focus on exchanging questions and answers than technical support groups. Furthermore, analysis of other collections of online discussion communities, including both corporate-sponsored communities (such as those provided by Dell, Hewlett.Packard, or IBM) and noncorporate communities (such as those associated with open-source systems) would further strengthen the generalizability of the empirical results.

Each of the theoretical premises about the nature of content boundaries and the effects of reshaping behaviors also could be examined at the level of specific messages, individual participants, or dyads, providing alternative tests of the general theoretical model. The analysis in this paper was based on group-level measures of messages and members. We could not take into account characteristics of particular messages by doing detailed content analysis and therefore may have overestimated community responsiveness. Some messages that appear to be replies to prior threads, for instance, might have been repeating a previous question or introducing completely new subjects and thus have been mis-measured. Future work might theorize and empirically examine how content boundary-reshaping behaviors affect the helpfulness and meaningfulness of replies by using machine learning techniques.

Last, the nature of the data and analysis may raise questions about the meaning and practical significance of the results. The size of the data set (N = 140 groups × 60 months = 81400) raises questions about the meaning of statistical significance standards as they have been traditionally applied in information systems research. Although fundamental questions about the interpretation of statistical results when working with larger samples are beyond the scope of this work, the lack of a significant correlation between the measures of absolute and relative boundary reshaping and the failure to find a significant relationship between cross-posting distance and member retention suggest that the significant results were not simply a result of excessive statistical power. Similarly, the apparently small size of the coefficients may lead to questions of whether the empirical results are of practical significance. Again, even if the basic question of what constitutes a practically significant result is beyond the scope of this paper, it is important to note that the analysis presented here examines the month-to-month impact of infrastructure design choices in online discussion groups that have life spans of 5, 10, and even 20 years. Thus, not only is there ample opportunity for the monthly effects to compound, but the presence of feedback loops related to community member and message activity (Butler 2001) raises the distinct possibility of significant second-order effects arising from these direct effects. Thus although it is important for future research to consider the full implications of content boundary-reshaping behaviors, the arguments and empirical results presented here can, at a minimum, be seen as providing a basis for efforts to design, manage, and study content boundaries and boundaryreshaping behavior in online discussion communities.

## Conclusion

Taken together, the results in this study begin to reveal the complexity of the design and management problem faced by practitioners interested in developing online discussion communities. Rather than simplifying the problem by suggesting that certain effects and factors can be ignored, the proposed model outlines how the extent of content boundary reshaping, member dynamics, and community responsiveness are interrelated and highlights the tensions inherent in that system of relationships. From one perspective this can be disappointing. For the manager asking whether content boundaryreshaping behaviors are positive or negative, these results suggest that the answer is a paradoxical “yes.” At the same time, the model and empirical results help explain how a seemingly simple design decision about the technical capabilities of a community infrastructure can have such complex and often unexpected implications for the sustainability of the resulting communities. This model also suggests that at least in the arena of content boundary management, communities and community developers are most likely to be successful in making these decisions if they focus on the relative importance of and balance between the positive and negative effects of content boundary-reshaping behaviors.

More fundamentally, these results highlight the principle of online community design: that the ability to recognize and understand root causes and higherorder consequences (Sproull and Kiesler 1991) differentiates expert community developers from novices and ultimately determines the sustainability of a community as an ongoing site for social activity.
