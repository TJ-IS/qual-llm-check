# The Use of Impression Management Strategies to Manage Stock Market Reactions to IT Failures

Jason Triche<sup>1</sup>, Eric Walden<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Montana, jason.triche@umontana.edu <sup>2</sup>Texas Tech University, eric@ericwalden.net

Abstract

In this work we show how organizational impression management strategies can influence stock market reactions to information technology (IT) failures. We combine the resource-based view of the firm with organizational impression management strategies to analyze what strategies work with different types and causes of IT failures. We perform an event study on a sample of 214 IT failures over eight years and find that a firm’s choice of organizational impression management strategy has a significant effect on a firm’s market value. On average, over \$212 million in market value can be saved with the correct impression management strategy. For implementation failures, we find that assertive strategies are better than defensive strategies. Conversely, we find that for operational failures, defensive strategies are superior. Furthermore, we examine failures caused by human error and discuss the impacts. This research provides new theoretical insights to the resource-based view of the firm.

Keywords: Resource-Based View of the Firm, Organizational Impression Management, Event Study, Information Technology Failure

## 1 Introduction

Failure is a ubiquitous feature of information technology (IT), and leads to tremendous costs for firms (Beynon-Davies, 1995; Bharadwaj, Keil, & Mähring, 2009; Goldfinch, 2007; Liebowitz, 1999). The worldwide cost of IT failures has been estimated at over six trillion dollars (Sessions, 2009) and more than 127 million person-hours per year (Eddy, 2011). In addition to the financial costs, companies that experience these IT failures often encounter public image problems, which can shatter investor confidence in the capacity of companies to deliver IT services to customers and partners. Since IT capabilities are a significant competency of modern business, calling these capabilities into question can be particularly damaging (Mata, Fuerst, & Barney, 1995; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997).

While there may not be many shortcuts to repair the damage inflicted on customers, suppliers, partners, and investors, it may be possible to ameliorate the damage done to a company’s reputation by sending the right signals to financial markets. The nature of the failure seems to be an important factor in how the financial markets react to the failure (Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Naquin & Kurtzberg, 2004). If companies can use organizational impression management strategies (Bolino, Kacmar, Turnley, & Gilstrap, 2008; Elsbach, Sutton, & Principe, 1998) to portray specific impressions about the nature of the failure, they may be able to maintain more market value in the face of IT failure.

The aim of this work is to explore how organizational impression management strategies (Bolino et al., 2008; Elsbach et al., 1998) impact financial market reactions to IT failures. We follow prior literature on IT failures (Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Davis et al., 1992; Keil, Mann, & Rai, 2000; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; Lyytinen & Robey, 1999; Poulymenakou & Serafeimidis, 1997) to explore market perceptions, based on the resource-based view of the firm (Barney, 1991; Penrose, 1959; Wernerfelt, 1984) and impression management theory. We then test these theoretical predictions using event study methodology (Fama, Fisher, Jensen, & Roll, 1969).

Bharadwaj et al. (2009) used a resource-based view of the firm to show that different types of IT failures signal different competitive deficiencies to financial markets. This important work demonstrates that failure types affect markets. While IT failures will likely always be a problem for companies, how managers react to the IT failure is important. We extend Bharadwaj et al.’s work by identifying what managers can do to save companies from large financial losses. We accomplish this by synthesizing the resource-based view of the firm and impression management to identify actions managers can take to modify the signals IT failures send to financial markets. Thus, this work presents a solution to the problem identified in the research of Bharadwaj et al. 2009. Our research can provide guidance to IT managers and corporate executives so they can implement a plan to lessen the impact of losses to a company’s market value incurred by IT failures.

## 2 Theory Development

Before discussing failures, it will be useful to sketch out the underlying theory of what we measure. First, we conceive of firms as having resources. A resource is “anything which could be thought of as a strength or weakness of a given firm (Wernerfelt, 1984, p. 172).” The resource-based view of the firm (Barney, 1991; Penrose, 1959; Wernerfelt, 1984) examines how resources can be used to create superior longterm performance and long-term competitive advantages. The ability to deliver quality IT services to customers, partners, and employees can constitute an important firm resource (Bharadwaj, 2000; Mata et al., 1995; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997; Ray, Muhanna, & Barney, 2005; Tarafdar & Gordon, 2007; Wade & Hulland, 2004; Zhu, 2004).

Investors in stock markets evaluate the potential of these resources to arrive at a valuation of the company. As Warren Buffet notes, the key to investing is “determining the competitive advantage of any given company and, above all, the durability of that advantage” (Buffet, 1999, p. 220). Resources confer competitive advantage, and competitive advantage determines potential and, thus, the stock price of a firm. Events can occur that signal changes in the resources and, hence, the value of a firm’s stock. CEOs can be fired or hired, patents can be filed or expired, and stocks can be issued or repurchased.

For this research, the event we are concerned with is the failure of an IT system. IT failure is a breakdown that occurs anywhere in the information system’s infrastructure, which renders the IT system incapable of performing its intended tasks (Beynon-Davies, 1995; Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Goldfinch, 2007; Liebowitz, 1999). This infrastructure is made up of resources such as managerial competence, people, data, hardware, software, and the telecommunications network. Some of these resources, such as managerial competence, may be competitive resources (Mata et al., 1995), while others, such as hardware, may be noncompetitive resources (Mata et al., 1995). When one or more resources break down in a sufficiently capacious manner, the IT system cannot fulfill its designated functions, and this creates an IT failure. An IT failure can signal to the market that important resources are not as they were thought to be. IT failures can signal weak or improper use of IT resources that financial markets use to value a firm (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). The failure refers to the inability of the system to deliver the basic tenets of IT that support operations, support decision-making, and support and lead strategic initiatives for a company. They are multidimensional and have several subcomponents, including technical, behavioral, political, organizational, and cultural aspects. (Lyytinen, 1987). Examples of IT failures can be software failures, software errors/bugs, hardware failures, hardware errors/bugs, network outages, network errors/bugs, human error while interacting with an IT system, data representation errors, or data conversion errors. We contribute to the literature on IT failures by proposing ways that managerial communication with shareholders can mitigate the broader market effects of IT failures.

## 2.1 Failure Type

IT failures can be grouped into three major types: developmental, implementational, and operational. Developmental failures include issues with systems that do not meet specifications, are not developed within time or budget, are delivered but are not used, or fail to meet stakeholders’ expectations (Lyytinen, 1987). A well-publicized example of a developmental failure is Boeing’s 787 Dreamliner delays. In 2004, Boeing first announced that they expected the new, lightweight, state-of-the-art airplane to start commercial flights in 2008. By 2007, Boeing had received up to 677 orders for the new airplane; they initially announced a three-month delay due to a shortage of fasteners and incomplete software (Gates, 2013). After several years of delays, including those caused by software failures, the first 787 was delivered in 2011. This delay caused Boeing to lose orders, impacted airlines, and caused financial impacts to cities that scheduled 787 service (LeBeau, 2013). Note that we do not explore developmental failures here because they are rarely made public, and hence we cannot include these types of failures in our research framework.

Implementation failures occur when a new system is installed or accessed for the first time and encounters problems (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). One example of an implementation failure presented itself in March

2012, when United and Continental Airlines merged their reservation systems. United Airlines encountered several problems including flight delays, error-prone kiosks, and nonresponsive phone lines during the implementation weekend (Reuters, 2012). Although United and Continental spent months preparing for the new system, technical issues arose during implementation, which caused delays at airports across the world (Reuters, 2012).

Operational failures occur when an existing system fails and leads to business disruptions. The difference between operational and implementation failures is that operational failures occur on business systems that are working in production over a period of time. Operational failures can occur because of failures in equipment, hardware, software, and/or network issues. In another example from 2011, Amazon’s cloud hosting service went down, crashing sites like Reddit, Quora, FourSquare, parts of The New York Times, and approximately 70 other sites for days (Goldman, 2011). Unlike development failures, implementation and operational failures occur in realtime. Hence, there is no leakage of information to the market prior to the failure that can be addressed.

When a firm experiences an IT failure, it can become public knowledge before any negative effects are apparent. When publicized, it conveys information to financial markets, which update their collective evaluation of the value of the firm. There are many details included in such firm valuations, such as how much revenue was lost due to the failure and how much it will cost to fix the failure. However, one important component of the financial evaluation is what a failure says about firm resources and, hence, about its long-term performance potential (Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Charette, 2005; Li, Shang, & Slaughter, 2010).

Operational failures represent temporary glitches of existing information systems (Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Wade & Hulland, 2004). In cases of operational failure, since the firm delivered a service before the failure occurred, it is usually presumed that it will also do so after the failure. Thus, the failure does not cast doubt on the ability of the firm to deliver IT services over time, rather just represents a temporary problem (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). In contrast, implementation failures signal issues with a firm’s dynamic capabilities (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). Implementation is about delivering something new and future-facing. The ability to integrate IT into new systems to develop new business processes is paramount for the future prospects of a firm. Thus, implementation failures typically signal a much greater firm resource problem than operational failures. Therefore, we propose:

H1: Failure-type hypothesis: The magnitude of negative abnormal returns will be larger for implementation failures than for operational failures.

Note that our objective in this research is to replicate the 2009 work of Bharadwaj et al. and to extend it by examining impressions that companies send to their investors. Therefore, this hypothesis is a replication of Bharadwaj et al.’s second hypothesis. By replicating their hypothesis, we can test the robustness of their work and also calibrate our own model. Confirming this hypothesis would strengthen both the 2009 claims of Bharadwaj et al. and our own, while a rejection of this hypothesis would suggest that additional research is needed. Our contribution follows in discussing how to use managerial communication to mitigate the signaling effects of IT failure.

## 2.2 Responsibility

It has long been held that information technology, in and of itself, is not a competitive differentiator, but rather that IT management skills are a source of competitive advantage (Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Mata et al., 1995; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). Mata et al. (1995) found that of the four defined attributes— capital requirements, proprietary technology, technical IT skills, and managerial IT skills—only managerial IT skills contributed to sustained competitive advantage for a company. Bhatt and Grover (2005) found that IT infrastructure did not have an effect on a company’s competitive advantage, but IT business expertise did. Powell & Dent-Micallef (1997) also found that IT alone did not produce sustainable competitive advantages, but some firms in the retail industry gained advantages by leveraging intangible, complementary human and business resources. In other words, it is not IT itself, so much as what a firm does with IT, that creates competitive advantage. Deciding what to do with IT and actually being able to do it requires human skills. The proprietary technology systems—the hardware, software, and network of a company—can be imitated across companies in the same industry. Therefore, no one company can stand out above the others over a sustained period. However, how human resources use IT capabilities is what leads to a competitive advantage for a company.

When an IT failure occurs because of human error, it sends a much stronger signal to the financial market than when the failure is due to a technology problem. Human failure directly calls into question the source of the IT-related competitive advantage. Alternatively, a technology failure sends a signal about the quality of a firm’s commodities. Of course, technology failures can be an indication of some human failing, but they can also be the result of simple wear and tear. The strength of the signal is thus much stronger for human errors. The same is true when comparing human failures to failures that are not ascribed to a cause. In the absence of a clear culprit, the signal concerning the quality of IT human resources remains unclear. Due to the fact that human resources are more valuable to a firm because they lead to competitive advantages (Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Mata et al., 1995; Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997), IT failures caused by humans should be worse for a company than IT failures caused by technology. Thus, we propose:

H2: Human error hypothesis: The magnitude of abnormal returns will be larger for human failures than for technological failures or for failures without a clear cause.

## 2.3 Organizational Impression Management Strategies

Organizational impression management refers to “any action purposefully designed and carried out to influence an audience’s perceptions of an organization” (Elsbach et al., 1998, p. 68). Organizations use impression management to help influence the way their stakeholders (investors, customers, business partners, etc.) view the organization as a whole. In order to create, maintain, protect, or alter an image, an actor can use a variety of IM strategies or behaviors to create a desired impression (Bolino et al., 2008; Jones & Pittman, 1982; Tedeschi & Melburg, 1984).

Impression management strategies can be grouped into two broad categories: defensive strategies and assertive strategies. Defensive strategies are those that deny a situation or an event (Benoit, 1995; Coombs, 1998). Defensive strategies include organizational impression management strategies, such as attacking the accuser or denying the blame for an event (Benoit, 1995; Marcus & Goodman, 1991). For example, in 2012, customers of American Airlines experienced problems booking flights because of issues with their reservation system. American Airlines released a statement blaming it on their reservation system, Sabre Holding (Bacon, 2012).

Assertive strategies acknowledge the event, accept blame, and work to correct the error (Coombs, 1998; Marcus & Goodman, 1991). The goal of an assertive strategy is to focus on remediation. For instance, in 2010, Google’s Buzz social network began automatically connecting users together based on their email and chat usages without any regard for the privacy of the users. Customers complained about privacy issues. To deal with these repercussions, Google used the assertive strategy, issuing an immediate apology in the statement: “We’re very sorry for the concern we’ve caused and have been working hard ever since to improve things based on your feedback. We’ll continue to do so” (Coursey, 2010).

The type of strategy chosen is based on the type of impression an actor wants to portray to a target. Assertive strategies admit there is a problem and propose a solution. Admitting there is a problem that is under the firm’s control sends a signal that questions the firm’s ability to deploy resources. If a company admits there is a problem and accepts blame, then it signals to the investors that it had control over the resources that caused the IT failure and did not take appropriate actions to prevent the IT failure. Contrariwise, proposing a plan of action to address the problem sends a signal that in the future the firm will have a greater ability to deploy resources. The overall valence of the signal will generally depend on the relative importance of defending the current image and creating a better future image. Conversely, defensive strategies deny there is a problem and offer no proposal for fixing the problem, because it does not make sense to fix a problem that does not exist.

Choosing the correct organizational impression management strategy depends on the interplay between the cost of admitting mistakes and the benefit of proposing a plan to avoid them in the future. Investors are interested in the future value of a firm. The more an IT failure calls a firm’s competitive resources into question, the more it questions the future value of the firm. Hence, the best signal to send to financial markets when the competitive resources of a firm are in question is one that addresses fixing competitive resources and strengthening the future value of the firm; in other words, an assertive strategy. Alternatively, when an IT failure does question the competitive resources of a firm, the best signal to send is one that stresses that the effect is minor. If an IT failure occurs that has nothing to do with the firm’s competitive resources then the failure does not send a signal to investors about long-term weakness of the firm, and, therefore, there is no need for the firm to signal that it is fixing things, because there is nothing significant to fix. The best signal to send to the market when competitive resources are not in question is one that denies the problem or plays down its significance; in other words, a defensive strategy. As we have argued above, and as Bharadwaj et al. (2009) argued before us, implementation failures call into question the competitive resources of a company more than operational failures do. Implementations require companies to plan, organize, control, and monitor several processes at once. Implementation planning can range from hours to months to execute properly and can require organizations to utilize proper resources in order to implement the information system effectively and on time. On the contrary, operational failures occur when the information system is currently in production. These failures are often minor glitches that are, “less likely to have long-term implications since normal business operations often resume when the systems are restored,” (Bharadwaj et al., 2009, p. 68). Moreover, implementation is inherently forward-looking, signaling the firm’s future, while operations are backward-looking, signaling what the firm did in the past. “Operating failures involving existing systems are not expected to hamper a firm’s growth options, whereas implementation failures involving new systems may reduce the value of a firm’s growth options,” (Bharadwaj et al., 2009, p. 68). Thus, operational failures are less likely to call the firm’s competitive resources into question as compared to operational failures. Table 2 summarizes this point.

Implementation failures are likely to respond better to assertive strategies when the company pays the cost of admitting the problem in order to send an important signal that the competitive resources will be improved in the future. On the contrary, concerning operational failures, an assertive strategy still incurs the cost of admitting the problem, but the company stands to reap fewer benefits from sending a signal about the future of the competitive IT resources. Our argument hinges on implementation failures signaling more about IT competitive resources. Above we argue that, to the degree that IT capabilities represent a source of competitive advantage, assertive strategies are more beneficial. Here we argue that, to the degree that implementation failures are a greater competitive signal than operational failures, assertive strategies will lessen the impact on the loss of market returns for implementation failures as compared to using a defensive strategy. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: Assertive impression management implementation failure hypothesis: For implementation failures, an assertive strategy will lessen the negative market reaction.

Following a similar logic to that above, we argue that assertive impression management strategies will be better for cases of human failure than for technology failures. As mentioned, human errors are seen as being more indicative of problems with competitive resources. Thus, we maintain that assertive strategies will be more appropriate for human errors. ## 2.4 Severity

IT failures can vary greatly, from minor disruptions affecting a few customers with valid alternate solutions, all the way to a global outage with no alternate solution. According to the resource-based view of the firm, a major IT failure could send a strong signal to the financial market that a company does not have complete control over its own resources. Although an IT failure in itself is bad, a low-severity IT failure could signal to the financial market that the firm’s resources are not as deficient since the failure could have been much worse.

H5: Severity hypothesis: The magnitude of abnormal returns will be larger for high-severity failures than for medium- or low-severity failures.

Note that this hypothesis is a replication of Bharadwaj et al.’s (2009) hypothesis 3. This replication allows us to test the robustness of Bharadwaj et al.’s work and calibrate our own model. Confirming this hypothesis would strengthen both Bharadwaj et al.’s claims and our own, while a rejection of this hypothesis would suggest that additional research is needed.

## 3 Methodology

We use the event study methodology (Fama et al., 1969) using a Fama-French 1 factor model to examine how financial markets react to the information about IT failures and corporate organizational impression management strategies. In this study, we use the abnormal returns of a firm as a proxy for the market value of a firm. The abnormal stock market return allows researchers to attempt to isolate the effects of an event on a firm’s stock return without the interference of the overall effects of the market. The abnormal returns for the stock of firm i on day t are calculated as:

$$
A R _ {i t} = R _ {i t} - R _ {i t} ^ {*}\tag{1}
$$

where $A R _ { i t } =$ abnormal return for firm i on day $t , R _ { i t }$ = actual return for firm i on day t, and $R _ { i t } ^ { * } =$ rate of return of firm i on day t. In order to estimate the rate of return of firm i on day $t ~ ( R _ { i t } ^ { * } ) ,$ , we estimate the market of each firm’s stock return as:

$$
R _ {i t} ^ {*} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m t} + \varepsilon_ {i t}\tag{2}
$$

where $R _ { i t } ^ { * } =$ predicted return of firm i on day $t , \alpha _ { i } =$ intercept term that measures the sensitivity of $R _ { i t } ^ { * }$ to the market index, $\beta _ { i } =$ slope parameter that measures the sensitivity of $R _ { i t } ^ { * }$ to the market index $( \alpha _ { i }$ and $\beta _ { i }$ are firm-dependent coefficients to be estimated using ordinary least squares), $R _ { m t } =$ market rate of return using the Standards and Poor’s 500 as the index of the market, and $\varepsilon _ { i t } =$ random error term for the stock of firm i on day t. The daily abnormal return was aggregated on each of the IT failure dates (number of distinct IT failures in the data) over the two-day event window. The CAR for the two-day event window is defined as:

$$
C A R _ {- 1, 0} = \sum_ {t = - 1} ^ {0} A R _ {i t}\tag{3}
$$

This is a well-accepted methodology in the information systems literature (see Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Chatterjee, Vernon, & Zmud, 2001; Dehning¸ Vernon, & Zmud, 2003; Dos Santos, Peffers, K., & Mauer, 1993; Fornell, Mithas, Morgeson, & Krishnan, 2006; Gordon, Loeb, & Sohail, 2010; Hovav & D’Arcy, 2004; Im, Dow, & Grover, 2001; Ranganathan & Brown, 2006; Subramani & Walden, 2001).

## 3.1 Data

The data for this research consist of publicly traded companies that experienced an operational or implementation IT failure that was communicated to the public during the time frame of 2005-2012. IT failures can include many different domains, such as security breaches, cyberattacks, and the introduction of viruses, hardware crashes, and software glitches. The data in this study relate solely to network and system outages, hardware and software crashes, and hardware and software glitches.

This study does not examine security-related IT failures. In addition, we do not address product recalls or defects. Product recalls, defects, and security research may involve different causes, attributions of blame, and failure types that may or may not be consistent with IT failures.

The data gathering process consisted of several steps, which are outlined below. The following search terms were used in a Lexis/Nexis news search: network outage, system outage, software glitch, technical glitch, software bug, software failure, information technology failure, information system failure, computer failure, and computer glitch. We also ran a search with the phrase, “human/employee error/mistake” to account for human errors. An “!” was appended to the end of each search query in Lexis/Nexis to include a wildcard search. For example, “software fail!” included the results for “software failure” and “software failed.” In addition, each search phrase was appended with the phrase “and not power.” This excluded all of the incidents that involved power outages. We adapted the list of search terms from Bharadwaj et al. (2009). While including the search criteria from 2005-2012, the initial dataset consisted of 37,385 search results. Since Lexis/Nexis returns news articles from multiple sources like PR Newswire, Business Wire, and newspapers, many of these results overlapped.

In the second step, we sorted the search results by company in order to narrow the results; all news articles about an outage or a glitch that were not tied to a specific company were grouped into a category called “unclassified documents.” Since these outages did not involve a specific company, we discarded these articles. Thus, over the eight-year period, 11,589 articles remained.

In the third step, we eliminated all companies that were not publically traded companies at the time of their IT failure. In order to accomplish this, each company that experienced an IT failure was crossreferenced with Compustat’s list of publically traded companies for that date. In addition, we discarded any duplicate articles from different news sources. We retained the first published article on the failure or the communication from the company and used it for analysis. In most cases, this article was published by Business Wire or PR Newswire. In a few cases, these publications were from prominent newspapers. Since event studies are based on the efficient market assumption, we assume that information released by Business Wire, PR Newswire, or major newspapers are immediately incorporated into the market. This narrowed the dataset down to 2,282 results.

In the fourth step, we examined each remaining news article to determine if there truly was an IT failure. For example, some news articles mentioned a publically traded company identified in Step 3, but the IT failure mentioned within the article did not involve that company. The majority of these cases occurred in news articles that highlighted business news that grouped multiple news stories into one article returned by the search engine. Therefore, these articles were also excluded. After Step 4, 349 articles remained.

In order to try to isolate the effects of a single event on the returns of a company, other company announcements must be isolated. Using a two-day window (-1, 0) where the failure occurs at day zero and -1 represents one day prior to the failure, each IT failure event was checked to determine if other company announcements were made public. These other announcements included earning announcements, key leadership changes, and mergers and acquisitions (Dos Santos, Zheng, Mookerjee, & Chen, 2012; Hotchkiss & Strickland 2003; Martin & McConnell, 1991). We first excluded the company earnings announcements. Hotchkiss & Strickland (2003) offer evidence that corporate earnings announcements affect market reaction. Therefore, similar to other MIS research using event studies (Dos Santos et al., 2012), we excluded events that include an earnings announcement within the two-day window of the failure.

Similarly, top management changes within a publically traded company affect market returns (Martin & McConnell, 1991), as do mergers and acquisitions (Hotchkiss & Strickland, 2003). Therefore, we excluded events with announcements of top management changes or mergers and acquisitions within the two-day window of the failure. The remaining dataset consisted of 325 companies that experienced IT failures from 2005-2012.

We follow Bharadwaj et al. (2009) in using an estimation period beginning 120 trading days prior to the IT failure and ending two days before the failure (-120, -2). Our event window ranges one day prior to the failure to the day of the failure (-1, 0), which decreased our dataset from 325 unique IT failures to 210. There were multiple cases in which a news article was released the day of the failure, followed by other news articles about the same failure for many days after the failure, until the company fixed the issue or announced a postmortem report. Because we are interested in the change of the market value of the firm on the day of the failure, in this analysis, we only examined an event window of two days (the day before the failure and the day of the failure). Therefore, we excluded all news articles that appeared after the day of the failure. If a company had multiple news articles, we only analyzed the news article on the day of the failure. ## 3.2 Variables and Measures

There are three independent variables in this study: IT failure type, cause of failure, and organizational impression management strategy. IT failure type is a binary variable that represents the type of IT failure that the company experienced. The failures can be implementation failures (e.g., failures that are caused when a new or upgraded system is introduced to the public for the first time) or operational failures (e.g., established systems that temporarily stop working). As discussed before, IT development failures are not in the scope of this research. The value of one (1) is given to implementation failures and a value of zero (0) is given to operational failures. If the news article related to an IT failure of a system that was still in production and experienced a problem, we classified the IT failure type as an operational failure. Conversely, if the news article relates to an IT failure of a system that was being introduced or had been in production for less than a week, we classified the IT failure type as an implementation failure.

Cause of failure is a variable that represents who is to blame for the IT failure. The IT failure can be caused by human error or technology error. Rouse and Rouse (1983) proposed a classification scheme for human errors covering many different contexts. Although their classification scheme is broad, many of their classifications can be used in the context of human IT failures.

Another publication, from the Health and Safety Executive (HSE) Group—a national, British, independent watchdog for work-related health, safety, and illness issues—proposed a diagram of different types of human errors and violations. A modified diagram is located in Appendix Figure A1. HSE (1999) divided the human failure types by errors and violations and further divided those by skill-based errors and mistakes. Further definitions and divisions are noted in Table A2 and Figure A1.

These two classification schemes cover a broad context, not just IT failures. Combining the work from Rouse and Rouse (1983) and HSE (1999), we define human-caused IT failures as those that occur because of slips of action, process-based mistakes, knowledge-based mistakes, erroneous interpretation of results, unnecessary processes, processes executed in incorrect order, and immaturely stopped processes. Organizational impression management strategy is a variable that represents the organizational impression management strategy that the company uses to communicate to its investors. Each article is classified as a defensive strategy or an assertive strategy. There are cases where the company does not respond at all during the IT failure. In this situation, there are no organizational impression management strategies and the event is labeled as “no comment.”

Looking through the IT literature, we could not find a universal scale used to measure IT failure severity. Bharadwaj et al. (2009) created a scale to test the hypothesis that the severity of an IT failure would negatively affect the abnormal return of a firm. They measured IT failure severity with respect to how it disrupted its customers, regardless of how long the failure lasted. Using their scale, IT failures are classified as low-severity events when there are minor disruptions of narrow scope with valid workarounds. Alternatively, IT failures with major disruptions and broad scope with little or no valid alternatives are classified as high-severity events (Bharadwaj et al., 2009). All other failures are classified as mediumseverity events. For example, as previously mentioned, Verizon Wireless reported four outages in their 4G service from December 2011 to February 2012. Customers across the country complained about their 4G service being down (Hachman 2002). These customers could use the 3G service during this outage with no disruption to their normal processes. Therefore, this event would be classified as low severity. In another example, Wells Fargo experienced a nationwide ATM malfunction in February 2011 with little or no alternate solution for its customers. Customers who deposited checks and cash through the ATM had to drive to branches to deposit their checks, resulting in long lines at the bank branches. Therefore, this event would be classified as high severity.

## 3.3 Control Variables

Similar to the work of Bharadwaj et al. (2009), we use four control variables in this study: company size, industry type, company growth, and intensity of IT in the industry. We then added one more control variable called history of IT failure.

Company size is used as a control variable for almost all event studies in accounting, finance, and MIS research (see Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Chatterjee et al., 2001; Dehning et al., 2003; Dewan 2007; Dos Santos et al., 1993; Dos Santos et al., 2012; Han et al., 2012; Im et al., 2001; Ranganathan & Brown, 2006; Subramani & Walden, 2001; Yang, Lim, Oh, Animesh, & Pinsonneault, 2012). However, the specific measure of company size differs among studies. For example, the measure of total assets is used as a proxy for company size in some studies. (e.g., Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Chatterjee et al., 2001; Dehning et al., 2003; Han et al., 2012; Im et al., 2001). One of the control variables used by Bharadwaj et al. (2009) and Dewan & Ren (2007) is the natural log of the market value of firm equity. Im et al. (2001) use noncomputer capital to account for the amount of predisclosure information about IT investments. Bharadwaj et al. (2009) and Ranganathan & Brown (2006) use the log transformation of the number of employees of the firm, total sales, and the log transform of revenue. Chatterjee et al. (2001) use net income. In our model, we follow Bharadwaj et al. (2009) and Ranganathan & Brown (2006) and use the natural log of company size as the proxy for total assets.

Industry type is another control variable that is widely used in the event study methodology (see Bharadwa et al., 2009; Chatterjee et al., 2001; Dehning et al., 2003; Im et al., 2001; Ranganathan & Brown, 2006;

Subramani & Walden, 2001; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011; Yang et al., 2012). Industry type can be controlled by using the SIC code that is assigned for each company. The SIC code stands for the standard industrial classification code, which is a four-digit code assigned to businesses by the United States Office of Management and Budget to identify the primary business industry in which it conducts business. If the company belonged to an industry with a SIC code starting with a seven or eight (typically service companies) then the variable was coded as a zero (0). Otherwise, the variable was coded as a one (1).

The third control variable is company growth. Replicating the study of Bharadwaj et al. (2009), company growth is determined by the percentage increase in yearly sales from two years prior to the IT failure to the year prior to the IT failure.

The next control variable is the intensity of how IT is used in the specific industry. Again replicating Bharadwaj et al. (2009), this variable represents the average IT spending per industry. If the company belonged to the telecommunications, financial services/investment firms, banking, or credit industries, then we coded this variable as a one (1), otherwise it was coded as a zero (0).

The fifth control variable is history of IT failure. Some companies experience multiple IT failures within a short time frame. For example, between the periods of 2005-2012, Verizon experienced IT failures in 2005, 2006, 2011, and 2012. The investors’ reaction to subsequent crises is affected because of the number of previous crises (Coombs, 2004). Other companies like Northrop Grumman reported only one IT failure between 2005-2012. Therefore, we control for IT failure history in this study. If a company experienced one or more IT failures within the time frame of 2005-2012 we assigned a value of zero (0), otherwise a value of one (1).

Since the data sample consists of companies that experienced multiple failures over the period of 2005-2012, and there may have been multiple announcements about the same IT failure, the samples may be nonindependent. In addition to checking the robustness of our results, we also checked for information leakage (MacKinlay, 1997), which can occur if the IT failure is recognized by investors in the market before the official date on which we recognized it. We did not expect this to be an issue in this study, as the failures are generally both sudden and obvious, and because most of them occurred in the Internet age, and would thus be reported very quickly. However, we deemed it worthwhile to be sure. To check for information leakage, we considered the period from 20 days before to two days before the date of the failure (MacKinlay, 1997). We ran our model during this period and found that none of our regressors were predictors of cumulative abnormal return. That suggests that there was no particular information relevant to the nature of the failure that impacted market return in the period leading up to the failures. Given that markets had not been anticipating the failure, there was no information leakage before the event. This makes sense, because if the failures had been predictable, then firms would have striven to avoid them.

Consistent with Bharadwaj et al. (2009), we find that implementation failures result in significantly lower cumulative abnormal returns than nonimplementation failures, and therefore, H1 is confirmed. Thus, firms should strive to avoid IT implementation errors even more than they strive to avoid IT operational errors.

Contrary to H2, human-caused failures resulted in no significantly higher cumulative abnormal returns than did non-human-caused errors. Contrary to our prediction (H4), using an assertive strategy with human-caused failures did not result in a lower cumulative abnormal return. These results are surprising, given the bulk of literature that suggests that human IT resources are a major source of competitive advantage.

To better understand this finding, we re-examined the human failures to get a better sense of what they may have signaled to the markets. We found a dearth of failures that were attributed to human causes, and the ones that were reported amounted to tiny, almost comical blunders. For example, an outage at the Royal Bank of Scotland occurred when a technician pressed the wrong button during a routine software upgrade (Hawkes, 2012). In another situation, an employee typed in a web address incorrectly when updating a list of websites suspected of hosting malicious software (Robbins, 2009). Both of these events had major consequences—it cost Royal Bank of Scotland around \$100 million to fix the software issue in the former case, and Google issued a security warning for every site on the internet for 55 minutes—but the actual errors that were made were as trivial as hitting the wrong key, which is something that people do every day. What is important for us is that none of the human errors suggested that IT management was failing within the company. The vast bulk simply suggested that people are both mentally and physically clumsy.

In light of this, the observed results actually accord well with the resource-based view of the firm and impression management theory. Failures that do not send a strong signal that a competitive resource is in jeopardy are best handled with a defensive strategy because an assertive strategy simply focuses more negative attention on the failure. So, as long as the manual dexterity of IT employees is not a competitive resource of a firm, a manual dexterity-related failure should not signal a problem with competitive resources in the firm. This may not be the case for a concert pianist or a neurosurgeon, but for an IT employee, manual dexterity is not a competitive resource. Thus, a human error associated with manual dexterity—which comprised the bulk of human errors in the dataset—does not send a signal that a competitive resource may be compromised.

In accordance with our predictions, assertive impression management strategies had a positive moderating effect on implementation failures (H3). This result was robust to using CAR or SCAR, to OLS and a general linear model, and to winsorization. This suggests that it behooves companies to accept responsibility and provide a path forward when they experience an implementation failure.

Contrary to our prediction on severity, we could not find any significant effect on market reaction related to high-severity IT failures. Therefore, we also must reject H5. This is contrary to Bharadwaj et al.’s (2009) findings. However, given the difficulty of trying to classify severity, this result is not surprising. Even Bharadwaj et al. noted the difficulty of and lack of literature support for classifying severity as it relates to IT failures. We believe future research should be devoted to understanding severity associated with IT failures.

## 5 Discussion

Organizational impression management appears to have a significant effect on how stock markets react to news of IT failures, and the overall effect seems to depend on the type of failure. For implementation failures, using an assertive strategy—accepting blame and outlining a plan for avoiding failure in the future—leads to an average increase of 3% in the CAR. Although statistically significant, this would seem to be a small percentage increase. However, in dollar terms, it equates to an average of \$212 million in market capitalization saved. Thus, our work suggests that if firms were to use assertive strategies in response to implementation failures they could avoid losing over \$200 million in firm value—which is certainly a significant number for investors and directly meaningful for senior management. Furthermore, preserving corporate value allows a firm to better allocate resources to addressing the problems caused by IT failures. The purpose of capital markets is to exchange corporate ownership for cash in order to do business, and the amount of cash that can thereby be made available depends on the market value of company stock. Finally, stock valuation facilitates recruiting and retaining talented employees through the provision of stock options.

Because the value of impression management strategies strongly depends on specific circumstances, future research should investigate alternative contingencies. In particular, because this study only examines news that occurs on the day of the failure, the impression management strategy may be different for companies that take more than a day to address the issue. Future research could examine how impression management strategies work days or even weeks after the failure.

This research contains several theoretical and managerial contributions to the field of information systems. First, to our knowledge, this is the first time the resource-based view of the firm and organizational impression management have been combined in the attempt to explain why the market reacts differently than the resource-based view of the firm predicts. The resource-based view of the firm would theorize that implementation failures would be worse for a firm (H1). However, we discovered if a company communicates in a certain way about the failure, the market reacts differently than would be suggested by the resource-based view of the firm (H3). We believe we have enhanced the resourcebased view of the firm theory by trying to explain why the market reacts differently than the theory would suggest.

Second, organizational impression management theory literature has been scattered over the past decade and few articles have focused on the importance of the audience in organizational impression management strategies (Bolino et al., 2008). The focus of this study is how investors (i.e., audiences) react to the different organizational impression management strategies communicated by companies. Therefore, one theoretical contribution of this research is that it helps fill the gap in theory by focusing on the audience. Another current issue in using organizational impression management is that most research focuses on the microlevel, which makes it difficult to generalize (Bolino et al., 2008). A second contribution of our research is our examination of over 218 different news releases across 117 different companies; this allows us to attempt a generalization of our results across publically traded companies in the U.S.

Another theoretical contribution brings organizational impression management theory into IS research. More specifically, we introduce organizational impression management theory into the field of IT failures. Given the large impact of IT failures for companies, IS research has demonstrated significant interest in studying these failures (Beynon-Davies, 1995; Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Dalcher & Genus, 2003; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1988; Poulymenakou & Serafeimidis 1997). However, past IT failure research has primarily studied the impact of failures or how to prevent them. In contrast, our research is based on the assumption that IT failures occur and will always occur. Our contribution is to identify the best way for companies to communicate this failure to its investors in order to minimize the impact to the company. Therefore, this study attempts to introduce organizational impression management strategies to IS research in the context of IT failures.

The new synthesis of the resource-based view of the firm and impression management can potentially bring several new areas of research to the field of information systems. First, our findings could be used to build a new theory of managing IT failures. To our knowledge, there is no theory of managing IT failures, and the public relations literature does not take into account different types and causes of IT failures. This new theory could synthesize resourcebased views and impression management, while also bringing in other organizational-based theories, like the knowledge-based theory of the firm and organizational learning theory. Furthermore, this new theory could also take other failures, like security breaches, into account.

Second, this research only examined IT failures that were not security related. We believe that impression management strategies might be different for companies experiencing failures entirely within their purview than for companies experiencing, for example, security-related failures that involve an external component. Such cases may involve different impression management strategies. A new stream of research could examine security-related breaches and cybersecurity threats. This is a timely topic of discussion, since “one problem is that much cybercrime is buried and many companies prefer to lick their wounds in private, fearing damage to their reputation or lawsuits, if they confess to sloppy security” (Economist, 2014). Further research using this synthesized theory could also examine human versus technology determinants of innovation cycles for companies and how that is communicated to investors.

There are also limitations to our research. First, our work is limited to IT failures and to only two characteristics of such failures. Given the large effects we observed, it would be worthwhile to explore organizational impression management strategies for IT-related issues more broadly.

A second limitation of using the synthesis between the resource-based view of the firm and impression management is that we are at the mercy of what the company communicates to the public. It is possible that companies hide the truth or mislead investors, or that internal communication is different than reported by the media. This research only examined what the company communicated publicly—or did not communicate, in some cases—and did not take any contradictory narratives into account. This discrepancy could spark new research using the synthesized theory we present.

Our managerial contribution is that IT managers and corporate executives could use this framework in order to customize communications to investors based on the IT failure type or cause. According to our findings, there are significant impacts to market capitalization based on the impression management strategy that we used.

Our goal was to extend the work of Bharadwaj et al. (2009) on IT failures by studying impression management strategies and how they affect the market value of the firm with different failure types and causes of failures. Organizational impression management works, and works well, but it is a tool that needs to be used properly. The right strategy must be chosen in response to the specific nature of the IT failure. Our results suggest that firms can preserve, on average, \$212 million in firm value by using assertive organizational impression management strategies in response to IT implementation failures. However, the same strategy used for nonimplementation failures can make the exacerbate losses caused by an IT failure.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we synthesize impression management theory (Bolino et al., 2008; Elsbach et al., 1998) and the resource-based view of the firm (Barney 1991; Penrose 1959; Wernerfelt 1984) to develop a new perspective of how managers can modify the signals sent to financial markets by IT failures. We contribute to a wide literature on financial market reactions to IT failure (Bharadwaj et al., 2009; Cavusoglu, Mishra, & Raghunathan, 2004; Gordon et al., 2010; Telang & Wattal, 2005). We bring an important innovation to this literature: an actionable solution that managers can pursue. We show that managers can influence the market’s reaction to IT failure by sending the appropriate signal to the market to address the nature of the failure. The choice of the appropriate impression management strategy has significant effects on shareholder reactions. We hope this work stimulates a new direction in the exploration of how markets react to IT phenomena in general, by illustrating that there are actions that managers can take to shape investors’ impressions about IT activities.
