## Abstract

The advent of social networking websites presents further opportunities for criminals to obtain information for use in identity theft, cyber-stalking, and worse activities. This paper presents research investigating why users of social networking websites willingly disclose personal information and what sorts of information they provide (or not). The study employed an ethnographic approach of participation in the online community and interviews of community members, combined with a quantitative survey. The findings show that users are often simply not aware of the issues or feel that the risk to them personally is very low. The paper develops a preliminary theoretical model to explain the information disclosure phenomenon. It further recommends that government agencies or social networking websites themselves conduct campaigns to inform the public of these issues and that social networking websites consider removing some facilities. The study was conducted in an Australian context and focussed on the popular Facebook website. Journal of Information Technology (2010) 25, 126–136. doi:10.1057/jit.2010.18

Keywords: social networking websites; Facebook; privacy; information disclosure; identity theft

## Introduction

n 2007, after stealing the bag of a trainee teacher, a fraudster viewed the online profile of the victim and within three hours had succeeded in obtaining more than d400 from her credit account (Gray and Montague, 2007). In 2006, a 20-year-old woman received the following instant message: ‘Hey y I really like your Facebook pictures. You are very pretty. I look at your pictures every day and thought maybe we could meet sometime’. Although she immediately blocked the sender, she received hundreds of e-mails regarding her interests, classes, and friends. Further, her mobile phone rang constantly and when she answered, all she heard was a clicking sound from a restricted number. The woman blamed the amount of uncensored self-information she had on her Facebook profile (Klein, 2006). Such cases are not isolated incidents; online abuses such as identity fraud and cyber-stalking have become significant and growing problems.

Many jurisdictions have introduced laws to combat online abuse and required the owners of social networking sites to add more safeguards and privacy controls to protect users, including those in Australia. Bruce van der Graaf of the New South Wales Police Fraud Squad contends that merely knowing the identity and date of birth of the victim gives an identity thief the ‘key to the finance sector’ (Stolen

ID, 2008). In 2003, the Attorney General of Victoria declared he would introduce the first cyber-stalking law in Australia (Office of the Attorney General, 2003). More recently, the Australian Federal Minister for Home Affairs also recommended that ‘making, supplying, using or possessing identity information, with the intention of committing an indictable offence, all become potentially indictable offences themselves’ (Minister for Home Affairs, 2008).

Indeed, both government and non-government organisations have campaigned to warn people not to disclose personal information online. Yet in an experiment conducted in August 2007 to explore how inclined Facebook users were to share information, 78% of 200 random friend requests sent on Facebook exposed their current address or location, and 41% of respondents were ‘happy to reveal all’ (Sophos, 2007), potentially exposing themselves to a range of threats including fraud, online and physical stalking, and blackmail. In turn, this also exposes others to the risk of exploitation by identity thieves who, by using an identity for which they are not responsible, are unlikely to be held accountable for their actions.

As the number and types of people joining social networking websites continue to increase, early and necessary measures should be taken to prevent social networking websites and their participants from being abused. Facebook is the leading example of social networking websites. Javier Olivan, international manager at Facebook, has stated that ‘Our goal would be to hopefully have one day everybody on the planet on Facebook’ (Hosaka, 2008). This research seeks to explain the information disclosure behaviour of users of the major social networking site Facebook, from their perspectives and within their contexts. Such explanation could inform operators of social network systems and contribute to the development of policies and procedures to more effectively educate users.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: The next section reviews previous research in this area. The third section then describes the research question explored in this study. Next we describe the research method and the two subsequent sections discuss initial findings and detailed analysis respectively. The following section draws conclusions and makes recommendations whereas the final section acknowledges limitations and suggests further research.

## Literature review

The phenomenon of publishing personal information online is relatively recent. When SixDegrees.com was launched in 1997, the website initially attracted millions of users but closed down in 2000 because of financial difficulties. Andrew Weinreich, the founder of SixDegrees .com, stated that ‘SixDegrees was simply ahead of time’ (Boyd and Ellison, 2008).

Current social networking sites, however, are enjoying massive success, which is likely to continue. Facebook is the most popular social networking site, and according to recent statistics the site has more than 100 million users (Facebook, 2008). Such sites are also receiving huge investments; Facebook, for instance, received a \$240 million investment from Microsoft in 2007 (Malik, 2007).

In contrast to the increasing popularity of social networking sites, the degree of public concern about publishing personal information online appears to be falling: only 54.4% of respondents to a study of students (Caruso and Salaway, 2008) and 56% of respondents to a study of Internet users in general (Paine et al., 2006) were concerned about their privacy online, down from 70% of respondents who were asked similar questions by Jupiter Research in 2002.

A vivid example of decreased concern for privacy is the increase in members’ use of their real name as an online identifier, rather than the once-common use of pseudonyms or aliases. According to Gross et al. (2005), of the 100 student Facebook profiles they studied, approximately 89% of names on Facebook are valid, and it is this trend that has led some to describe Facebook as the ‘Google of people’ (Jarvis, 2007). Nevertheless, it is not only disclosure of real names that is the problem, since all kinds of data are published despite users’ awareness of privacy issues.

Cranor et al. (1999: 8) found that people are more comfortable about disclosing information if they believe they will not be identified. Carried out in 1999, their survey found that 76% of respondents were comfortable providing their email address, 69% their age, 54% their full name, 44% their postal address but fewer were willing to provide their income (17%) and phone number (11%).

However, even though Internet users may express concerns about privacy, they may still engage in risky behaviours (Campbell et al., 2001). A survey carried out by Computer Associates Inc. and the National Cyber Security Alliance of the United States indicated that of the 57% of participants of social networking websites who were aware of the risks of cybercrime, 74% had nevertheless given out personal information such as e-mail address, name, and birthday (Network Security, 2006).

Similarly, Govani and Pashley’s (2005) study of student Facebook users found that 84% were aware that they could change their privacy settings, but less than 48% had made use of the privacy settings. Further, even after being made aware of privacy settings within Facebook, very few subsequently changed their settings to restrict access to their information.

Another survey of North American college students revealed that although respondents ranked privacy policy higher than terrorism, almost 40% of the students who claimed to be concerned about protecting their class schedule still posted it on Facebook. The study concluded that there was ‘little or no relation between participants reported privacy attitudes and their likelihood of providing certain information’ (Acquisti and Gross, 2006).

Other studies have supported the notion that disclosure of personal information on Facebook is widespread. Strater and Richter’s (2007) albeit small (n ¼ 12) study of students found that 67% of participants had their profiles available to the public and did not restrict access to personal information to friends only; similarly, Kolek and Saunders (2008) found that only 11% of Facebook users restricted access to their profiles (Kolek and Saunders, 2008).

Clearly, disclosure of personal information to the public is a widespread phenomenon, and there are other factors involved beyond a user’s awareness of any risks associated with such disclosure. Caruso and Salaway (2008) report that age group is a key factor; users from the ‘Net Generation’ (between 18 and 24 years old) were more likely to reveal their last name, email address, and date of birth than their older counterparts.

As well as age, Govani and Pashley (2005) argue that in order to know why Facebook users disclose personal information it is important to examine the reason behind the use of the website. Although this conclusion was reached while access to Facebook was restricted to students and therefore may not be representative of society as a whole, it is supported by other student studies. For example, Ellison et al. (2007) conclude that Facebook is used to maintain or intensify already existing relationships, and Lampe et al. (2006: 167) demonstrate that Facebook is used to learn more about people users already know offline rather than to form new connections. Hargittai’s (2008: 293) investigation of the differences between student users and non-users of social networking websites in general indicates that ‘online actions and interactions cannot be seen as tabula rasa activities, independent of existing offline identities. Rather, constraints on one’s everyday life are reflected in online behaviour’.

Similar conclusions have been reached in more generalisable studies such as Mantovani’s (2001) much earlier study of online interaction, which concluded that patterns of social interaction are largely similar both online and offline, and Mullen’s (2005) finding that the stronger the relationship between people, the more likely they are to communicate using any electronic medium – including the telephone, e-mail, short-message system (SMS), and Internet-based instant message services.

It is not just past experience with specific individuals that influences behaviour, but past experience in general. Kobsa (2007) argues that research evidence has shown that trust directly influences the information disclosure behaviour of Internet users, adding that ‘positive past experience is an established factor for trust whose impact on the disclosure of personal information is well supported’ (Kobsa, 2007: 28). The topic of trust more generally is considered in a large body of research, which cannot be reviewed in the space available. For example, the reader is referred to the work of Hardin (2002, 2004).

As well as past experience, the user interface or design elements of a website and its operational characteristics, such as updates since last visit or links to outside sources, may increase the trust users assign to a website (Fogg 2003). On the other hand, Brusilovsky et al. (2007) maintain that the reputation of the website operator is a key factor influencing the trust of users and the willingness of users to disclose personal information.

With a view to comparing college students’ concerns regarding trust between Facebook and MySpace, Dwyer et al. (2007) found a greater degree of trust and more willingness to share information among Facebook users, where the basis of trust relied on the capacity of users to evaluate other users. Similarly, Boyd and Heer (2006) argue that users interpret the context of a situation to determine what is socially appropriate, and that user profiles are part of the social context by which other users judge social norms and appropriateness.

Indeed, members of online communities ‘in some sense enter a social, if not a physical, space’ because pictures and videos of other people can be seen by remote users (Kendall, cited in Watson et al., 2006). The number of users of social networking sites continues to increase as teenagers respond to continually decreasing ‘access to public spaces’ where they can be themselves, and among friends, thus prompting a desire to access virtual communities to ‘(re)create private and public youth space while physically in controlled spaces’ (Boyd, 2006).

Undoubtedly, boundaries of public, physical spaces are clear; boundaries of virtual spaces are not. As Meyrowitz (1990) notes, online situations are essentially the patterns of access to information, and as such they may be convoluted or invisible. Drawing on Goffman (1959, 1963), Dell and Marinova (2002) argue that this would lead to more conservative disclosure of personal information online, because of difficulties delineating one audience from another.

However, this theoretical prediction is at odds with the empirical evidence that many users are willing to publish large amounts of personal information online for potentially the whole world to see; it seems that the Internet creates an ‘illusion of intimacy that could prove dangerous in the reality’ (Skiba, 2007: 215).

Reducing this disconnect between user attitudes and behaviour in the design of social networking websites is addressed by Lipford et al. (2008), who propose an improved tab interface in order to help users appreciate how various pieces of information are presented to different audiences. Such a user interface would allow users to more accurately understand which information is presented to whom and addresses the fact that user interface design can influence the trust users assign to a website, as indicated above.

However, there are undoubtedly factors other than trust in a website that influence information disclosure. Some factors already identified above include age group, offline interaction patterns, and past experiences. But, these do not adequately explain why users deliberately choose to publish key pieces of information to a wide audience. Further, much of the past research refers to exclusively student-based samples. These research gaps require further investigation to understand why the phenomenon of disclosure of private information on social networking sites occurs despite knowledge of potential consequences and ways to prevent them. Such understanding may enable the development of practical actions and capabilities to reduce such risks to the public. These goals form the basis for the current project, for which a specific research question is described in the next section.

## Research question

The literature review above identifies a significant issue about which relatively little is known: why users of social networking sites continue to expose themselves to risks such as identity theft, damage to their future careers or even the risk of personal harm by publishing personal information online. This research seeks to identify factors involved in the information disclosure behaviour of users of the major social networking site Facebook. Our overall research question is thus:

What factors lead Facebook users to disclose or conceal personal information online?

This is a significant issue. Such decisions are essentially the result of decreasing concern with online privacy issues, which, as discussed above, has led to a situation in which millions of users are at risk. The Information Commissioner’s Office in the United Kingdom states that 4.5 million people aged 14 to 21 are putting them at risk of identity fraud or ruining their future careers by posting private information in social networking sites (Brooks, 2007).

Banning the use of social networking websites is practically impossible and undesirable, so programmes to promote awareness of the dangers of disclosing personal information are imperative. Initiatives like the NetAlert – Protecting Australian Families Online or Get Safe Online in the United Kingdom are a significant step in the right direction, although given the willingness of users to publish personal details online, their effectiveness to date may be limited.

Understanding how such programmes can be improved requires understanding of what motivates people to engage in or refrain from risky online behaviour. Previous research suggests that such behaviours occur on a large scale; however, little is known of the factors that lead people to knowingly disclose information that may expose them to risk.

## Research method

Past research has identified a problem vis-a\`-vis users of social networking sites publishing personal information online and thus exposing themselves to potentially serious risks. The main purpose of this study is to identify factors relevant to users’ information disclosure behaviour. The research considers a topic that is social in nature, seeks to investigate human behaviour, and examines users’ subjective perceptions of concepts such as privacy and trust. For this reason an interpretivist framework is used.

Because of the open-ended, theory-building nature of the research question and the somewhat unfamiliar domain, the research design included three stages. It began with an exploratory stage to gain overall understanding of the context and the phenomenon. Next, it pursued more focussed data collection to identify trends, factors, and initial relationships (potential theory components). Finally, the research design sought to hone in on an understanding of how the factors influence the outcomes of private information disclosure (or non-disclosure), including which kinds of private information. These three stages were conducted from March to May 2008. Each stage is described in turn below.

The first, exploratory stage involved participant observation, the objective of which was to go where the users are and appreciate the state of being a member of Facebook. The first author immersed himself in the Facebook community, becoming an ‘introspective observer’ (Denzin, 1971) in order to better understand the Facebook community from the point of view of a participant. Thus, a Facebook account was created and linked to the Australia ‘Network’, which at the time the research was conducted was the only way to search for users based on gender, age groups, religious and political views, interests and other factors. (Facebook has recently removed all ‘Network’ pages and promoted the use of ‘Groups’ instead, which currently have no browse feature.) The researcher also modified his profile, adding his name, date of birth, photos and education history so as to fit seamlessly in the context of the population.

The observations obtained during the first research phase were explored in a less individual, more generalisable way in the second, survey phase of the research, in which a questionnaire was developed and sent to Facebook users. The purpose of the questionnaire was two-fold: to obtain the views of more Facebook users for a broader view of the situation, and to refine the focus for the final stage of the research. Since it was not possible to obtain a mailing list of all Facebook users for random sampling, the snowball sampling method was used during this phase. Data from the survey were analysed using quantitative data analysis methods, including measures of central tendency and associations between variables.

Results from the questionnaire identified key issues for the researcher to address in the interviews that followed in the third and final stage. Online interviewing was chosen as the most suitable method to investigate the research questions, not only because no other method would have a similar ability to build a relationship between the respondent and the researcher, but also because interviews enabled the researcher to consider ‘local conditions and local values’ (Lincoln and Guba, 1985: 40). Participants were contacted both through the mailing system of Facebook and through word-of-mouth. In-depth, ethnographic interviews (Spradley, 1979) were then conducted and the qualitative data were analysed using Domain Analysis (Spradley, 1979; Atkinson and Abu El Haj, 1996), as described below.

Ethnographic interviews are described by Spradley (1979) as ‘friendly conversations’ guided by a specific research agenda, in this case to investigate issues surrounding privacy, disclosure of information online, and awareness of the risks. The interviewer must make the purpose of the interview clear to the participant for ethnographic interviews to be successful. Rapport must also be developed in the beginning of the process before information elicitation can begin (Spradley, 1979). Questions become more and more specific over time (Sorrell and Redmond, 1995), beginning with general, descriptive ‘grand tour’ style questioning in which participants are asked to describe spaces, specific time periods, events, people, activities, or objects (Spradley, 1979). Subsequent questions become more explicit; structural questions are used to organise and verify information gleaned from the informant, and to ask for explanations (Spradley, 1979; Sorrell and Redmond, 1995). Finally, contrast questions, which involve asking participants to compare different terms, can be employed to help understand the meanings of key words used by participants (Spradley, 1979; Sorrell and Redmond, 1995).

It is up to the researcher to decide when the interview should develop from one form of question to another, and often different types of questions will be used concurrently (Spradley, 1979; Sorrell and Redmond, 1995). Ethnographic interviewing requires trust between interviewer and participant, and developing this rapport with participants is a difficult process that must be tailored to each individual (Spradley, 1979).

The sample of interviewees was not intended to be representative in a statistical sense as random sampling is inappropriate for ethnographic research as ‘a random sample of people does not constitute a random sample of culture’ (Handwerker, 2005). Rather, purposive sampling was chosen to enable the researcher to encompass as large a scope of data as possible and a diversity of users, rather than to generalise. The interviewees were selected to reach users of different gender, age groups, relationship status, and profile accessibility. The interviewees also included past victims of stalking and identity theft. Further interviewee selection was stopped when informational redundancy (saturation) had been reached.

Since the research conducted involved humans as participants, the researchers endeavoured to conduct the research ethically and followed a university code of ethics. Frankel and Siang (1999) argue that researchers should maximise possible benefits and minimise harms and risks to their subjects. Although physical risks were less likely, the participants were treated with courtesy and respect, were informed about the nature of the research and its requirements, and were informed that they had no obligation to participate. The participants were also given reasons for the survey and the researcher promoted awareness of the research problem, thus contributing to the gain of the society. As Orb et al. (2001) points out, interviews often involve sharing secrets, and it was the moral obligation of the researchers not to abuse the trust gained through the relationship built between the researcher and the participant. Data gathered were kept safely and are not attributed to identifiable individuals in reporting the research.

The qualitative interview data were then analysed using Domain Analysis (Spradley 1979; Atkinson and Abu El Haj, 1996). Domain Analysis is a method for analysing qualitative data which ‘allows the participants to identify for themselves the topics and issues of importance’ (Atkinson and Abu El Haj, 1996: 438), and aims ‘to reconstruct the categories used by subjects to conceptualise their own experiences and world view’ (Goetz and LeCompte, cited in Lincoln and Guba, 1985: 335). The process involves four steps, culminating in a model which is grounded in the data rather than imposed from a theoretical perspective. As a research approach, it is similar to, but different from the various grounded theory approaches (cf. Strauss and Corbin, 1990; Glaser, 1992).

The next section describes the data gathered during the three research stages and initial findings.

## Overview of research activities and results

## Stage 1

A total of 300 profiles of users under the Australia ‘network were selected at random using Facebook’s then available browse feature, and over 50 h were spent observing everyday life on Facebook. Not only could the researchers view profiles of many other people, but this was possible without the knowledge of the individuals whose profiles were being viewed. Personal information that could be read straight from profiles included name, relationship status, friends list, photos, date of birth, work, and education history. The comment feature of Facebook, the ‘Wall’, was also a rich source of information about the current activities of users, although the messages were typically quite brief.

Ninety-six per cent of the profiles examined were publically available and, among those that were viewable, 98% used a real – or real-sounding – name. It is impossible for the researchers to accurately tell whether these names were indeed the users’ real names; however, the Facebook sign-up process explicitly asks users for their first and last names and the proportion using a real-sounding but false identity is likely to be low. Ninety-four per cent of viewable profiles included their relationship status, 89% of viewable profiles included a date of birth, and 35% of viewable profiles included work information. However, contact information was far less common: only 12% of viewable profiles included an email address, 1% included a physical address, and 2% included a telephone number.

These results diverge noticeably from the previously noted findings of Cranor et al. (1999); as Table 1 illustrates, people today (at least on Facebook) appear more willing to disclose descriptive information, such as age and name, but less willing to disclose contact information.

A likely explanation is that users increasingly seek to avoid harassing phone calls, spam emails, or similar sideeffects from disclosing contact details, but perceive no disadvantage to publishing descriptive information about themselves.

As well as information about themselves, Facebook also allows members to provide information about the relationships they have with each other, such as ‘In my family’, ‘We dated’, ‘Worked together’ and so on. This relationship information can prove to be valuable for social engineering criminals. For example, assuming person A leaves their profile public, criminal Z may be able to view with whom A has travelled, lived or attended school, and to identify A’s, family and colleagues. Armed with such information, criminal Z would be able to fabricate a profile to misrepresent A or to obtain further information on A by acting on A’s behalf.

The overall experience of the researcher who undertook the participant observation in Stage 1 was that there appeared to be a large number of Facebook users who were actively disclosing information that could be used for the purpose of identity theft or other exploitative activity. This phenomenon was investigated further in the questionnaire, which is described in the following section.

## Stage 2

A questionnaire was employed in the second stage. This was informed by the observations gained during Stage 1 and consisted of a mix of multiple-choice, dichotomous and open-ended questions pertaining to the methods used to secure personal information, the reasons for use of social networking websites, the information users are willing to share, and users’ understanding of cybercrime ‘phishing’.

Of the 215 individuals who received the questionnaire, 51 responded, giving an overall response rate of 24%. Although we did not gather information on age or whether the person was a student or not, we did gather data on length of use of the Internet. The diversity in the number of years using the Internet (0% less than 1 year, 14% 1–3 years, 20% 4–5 years, 40% 6–8 years, 10% 9–10 years, and 18% over 10 years) indicates that many of the respondents were not students, in line with the likely change in the population using Facebook since allowing non-students.

Of the respondents, 67% claimed to restrict access to their profiles and another 31% selected ‘I don’t put any personal information’. Only one respondent (2%) reported putting fake information in their profile. Fourteen per cent of respondents admitted to not protecting their personal information. Interestingly, 74% of all respondents reported a willingness to disclose their date of birth, a proportion not dissimilar to that observed during Stage 1. That so many users appear ready to disclose their date of birth is surprising given the usefulness of this information to identity thieves. Table 3 shows that while 72.55% of respondents believed that they were not safe from identity theft, over three quarters of those respondents published their date of birth anyway, a rate even higher than among those who did believe they were safe from identity theft! In contrast, only 10.81% of users who believed they were not safe from identity theft were willing to publish their home address, a rate substantially less than for those who believe they are safe.

A possible interpretation of these results is that most respondents are generally aware of the risks involved in publishing certain types of information but not others. The risk involved in publishing your home address is easy to understand and therefore many people are unlikely to take such a risk. In contrast, while many people may have some awareness of identity theft, they do not understand the role date of birth plays in it. Similarly, while 69% of respondents provided a definition of ‘phishing’, only 12% were accurate, further suggesting that while they may be generally aware of the issue, most respondents do not have a strong understanding of it.

Another possible interpretation is that users’ are aware of identity theft in a general sense but believe it would not happen to them individually. This familiar phenomenon, in which the level of risk to oneself is perceived to be lower than to people in general – regardless of whether such a perception is justified, has been termed ‘unrealistic optimism’ (Sjo¨berg, 2000). Eighty-eight per cent of respondents said they used social networking websites to ‘keep up with my friends and family’ and so may believe themselves to be invisible to identity thieves.

A third possibility is simply that users understand the risks involved in disclosing personal information online but consider this to be a risk worth taking.

Stage 2 suggests that a sizable proportion of users are willing to publish personal information online – despite a belief that they are at risk of identity theft. To investigate why this is so the research continued to the qualitative investigation in Stage 3, described in the following section.

## Stage 3

The third research stage further explored what drives users to publish personal information online. Ethnographic interviews (Spradley, 1979) with 25 individuals were conducted, 18 of which were contacted and interviewed through Facebook’s mailing system and the remaining seven of which were interviewed face-to-face and via telephone. The sample included 14 female and 11 male participants. There were 18 participants in the 21–30 years age range, 1 participant in the 31–40 years range, and 5 participants in the 41–50 years range. The interviewees included users who had fake information on their profiles as well as users who had been the victim of stalking and impersonation. Data were also collected from users who claimed not to use social networking websites and from users with more than 300 connections.

The first stage in the domain analysis process (Spradley 1979; Atkinson and Abu El Haj, 1996) is to segment the data into ‘units of meaning’. Rather than line-by-line coding or similar coding methods based on syntactic units such as sentences or paragraphs, this approach involves separating a text where the participant’s purpose changes, thus taking into account Henri’s (1991) observation that texts should be analysed based on their meaning, not syntactical structures. In order to illustrate the process, an example of the segmenting and categorising process is provided in Table 4. (Note that quotations from online interviews are reproduced verbatim, including spelling and grammar errors, which are common in chat and message facilities.)

In the second stage of domain analysis, the units of meaning were organised into preliminary categories. This entails looking for similarities and patterns in the data, and also making sure to include sufficient information about the context from which the units of meaning were derived (Seaman, 1999). The preliminary categories developed are illustrated in Table 5.

In the third step, the preliminary categories were refined and consolidated to obtain a list of the dominant categories, or domains, and the units of meaning were allocated to their respective domains and scrutinised to identify relationships between the domains. The domains identified were:

\- Value: The importance the user places on her or his identity, their trust in other users and their personal judgements of online situations.

\- Context: The users’ context which affects her or his activities and behaviour, including their objectives, lessons learnt from others or from past experience, and other elements of their surroundings.

\- Behaviour: The actions the user makes within social networking websites, including sharing or withholding personal information.

The first domain, Context, includes contextual factors that influence users’ information disclosure behaviour. Thi includes a user’s objectives for using social networking sites in the first place and largely consists of reasons in favour of publishing personal information, as illustrated in the following representative quotations:

i only use Facebook to keep in contact with my friends. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

I use my Facebook as a kind of CV. (Male student aged 21–30 years)

I figure that an online persona, carefully crafted, gives you control over how people see you. (Male student aged 21–30 years)

I post these details so that people know me better (Female aged 21–30 years)

thats what i thought people wanted to hear (Female student aged 21–30 years)

However, Context also includes outside influences such as friends and co-workers, which can even be the motivation behind joining Facebook in the first place, as the following example makes clear:

I really signed up only because my friends forced me to. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

I started with my space but found it annoying, then everyone at work signed up with facebook so I did too. (Working female aged 41–50 years)

Other people, the mass media, and previous personal experience can all make an individual user aware of the risks of publishing personal information online, as the following examples illustrate:

i guess in a way i spose from tv, ads and people tlking you learn that its not such a safe thing to put everything up about yourself. (Female student aged 21–30 years) i think i am the same offline perhaps more vigilant online though because you hear so many horror stories. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

However, while many participants had heard of identity theft from third-party sources, a few participants were cynical about such stories. This cynicism was based on their own experiences, which had been positive.

I’ve only had wonderful experiences via the net and have met people I never would have encountered or thought I would have particularly liked prior to a couple of forums I have been involved in. (Working female aged 41–50 years)

In their study of Internet users’ intention to conduct e-commerce transactions, Dinev and Hart (2006) found that privacy concerns are influenced by social awareness and Internet literacy, both of which are likely to increase with age and online experience. Although the focus of their study was different – e-commerce sites rather than social networking sites – their findings are similar to the notion that a user’s personal experience and outside influences would affect privacy concerns relating to social networking sites. This holds out the possibility that it may be possible to extend our findings re. users’ information disclosure behaviour beyond social networking sites.

The second domain, Value, refers to each individual user’s values relevant to their personal sense of privacy. Value can be shaped by Context as individuals are influenced by the media and others around them. Value includes the value individuals place on their own privacy, as illustrated in the following comments.

I’m a fairly private person generally, and don’t easily let new people into my inner circle. (Working male aged 21–30 years)

I tend to keep things pretty private when it comes to online sites such as Facebook. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

i would tell people about me honestly and if they didnt like certain things about me then that was there own problem. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

Value also encompasses the value users place on their own identity and whether they consider themselves a likely target for identity theft – and thus whether there is any risk in publishing personal information. The naı¨ve belief that a normal, unremarkable person’s identity has no value to an identity thief, exemplified in the following quotations, was common.

i don’t think anyone would want my identity! (Working male aged 41–50 years)

guess I figure my identity isn’t worth stealing – I’m probably a bit naive/blase about that. (Working female aged 41–50 years)

Users who hold such a belief are more likely to disclose personal information. Conversely, if the user’s trust in the ‘generalised other’ (c.f. Mead, 1934) is low, as exemplified in the following quotations, they are less inclined to disclose personal information.

I guess I trust that the people on Facebook will do the right thing and not abuse the concept. (Working female aged 41–50 years)

It’s the trust factor really you don’t know who you can trust these days (Female student aged 21–30 years)

It has to be a comfort thing with me, if I pick up on a weird vibe from someone then I will keep it extremely brief. (Working female aged 21–30 years)

One thing I do do though, is get a feel for the person I am dealing with. (Working male aged 41–50 years)

Context and Value both influence a user’s Behaviour – the third domain to emerge from the domain analysis process. The relationship between Value and Behaviour is clear and intuitive – if a user is uneasy about others knowing their personal details in general, they are unlikely to post those details online.

I do worry about publishing my personal info. (Working male aged 21–30 years)

I’m not the kind of person who would accept anyone as a Friend if I haven’t met them personally – in which case I’d have given them most of that info anyway. (Working male aged 41–50 years)

The interviews revealed relatively few cases where users had this kind of concern. Most were willing to post information because their objective was to communicate with family and friends and were unaware of the risks involved; the following comment was typical.

Full name for searchability – so friends can find me. (Male student of undisclosed age)

However, if users had heard stories of identity theft, they would also be less likely to divulge their own details, as in the following example.

Identity theft would be one of my concerns and that is why I never publish more full details nor do I publish everything correctly their are purposeful mistakes. (Working male aged 21–30 years)

The relationship between Context and Behaviour is a key finding as it provides an explanation for the contradiction between empirical evidence and the theoretical prediction of Dell and Marinova (2002): that users are only likely to become less likely to divulge personal information online when they become aware of and appreciate the risks of doing so.

Technical solutions, such as that proposed by Lipford et al. (2008) can help reveal these boundaries. The current research also indicates that users more fully comprehend the boundaries as a consequence of experience, perhaps by having one’s information misappropriated or perhaps by hearing of such cases. A final, interesting example of how personal experience can affect a user’s information disclosure behaviour was triggered by the research itself.

d.o.b i guess i ‘thought’ it was harmless y however now that i am thinking about it y i am going to delete it. (Female student aged 21–30 years)

This shows that it is possible to alter users’ behaviour through education. It seems that the ‘default’ behaviour of users is typically to publish information for the benefit of others, and it is only after information about identity theft and associated risks is communicated that users will consider altering their behaviour. This insight has led to the recommendations in the following section.

## Conclusions and recommendations

The research question posed by this paper was to identify factors that lead Facebook users to disclose personal information online. An answer to this question is provided in the preliminary theoretical model of users’ information disclosure behaviour described above.

The findings in this paper enrich theoretical understandings of online information disclosure behaviour. Dinev and Hart (2006) demonstrated that a positive relationship exists between social engagement and social awareness and concern for privacy; the current work shows that concern for privacy is an interim step on the way to influencing information disclosure.

The current findings also relate to observations that decisions about disclosing personal information are influenced by the context of the exchange (Boyd and Heer, 2006; Caruso and Salaway, 2008). This research extends this idea not just to the context of the exchange per se, but the wider context of the individual, including their objectives and personal history.

Recognising the implication of personal history and context can usefully inform governments and other relevant bodies, whose objective is to protect the public and minimise the incidence of identity theft and related problems.

Knowing why people are depriving themselves from their privacy not only addresses the problem and ensures that users of the sites can make informed choices but also provides a knowledge base for future generations. Knowing what influences the users to post personal information online empowers governments and relevant authorities to tackle the root of the problem.

Based on the explanations above, we recommend two new additions to the body of advice for designers of social networks systems:

\- Stage 3 revealed that many users have a naı¨ve sense that online communities are safe, thus leading to risky information disclosure behaviour. However, this stage also revealed that users’ behaviour is influenced by information they receive from others, and therefore it is likely that the provision of appropriate advice could help minimise the problem. Thus, we recommend that social networking sites such as Facebook more effectively communicate to users the risks involved with divulging personal information. (For how one might do so, see also the third recommendation below.)

\- Second, given that there will inevitably be some users who will disclose information, sites such as Facebook should consider reviewing whether certain types of information – date of birth, for example – should be made public at all, and whether it is feasible for sites to be modified to prohibit doing so.

Furthermore, we make a third recommendation to governments, other bodies concerned with protecting the public from crimes such as identity theft and cyberstalking, and designers and operators of social networking websites:

\- Third, this research identified that users’ knowledge of specific cases where personal information has been abused can influence users’ behaviour. Thus, in the same way that other public information campaigns have used real-life cases, we recommend that government and other organisations responsible for public information campaigns should consider communicating examples of identity theft and other, similar issues and specifically that they show that seemingly innocuous information such as date of birth is used in identity theft. Note that this could also be carried out on the social networking websites themselves.

It is the authors’ hope that by following these recommendations, the overall risk of identity-related crimes can be reduced.

## Limitations and future research

All studies have their limitations, and this one is no exception. First, it should be noted that this study limited itself to Facebook, which has a large share of the social networking ‘market’. However, extending this research to other social network systems would be valuable in order to determine whether the findings in this study can be applied more broadly, and to reveal differences (if any) between different communities.

Another limitation is the use of snowball sampling in Stage 2, because of the impossibility of obtaining Facebook’s user list in order to conduct random sampling. This has prevented the use of statistical techniques that require a probability sample. However, we believe that the survey sample was diverse enough to consider that it was not only students in the sample and gave good data to seed stage 3 of the research.

Similarly, the study was restricted to Australian users and should be replicated or otherwise extended into other national and cultural contexts, to determine if the findings can also be applied internationally.

Based on the data gathered, this study did not identify detailed concepts relating to trust, although trust clearly plays a part. Further work along the lines of Hardin (2002, 2004) may be fruitful in gaining a deeper understanding.

Further, this study did not identify or investigate individual attitudes toward risk, which may also affect users’ decisions to disclose information. Further investigation of the way different users’ attitudes toward risk affect their online information disclosure behaviour is clearly warranted in future work.
