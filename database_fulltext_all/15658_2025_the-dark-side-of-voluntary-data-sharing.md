# THE DARK SIDE OF VOLUNTARY DATA SHARING<sup>1</sup>

Xi Li Faculty of Business and Economics, HKU Business School, University of Hong Kong Hong Kong, CHINA {xili@hku.hk}

Bingqing Li Department of Marketing, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech Blacksburg, VA, U.S.A. {bingqing@vt.edu}

Zhilin Yang Department of Marketing, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong Hong Kong, CHINA {forrest.yang@cityu.edu.hk}

To balance the need for privacy and the benefits of big data analytics, regulators around the world are giving consumers control over their data, allowing them to choose whether or not to voluntarily share their purchase history data with firms. Intuition suggests that voluntary data sharing benefits consumers who can now choose to share their data only when it is profitable to do so. To investigate this argument, we built a model in which a monopolistic firm sells a repeatedly purchased product to consumers over two periods, and consumers decide whether or not to share their purchase history data with the firm, who can use it in the future to pricediscriminate against them. We found that, compared to when data collection is completely outlawed, voluntary data sharing can benefit the firm but at the consumer’s expense. Moreover, regulations that mandate firms to better protect consumer data against data breaches can backfire on consumers. Finally, we show that under voluntary data sharing, a firm’s ability to offer consumers a monetary incentive to share their data can improve profits without hurting consumers. Taken together, these findings underscore the surprising effects of voluntary data sharing and caution public policymakers of how certain data policies that, on the surface, seem purely beneficial can lead to unintended consequences.

Keywords: Voluntary data sharing, price discrimination, privacy, public policy, GDPR

## Introduction

## Motivation and Research Question

With the explosion of new information technologies, such as internet cookies, clickstream information, loyalty cards, automatic data-gathering devices, and facial recognition algorithms, firms across a wide range of industries collect massive amounts of data from their consumers. According to the 2021 Big Data and AI Executive Survey, 99% of Fortune 1000 companies are currently investing in big data and artificial intelligence initiatives, with 62% of them investing more than \$50 million in these assets (New Vantage Partners, 2021). As mathematician and entrepreneur Clive Humby stated: “Data is the new oil.”

But how do individuals feel about this hunt for data? According to a survey from the Pew Research Center, 91% of respondents believe they have lost control over how personal information is collected and used, 61% would like to do more to protect their privacy, and 66% feel that current laws and policies are insufficient in protecting their privacy (Rainie, 2018). After surveying 1,000 consumers, Anant et al. (2020) noted that “consumers are becoming increasingly intentional about what types of data they share with whom. They are far more likely to share personal data that are a necessary part of their interactions with organizations.” Massive data privacy scandals, such as the Facebook-Cambridge Analytica scandal, have only further raised consumers’ privacy concerns, placing pressure on legislators to strengthen state laws and give enforcers more power to penalize firms that exploit consumer data (Ng, 2019).

Tech firms and industrial experts, on the other hand, often criticize privacy protection, arguing that it stifles modern society’s workings, not to mention advancement, and may backfire on consumers. For instance, Huddleston (2021) argued that “stringent data privacy regulations have significant economic and other social costs that policymakers should carefully consider,” and that “privacy regulations can also negatively impact other benefits for society including free speech, consumer choice, and scientific research.” Clemens Otte, senior manager of digitalization with the Federation of German Industries (BDI), even suggested that Germany’s strict data-protection laws may be standing in the way of the country’s ability to seize digital opportunities. “We need less protection in general,” said Otte, “otherwise, we can’t innovate for the chances digitalization brings to us” (Belson, 2017). Jia et al. (2021, p. 661) also noted that “the services, tools, and products being made possible by the increased availability of data are bearing benefits for data subjects and data holders alike.” In an attempt to avoid such outcomes, U.S. tech giants such as Facebook and Google have spent millions of dollars lobbying against federal privacy laws (Feiner, 2021).

To balance the need for information privacy against the benefits of big data analytics, policymakers around the world are taking a mild approach to regulating firms: Instead of imposing a complete ban on data collection, they are requiring firms to offer individuals the choice of whether or not to voluntarily share their data with firms. In the European Union (EU), the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) states that firms must obtain informed consent from data subjects to collect and process their data. In addition, data subjects have the right to object to the processing of personal data and the right to have their personal data erased, known as the “right to be forgotten.” Likewise, in California, the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) provides residents with the right to have personal information collected from them deleted, and the U.S. Federal Trade Commission suggests that “best practices include ... giving consumers greater control over the collection and use of their personal data” (Federal Trade Commission, 2012). To comply with such regulations, most firms do not mandate that consumers share data to use their services, but instead offer benefits such as personalized prices, tailored product recommendations, and express checkout if they do.

At first glance, such regulations would seem to allow individuals to capitalize on big data technologies without sacrificing their privacy. As pointed out by Christopher Tonetti, associate professor of economics at the Stanford Graduate School of Business: “Giving consumers ownership of their data is better than simply outlawing the use of that data by firms—or giving people greater access to their data and control over its content—because it allows consumers to profit from their data and promotes the broad use of it” (Wall Street Journal, 2019). A recent study also found that the majority of consumers are willing to share their data for the right reasons (Global Data & Marketing Alliance, 2022). Intuition suggests that giving individuals control over their own data can only benefit them since, if data sharing truly hurts individuals, they now have the option to decline to do so. However, whether or not this “intuition” holds demands scrutiny.

## Model Description

For this study, we developed a game-theoretic model to investigate the consequences of voluntary data sharing. We considered a monopolistic firm selling a repeatedly purchased product to consumers over two periods. In the first period, each consumer decides how much to purchase from the firm and whether or not to share their purchase information at a privacy cost. We assumed that the consumer can purchase products in excess in the first period and carry them forward for consumption in the second period. If the consumer opts in to share their data (e.g., through joining the firm’s loyalty program), the firm will use it to pricediscriminate against them in the second period. If the consumer opts out, the firm cannot condition its secondperiod price on their first-period purchase history and will offer the consumer a non-discriminatory price. We examined equilibrium prices, firm profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare under voluntary data sharing and discuss how they are moderated by the consumer’s privacy cost.

## Main Findings

Based on the model characteristics, we make several noteworthy observations. First, we found that a consumer is willing to share their purchase data when the first-period price is moderate and the privacy costs are not too high. The intuition is that, by opting in to data sharing, the consumer gains the opportunity to purchase excessively in the first period and carry the products forward to the second period, thereby decreasing their second-period residual demand and forcing the firm to cut down its second-period price. The lowered second-period price benefits the consumer. If, however, the consumer opts out of data sharing, the second-period price will be independent of the consumer’s first-period purchase decision, and the opportunity to purchase excessively no longer exists.

Second, when the privacy cost is not too high, the firm raises its first-period price to exploit the consumer’s incentive to stockpile. Then, even though the consumer can enjoy a lower second-period price by purchasing excessively in the first period, this benefit is offset by the consumer’s loss via the high first-period price and the privacy cost; as a result, consumers are both individually and collectively worse off compared to if data collection were simply banned. However, despite it being ex ante optimal for the consumer to commit to not sharing their data, they cannot help but opt in ex post (i.e., after observing the first-period price), and the firm profits at the consumer’s expense. This finding unveils the dark side of voluntary data sharing.

Third, regulators can take measures to reduce the consumer’s privacy cost such as by mandating firms to better protect consumer data from breaches, which may appear to only benefit consumers; however, we found that such a reduction may actually backfire. The intuition is that, as the privacy cost becomes lower, the consumer becomes more willing to share their data. In this case, the firm can charge a higher first-period price and still expect the consumer to opt into data sharing, despite their resulting loss.

Finally, we considered a scenario in which the firm offers consumers incentives to share their data. We found that under voluntary data sharing, the firm’s capability to offer incentives improves its own profit without hurting consumers. In total, our results underscore the nontrivial effects of voluntary information sharing on firms, consumer surplus, and social welfare and offer public policymakers guidance on optimal ways to regulate data collection and use.

## Contributions and Implications

Our work contributes to the research on information privacy by examining the effect of voluntary profiling on firm profits and consumer welfare. We compared the equilibrium outcomes with and without voluntary profiling and found that contrary to conventional wisdom, which states that voluntary profiling benefits consumers, the opposite may be true when consumers are capable of stockpiling products. Specifically, voluntary profiling can lead to higher prices, which benefit firms at the consumer’s expense. This finding expands our understanding of information privacy by showing the downside of voluntary profiling.

Our research has important implications for both firms and public policymakers. Our findings first exemplify how firms can improve their profits simply by offering consumers options to share their purchase data and using that collected data to price-discriminate. Additionally, when consumers privacy costs are high, firms should take measures that reduce those costs and provide monetary incentives to encourage consumers to share their data. Our findings also inform public policymakers that voluntary data sharing does not necessarily benefit consumers. Moreover, mandating that firms enhance their data security may lead to higher prices and a lower consumer surplus. These effects must be taken into consideration when regulating data collection and sharing.

## Related Literature

Our research contributes to the vast literature on information privacy (Bélanger & Crossler, 2011; Lee et al., 2011; Pavlou, 2011; Reeck et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2011). Specifically, we consider endogenous privacy, a concept that allows individuals to decide how much private information to reveal to firms. Hann et al. (2008) investigated consumers’ efforts to conceal themselves to deflect marketing and found that concealment by low-benefit consumers may increase the efficiency of solicitations and induce firms to engage in more marketing. Chen and Stallaert (2014) studied the effect of behavioral targeting on firm profits and consumer welfare, showing that consumers are (weakly) better off when given the choice of opting out of or opting in to behavioral targeting. Casadesus-Masanell and Hervas-Drane (2015) considered a monopolist using imperfect technology to identify consumers and showed that the consumer’s ability to withhold or hide their information may reduce the consumer surplus. Montes et al. (2019) investigated a model in which competing firms use consumers’ private information to price-discriminate but allowed consumers to pay a privacy cost to avoid being identified. They found that competing duopolists do not always benefit from higher privacy costs and determined that it is optimal for data vendors to sell to one firm only. Ichihashi (2020) studied the trade-offs of consumer information disclosure: While more disclosure allows consumers to receive better recommendations, it also puts them at risk of price discrimination. However, Ichihashi showed that to encourage consumer disclosure, firms should commit to not using the information in making its pricing decisions. Empirically, Goh et al. (2015) showed that consumer actions to avoid advertising exert externalities on other consumers. Burtch et al. (2016) examined consumers’ incentives to hide their own information in online crowdfunding. Koh et al. (2020) found that consumers with low privacy costs are likely to voluntarily disclose their information in exchange for monetary benefits. Our research contributes to this literature by investigating consumers’ voluntary sharing of their purchase histories and demonstrates that voluntary data sharing can benefit firms at the consumer’s expense when firms can use the data for price discrimination.

Conitzer et al. (2012) and Koh et al. (2017) are closely related to our study, but we still note distinct differences. To begin, Conitzer et al. considered a model in which a firm pricediscriminates against consumers based on their purchase history and consumers could delete their purchase histories at a cost (e.g., purchasing a VPN to hide their identity). While our study assumes that the consumer does not incur any cost to conceal their purchase history, the consumer will incur a privacy cost by sharing their information with the firm. In addition, our study assumes that demand is continuous, and the consumer can stockpile for future consumption. Given these differences in models, our study’s results are contrary to those of Conitzer et al. Whereas Conitzer et al. suggest that if the cost to hide from firms is zero, consumers will always choose to hide, in our model, the consumer chooses to share their data even under positive privacy cost conditions.

Koh et al. (2017) considered a model of voluntary profiling and showed that this strategy does not necessarily improve consumer welfare. Their underlying mechanism is that voluntary profiling allows firms to identify high-valuation consumers who have no incentive to participate and then set a higher price for them. In other words, a consumer’s nonparticipation decision signals their valuation to the firm, allowing the firm to price-discriminate against them. Our model, by contrast, assumes that all consumers have the same valuation; thus, the above signaling effect does not exist. As such, our mechanism is completely different from theirs.

In our model, the firm can condition its price on the consumer’s previous purchase history, a practice known as behavior-based pricing (BBP). A series of research efforts have shown that BBP works to the detriment of a firm, be it in a monopoly or duopoly (Acquisti & Varian, 2005; Fudenberg & Tirole, 2000). Nonetheless, when consumers cannot observe whether or not the firm practices BBP, the firm will do so, as long as the implementation cost is low (Li et al., 2020). Mehra et al. (2012) investigated behavior-based pricing of software updates using a model involving an incumbent and an entrant. They found that in equilibrium, the incumbent loses market share to the entrant and that the profits of both firms may decrease due to consumers’ switching costs.

While our model also allows a firm to condition its secondperiod price based on the consumer’s first-period purchase history, we extend this literature from two perspectives. First, we allow consumers to decide whether or not to share their purchase data with the firm through a privacy cost. Second, we allow the consumers to stockpile and carry unused products forward for consumption in the future. These decisions allowed us to obtain new insights: While the literature has found that a firm’s ability to collect and use consumers’ purchase histories hurts the firm while benefiting consumers, our model shows that this can improve firm profits at the consumer’s expense. This finding helps rationalize why, in practice, firms are willing to invest in the collection of consumers’ purchase history data (Acquisti & Varian, 2005, p. 373).

Finally, our study contributes to the ongoing debates surrounding the repercussions of privacy regulations. While previous studies have shown that the GDPR and other privacy regulations have effectively reduced the collection and use of consumer data (Johnson et al., 2023; Peukert et al., 2022), it remains unclear whether such regulations truly benefit consumers. On the positive side, Argenziano and Bonatti (2021) endogenized the data linkage formation between firms under various privacy regulations and found that voluntary consent requirements are beneficial to consumers in equilibrium. Furthermore, Godinho de Matos and Adjerid (2022) showed that enhanced consent is effective in increasing privacy protection. On the negative side, Ke and Sudhir (2023) discovered that data security mandates can hurt consumers, and Zhao et al. (2023) found that privacy protection reduces consumers’ search effectiveness. We contribute to this debate by documenting an unintended negative consequence of voluntary data sharing, a now commonly adopted privacy regulation: Under voluntary data sharing, consumers have an incentive to share their purchase quantity data with the firm, hoping to obtain better deals in the future. Anticipating this, the firm can exploit consumers by increasing its current period price, which benefits the firm at the consumer’s expense. Our research cautions public policymakers about this unexpected effect of regulating data collection and sharing.

Note that in our model, all consumers have the same valuation of and demand for the product. This condition differs from the existing BBP literature, which typically assumes that consumers are heterogeneous in their valuations and that their first-period purchase decisions reveal their specific valuation to the firm. In this regard, standard rationales for data collection do not exist in our model (Acquisti & Varian, 2005). Moreover, because the firm does not infer the consumer type using their data-sharing decisions, we rule out the canonical argument for the downside of data sharing, which states that consumers’ data-sharing decisions signal their valuations to the firm (Koh et al., 2017). Nonetheless, we show that when the firm observes its consumers’ purchase histories, consumers’ first-period purchase decisions will still affect the firm’s second-period prices, albeit through different channels.

Voluntary data sharing: This study focuses on voluntary data sharing, assuming that a consumer can decide whether or not to share their purchase history for the firm to collect, store, and use. Here, “purchase history” refers not only to whether or not a consumer makes a purchase but also to the quantity the consumer purchases.

While some may argue that firms always have the power to observe their consumers’ transaction details, in certain scenarios, this decision can be made by the consumers themselves. For instance, in an offline setting, consumers can make a direct purchase without revealing their identity. Conversely, they can, for example, sign up for a membership account and join the firm’s loyalty program, which effectively reveals their information to the firm. In an online setting, if the firm tracks consumers’ data through their IP addresses, consumers can employ a VPN or a proxy or switch to a different device to avoid being tracked. If the firm tracks through cookies, consumers can erase or block their browser cookies. If the firm tracks through account information, consumers can remain anonymous by creating a new account, maintaining multiple online identities, and/or paying with a different or virtual credit card.

It is worth noting that under the GDPR, consumers can also ask the firm to erase their transaction records or, at the very least, request that they not be used for marketing purposes. On the right to be forgotten, Article 16 of the GDPR states: “The data subject shall have the right to obtain from the controller the erasure of personal data concerning him or her without undue delay and the controller shall have the obligation to erase personal data without undue delay.” Note that purchase history is considered to be personal data: According to the European Commission (2016), “[personal data] includes all data observed about the data subject during the activities for the purpose of which the data are collected, such as a transaction history or access log.” Article 6(1)(a) of the GDPR also states that data processing used for price discrimination is only lawful when consumer consent is given; without consent, the firm may store consumers’ purchase data but cannot use it for price discrimination. In line with these regulations, we allow the consumer to decide whether or not to share their purchase history with the firm—which will then use this data to price-discriminate against them.<sup>2</sup> If the consumer opts in, the firm can condition its second-period price offer using their first-period purchase history; otherwise, it offers the consumer a non-discriminatory second-period price that is independent of their purchase history.

Privacy cost: We assume that consumers are concerned about their privacy. Thus, in sharing their data with the firm, the consumer incurs a privacy cost of ?? ≥ 0. Such costs include the burden of receiving unwanted marketing promotions (Hann et al., 2008; Gal-Or et al., 2018), the risks of data leakage and breaches (Milne & Rohm, 2000), the hassle of signing up for an account or a membership (Krafft et al., 2017), and the complexity and effort of managing privacy (Lee et al., 2021). The privacy cost does not include potential loss (or gain) from the firm’s price-discriminatory practices (Koh et al., 2017), which are explicitly modeled in the paper. While we assume that the privacy cost is exogenously given, our main results continue to hold when the firm endogenously determines its privacy cost.

Certain works in the literature assume that consumers do not incur a privacy cost when sharing their data but may have to accept a concealing cost when opting out of data collection (Conitzer et al., 2012; Montes et al., 2019). For instance, consumers incur the cost of purchasing a VPN to remain anonymous to the firm. Moreover, when using guest checkout, which limits the data that the firm can collect, consumers must accept the hassle of entering their billing and shipping information at the time of purchase. However, our results are strengthened under the assumption of a concealing cost: that is, such a cost allows the firm to more easily obtain consumer data, which will further improve its profits at the consumer’s expense.

Solving the consumer’s problem yields:

$$
Q _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} 2 - 2 p _ {1}, & \text {if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1 - \tilde {I}}{2}, \\ 1 - p _ {1}, & \text {if} \frac {1 - \tilde {I}}{2} <   p _ {1} \leq 1, \\ [ 1 - p _ {1}, 2 - 2 p _ {1} ], & \text {if} p _ {1} = \frac {1 - \tilde {I}}{2}. \end{array} \right.\tag{6}
$$

In Equation (6), when $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } < \frac { 1 - \tilde { I } } { 2 } , } \end{array}$ the consumer takes advantage of the low first-period price to build up their stockpile. When $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 - \tilde { I } } { 2 } < p _ { 1 } \leq 1 } \end{array}$ , the consumer does not seek to stockpile. Finally, when $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } = \frac { 1 - \tilde { I } } { 2 } } \end{array}$ , the consumer is indifferent about purchasing any quantity within the closed interval $[ 1 - p _ { 1 } , 2 - 2 p _ { 1 } ]$

Now, we solve for the firm’s belief in the subgame. In equilibrium, the firm’s belief must be fulfilled $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , I = \tilde { I } )$ . Solving for ?? and ??̃, we obtain:

$$
I = \tilde {I} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} 0, & \mathrm{if} \frac {1}{2} \leq p _ {1} \leq 1, \\ 1 - 2 p _ {1}, & \mathrm{if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1}{2}. \end{array} \right.\tag{7}
$$

Consistent with intuition, when the first-period price is high, the consumer will not stockpile any products and will wait for a lower second-period price. When the first-period price is low, however, the consumer is willing to purchase in excess in the first period and carry their extra products forward into the second period.

In this benchmark, the consumer does not need to make a datasharing decision. Therefore, we directly move to the first stage in which the firm chooses a first-period price $p _ { 1 } \leq 1$ to maximize its total profit from both periods:

$$
\pi = Q _ {1} p _ {1} + \pi_ {2}.
$$

Simple algebra yields that

$$
\pi = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{4} + p _ {1} - p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} \leq p _ {1} \leq 1, \\ 2 p _ {1} - 2 p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1}{2}. \end{array} \right.\tag{8}
$$

As discussed above, the firm either charges a low price $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } < \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ to induce consumer stockpiling or charges a high price $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 } { 2 } \leq p _ { 1 } \leq } \end{array}$ 1 to prevent consumer stockpiling. The following lemma summarizes the equilibrium outcome.

Lemma 1: Considering the no-data-collection benchmark, equilibrium prices are $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } = p _ { 2 } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ and consumers do not stockpile in equilibrium. The firm’s profit is $\textstyle \pi = { \frac { 1 } { 2 } }$ and the consumer surplus is $\begin{array} { r } { C S = \frac { 1 } { 4 } } \end{array}$

The intuition is as follows. As analyzed above, consumers are willing to stockpile only when they expect a price rise in the future (i.e., when the firm charges a low first-period price $p _ { 1 } )$ The firm, by contrast, does not have an incentive to distort its first-period price downward, which would hurt its first-period margin. In equilibrium, the firm charges a static price $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } . } \end{array}$ Given this price, consumers do not stockpile; therefore, the firm again charges a static price $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 2 } = \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ in the second period. As a result, the equilibrium outcome is a simple repetition of the static outcome.

## Model Analysis

We now analyze the main model, in which the consumer has control over their data and decides whether or not to share it with the firm. For ease of exposition, we assume that in the second stage of the first period, the consumer first makes their data-sharing decision and then their first-period purchase decision (as opposed to making the two decisions simultaneously). We show in the Appendix that this change does not affect the equilibrium outcome.

Again, we used backward induction to find the subgame-perfect equilibrium. We consider two subgames that begin with consumers’ data-sharing decisions.

## The Consumer Does Not Share Data with the Firm

First, considering the case in which the consumer, upon observing $p _ { 1 }$ , chooses not to share their data, the analysis is similar to that of the no-data-collection benchmark, and we obtain the firm’s profit and consumer surplus as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \pi = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {1}{4} + p _ {1} - p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} \leq p _ {1} \leq 1, \\ 2 p _ {1} - 2 p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1}{2}. \end{array} \right., \\ C S = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {5}{8} - p _ {1} + \frac {p _ {1} ^ {2}}{2}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} \leq p _ {1} \leq 1, \\ (1 - p _ {1}) ^ {2}, & \text {if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1}{2}. \end{array} \right. \end{array}
$$

## The Consumer Shares Data with the Firm

Now, we consider an alternative scenario in which the consumer decides to share their data with the firm. In this case, the firm observes the consumer’s first-period purchase decision $Q _ { 1 }$ and can condition its second-period price offer on it. In this sense, there is another subgame that begins with the consumer’s decision on $Q _ { 1 }$

Again, we use ??̃ to denote the firm’s belief about consumer inventory, which is dependent on $Q _ { 1 }$ (this differs from the nodata-sharing case in which ??̃ does not depend on $Q _ { 1 } )$ . Following the analysis of the previous section, the second-period equilibrium outcome is:

$$
p _ {2} = \frac {1 - \tilde {I}}{2}, \pi_ {2} = \frac {(1 - \tilde {I}) ^ {2}}{4}, C S _ {2} = \frac {(1 + \tilde {I}) ^ {2} + 4 (1 - \tilde {I}) I}{8}.
$$

Now, considering the first period, after choosing $Q _ { 1 }$ , the consumer chooses $Q _ { c }$ to maximize their residual payoff:

$$
C S ^ {\prime} = \mathcal {U} _ {1} (Q _ {c}) + C S _ {2} (I),\tag{9}
$$

subject to $0 \leq Q _ { c } \leq Q _ { 1 }$ , where $\mathcal { U } _ { 1 } ( Q _ { c } )$ is the consumer’s payoff from consuming $Q _ { c }$ units in the first period and $I = Q _ { 1 } -$ $Q _ { c }$ in $C S _ { 2 }$ . Solving the consumer’s optimization problem yields:

$$
(Q _ {c}, I) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \left(\frac {1 + \tilde {I}}{2}, Q _ {1} - \frac {1 + \tilde {I}}{2}\right), & \text {if} \frac {1 + \tilde {I}}{2} <   Q _ {1}, \\ (Q _ {1}, 0), & \text {if} Q _ {1} \leq \frac {1 + \tilde {I}}{2}. \end{array} \right.\tag{10}
$$

Now we solve for the equilibrium outcome of the subgame starting from the choice of $Q _ { 1 }$ . The firm’s belief must be fulfilled $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , I = \tilde { I } )$ . Solving for ?? and ??̃, we come up with:

$$
I = \tilde {I} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \frac {2 Q _ {1} - 1}{3}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} <   Q _ {1}, \\ 0, & \text {if} Q _ {1} \leq \frac {1}{2}. \end{array} \right.\tag{11}
$$

Unlike the no-data-sharing case, now the firm’s belief, ${ \tilde { I } } ,$ is a function of $Q _ { 1 }$ so long as $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { 2 } } < Q _ { 1 }$ . Accordingly, we derive:

$$
p _ {2} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} \frac {2 - Q _ {1}}{3}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} <   Q _ {1}, \\ \frac {1}{2}, & \text {if} Q _ {1} \leq \frac {1}{2}, \end{array} \right.\tag{12}
$$

which is also a function of $Q _ { 1 }$ , implying that the firm incorporates the consumer’s first-period purchase decision into its second-period pricing decision and reduces the secondperiod price as the consumer purchases more in the first period.

This result starkly contrasts with extant BBP literature: Under BBP, the firm charges new consumers a lower price and existing consumers a higher price in the second period (Acquisti & Varian, 2005). Put differently, the second-period price increases with the consumer’s first-period purchase. Equation (12), by contrast, states that the second-period price decreases with the consumer’s first-period purchase, meaning the firm rewards, as opposed to punishes, consumers who purchase aggressively in the first period.

The rationale is as follows. In our model, a high $Q _ { 1 }$ reveals to the firm that the consumer has a high inventory and hence a lower second-period residual demand, which forces the firm to charge the consumer a lower second-period price.

Next, we consider the consumer’s first-period purchase decision: Given the first-period price $p _ { 1 } \left( p _ { 1 } \leq 1 \right)$ , the consumer chooses $Q _ { 1 }$ to maximize their total payoff over both periods, $C S = - c - Q _ { 1 } p _ { 1 } + C S ^ { \prime }$ . Solving the consumer’s problem, we get the consumer’s optimal first-period purchase decision when sharing their data with the firm:

$$
Q _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 2 - \frac {9 p _ {1}}{4}, & \text {if} p _ {1} \leq \frac {3}{5}, \\ 1 - p _ {1}, & \text {if} \frac {3}{5} <   p _ {1} \leq 1. \end{array} \right.
$$

Note that here the consumer has already decided to share their data with the firm. As such, ?? is a sunk cost and does not affect the consumer’s purchase decision. The consumer surplus is:

$$
C S = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - c - 2 p _ {1} + \frac {9 p _ {1} ^ {2}}{8}, & \text {if} p _ {1} \leq \frac {3}{5}, \\ \frac {5}{8} - c - p _ {1} + \frac {p _ {1} ^ {2}}{2}, & \text {if} \frac {3}{5} <   p _ {1} \leq 1. \end{array} \right.
$$

The firm’s profit is:

$$
\pi = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 2 p _ {1} - \frac {2 7}{1 6} p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} p _ {1} \leq \frac {3}{5}, \\ \frac {1}{4} + p _ {1} - p _ {1} ^ {2}, & \text {if} \frac {3}{5} <   p _ {1} \leq 1. \end{array} \right.
$$

Note that consumers stockpile whenever $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } < \frac { 3 } { 5 } . } \end{array}$ This result is in stark contrast to that in the no-data-sharing case in which consumers only stockpile when $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } < \frac { 1 } { 2 } } \end{array}$ . Put differently, consumers are more willing to stockpile when the firm observes their purchase data. This result arises because when the firm does not observe $Q _ { 1 }$ , the second-period price $p _ { 2 }$ does not depend on $Q _ { 1 }$ either. When the firm observes $Q _ { 1 }$ , however, the second-period price decreases with $Q _ { 1 }$ , as shown in Equation (12). As such, consumers have a stronger incentive to purchase in excess in the first period and carry inventory forward into the second period to obtain a lower second-period price.

## The Consumer’s Data-Sharing Decision

Next, we compare the consumer’s payoff when the consumer does versus does not share their data with the firm and arrive at the following lemma.

Lemma 2: When $p _ { 1 } < \frac { 3 } { 5 }$ , there exists $\bar { c } > 0$ , such that when $c <$ $\bar { c } ,$ consumer opts in to data sharing, where

$$
\bar {c} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \frac {p _ {1} ^ {2}}{8}, & \text {if} p _ {1} <   \frac {1}{2}, \\ \frac {(1 - p _ {1}) (3 - 5 p _ {1})}{8}, & \text {if} \frac {1}{2} \leq p _ {1} \leq \frac {3}{5}. \end{array} \right.
$$

Lemma 2 illustrates that the consumer is willing to share their data, provided that the first-period price and privacy cost are not too high. The threshold for data sharing, ??̅ , can be interpreted as the benefit of data sharing, and the consumer becomes willing to share their data when this benefit exceeds their privacy cost. Figure 1 illustrates how the benefit of data sharing changes with $p _ { 1 }$

The benefit of data sharing increases with $p _ { 1 }$ when $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } \leq \frac { 1 } { 2 } . } \end{array}$ As discussed above, the consumer benefits from data sharing as data sharing offers the consumer an option to purchase aggressively in the first period, thereby forcing the firm to cut down on its second-period price and benefiting the consumer. When $p _ { 1 }$ is already low, the consumer can directly satisfy their second-period demand via advance-buying at a low first-period price as opposed to waiting for a low $p _ { 2 }$ . In this case, the consumer’s second-period residual demand is low, so they care less about the second-period price; accordingly, the benefit of data sharing is also low. In the extreme case of $p _ { 1 } = 0$ , the consumer shifts their entire second-period demand to the first period by stockpiling ?? = 1 unit; as such, the consumer’s second-period residual demand is 0, and the value of data sharing vanishes. As $p _ { 1 }$ grows, a lower second-period price becomes more critical, and the benefit of data sharing increases.

When $\begin{array} { r } { \frac { 1 } { 2 } \leq p _ { 1 } \leq \frac { 3 } { 5 } , } \end{array}$ the consumer who decides to share their data can, once again, purchase aggressively in the first period to secure a low second-period price. $\mathsf { A s } \ p _ { 1 }$ increases, it becomes increasingly costly for the consumer to purchase aggressively in the first period; accordingly, the consumer cuts down on their first-period purchase. The firm observes this information through the consumer’s data and raises the second-period price for them, effectively diminishing the benefit of data sharing. Finally, when $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { 1 } = \frac { 3 } { 5 } , } \end{array}$ the high firstperiod price prevents the consumer from purchasing excessively in the first period, and they do not carry any extra inventory; then, since the consumer cannot benefit from a lower second-period price even if they decide to share their data, the benefit of data sharing becomes nil.

These results suggest that the firm can use its first-period price to sway consumers’ data-sharing decisions. By charging consumers a moderate first-period price, the firm raises the benefit of data sharing and induces consumers to voluntarily reveal their information.

## The Firm’s First-Period Pricing Decision

Next, we investigate the firm’s first-period pricing decision. The firm may charge a price $p _ { 1 } \leq 1$ that either induces or discourages the consumer from sharing their data. Comparing firm profits under these two scenarios, we come up with the following proposition. Proposition 1: The firm’s first-period pricing decision is as follows:

$$
p _ {1} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \frac {1 6}{2 7}, & \text {if} c \leq c _ {1} = \frac {1 1}{5 8 3 2}, \\ \frac {1}{5} (4 - \sqrt {1 + 4 0 c}), & \text {if} c _ {1} \leq c \leq c _ {2} = \frac {1}{3 2}, \\ \frac {1}{2}, & \text {otherwise.} \end{array} \right.
$$

When $c \leq c _ { 2 } ,$ consumers share their data with the firm. When $c > c _ { 2 } ,$ , consumers do not share their data.

Figure 2 illustrates the result of Proposition 1. As can be seen, the firm’s pricing decision hinges on the consumer’s privacy cost. When the privacy cost is low $( c \leq c _ { 1 } )$ , the firm charges the consumer a high first-period price to enjoy a high first-period margin. Despite this high price, the consumer is still willing to purchase aggressively from the firm to build up their stockpile, which helps the consumer secure a lower second-period price.

When $c _ { 1 } < c < c _ { 2 }$ , the elevated privacy cost discourages the consumer from sharing their data. As such, the firm lowers its first-period price to induce the consumer to purchase aggressively and build up their stockpile. Therefore, $p _ { 1 }$ decreases with ??.

## Managerial and Policy Implications

At first glance, voluntary data sharing appears to work to the consumers’ benefit: When there are benefits to be gleaned, consumers can opt in to data sharing and otherwise opt out and be left unaffected. In either case, consumers would seem to be weakly better off with voluntary data sharing.

Our analysis suggests, however, that this intuition does not necessarily hold. Most notably, we found that when the privacy cost is not too high, voluntary data sharing benefits the firm at the consumer’s expense. The intuition is that, though ex post consumers are indeed better off with voluntary data sharing, they are individually and collectively worse off from an ex ante standpoint since the firm raises its first-period price to exploit the stockpiling incentive, which significantly harms consumers’ first-period payoff. The firm, by contrast, benefits from both the high margin and high sales earned in the first period as a result of voluntary data sharing.

Our results offer important considerations for firms and public policymakers. For firms, our results show that simply introducing voluntary data sharing can significantly improve profits. For instance, when ?? = 0, voluntary data sharing leads to an 18.5% improvement in firm profits compared to what is earned under the no-data-sharing benchmark.

Moreover, it follows immediately from the equilibrium outcome that firm profit weakly decreases with ??. Therefore, the firm should take measures to reduce consumers’ privacy costs (e.g., enhancing data security, committing to not sending ads to consumers), which will reduce the expenses associated with sharing data and can further raise the firm’s first-period price, allowing the firm to benefit from increased margin.

As for policymakers, our findings caution that voluntary data sharing can hurt consumers and that proactive stance must thus be taken on regulating it. In certain scenarios, banning data collection altogether may benefit consumers more than voluntary data sharing.

In addition to regulating firms’ data collection and usage, many regulators are mandating that firms better protect consumer data against breaches and leaks. For instance, under the GDPR, data controllers are now required to take appropriate technical and organizational measures, such as using pseudonymization and encryption, to protect data. Moreover, data controllers must report any personal data breach to the supervisory authority and address the breach within 72 hours. The Australia Privacy Amendment (Notifiable Data Breaches) Act 2017 goes a step further by requiring firms to take remedial actions following a data breach. Such data-protection regulations alleviate the risk of data breaches and mitigate risks if they occur, but do they only benefit consumers?

In our model, enhanced data protection can be reflected by a reduction in consumers’ privacy cost ??. When ?? is low enough $( \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , c \leq c _ { 1 } )$ , better data protection does benefit consumers, thanks to the reduced privacy cost—this result is consistent with our expectations. However, when $c _ { 1 } < c < c _ { 2 }$ , the consumer surplus increases with ??, suggesting that enhanced data protection can actually backfire on consumers. This may happen because when the privacy cost decreases, consumers are strongly motivated to share their data in exchange for a better second-period price. Knowing this, the firm can take advantage of the consumer by raising its first-period price and still expect the consumer to participate in data sharing. In other words, better protection (i.e., lowered privacy costs) induces more data sharing, which can be exploited by firms. In much the same way, social welfare may also decrease with better data protection. These results collectively suggest that data protection can lead to unintended consequences.

## Endogenous Privacy Cost

Thus far, we have assumed that consumers’ privacy cost, ??, is exogenously given. However, it is plausible that the firm could, at times, influence the privacy cost (e.g., enhancing data security, committing to not sending ads to consumers). We thus ask: What is the equilibrium outcome when the firm has this control over the privacy cost?

To address this issue, we added a new stage to the beginning of the game during which the firm chooses its privacy cost, ??, which is observed by consumers. The rest of the model remained unchanged. It follows immediately that the firm’s profit is maximized at a low privacy cost $c \leq c _ { 1 }$ (the firm is indifferent about choosing any $0 \leq c \leq c _ { 1 } ) .$ , which allows it to charge a high first-period price $\textstyle p _ { 1 } = { \frac { 1 6 } { 2 7 } }$ . At this privacy cost, the firm’s profit is $\textstyle \Pi \ = \ { \frac { 1 6 } { 2 7 } }$ , higher than in the no-datacollection benchmark, while the consumer surplus is $C S =$ $\textstyle { \frac { 1 7 } { 8 1 } } - c$ , lower than in the no-data-collection benchmark. As such, our main finding continues to hold under an endogenous privacy cost in that voluntary data sharing continues to benefit the firm at the consumer’s expense.

## Incentives for Data Sharing

In the basic model, the firm induces data sharing only through its first-period price. In practice, however, firms often use other means to induce voluntary data sharing (Koh et al., 2020). For example, firms often offer consumers a bonus for signing up for loyalty programs. A survey by Insights Network found that 79% of consumers said they want to be compensated when their data is shared (MarTech Series, 2018). In general, it is legitimate for firms to offer consumers benefits in exchange for their data. For instance, according to CCPA: “Businesses can also offer you promotions, discounts, and other deals in exchange for collecting, keeping, or selling your personal information.”

Given this common business strategy, we extended our basic model to allow the firm to offer consumers incentives in exchange for their data. We revised the model in the following way: In the first period, the firm offers consumers a first-period price $p _ { 1 }$ as well as a data-sharing incentive $B \geq 0 ( \mathrm { e . g . }$ ., a signup bonus). A consumer then decides how much to purchase from the firm and whether or not to share their purchase history in exchange for the bonus. If the consumer opts in to share their data, the consumer pays the privacy cost ?? but enjoys the bonus ??. The remainder of the model remained unchanged.

We solve for the equilibrium outcome and summarize the result in the following proposition:

Proposition 3 Assuming that the firm can offer consumers an incentive for data sharing, in equilibrium, the firm’s first-period strategy is:

$$
(p _ {1}, B) = \left\{ \begin{array}{c l} \left(\frac {1 6}{2 7}, 0\right), & \text {if} c \leq c _ {1} = \frac {1 1}{5 8 3 2}, \\ \left(\frac {1}{5} (4 - \sqrt {1 + 4 0 c}), 0\right), & \text {if} c _ {1} \leq c \leq c _ {2} = \frac {1}{3 2}, \\ \left(\frac {1}{2}, c - \frac {1}{3 2}\right), & \text {if} c _ {2} \leq c \leq c _ {3} = \frac {7}{6 4}, \\ \left(\frac {1}{2}, 0\right), & \text {otherwise}. \end{array} \right.
$$

Consumers share their data when $c \leq c _ { 3 }$ , and do not share otherwise.

The above equilibrium outcome differs from that of the basic model (see Proposition 1) only when $\begin{array} { r } { c _ { 2 } < c < c _ { 3 } : } \end{array}$ If the firm cannot offer the consumer an incentive, the consumer opts out of data sharing; in the same regard, if the firm can offer an incentive, the consumer opts in. The reason is as follows: When $c _ { 2 } < c \leq c _ { 3 }$ , the privacy cost is so high that, regardless of the first-period price $p _ { 1 }$ , the consumer is reluctant to share their data with the firm unless they receive something in return. The firm prefers the consumer to share their data so that it can induce the stockpiling effect; as a result, the firm becomes more willing to offer an incentive to the consumer.

When $c > c _ { 3 }$ , the privacy cost is so high that the firm must offer the consumer a significant bonus to incentivize them to share their data. Ultimately, the cost of the bonus becomes so high that it offsets the benefit of the shared data. As a result, the firm forgoes incentivizing the consumer, who then does not share their data.

Next, we analyze the effect of the incentive on firm profits, consumer surplus, and social welfare. The following proposition summarizes our results.

Proposition 4 When the firm offers consumers an incentive for data sharing, both firm profits and social welfare are higher than if the firm had not offered the incentive. Meanwhile, the consumer surplus is left unaffected.

While there are concerns that “[allowing individuals to sell their data] would entice [them] to give up more of their privacy without adequate reward and would stymie the flow of information” (Wall Street Journal, 2019), Proposition 4 suggests that incentives for data sharing are a tool that improves firm profit and social welfare without hurting consumers. This result is illustrated in Figure 6. The intuition is that the incentive and the lower second-period price cover the privacy cost, leaving the consumer indifferent about sharing their data (and the consumer shares their data). Meanwhile, the firm benefits from the stockpiling effect induced by the consumer sharing their data. Social welfare is also higher because the second-period price becomes lower and the market becomes more efficient.

The above result suggests that firms should consider offering consumers monetary incentives in exchange for sharing their data when the privacy cost is high. Public policymakers should also encourage firms to offer such incentives, which can improve social welfare without hurting consumers.

## Conclusion

In this paper, we develop a model in which a firm sells a repeatedly purchased good to consumers over two periods, and consumers can decide whether or not to share their purchase history data with the firm. If a consumer chooses to share, the firm can condition its second-period price on the consumer’s first-period purchase history; otherwise, the firm cannot use the consumer’s purchase history data and must offer them a non-discriminatory price in the second period.

Our main finding is that compared to the case in which data collection is outlawed or infeasible, voluntary data sharing can benefit a firm—but primarily at the consumer’s expense. This main finding suggests that firms should provide consumers with the option to voluntarily share their data while informing them that their data will be used for price discrimination. Our results also caution public policymakers that voluntary data sharing does not necessarily benefit but may in fact hurt consumers and that the negative effects of voluntary data sharing must be taken into consideration when regulating the collection and use of consumer data.

Figure 6. Data Sharing Incentives and Firm Profits

Furthermore, we found that better data protection that decreases the privacy cost can again benefit firms but hurt consumers. This result is in stark contrast to common wisdom, which suggests that better data protection would only benefit consumers. Given this finding, we recommend that firms take measures to enhance their data protection, which will significantly improve their profits. Simultaneously, we remind policymakers that regulations that mandate that firms better protect consumer data can lead to unintended consequences and even harm consumers.

Finally, we explored a scenario in which the firm offers consumers incentives in exchange for their data. We found that, under voluntary data sharing, such incentives can improve firm profits and social welfare without harming consumers, highlighting the upside of this practice. As such, we recommend that firms provide consumers with incentives that induce their data sharing behavior and suggest that public policies should be developed to encourage firms to adopt this practice.

Our work has a few limitations. First, we assume that consumers are homogeneous and consider representative consumers in the model. Future research could relax this assumption and analyze a game with heterogeneous consumers. Second, we assume that the firm uses consumer data for price discrimination only; however, in practice, the firm may also perform other services such as product recommendation and customization using the collected information. Future research could investigate how other uses of consumer data would affect the equilibrium outcome. Lastly, in our current model, consumers are rational and seek to maximize their expected payoff. In reality, consumers are often subjected to psychological effects such as fairness concerns (Li & Li, 2023b); as such, it would be helpful to investigate the equilibrium when consumers are subject to behavioral biases.
