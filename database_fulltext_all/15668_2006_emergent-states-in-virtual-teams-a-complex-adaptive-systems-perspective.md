## Abstract

Research on virtual teams (VTs) has proliferated in the last decades. However, few clear and consistent theoretical attempts to integrate the literature on VTs in a systemic way have emerged. This paper uses the complex adaptive systems (CAS) perspective to integrate the literature on emergent states in VTs. According to this general framework, VT effectiveness depends on the interaction between three levels of dynamics: local, global and contextual. Team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict are described as states that emerge from the interactions among the VT members and as parts of global dynamics, they impact on VT effectiveness, and in the same time they are influenced by the outcomes of the VT. The insights on this bidirectional causality as well as other benefits of using the CAS framework to improve our understanding of VTs are discussed in the paper. It also provides an overview of artificial simulation models as well as simulation results concerning the emergence of the four states described in the CAS framework and discusses severa ways to improve the accuracy of the simulation models using empirical data collected in real VTs.

## Introduction

O <sup>wing</sup> <sup>to</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>competitive</sup> <sup>pressures</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>globalization</sup> <sup>of</sup>the marketplace, contemporary organizations are the marketplace, contemporary organizations are forced to find more flexible and versatile structures in order to adapt and be (stay) competitive (Finnegan and O’Mahony, 1996; Townsend et al., 1998). Virtual ways of organizing (Schultze and Orlikowski, 2001) and in particular virtual teams (VTs) (Breu and Hemingway, 2004) are among the best means to achieve this flexibility and versatility, first, because they allow the coordination of work across geographical and time zones, and second, because they are strongly supported by the innovative communication solutions offered by the fast technological development (Lipnack and Stamps, 1997; Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Kirkman et al., 2004; Hertel et al., 2005). The use of information technology (IT) in contemporary organizations is traditionally considered a strategic advantage (Powell and Dent-Mitcallef, 1997; Corea, 2006), an enabler for knowledge sharing (Huysman and Wulf, 2005) and innovation (Jeyaraj et al., 2006). However, the literature on VTs acknowledges both positive and negative influences of IT on team dynamics and effectiveness (Jarvenpaa and

Leidner, 1999; Yoo and Alavi, 2001; Martins et al., 2004; Hinds and Mortensen, 2005).

Although the literature on the impact of IT on team dynamics and especially on the emergence of team level phenomena is growing, no systematic attempt has been made to integrate it using the complex adaptive systems (CAS) framework. Previous attempts (Furst et al., 1999; Martins et al., 2004) used the open system perspective to explain how VTs work and argued that VT effectiveness depends in a linear fashion on a set of inputs and processes. Nevertheless, VTs are rather complex socio-technical entities and their effectiveness depends on a set of nonlinear bidirectional causal relations among different levels of dynamics (Arrow et al., 2000; McGrath et al., 2000; Ilgen et al., 2005). CAS are systems that abound in nonlinearities (Holland, 1995), and have emergent properties that result from the interactions among the agents within the system (Anderson, 1999). Therefore, the CAS framework seems more suitable to explain the effectiveness of VTs. In this context, especially relevant is the concept of emergent states, which are important antecedents of team effectiveness (Marks et al., 2001; Ilgen et al., 2005) and are heavily influenced by the use of IT (Warkentin et al., 1997; Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2002; Breu and Hemingway, 2004). Hence, there is a need for scholars to reflect on the accumulated body of knowledge on the use of IT in teams using CAS concepts.

My goal in this study is to use the concept of emergence (central to the CAS framework) to provide a new theoretical understanding for some of the epiphenomena of VTs’ dynamics that have been, so far, studied in isolation. I argue that team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict are interdependent states that emerge from interactions among VT members, and are essential drivers for VT effectiveness. I also explore the impact of IT on the emergence of these states. By combining the insights from the CAS framework with the empirical data on the emergence of team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict, my study seeks to provide a basis for matching the real-world phenomena (emergence in teams using IT) with the artificial simulations. The literature review is conducted by extracting articles from the ABI/INFORM database using the keywords: ‘virtual teams’, ‘emergence’, ‘complex adaptive systems’ and ‘complexity and teams’.

## A CAS model of VT effectiveness

A VT can be defined as a socio-technical system, composed of individuals who are geographically and/or organizationally or otherwise dispersed and who interact with one another using information technologies in order to accomplish common goals. This broad definition is in accordance with previous approaches (Martins et al., 2004; Hertel et $a l . ,$ , 2005) and does not limit the type of task performed by the team, the length of time the team remains together, and the span of membership across multiple functional, departmental or geographic areas (Curs¸eu et al., in press).

Because VTs are increasingly used in various organizational contexts as essential tools for integration (Lipnack and Stamps, 1997; Martins et al., 2004), understanding the factors that drive their effectiveness is of great managerial importance. Teams in general and VTs in particular are complex entities composed of several subsystems (individual team members) and embedded in larger systems (e.g., organizations). Their dynamics unfolds in time in a nonlinear fashion depending on the nature of the interactions among the team members as well as on the past team experiences or anticipated future events (McGrath et $a l . ,$ 2000). The CAS framework will be further on used to better understand the dynamics of $\mathrm { V T } \mathbf { s } ,$ specifically the effects of using IT on the emergence of socio-cognitive and affective states $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ cohesion, trust, team cognition).

A unified and universally agreed upon theory of CAS does not yet exist (Anderson, 1999), however, it is generally accepted that CAS are nested systems (composed of several loosely coupled agents and embedded in larger systems), that evolve in a nonlinear fashion depending on, and in the same time influencing, the evolution of its individual agents and the dynamics of the larger system in which the CAS is embedded, making the outcomes of the CAS difficult to predict and its history irreversible (Casti, 1992; Gell-Mann, 1994; Holland, 1995; Dooley, 1996, 1997; Cilliers, 1998; Anderson, 1999; McGrath et al., 2000).

From a CAS perspective, team effectiveness is not a linear function of several factors or inputs (as it has been described in the open system framework, see for details Gladstein (1984), Hackman (1987), Ilgen et al. (2005) or McGrath (1984)). Team effectiveness results from the complex interaction of three levels of causal dynamics: local, global and contextual dynamics (Arrow et $a l . ,$ 2000; McGrath et al., 2000). The main factors related to the three levels of dynamics are depicted in Figure 1. Local dynamics refer to the way in which the agents interact within the teams. As depicted in Figure 1, this level of dynamics is related to the way the team members are engaged in the task using IT and to the way they interact and use their skills, knowledge and expertise as well as the available resources to perform the task. The second level of dynamics (global) involves the variables that emerge at the systemic level. The emergent states are variables that describe the team as a whole, and they emerge from and in the same time shape the local dynamics of the team. Finally, the contextual dynamics reflect the impact of the variables that belong to the larger system(s) in which the team is embedded. As Figure 1 shows, the societal factors as well as organizational factors create the level of contextual dynamics that influence and in the same time is influenced by the local and global levels of causal dynamics. Team effectiveness is therefore the result of the complex interactions that occur between the three levels of causal dynamics and then feeds back and reshape these levels of causal dynamics. VTs with similar initial states (see the variables depicted in the left side of Figure 1) can follow different paths and end with different outcomes over time depending on the complex patterns of interaction between the three levels of causal dynamics.

The effectiveness of VTs is therefore difficult to study using simple causal models. Because of this bidirectional causality and of the rich interconnectedness across systems and levels it is almost impossible to keep some elements constant and to study others in complete isolation. As an alternative, computational models are a commonly used research method for CAS. These simulations have begun to be used in organization science with remarkable results (see for an excellent overview the book edited by Prietula et al. (1998)). Simulation environments (e.g., cellular automata, neural networks or genetic algorithms, see for details Nowak and Vallacher (1998) and Anderson (1999)) are designed to study the way in which a targeted CAS functions in certain (sometimes critical) conditions. These conditions that are either rare or difficult to manipulate in real-life settings are artificially created. The study of VTs will certainly benefit from using this research strategy in a domain in which the voluntary manipulation of performance-relevant factors is restricted by organizational or societal factors. In a more pragmatic perspective, simulation studies based on computational models can be performed with less effort, fewer resources and less time than real VT studies. However, fieldwork and empirical data collected in real teams can be used to define in simulations the characteristics of the agents and their interactions (model-based simulations). The simulation results may be further on used to clarify the ambiguities in real-life data and to make the theoretical models more comprehensive and accurate (Carley, 2002; McKelvey, 1999a).

## Emergence and emergent states in VTs

The process of emergence is almost always discussed in relation with self-organization. This relationship is still a matter of debate: some authors consider that emergent states result from self-organization (Barton, 1994; Holland, 1998; Goldstein, 1999, 2000), others argue that self-organization is an emergent phenomenon (Smith and Stevens, 1996), while still others acknowledge that emergence is possible without self-organization and selforganization without emergence (Wolf and Holvoet, 2005). According to Ashby, a self-organized system is a ‘system that starts with its parts separate (so that the behavior of each is independent of the other’s states) and whose parts then act so that they change toward forming connections of some type’ (Ashby, 1962, 266). In this sense, a VT is certainly a self-organized system since it starts as a collection of individuals holding different cognitive schemata and ends up as a system of interconnected parts that have shared schemata and act in a coordinated fashion.

In the most general sense, emergence refers to the new patterns of behaviors, coherent structures or states produced by the interaction between the individual agents in a system, patterns that cannot be predicted from the characteristics of the individual agents alone, or from the rules of their interactions (Mihata, 1997; Goldstein, 1999, 2000). Concerning its relation with self-organization this paper builds on Goldstein’s (1999) conceptualization of emergent phenomena in CAS. According to Goldstein (1999) emergent phenomena result from self-organization and they share four characteristics. They are: (1) new states that have not been previously identified or observed in the system, (2) interrelated in the system but maintain a certain sense of identity over a certain period of time, (3) can represent the system as a whole, (4) are the product of a dynamic process of co-evolution, and (5) they can be observed and evaluated (Goldstein, 1999, 50).

In the literature on teams, emergent states are considered as epiphenomena created by the social interactions among team members. Marks et al. (2001) define emergent states as ‘constructs that characterize properties of a team that are typically dynamic in nature and vary as a function of team context, input, processes and outcomes’ (Marks et al., 2001, 357). For VTs, four such emergent states receive considerable attention: team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict. These states are elements of the global dynamics (McGrath et al., 2000) of VTs. They emerge from contextual factors (organizational context and organizational environment) and subsequently impact team processes and characteristics (local dynamics). Through their interaction with local and contextual dynamics, the emergent states influence VT effectiveness and they are influenced by the feedback related to team outcomes. Therefore, the relationship between the emergent states and team performance is neither simple nor linear. They seem to be antecedents for team effectiveness (Ilgen et al., 2005), and at the same time they are influenced by the feedback related to team outcomes (Marks et al., 2001).

The impact of communication technology on these states has been acknowledged in several instances (Warkentin et al., 1997; Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1999; Hislop, 2002; Hinds and Bailey, 2003; Jarvenpaa et al., 2004; Schultze and Orlikowski, 2004; Hinds and Mortensen, 2005). However, communication technology is not the only type of IT used by VTs (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998; Dennis et al., 2001; Arnott and Pervan, 2005) and therefore it is not the only one to affect these emergent states. Any type of group support system (GSS) is also closely linked with the emergence of these states because it impacts the interaction among the team members (Pauleen and Yoong, 2001). In order to explore the impact of technology on team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict this paper uses the taxonomy of GSS technology environments introduced by Zigurs and Buckland (1998). These authors distinguish between three types of GSS tools: communication support refers to technological tools that allow communication among the team members (this type is fundamental for VT), information processing support refers to technological tools that facilitate the gathering, aggregation and evaluation of information, and finally process structuring support refers to technological tools that enhance the processes by which group members interact in order to perform the task (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998). The role of these three types of GSS tools for the emergence of team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict will be discussed in the following sections.

## Team cognition

The concept of team cognition has been extremely popular during the last decade as an explanatory factor for team processes and performance (for an excellent overview see Salas and Fiore (2004)). Team cognition ‘emerges from the interplay of the individual cognition of each team member and team process behaviors’ (Cooke et al., 2004, 85). Although team cognition is defined as a team level phenomena emerging from the interactions among team members (Cooke et al., 2004; Curs¸eu, 2003), previous research on team cognition mostly focused on the shared nature of the individual knowledge structures within a team. It has been stated, that in order for a team to be effective, the team members have to share different types of knowledge structures (Klimoski and Mohammed, 1994; Mohammed et al., 2000; Mathieu et al., 2000). Empirical studies also show that in order to maximize the likelihood of a certain knowledge structure to be remembered and used during group interactions, group members have to share as much task-related information as possible (Stasser and Titus, 1985, 1987). Therefore, shared cognition approaches have dominated the research on team cognition in the past few decades (Rentsch and Woehr, 2004). However, by focusing only on what is being shared within a team and by ignoring the interactions among team members, the shared cognition approaches overlook the very essence of team cognition: its emerging nature.

A previous approach that addresses the issue of team cognition as an emergent team level phenomenon is proposed by Curs¸eu (2003). This theoretical approach describes information processing in teams as the interaction between three subsystems: selection, memory and communication. A team selects a part of the available information, and through communication develops representations that are stored in team’s long-term memory. A representation, simply stated, refers to a particular way in which a team understands a body of knowledge. This is referred to as the group’s frame of reference, cognitive collective representation or team cognition (Curs¸eu, 2003).

The emergence of team cognition is depicted in Figure 2. Team members use individual schemata to make sense of a particular knowledge domain. Individual schemata are essential in shaping the individual behavior and in interpreting the behaviors of the other team members. Team members interact in a fashion that is dictated by the coordination mechanisms (the norms, rules and routines) implemented within the team. Through these repetitive interactions, individual schemata are continuously adjusted (individual schemata co-evolve). Hence, a dynamic process of structuring and restructuring team cognition takes place up to the moment when the team members reach consensus or they agree upon a particular way of understanding the knowledge at hand. When a team reaches consensus, team representation or team cognition is stabilized in a state of equilibrium and it will be reflected in a team’s actions (e.g., to make decisions or solve problems – for a graphical illustration see Figure 2).

As presented in this model, team cognition is a new phenomenon that emerges from a self-organizing system, it is illustrative for the way in which a team as a whole understands a body of knowledge, it results from a dynamic process of coevolution of individual schemata and therefore it cannot be reduced to the sum of individual schemata (see Curs¸eu (2003) for an empirical illustration).

The use of virtual communication tools has a strong influence on the development and use of team cognition. First, virtual communication tools provide the opportunity for equal participation within VTs. Because the IT has a rather reduced potential in facilitating the exchange of social cues and implicit knowledge (Hollingshead, 1996; Hislop, 2002), it reduces the effects of differences in status that are triggered by cues like appearance, presence or vocal inflexion (Zigurs, 2003). Second, IT also has a positive effect on the number of alternative solutions generated as well as on the performance in brainstorming tasks (Valacich et al., 1992; Valacich and Schwenk, 1995). However, the greater information pool that is available in VTs is not followed by a proper informational integration because of the negative effects of IT on planning and coordination. Novel technology in particular, combined with a lack of experience in using communication technology, hinders planning and coordination activities, and therefore negatively influences the informational integration in VTs (Hollingshead et al., 1993).

Although in theory the use of IT provides the opportunity for equal participation in VTs, the other hindering effects of using IT to communicate makes the participation unequal. According to the CAS framework, the products of complex processes in a CAS often follow a power law distribution<sup>1</sup> (Carley, 2002). The discovery of power laws for particular CAS is important because once identified they can be used to ‘generate and select realistic topologies for computational theorizing purposes’ (Carley, 2002, 218). Social network research emphasizes that a relatively small number of agents within the social network has the largest number of connections, or contribution to the network (power law distribution – Ebel et al. (2003)). Such a power law for VTs suggests that only a few VT members have a large contribution to the team cognition and most of the agents in a VT have only a modest contribution to the team cognition.

In order to further illustrate the use of CAS concepts in understanding the dynamics of VTs, this proposition is tested in a data set used by a previous research to investigate the effects of communication environment on group decision-making (Curs¸eu, 2003). Forty-two teams (126 team members) using synchronous virtual communication (virtual chat) had to perform a decision-making task and transcripts of their discussions were used for the analysis. In order to compute the centrality of each member in the virtual communication network, two individual contribution indices were computed. The first one is the number of individual contributions to the team (number of interventions made by each member in the chat room). Teams, however, differed in the amount of time they spent to analyze the decision; therefore a second adjusted index was computed using the average length of team debate. This adjusted index was obtained by dividing the number of individual statements with the time needed by the team to reach consensus. Furthermore, both indices were ranked in decreasing order of individual contributions. The values of the two indices were plotted against their ranks.<sup>2</sup> The two scatter plots are presented in Figure 3, showing that indeed, a rather small number of team members had a very large contribution to the team and the majority of the team members had a moderate contribution.

A visual inspection shows downward regression slopes for both variables (see also Figure 4 showing the value of the regression coefficients (R)). However, the relation is not perfectly linear (as in the classic rank-size studies, see for example Zipf (1949) and Andriani and McKelvey (2005)), both distributions are slightly concave and there seems to be a cutoff in both distributions (see Figure 4) that may indicate a transition from a lognormal to a power law distribution (West and Deering, 1995). We can therefore conclude that the distribution of individual contributions to team cognition in a VT is highly skewed to the right and it may be fitted by a power law.

Although the use of communication technologies imposes constraints on the nature and the quality of information shared by the agents in a VT (Hollingshead, 1996; Hislop, 2002; Schultze and Orlikowski, 2004), the use of other GSS tools (e.g., for information processing or process support) can contribute to the reduction of information integration deficits in VTs (Ciborra and Andreu, 2001; Hendriks, 2001; Breu and Hemingway, 2004; Huysman and Wulf, 2005). Especially the use of information processing support, directly contributes to the integration of task relevant knowledge (Zigurs and Buckland, 1998; Malhorta et al., 2001). Process structuring tools facilitate team processes like coordination, cooperation and planning as well as the development of transactive memory systems in VTs and in this way they indirectly impact the emergence of team cognition (Alavi and Tiwana, 2002).

A better fit between task and technology, as well as the use of communication tools that allow for immediate feedback, transfer of symbols, multiple simultaneous conversations, rehearsed communication, and have the ability to maintain a history of communications, foster the emergence of team cognition (Dennis et al., 2001; Maruping and Agarwal, 2004; Curs¸eu et al., in press). The emergence of team cognition is a process very similar with the process through which a formal NKC model (Kauffman Fitness Landscape Model) reaches equilibrium. The agents (N is the number of agents in the system) in such a model change their energy or fitness level through their connections with the other agents (K is the number of connections in the system) and as a consequence of connecting with other agents outside the NK system (C is the number of the agents outside the NK system) until the model reaches stability (see for details Kauffman (1993), McKelvey (1999b) and Maguire et al. (2006)). Changes in agents’ schemata during the interactions in a VT are similar with the change in the fitness levels of the agents in a NKC model. A fully connected internal network with no connections with other external agents (C ¼ 0) should lead to complexity catastrophe according to the NKC model. This describes the case of a VT that evolves on its own isolated from the environment. However, VTs are not isolated from the environment and because they are composed of diverse members, the connections with outside agents are likely to be numerous (for a comprehensive view on the relationship between team diversity and the external social network range see Reagans et al. (2004)). In this sense, the connections with outside agents will act as a force that will keep the VT away from complexity catastrophe (see for details McKelvey (1999b)).

However, it must be noted that a sensitive difference between the emergence of team cognition and the NKC model is that in a VT the connections for a team member change over time (see the power law section above), while in an NKC model, the number of connections remains the same for each agent. Also, the members of real VTs are more complex than the attributes assigned to NKC agents and, therefore, in the same systemic conditions they may act differently. Taking these matters into account, a closer similarity of the emergence of team cognition is with neural networks which allow the changes of both the values of activations of the units as well as the weight of connections among them (McKelvey, 1999b).

Attractor (or Hopfield) neural networks are composed of interconnected units. Each unit in the network receives two sets of inputs: external (from outside the network) and internal (form the other nodes of the network). The network functions in a constraint satisfaction fashion and it reaches stability in the so-called attractor states in which the energy function is minimal (Hopfield, 1982). The energy landscape for such a network can contain several attractors depending on the input-output pairs presented to the network. According to Hopfield (1982) the emergent properties (energy landscape) of such networks result from a nonlinear relationship between the inputs and outputs. The attractor(s) in the energy landscape are similar with the state(s) in which VT cognition reaches stability. The network ‘remembers’ a state even from partial inputs, in the same way in which VTs use an already stored collective representation in situations that resemble the initial conditions in which the representation was developed. The network can also simulate situations in which a new representation is developed, such as when different learning rules are used, or when there is no reference to previously stored patterns or inputs (Storkey and Valabregue, 1999). Also, the polarity and the weight of the connections among the units can be changed and therefore it is possible to implement media richness as an essential factor that influences the emergence of team cognition in VTs. In conclusion attractor neural networks could be the connectionist alternative to the NKC models because of their greater flexibility in accommodating multiple real-life constraints associated to VTs.

## Trust

According to Kanawattanachai and Yoo (2002), one of the fundamental factors that are believed to be important in determining the success and failure of VTs is trust. Trust can be defined as the

willingness to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party (Mayer et al., 1995, 712).

Trust is a set of shared expectations that emerge from the interactions among team members and therefore the most important source of trust is the quality of interactions among the team members especially in the beginning of the VT life (De Rosa et al., 2004).

It seems that informal communication, which overlaps with task-related communication, is important for the emergence of trust in VTs (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1999; Jarvenpaa et al., 2004). Since the informal communication (not related to task) is less likely to occur in VTs, the emergence of trust might be endangered by the use of communication technology (Hiltz et al., 1986). Trust, however, is an important driver for VT performance as well as for the emergence of VT cognition. First, because it allows the development of strong interpersonal ties and it fosters the interpersonal contact and information exchange among the team members (Iacono and Weisband, 1997; Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1999) and second, because it reduces the need for monitoring, increases team members’ satisfaction and escalates team effort and motivation (Kanawattanachai and Yoo, 2002).

Next to the use of IT, another factor that might endanger the development of trust in VTs is linked to team permanency. Since most of the time VTs are temporary teams, there is not sufficient time for trust to emerge (Coppola et al., 2004). The most relevant stage in a team’s life for the emergence of trust is the forming stage. Almost all authors of conceptual work on VT management recommend that, in order to enable trust to emerge, in the beginning of VT work, all members should meet each other face-to-face (Lipnack and Stamps, 1997; Warkentin et al., 1997; Coutu, 1998; Warkentin and Beranek, 1999; Suchan and Hayzak, 2001; Martins et al., 2004). Face-toface meetings are essential for the emergence of trust also during the VT projects because they can contribute to clarify possible misunderstandings associated with the use of communication technology (O’Hara-Devereaux and Johansen, 1994).

Agent-based simulations support the fact that the development of trust is essential in information exchange and performance in artificial societies. Agents only engage in transactions if a certain threshold of trust is present (Castelfranchi and Tan, 2002). In one simulation experiment, Prietula and Carley (1999) assign a trust function to each agent in the model and show that in the case of homogeneous groups, honest groups (with no liars) performed better only under stable environmental conditions, while in the case of heterogeneous ones, honest groups outperformed always the groups with at least one liar. Because VTs are inherently diverse, it is reasonably to argue that trust foster VT performance through its impact on the information exchange and the emergence of VT cognition. In another study, Prietula and Carley (2000) show that emergent trust in an agent-based simulation generates more durable information coalitions among the agents and it reduces the information withheld and the conflict. Concerning the evolution of trust in artificial societies, Ramchurn et al. (2004) note that if trust does not emerge in the first stages of the simulation, the future interactions between the agents will be compromised and Falcone and Castelfranchi (2001) showed that trust creates trust, while initial distrust blocks the emergence of trust in the next stages of the simulation.

We can, therefore, conclude that trust is an enabling factor for the emergence of VT cognition and consequently, for VT performance and the first stages of team interaction, are essential for the development of trust.

## Cohesion

Two conceptualizations of cohesion are of most importance in the literature on teams: the first comes from the psychological literature and sees cohesion as a psychological state or shared desire of the team members to remain in the team, while the second comes from sociology and defines cohesion as a set of social network characteristics.

The most influential definition of team cohesion as a psychological state is the one introduced by Leon Festinger (1950). He defined team cohesion as ‘the resultant of all the forces acting on all the members to remain in the team’ (Festinger, 1950, 274). Therefore from the very beginning, cohesion was defined as an emergent state, resulting from the interactions among team members, their attitudes toward each other (the degree of perceived interpersonal congruence and attractiveness) or the prestige of the team. Both routes involve feedback loops. The first feedback loop is related with the interactions among the team members and it is therefore influenced by the IT used by the team. Warkentin et al. (1997) compare face-to-face student teams with virtual ones and report higher levels of cohesion in face-to-face teams than in VTs. A first conclusion will be that the use of IT to communicate has negative effects on the development of cohesion because it reduces the exchange of relevant social cues.

Concerning the second conceptualization of cohesion (in sociological terms), it is generally agreed that a team is cohesive if the team’s social network has particular structural characteristics (e.g., high density, or that it can be described as a clique) (Friedkin, 2004). This sociological approach on cohesion can be linked with the CAS framework to the extent it describes the team as a social network and the structural characteristics of this network result from self-organization. In this case, it is not a psychological state that emerges from team processes and interactions but it is a particular network structure (structural cohesion, see for details Moody and White (2003)). This perspective implies that teams are nested structures and highly cohesive teams are embedded in less cohesive ones and the features and properties of a highly cohesive team are beyond any individual actor in the team. In a more technical sense, structural cohesion refers to the minimum number of actors in a social network who, if removed would generate the dissolution of the entire network (Moody and White, 2003).

Certainly the use of synchronous communication tools will allow the development of a dense communication network within the team, and they support the emergence of structural cohesion. A dense network in a VT emerges when all team members receive and react to any particular message that it is being sent in the team. Therefore, the emergence of structural cohesion in VTs is strictly limited by the capacity of the IT to allow simultaneous communication on various channels and immediacy of feedback. The use of other IT tools may also facilitate the emergence of cohesion in a VT (see for example Malhorta et al. (2001)).

As an emergent psychological state, cohesion is closely related with team processes, outcomes, as well as with the other emergent states. If team cohesion is high, team members will be more committed to the teams and more motivated to accomplish their tasks (Beal et al., 2003). As a state, cohesion is, therefore, expected to have a positive influence on team performance. Empirical evidence synthesized in two meta-analyses shows small and positive correlations between cohesion and team performance (for details see Evans and Dion (1989), Mullen and Cooper (1994)). For the particular case of VTs, the literature is rather scarce and the results concerning the relation between cohesion and VT performance are mixed. Gonzalez et al. (2003) reported that cohesion is positively related with VT performance, while Aiello and Kolb (1995) reported that cohesion does not have a significant relation with the performance of VTs. Cohesion in VTs positively influences the perceptions and attitudes of team members on the richness of communication technologies used by the teams (Yoo and Alavi, 2001). In conclusion, cohesion is a state that emerges from team processes and it is has consequences for both: the individual members of the VT (it produces positive membership attitudes) and the team as a whole (in general it increases performance and the quality of team processes).

The emergence of cohesion also influences and it is influenced by the emergence of team cognition. Cohesion as a psychological state will most probably speed up the consensus reaching process in VTs and team cognition will reach stability much faster in cohesive VTs than in a less cohesive ones. In terms of performance this is not always a positive influence. The groupthink phenomenon describes decision-making fiascos in which highly cohesive groups reach consensus very fast without exploring relevant alternatives (see for details Janis (1972)). Cohesion may, however, be beneficial for VT performance when the time to make a decision is the most important performance criteria. For example in the case of cockpit crews the speed of decision-making process is essential, therefore the cohesion of the crew can be beneficial for team performance.

High levels of cohesion in an NKC model may, however lead to complexity catastrophe (see for details Kauffman (1993), McKelvey (1999b)). The functioning of CAS is based on heterogeneous agents – see diversity as a basic property of CAS in Holland’s (1995) approach – however, overly connected networks seem to dramatically reduce the diversity in the system and in time, even the fittest agent in the group will have similar characteristics with the other agents (McKelvey, 1999b). The rapidity of reaching the complexity catastrophe increases with the number of internal connections among the agents in the system (K). Therefore, the NKC model dynamics and cohesion seem work at cross purposes and further explorations are needed in order to clarify the implications of cohesion for the dynamics of VTs using this particular simulation framework.

Social network simulations shed some more light on this issue. As Stocker et al. (2001) showed in a study using Boolean models, the diffusion of ideas and information through social networks depends on the level of cohesion in the network. How well the ideas are exchanged and how fast is the consensus reached, depend on the level of cohesion in the social network (Stocker et al., 2001). These simulation results have implications for the way in which team cognition is conceptualized here. The level of cohesion will most probably positively influence the complexity of team cognition. A higher cognitive complexity should in general foster VT performance (Curs¸eu and Rus, 2005).

However, the connectivity of VT’s social network does not follow a uniform distribution and is rather approximated by a power law distribution (see Figure 4) and as shown by Stocker et al. (2002) in a simulation study, changes in the highly connected nodes will induce important changes in the network, while changes in the less connected nodes will have a lower impact on the network as a whole. According to their simulation, changes in the individual schemata of the VT members are very likely to occur if the interventions are made toward the most central agents in the network. This leads us to a new point of divergence between VT dynamics and the CAS framework, namely, the emergence of leadership. CAS are virtually leaderless (Eisenhardt and Bhatia, 2002). The emergence of leadership is, however, a central issue for the dynamics of VTs. Further research (and especially simulation studies) should, therefore, explore in more depth the association between the structure of the social network and the emergence of leaders in VTs.

## Conflict

The literature on intrateam conflict makes a distinction between relationship conflict, which refers to interpersonal incompatibilities and frictions among the group members resulting in tension, annoyance and animosity and task conflict which refers to the disagreements among the team members about the content of the task due to different view points, opinions and ideas (Jehn, 1995). Two sources of conflict are relevant for VTs. The first one refers to the limitations imposed on the interaction among the VT members by the use of communication technologies and the second one is the inherent heterogeneity of VTs (Hinds and Bailey, 2003).

The exchange of explicit task-relevant knowledge in VTs is similar to the task-relevant exchanges in co-located teams. However, the ability of IT to transmit implicit social knowledge (implicit social cues that are essential for the development of trust and cohesion) is lower than what is possible with face-to-face communication (Hollingshead, 1996; Hislop, 2002). This can contribute to increased misunderstanding among members (Warkentin et al., 1997) eventually leading to conflict, both task and relationship related. Adding to this the heterogeneity of VTs (team members are more likely to represent different cultures, locations and functions), it is clear that task and relationship conflicts are more likely to emerge in virtual than in co-located teams. In an empirical study, Hinds and

Mortensen (2005) confirm these theoretical propositions and show that the emergence of relationship conflict is higher in virtual than in co-located teams. Relational conflict is also associated with a lower number of relational links among team members, reduced cohesion, slow consensus and underdeveloped team identity, and it blocks the emergence of trust (Hinds and Bailey, 2003; Hinds and Mortensen, 2005).

In general, in tasks involving information processing, task conflict is beneficial for team performance. It increases the quality of discussions as well as the acceptance of decisions and satisfaction with the team outcome. Relationship conflict has the opposite effect because it reduces satisfaction and commitment toward the team. This is due to the fact that it increases stress and anxiety and, therefore, limits the information processing abilities of the team members (Jehn, 1995; Jehn et al., 1999; Simons and Peterson, 2000). Therefore, task conflict is beneficial for the emergence of complex cognitive structures at the team level, while relationship conflict has the opposite effects.

In an agent-based model, Baets et al. (2005) show that conflict is disruptive for the dynamics of the system if it emerges from opposed group norms and values (comparable to relationship conflict), rather than from opposed individual opinions (equates to task conflict). Their simulation uses agents from several groups that are described by individual schemata, as well a common schemata shared within a group. Interactions based on opposed individual views do not create disruptive conflicts, but high levels of conflict emerge from interactions based on opposing group values. Therefore, the insights from CAS suggest that relationship conflict is very likely to be elicited by the diversity of the VT, rather than by the limitations associated with the use of IT to communicate. Similar results are pointed out in the team diversity literature, showing that the social categorization processes associated with the fact that VT members belong to different external subgroups are very likely to trigger relationship conflict (Jehn, 1995; Simons and Peterson, 2000). Interesting issues still open for exploration in computational experiments are: (1) the extent to which task-related conflict is associated with the use of IT in VTs; (2) the way in which the use of IT and VT diversity interact in generating task and relationship conflict and (3) the way in which the use of IT influences the association between task and relationship conflict.

Emergent trust seems to play an important role in the emergence of conflict as shown in the simulation conducted by Prietula and Carley (2000), result which is supported by empirical data on real teams (Simons and Peterson, 2000). In conclusion, it seems that (1) relationship conflict has disruptive effects for CAS, while task conflict seems to be beneficial for CAS, (2) the two types of conflict are likely to be triggered by independent factors and (3) the emergence of conflict in VT impacts on and it is influenced by the other emergent states.

## Discussion and conclusions

In this paper, I use the CAS framework to bring together, previously disparate streams of research on team cognition, trust, cohesion and conflict and to shed light on these concepts as emergent phenomena in VTs. My analysis also tackles the role of IT on the emergence of these states in VTs and reviews the most important results on the emergence of trust, cohesion and conflict coming from artificial simulations.

A first contribution of my paper is its analysis of the impact of IT on the emergence of team cognition. Team cognition is an emergent state with major implications for information processing in VTs and, since most of the VTs used by organizations perform tasks that rely, one way or another, on information processing, this is a highly relevant issue with both practical and theoretical implications. A better management of IT-related factors that influence the emergence of cognition in VT is surely impossible without first knowing which these factors are. It is also theoretically relevant because the mechanisms that influence the emergence of VT cognition are not yet fully explored. Agent-based modeling experiments could further explore the role of the IT on the emergence of VT cognition and on the co-evolution of the individual team member’s cognitive structures. As Carley (2002) states, the formalization of IT effects has been overlooked almost entirely in the agentmodeling studies. IT also has been studied as independent from the social network (Huysman and Wulf, 2005) and this aspect should also be corrected in forthcoming simulations. By integrating the literature on the effects of IT on the emergence of team cognition, my study takes a step forward toward the formalization of the IT implications in team dynamics and outcomes.

The reflections on the development of the other emergent states as well as their interdependence are another contribution of this paper. These states are closely related with each other and closely related with VT processes and outcomes. They emerge from the interactions taking place among VT members, and afterwards they influence VT processes; they are influenced by the feedback concerning team outcomes, and afterwards they impact on these outcomes. This is a complex process of interdependency that cannot be captured or studied with simple linear models. Agent-based computational experiments can shed more light into the relations between these emergent states as well as into the nature of their relation with team processes and outcomes. A summary of the impact of IT on the emergent states, as discussed in this paper, is presented in Table 2.

A particularly interesting aspect that needs further exploration in simulation studies concerns the consequences of variety (diversity) in VTs which, according to the empirical results mentioned before, is supposed to have a positive impact on VT’s cognitive complexity as well as a positive impact on conflict. VT’s cognitive complexity enhances VT performance, while relationship conflict is detrimental to VT performance. Therefore, future agent modeling studies should address the mechanisms through which this tension can be solved. As the discussion of team cohesion shows, however, the CAS concepts and interpretations may work at cross-purposes with the empirical results from the field studies. Hence, further attempts are needed in order to solve this ‘creative tension’ and push forward our understanding of VT dynamics.

In this paper, I also argue that the contributions of the VT members to team cognition can be fitted by a power law distribution. The discovery of power laws for particular CAS is important because once identified they can be used to design accurate simulation models (Carley, 2002; Eisenhardt and Bhatia, 2002; Maguire et al., 2006). The fact that the centrality of the VT members in the social network of the team may follow a power law distribution opens new ways for simulation studies. In NKC simulations it has already been argued that by allocating the same number of connections among the actors induces serious restrictions for the evolution of the system (for details see McKelvey (1999b)). As the results presented here suggest, in real VTs the centrality of team members in the social network of the team does not follow such a uniform distribution, but rather a power law distribution. In this respect the paper argues that neural networks models could be a more viable alternative to the NKC models to simulate the emergence of VT cognition because of their better capacity to implement constraints related to the diversity of the agents, as well as to the nature of their relationships.

The use of CAS concepts to make sense of VT dynamics has therefore several merits, yet such an approach is not a problem-free one. It is certainly useful for understanding VT effectiveness as a result of complex interactions among different levels of causal dynamics and for exploring the interdependence between different emergent states. Nevertheless, it should be noted that VT members have more complex attributes than the attributes describing the agents in a CAS. Also, the nature of interpersonal interactions in real VT is sometimes regulated by complex norms and rules. These two aspects alone could lead to a level of complexity that generates intractable modeling problems. This aspect, however, should not be used as an excuse for oversimplification, a problem often encountered in present model-based studies. It is essential that the models simulate as well as possible the characteristics of real VTs, so that the computational experiments will provide better insights and fill in the gaps in our understanding of VT dynamics. The results of these computational experiments could ultimately be used in the systemic design of effective VTs as well as background for developing training strategies for VTs.

Petru L. Curs¸eu (b.1975, Romania) received his Ph.D. in Organizational Psychology at Tilburg University in 2003 with the thesis Formal Group Decision-Making. A Social Cognitive Approach.
