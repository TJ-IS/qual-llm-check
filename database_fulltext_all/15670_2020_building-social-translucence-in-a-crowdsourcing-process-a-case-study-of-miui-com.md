# Building social translucence in a crowdsourcing process: A case study of Miui.com

Sixuan Zhang<sup>a</sup>, Shan L. Pan<sup>b</sup>, Tao hua Ouyang<sup>a,⁎</sup>

<sup>a</sup> School of Economics and Management, Beihang University 37 Xueyuan Road, Haidian District, Beijing, 100191, China <sup>b</sup> UNSW Business School, the University of New South Wales, Sydney, NSW 2052, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online collaboration Crowdsourcing process Social translucence Case study

## A B S T R A C T

This study aims to advance research on crowdsourcing processes by exploring practices that foster collaboration between internal employees and external users. We conduct a case study of Miui.com, a crowdsourcing platform launched by one of the world s leading smartphone makers and also a Fortune 500 company, Xiaomi Inc. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings complement previous work on crowdsourcing by showing that collaboration between employees and users can be promoted by building social translucence into the crowdsourcing process.

## 1. Introduction

Crowdsourcing refers to a set of activities in which a sponsoring company outsources a task to a “crowd” of external users in the form of a <sup>fl</sup>exible open call on an IT-enabled platform [1–3]. Prior research on crowdsourcing processes emphasizes the importance of leveraging user knowledge to capture value for hosting <sup>fi</sup>rms [4,5]. Many studies have focused on examining factors (i.e., user motivations, user characteristics, user roles, community structure, platform design, etc.) that shape user contribution and participation in the crowdsourcing processes $[ 4 , 6 - 9 ]$ . Moreover, much research has primarily focused on in vestigating how <sup>fi</sup>rms promote collaboration between external users in their interactive activities (i.e., ideation, commenting, voting, etc.) [10–13]. These studies have also shown that such collaboration increases the possibility of value co-creation among crowds of external users [14,15].

Recent studies on crowdsourcing processes have begun to emphasize the imperative role of internal employees in co-creating value with external users and have called for research to better understand collaboration between internal and external participants [16 18]. Yan et al. [19], for example, have illustrated the positive innovation outcomes brought by employees’ participation in the crowdsourcing process. They have also highlighted the importance of having sustainable interactive collaboration between external product users and internal employees for the long-term success of crowdsourcing communities. Nonetheless, little empirical study has been done to explore the practices through which collaboration between internal employees and external users is achieved.

This study aims to advance research on crowdsourcing processes by gaining an understanding of practices that foster collaboration between internal employees and external users. Its importance can be summar ized as follows. First, crowdsourcing outcomes brought about solely from collaborative interactions between external users may not re<sup>fl</sup>ect a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s desire for value creation [20]. For example, in crowdsourcing for innovation processes, the ideas that are voted most popular by crowd users may not be the ones that are most likely to create value for a <sup>fi</sup>rm vis-à-vis a successful business model [15]. In such circumstances, internal employees and external users are required to collaboratively develop and identify the “best” ideas that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the sponsoring company’s value creation intentions while not transgressing the values of crowd users [21]. Hence, acquiring knowledge related to practices that promote collaboration between internal employees and external users is imperative because the generation of an acceptable crowdsourcing output relies on collaborative interactions between both the internal and the external sides [22].

Second, collaborative input from internal employees drives valuable contributions from the user side [23]. For example, Dahlander and Piezunka [22] have found that internal employees’ active contributions and reactions in crowdsourcing processes motivate contributions from external product users. Nambisan and Baron [24] have highlighted that quick feedback from internal employees on user input helps to cultivate a sense of partnership with the company among product users, which encourages user contribution on the crowdsourcing platform. By contrast, other studies have indicated that a lack of collaborative e<sup>f</sup>ort from internal employees may lead to unexpected crowdsourcing di<sup>fi</sup>- culties, such as misunderstanding of proposed problems [25], negative crowd behaviors (e.g., criticism) [26], and receiving numerous contentfree comments [15]. In sum, while these studies provide certain insights in terms of the importance of collaborative input from internal em ployees, there has been little systematic empirical research exploring practices that promote collaboration between internal and external parties. Hence, studies that produce evidence to help managers and researchers to understand these practices are of particular value to both scholars and practitioners.

We conduct a case study of Miui.com, a crowdsourcing platform launched by one of the world’s leading smartphone makers, Xiaomi Inc., which is also a Fortune 500 company. On this platform, Xiaomi’s enthusiastic customers (known as “Mi Fans”) collaborate with Xiaomi’s engineers and project managers to decide on and test weekly updates of Xiaomi’s Android-based user interface, MIUI. We draw upon social translucence theory in concert with previous literature on crowdsourcing processes to explore how Xiaomi fosters collaborative interactions between its employees and Mi Fans. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that, by incorporating social translucence into its crowdsourcing process, Xiaomi has successfully achieved collaboration between its employees and Mi Fans.

The paper is organized as follows. First, the theoretical background and research methodology are outlined. Following these are the case analysis and discussion. Finally, the paper ends with considerations for future research, an acknowledgement of the study’s limitations, and a conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical development

## 2.1. Collaborative interactions in crowdsourcing processes

The few studies that touch upon the collaborative interactions between internal employees and external users suggest that collaborative inputs from one side might be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their awareness and interpretation of the activities and movements of the other side [24,27–29]. On the external side, for example, users want the opinions they voice in a discussion thread to be “heard” by the company [30]. Employees’ activities, such as commenting in a discussion among users and responding directly to a user’s posting, might be interpreted as a sign that the company is paying attention to its users’ voices. When they are aware of such employee activity, users are thus motivated to make collaborative contributions to discussion threads [22,31]. On the internal side, employees’ behavioral decisions might also be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by their knowledge of user activities. For example, employees are likely to choose an idea as an implementation candidate when they notice that a great number of users have voted for the idea [20].

In addition, in a crowdsourcing platform, most collaborative inter actions (e.g., online conversations) between internal employees and external users are fundamentally social processes [32–34]. Hence, participants might use the knowledge learnt in their daily social interactions to structure their online collaborative activities [35]. Some crowdsourcing systems may expose certain meta-data of a posting (i.e., sender, device, time, location, etc.) to deliver messages such as how, why, where, and when it was made, and who made it. [36]. Using their social knowledge, participants of one side (e.g., users) are able to make inferences based on the exposed messages of the other side (e.g., employees) and such inferences may further guide their collaborative behaviors. For example, the exposure of meta-data that an employee just made a rapid response to a newly posted idea in his ofice during lunch time indicates that he might be interested in the topic (as he replied during lunch time) but may leave the conversation soon (as he is in the ofice and will go back to work later). A user who is interested in the employee’s response may either quickly reply to the employee or launch a private chat with him (if possible) but will <sup>fi</sup>nish the private conversation as soon as possible. In such cases, social knowledge accumulated in our daily social interactions in the physical world helps to promote e<sup>f</sup>ective collaboration in the digital world.

Combining these two perspectives, we argue that collaboration between internal employees and external users might be constructed by their mutual awareness of each other’s online activities along with their interpretations of those activities based on experience and expertise acquired from previous social interactions. We therefore adopt social translucence theory [37] as the theoretical foundation of this study because it claims that individuals develop interpretations of each other’s activities based on their previous social experiences. Such interpretations might further structure their own collaborative activities in their interactions with others over computer-mediated network [38]. Next, we describe this underlying theory in detail.

## 2.2. Social translucence theory

The theory of social translucence aims to take the proprieties that support collaboration in face-to-face communications and map them onto interactions occurring within computer-mediated networks [39]. It argues that, in our daily interactions within physical spaces, we make a large number of behavioral decisions based on our perception and interpretation of the actions of others [37]. For example, we decide to “wrap up a talk when the audience starts <sup>fi</sup>dgeting” [37, p.60]. By paying attention to the actions of others (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>dgeting), we extract certain socially important information (e.g., the audience is tired of the talk) and behave accordingly (e.g., wrap up the talk) [40]. Hence, collaboration in our daily face-to-face interactions is promoted. Moreover, from these experiences, we have developed an exquisite sensitivity to the actions of others and tend to use the social information acquired from experience of those actions as the basis for planning our own movements [38]. Therefore, social translucence theory argues that collaborative interaction in digital spaces might also be supported when participants structure their behaviors based on their interpretations of the social information extracted from others’ online actions [36].

In computer-mediated environments, however, certain user activities that contribute to the interactive context, and the associated social information, remain invisible [41,39]. For example, in crowdsourcing, cues about the presence and activity of users in a discussion thread, as well as who might be paying attention to the thread (e.g., who are clicking and typing in the discussion thread), are normally hidden in the database. In circumstances like this, our social knowledge and expertise acquired from our daily face-to-face interactions lose their utility [35]. Hence, Erickson and Kellogg [37] have proposed three guiding principles that enable individuals to draw upon their social experience from face-to-face communication to structure their online behaviors in computer-mediated interactions: visibility, awareness, and accountability.

As the most basic principle, visibility emphasizes the importance of making socially important information visible [42]. As much social information is extracted from actions that are oriented toward others in an interactive context, social translucence theory argues that we should make those actions visible in computer-mediated environments [36]. Visibility gives rise to awareness. Having seen the actions of others, we sense and absorb the social information from these actions and utilize it as a basis for formulating our responses [43]. Finally, accountability arises from awareness. Our social expertise and cultural expectations, developed in face-to-face communication, come into play to govern our newfound awareness, driving us to be accountable for behaving in a socially acceptable manner [44]. In the context of crowdsourcing, for example, when users see instant feedback from internal employees to their posted ideas, they are aware that internal employees are paying attention to their contributions [45]. Hence, social customs and the norm of reciprocity dictate those users to be accountable for providing rapid responses to the feedback.

In addition, the theory adopts the term translucence rather than transparency because it admits that there is a tension between visibility and privacy in the physical world [37]. Hence, instead of making all online activities visible, translucence emphasizes that computermediated networks should be only partially transparent, to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the privacy aspect of face-to-face communication [46]. For example, like a private one-to-one chat that is normally invisible in the physical world, a private online chat between a user and an employee should also be hidden in a crowdsourcing platform. However, Erickson and Kellogg [37] argue that seeing that two people are chatting (online or o<sup>fl</sup>ine) without knowing what they say delivers useful social information while also protecting their privacy to some extent. Hence, they suggest that scholars should pay attention to the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s between “a blindly opti mistic approach of making everything visible” and “a trenchantly pessimistic approach of putting privacy above all else” (p.75).

Moreover, translucence not only encapsulates the tension between visibility and privacy but also manifests itself as constraints in face-toface communication [46]. Constraints refer to barriers that reduce the visibility of activities in collaborative interactions. Awareness and accountability may also come into play: individuals that are aware of constraints may adjust their own behavior accordingly. For example, when noticing a large amount of ambient noise (constraint), speakers increase the volume of their speech to make sure that they can be heard by their audience [37]. Similar situations occur in digital spaces. Users on crowdsourcing platforms may be aware that certain ideas with low votes are “silently” selected (constraint) by internal employees as implementation candidates. Hence, they might think they are accountable for giving voice to highly voted ideas that are dismissed by the spon soring company.

To sum up, social translucence theory emphasizes the importance of making visible certain online activities in computer-mediated networks. When aware of what is happening, online participants extract socially important information from these activities and hold themselves accountable for their behavior based on this awareness along with their social and cultural expectations. The theory also emphasizes that online participants may be aware of the constraints on the visibility of online activities and might behave accordingly. This is why “translucence” rather than “transparency” takes on great importance. In a translucent collaborative system, the proposed principles – visibility/constraint, awareness, and accountability<sup>1</sup> – may e<sup>f</sup>ectively guide participants’ ac tivities and support collaboration among them.

We claim that social translucence theory o<sup>f</sup>ers us a lens through which to advance our understanding of the practices that foster collaboration between employees and customers in crowdsourcing processes. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it provides us with a theoretical basis upon which the following research question might be answered: How does social translucence promote collaboration between internal employees and external users in the context of crowdsourcing? Having now described the theoretical background, we next describe the research method.

## 3. Method

We use a case-based research methodology because it is appropriate for such an exploratory study [49,50]. The method provides for us an opportunity to unearth the mechanisms that are embedded in a contextrich research environment [51] and enables us to explore pertinent “how” questions [52,53]. The theoretical lens of social translucence and the existing knowledge of collaboration between employees and users in crowdsourcing processes serve as the sensitizing device at the outset of this study, allowing us to collect data with certain expectations based on prior knowledge [54]. In addition, the iterative process of data collection and analysis allows the new and serendipitous <sup>fi</sup>ndings that were not identi<sup>fi</sup>ed at the early stage of the study to emerge from the <sup>fi</sup>eld data of the studied <sup>fi</sup>rm, o<sup>f</sup>ering an opportunity to engage in accumulating knowledge in unexplored areas [55].

To select the case, we adopted a revelatory case research method because it allows the exploration of new and interesting phenomena by analyzing representative, novel, or extreme cases [20,56]. The case of Xiaomi and its crowdsourcing platform Miui.com was selected. This constitutes a unique case in that it allows us to understand the new and interesting phenomenon of collaboration between internal employees and external users in a socially translucent crowdsourcing process. In addition, a case study is considered revelatory if, through it, the researchers have the opportunity to observe a phenomenon previously inaccessible to scienti<sup>fi</sup>c investigation [57,58]. We considered the case of Xiaomi and Miui.com as revelatory as our long-term cooperative relationship with the company’s research institute helped us to obtain detailed, relevant, and complete data that it was not previously possible to access elsewhere.

Xiaomi Inc., the Chinese electronics and software company based in Beijing, one of the world’s leading smartphone makers, and also a Fortune 500 organization, is famous for its crowdsourcing platform, Miui.com, where the company involves its enthusiastic customers (known as “Mi Fans”) in generating novel ideas and testing weekly updates for its Android-based user interface, MIUI. Miui.com is designed as a socially translucent system in which it has been incorporated with various abstract representations that portray interactive activities of both internal employees and external users. On Miui.com, Mi fans voluntarily propose ideas for weekly updates of MIUI. Internal project managers and engineers rapidly review and evaluate those ideas and choose the innovative and implementable ones as weekly implementation candidates. In addition, Xiaomi puts the updated version of MIUI online each week. Mi fans test the weekly updates and post the detected bugs on Miui.com. Employees quickly review the reported bugs and <sup>fi</sup>x them. The collaboration between internal employees and Mi fans on Miui.com not only helps Xiaomi reduce the R&D costs of MIUI but also drives Mi fans to build a close relationship with Xiaomi s employees and generate great enthusiasm about Xiaomi’s products [59].

## 3.1. Data collection

Data were collected from both primary and secondary sources [60]. Interviews were our source of primary data. The secondary data complemented the interviews and o<sup>f</sup>ered additional sources for data triangulation. Our engagement with the company included <sup>fi</sup>ve visits to Xiaomi in Beijing, where we participated in meetings, talked with sta<sup>f</sup>, and inspected facilities. All the interviews were arranged through a contact person who possessed a strong academic background and also understood well the purpose of our study. Questions for the initial in terviews were wide ranging. We revised our interview protocol once new insights emerged from previously collected data. The <sup>fi</sup>nal protocol is presented in Appendix 1.

In total, we conducted 25 semistructured in-depth interviews, ranging from one to two hours. All of the interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim, resulting in 285 pages of single-spaced text. Our interviewees were 14 internal employees and 11 Mi fans. All the employees were either project managers or engineers in charge of the weekly updates of MIUI. They mentioned that they read posts and interacted with Mi fans on Miui.com for at least <sup>fi</sup>ve hours a week to get weekly update ideas and detect bugs. Hence, these employees were suitable candidates who helped us to answer our research question from the internal side. In addition, the contact person helped us approach Mi fans who were considered active crowdsourcing participants based on their collaborative inputs on Miui.com. Talking with those Mi fans who agreed to participate in our interviews o<sup>f</sup>ered us opportunities to gain knowledge to explore our research question from the external side. ## 3.2. Data analysis

In the data analysis process, we engaged in unraveling mechanisms that explain how social translucence promotes collaboration between internal employees and external users in the context of crowdsourcing. To do so, we relied primarily on our interview data, triangulated with the secondary data. Social translucence theory served as the sensitizing device that provided us with a set of pertinent constructs with which to frame our data analysis [54,62]. The data analysis process included three phases. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst phase of analysis, we adopted open coding techniques [63] to generate our initial codes. We began by identifying from the interview transcripts instances in which informants described interactions between employees and Mi fans on Miui.com. We then created codes for each instance that depicted what types of activities in the interactions were made visible, how they were made visible, what the informant learned from the visible activities, how that new knowledge structured their later activities on Miui.com, constraints on visibility, and how awareness of such constraints a<sup>f</sup>ected their later online acts. In this step, we adhered strictly to the informants’ language and made no attempt to distill codes [64]. In all, 52 di<sup>f</sup>erent code were generated.

The purpose of the second phase of analysis was to form initial summaries that open the black boxes [65] and show how a socially translucent crowdsourcing process fosters collaboration between internal employees and external users. We started with axial coding techniques that grouped produced codes that were conceptually similar into an umbrella theme [63,66]. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we began by seeking similarities and di<sup>f</sup>erences between produced codes. Then, we focused on connecting and grouping the codes into themes. Following that, we gave those themes labels/descriptors and further organized them into three summaries that provided tentative explanations in response to our research question. As shown in Table 2, the 26 themes generated in this step were organized into three summaries.

In the third phase of analysis, we followed an inductive process to generate abstractions of our summaries. A primary goal of this step was to unearth the explanatory “deep structures” under the descriptive “surface structure” [67] by re<sup>fi</sup>ning the tentative explanations. Speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>rst identi<sup>fi</sup>ed themes that explained the researched phenomenon at an abstract level but lacked theoretical referents in extant research (e.g., a sequential structure embedded with shared social norms) [64]. We also paid attention to themes that suggested extensions of existing knowledge. For example, the two awareness themes in the second summary highlighted the importance of employee awareness of user attention and interests, while previous literature only emphasized user awareness of attention and interests from employees [22,68,69]. Then, we focused on distilling themes that lacked theoretical implications (e.g., expectations of the other party) and aggregate the remaining themes into dimensions. For example, the “who knows what” and “who knows whom” themes were merged into the dimension “awareness of others’ knowledge distribution and social relationships.” The <sup>fi</sup>nalized abstractions of the three summaries are shown in Fig. 1.

For the themes listed in the last column of Table 2, we aggregated them into another two dimensions depicting the constraints on visibility (invisibility of social information and incorrect inferences). Social translucence theory claims that awareness of constraints on visibility will further structure online collaborative behaviors, but we realized that our identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dimensions lacked such an explanation. Hence, we went back to recode our data, grouped codes into themes, and <sup>fi</sup>nally produced another four dimensions (awareness of the invisibility of social information, awareness of the incorrect inferences of social information, knowledge exploration, and knowledge integration). The six dimensions formed two more abstractions of summaries (Fig. 1) that helped to answer our research question. During the abstraction process, we consistently ensured the alignment between abstractions and previous theories. We also engaged in seeking new, serendipitous knowledge through the re<sup>fi</sup>nement of the tentative explanations. Our notes from the casual conversations and the participation in Miui.com contributed to this phase of the analysis. The <sup>fi</sup>nalized abstractions func tioned as the basis for our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

In the data analysis process, we consistently ensured alignment of data, theory, and <sup>fi</sup>ndings [54]. We applied the rule of triangulation [70] by using multiple data sources (i.e., interviews, observations, news reports, etc.) to <sup>fi</sup>lter the false preconceptions of interviewees and researchers. Theoretical saturation was reached when no new elements were any longer emerging in the analysis process and the addition of new information did not change our understanding of the phenomenon [71]. Finally, using a member-checking technique [72], we crossvalidated our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by inviting two Xiaomi employees and three Mi fans to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the accuracy of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. In the next section, we describe our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 4. Findings

In this section, we present the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our case study. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we describe the <sup>fi</sup>ve abstractions of summaries illustrated in Fig. 1.

## 4.1. Awareness of a sequential structure drives expected contribution

Representations that portray activities that contribute content: Online conversation is the most common form of interaction on Miui.com. Both employees and users join a crowd conversation by contributing content such as text and images. On Miui.com, “activities that contribute content” refer speci<sup>fi</sup>cally to posting behavior in the discussion threads. Generally, the posting of an idea or a detected bug starts a discussion thread. Follow-on participants (employees or Mi fans) contribute to the discussion thread by commenting on the posted idea or bug. As shown in Tables 1 and $^ { 2 , }$ the status of each discussion thread (which appears before the thread title) is visible. Di<sup>f</sup>erent statuses are set by employees, Mi fans, or the crowdsourcing system. The following quote suggests that every change of status signals to participants that certain actions regarding content have happened in the discussion thread.

“I like posting ideas that pop into my mind. Every time I posted something, I was waiting for the responses from them [employees], collected or dropped. I once proposed a new feature for the UI that could help users to better manage files on the phone. The idea sparked a heated discussion among Mi fans but did not get attention from Xiaomi in the week it was posted. I was much concerned about the company’s attitude. I checked the status occasionally to see whether some internal member had responded. The status changed to ‘Supplementary materials needed’ a couple of days later. I knew some employee wanted me to clarify, so I clicked on the thread and read his comment. He seemed to be confused by my initial description and some of my later comments that responded to others’ comments. Then I clarified my thoughts.”

How such representations drive expected contribution: The above quote also indicates that the visible status of each discussion thread (e.g., “Supplementary materials needed”) shows certain important social information (expectations) accompanied by actions contributing content to the discussion thread. Instead of inputting comments that are unhelpful for the ideation and testing processes (e.g., comments that are emotive, highly prescriptive, or content-free), Mi Fans are aware of the expected attributes of possible future contributions to the discussion thread and are held accountable for following the instructions and guidelines that each status indicates (e.g., “clari<sup>fi</sup>ed my thoughts”). In addition, aware of the status, employees are also accountable for making expected contributions and guiding each thread in the right direction.

“The ‘New idea’ status delivers the evaluating and commenting task to us. When responding to the idea, we understand that we have five options: ask for more details, call for more discussion, collect the idea, drop it, or combine it with similar ones.”

More importantly, such representations help to form a shared sequential structure for each discussion thread among participants on Miui.com. It re<sup>fl</sup>ects the social dynamic and context around each thread and illustrates a clear ideation history or process of a bug’s being detected and <sup>fi</sup>xed. Every participant can see the status of a discussion thread and utilize it as a resource for structuring their activities regarding content in that thread. For example, the “Combined” status of a discussion thread for an idea shows that an employee has combined the posted idea with similar ones into a new thread and Mi fans who are interested in the posted idea are expected to switch their attention and make contributions to the new thread (a practice of idea recombination). An Mi fan can still comment on the old thread but in doing so breaks the shared social norms that govern all discussion threads on Miui.com.

In addition, activities that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the extent to which participants are currently involved in a discussion thread are also visible. A light/dark icon inside each participant’s pro<sup>fi</sup>le signals whether he is currently logged on. A participant who is “talking” (typing) or “listening” (clicking and scrolling) to the thread can see who else are also “paying attention” to the thread at the same time by clicking the “current participants in the thread” icon.

“I like clicking the icon [the ‘current participants in the thread’ icon] since it tells me things like the audience size, whether Mi fans are gath ering or dispersing, and whether Mi fans I know have taken part in the discussion.

Hence, one side of participants can easily understand the extent to which the other side of participants have been/are currently involved in the discussion. Our data suggest that such understandings deliver knowledge of interests and attention of participants, which further drives active participating behavior of both Xiaomi’s employees and Mi fans. We elaborate this point in two aspects.

First, a contribution board shows the extent to which participants have been involved in the conversation and also re<sup>fl</sup>ects the interests and attention of participants of both sides. Participants of one side tend to actively participate in conversations that attract much attention from the other side. For example, noticing that many internal employees have commented in a conversation, Mi fans may sense that conversa tion draws attention from the company, and thus tend to be more in volved in the conversation. Aware of a crowded discussion among Mi fans, employees may realize that the discussion interests Mi fans and may o<sup>f</sup>er important knowledge on ideas or bugs. Hence, they tend to skim through it and join the conversation. The two quotes below from an Mi fan and an internal employee vividly illustrate our points:

“I think the board somehow reflects the taste of the company and such taste may drive Mi fans toward some conversations. I once proposed a way that might help to enhance the security of the fingerprint payment app in the interface. It stimulated little discussion among Mi fans at the beginning. Then the idea received attention from two engineers and they posted some comments to discuss the idea with me. Their names were on top of the board, signaling that perhaps the company was interested in the idea. Then Mi fans started to join the discussion.”

## 4.3. Awareness of knowledge distribution and social relationships drives eficient targeting

Representations that portray activities that build relationships: Each participant on Miui.com has a personal page linked to his/her name. On each personal page, a viewer can see the digital badges a participant has received, the communicative exchange between him and others, and the participants he has ties with. These three types of representations indicate “activities that build relationships,” which are described as the “contacting” or “friending” behaviors that have hap pened in a participant’s personal page. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, an employee may send a special badge to an Mi fan to praise his e<sup>f</sup>orts in testing updates and detecting bugs. Such an activity also indicates an attempt to create a relationship between the employee and the Mi fan. An Mi fan can make a post on an employee’s personal page to contact the employee. A participant’s visible communication network on his personal webpage also shows the “friending” activities between him and his “friends” on the platform, as illustrated by our data:

“I wanted to thank him for his help in solving the bugs. I sent a badge to him [a Mi fan]. Also, I’d like to know more about him since he seemed to be an expert in system configuration.”

“From there [the communication network], I know he [an engineer] is a guy who wants to be friends of Mi fans on the platform.”

How such representations drive e<sup>fi</sup>cient targeting: These dis plays reveal information that can be utilized to improve participants awareness of knowledge distribution (who knows what) and social relationships (who knows whom). Such knowledge can further structure participants’ behavior and foster e<sup>fi</sup>cient targeting between employees and Mi fans. Next, we demonstrate this point from two perspectives.

First, the knowledge distribution information of employees can help Mi fans to target the right Xiaomi employee to evaluate a posted idea or solve a detected bug. Mi fans are able to make inferences regarding “who knows what” from the badges that an employee has received and utilize these inferences to make behavioral decisions. For example, an Mi fan mentioned: “A number of ‘functional bug solver’ badges in an employee’s personal page indicates that he has been praised by Mi fans many times because of his knowledge and expertise in solving functional bugs.” Aware of this “who knows what” information, Mi fans may attempt to “draw the employee’s attention to a newly posted functional bug.” In addition, Mi fans’ option to see not only that employees are chatting with others but also the content of the messages exchanged also helps them to develop an accurate perception of “who knows what” in Xiaomi and makes it easy for Mi fans to <sup>fi</sup>nd the correct employees for help:

“The bug was posted. I waited for a couple of days and the problem wa still there. The app was stuck in that step every time I used it. Then it suddenly came into my mind that an engineer I knew had discussed the app with a Mi fan on his page… so I posted a comment on his page.”

Furthermore, Mi fans’ “targeting” and “notifying” behaviors based on the awareness of “who knows what” also improve employees’ e<sup>fi</sup> ciency in targeting posted ideas and bugs in their own areas of practice. The following quote from an engineer illustrates this point:

“Even in this specific area, I’m not able to handle all the bugs. Some are beyond my ability. We face numerous bugs detected by Mi fans every week and I need to find those I can solve. Sometimes, Mi fans may tell me which bugs I can remove on my page. It seems that they know me more than I do.”

Second, the social relationship information helps both sides to easily target participants from the other side whom they want to ask for help. The fact that participants can see with whom others chatted and whom are listed in those others’ communication networks provides an easy way for participants on Miui.com to become aware of “who knows whom.” Such awareness enables participants on the one hand to <sup>fi</sup>nd out who they know could help them to gain an introduction to parti cipants on the other hand with whom they want to communicate:

“If that bug really existed, then it would cause a lot of trouble…but I needed to know in which step he encountered that problem. I asked him to provide more detailed information but no response was received. I clicked his name and noticed that he was connected with one of my colleagues… so I reached out to that colleague first…”

In addition, our interviewees indicated that they may utilize their knowledge of “who knows whom” to infer “who knows who knows whom.” For example, an Mi fan said:

“I wanted to find a Xiaomi engineer who had expertise in solving communication issues with the user interface, but I didn’t know who was able to do that… I just knew that one of my friends on the platform knew a Xiaomi engineer. I asked my friend to ask that engineer if he knew any engineer who could handle that bug.”

In sum, our analysis indicates that displays showing activities that build relationships deliver important information regarding knowledge distribution (who knows what) and social relationships (who knows whom). The “who knows what” knowledge not only helps Mi fans to easily target the right employees but also helps employees to easily target posted ideas and bugs in their areas of expertise. The “who knows whom” knowledge helps both sides to easily target participants on the other side with whom they want to interact. Hence, both types of knowledge function to promote e<sup>fi</sup>cient targeting between employees and Mi fans. Next, we discuss constraints on visibility and how such constraints in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees’ and Mi fans’ activities on Miui.com.

4.4. Awareness of the invisibility of social information drives knowledge exploration

Invisibility of social information: On Miui.com, the abstract representations of activities do not convey all the important social information that participants could utilize as a resource to structure their interactive behaviors. Such constraints are manifested in the representation of activities that contribute content and the representations that reveal “who knows whom.” Participants on both sides may be aware of such constraints and tend to seek ways of exploring the hidden knowledge to overcome the constraints.

How such constraints drive information exploration: First, our data suggest that social information regarding how and why certain content is made is hidden in the database. For example, the “Answered”/“Collected” status shows that an employee has decided to drop/collect the posted idea. However, Mi fans may be aware that the internal process causing such decisions is invisible. Hence, they intend to search for unknown social information such as how and why those decisions are/have been made. They seek ways to “give voice to highly voted ideas that are not collected by the company or raise questions as to why certain unpopular ideas are accepted by the company.” Mi fans may take similar explorative actions when highly voted bugs are ignored by internal employees. As an Mi fan mentioned:

“They updated the mail app and then it could not connect to Exchange. A

Mi fan reported this bug and a lot of votes followed. I encountered the same problem. Then I noticed that the status was still ‘new detected bug four or five days later while some mail bugs with a low number of votes were removed. I didn’t know why and Xiaomi didn’t explain either. Thus, I launched a private chat with an engineer whom I knew was in charge of cleaning bugs for the mail app.”

In addition, as shown in Tables 1 and 2, most of the statuses are set by employees and thus certain activities of Mi fans, along with relevant social information, might be invisible. For instance, an employee said: “I changed the status of a discussion thread to ‘Further discussion needed’ since I needed to hear more from Mi fans. Then, I was interested in things such as how crowded the discussion was and whether the discussion was deep enough.” Aware that those types of social information are invisible (constraints), he found himself “frequently scrolling the conversation his tory” to explore the hidden knowledge so as to decide to drop/collect/ combine the posted idea (accountability).

Furthermore, although the third-party observer can infer “who knows whom” from a participant’s chat history and communication network, the strength and nature of the ties between a participant and his friends are invisible. Such social ambiguities are consistent with constraints in real-world situations. We may know two persons are friends but most of the time we do not know how often they interact with each other (strength of tie) and what types of topic they discuss privately (nature of tie). In such circumstances, both sides may attempt to infer the strength and nature of those relationships by exploring information from other sources. As one Mi fan said:

“I guessed they [a project manager and a Mi fan] might know each other very well because that Mi fan had sent a couple of badges to him [the PM] and they had much discussion on the project manager’s page. Then, I asked that Mi fan to help me approach him.”

## 4.5. Awareness of incorrect inferences of social information drives knowledge integration

Incorrect inferences of social information: The abstract representations of activities do not always lead to a correct awareness of social information. This form of constraint lies in the representations of activities that re<sup>fl</sup>ect involvement and the representations that improve participant understanding of “who knows what.” Awareness of such constraints drives participants of both sides to integrate knowledge acquired from various information sources to improve the accuracy of inferences.

How such constraints drive knowledge integration: On Miui.com, many of the involvement activities participants “see” are inferences. For example, a great number of comments by Mi fans in a thread does not mean that it is a “crowded” discussion. It is possible that a small number of Mi fans have input many comments. Maintaining such social ambiguities is crucial in building social translucence, and these ambiguities actually re<sup>fl</sup>ect “constraints” in face-to face communication. Drawing upon the social knowledge from their daily interactions within the physical world, participants may be aware of these constraints and adjust their behaviors.

It is like how just because someone looks at you, it doesn t mean that he is interested in what you are talking about. You hear a lot of buzz but it might be from only a few enthusiastic Mi fans. We are facing tons of ideas and bugs every day. Sometimes, we have to do some research to figure out the most popular ideas among Mi fans, or make decisions based on the company’s strategy.”

On Miui.com, such “research” is manifested in participants’ behavio of integrating knowledge from di<sup>f</sup>erent sources. As shown in the quote below, an Mi fan who just read an interesting post from an internal employee noticed that the employee had logged on (a “log on” icon in his pro<sup>fi</sup>le lit up). Although he wanted to launch a private chat with the employee, he also realized that the employee might be participating in other discussions as he was not listed under “current participants in the thread” icon. Hence, he chose to reply to the employee’s post in the thread instead. Such a behavioral decision is based on the integration of knowledge from two representations.

“I knew he had logged on but might be handling other things at that moment. It is like in QQ [the Chinese version of MSN] when someone set his status as ‘Busy.’ I replied to him in the thread and I knew he would notice. He might choose to reply to me immediately. I just guessed that he might not want to have a long private chat with someone to discuss that idea at that time.”

In addition, the awareness of knowledge distribution inferred from the representations might also be incorrect. For example, seeing an employee’ badges does not always lead to a correct awareness of “who knows what.” Receiving a couple of badges from Mi fans in a certain area does not mean that an employee knows how to solve that type of bug. He might just have noticed a crowded discussion about a detected bug, noti<sup>fi</sup>ed someone he knew who was able to solve that bug, and then replied in that discussion thread. Mi fans may, however, notice this constraint and integrate the knowledge drawn from di<sup>f</sup>erent representations to further structure their interactive behaviors:

“Although he had a badge entitled ‘System bug solver,’ from the chat messages I knew he might not be the person who actually solves the problems. However, I knew he should know someone who was able to clean the bug. Otherwise he would not get that badge… so I launched a chat with him.”

## 5. Discussions

## 5.1. Summary of findings

In this study, we begin by identifying an area that had received little attention in previous crowdsourcing research [52,73]: practices in which hosting <sup>fi</sup>rms can engage to promote collaboration between employees and users in crowdsourcing processes. The few extant stu dies related to collaborative interactions between internal employees and external users suggest that social translucence theory o<sup>f</sup>ers us a lens through which to advance our understanding in this unexplored area. More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it provides us with a theoretical basis to address the following research question: How does social translucence promote collaboration between internal employees and external users in the context of crowdsourcing?

Through investigating the socially translucent crowdsourcing process of Miui.com, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that Xiaomi has built certain abstract representations into the platform to portray three types of hidden activities: activities that contribute content, activities that re<sup>fl</sup>ect involvement, and activities that build relationships (visibility). Employees and Mi fans infer socially salient information from these abstract representations to improve their knowledge of 1) a sequential structure embedded with shared social norms, 2) interests and attention of participants from the other side, and 3) others’ knowledge distribution and social relationships (awareness). This social knowledge leads to expected contribution, active participation, and e<sup>fi</sup>cient targeting on the part of both Xiaomi’s employees and Mi fans (accountability). In addition, we also <sup>fi</sup>nd two types of constraints on visibility (invisibility and incorrect inferences of social information) and illustrate how participants’ awareness of these constraints drives their knowledge ex ploration and integration behaviors.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings complement previous work on crowdsourcing by showing that collaboration between employees and users can be promoted by building social translucence into the crowdsourcing process. In answering our research question, we identify <sup>fi</sup>ve abstractions of summaries that help to explain how a socially translucent crowdsourcing process fosters collaboration between employees and users. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings advance research on crowdsourcing processes, as we discuss in the following sections.

## 5.2. Theoretical implications

Four important theoretical implications can be drawn from our <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings hold implications for research on the content production process in crowdsourcing. Previous research indicates that crowdsourcing web-based architecture rarely o<sup>f</sup>ers any guidance to participants regarding the production of their comments in a dis cussion thread [15]. Such a structural weakness leads to certain unin tended consequences such as little discussion in a thread (e.g., Lego Mindstorms), many unvalued replies $( \boldsymbol { \mathrm { e . g . } } ,$ , IBM Innovation Jam), and little idea combination and recombination (e.g., GE’s Ecoimagination Challenge) [74]. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings address this architecture problem by suggesting that crowdsourcing sponsoring companies can build a translucent sequential structure to guide the content production process in crowd discussions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, on Miui.com, by joining discussions that follow the sequential structure and by learning to recognize the persistent traces left by previous discussion threads, internal employees and Mi fans generate a shared awareness of social norms and behavioral patterns embedded in the structure. Such an awareness guides them to contribute expected content, therefore fostering crowdsourcing colla boration and co-creation between them.

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the view that users’ participation in crowdsourcing conversations is motived by their awareness of the interest and attention the conversations have received from employees [22,68,69]. As shown in our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, by building abstract representations that portray the activities that re<sup>fl</sup>ect the involvement of internal employees, Xiaomi signals to Mi fans that the <sup>fi</sup>rm is paying attention to certain ideas or bugs, thereby motivating users to more actively join those conversations. In addition, our study extends this view by showing that employees’ participation in crowd discussions is also driven by their knowledge of the attention and interest the discussions attracts from users. Although many scholars have examined the parti cipation of external product users [e.g., 75–77], empirical research investigating the participation of internal employees is still nascent [78]. Successful crowdsourcing depends on reciprocal participation from both sides; we thus emphasize the equal importance of visualizing activities re<sup>fl</sup>ecting involvement of both external users and internal employees. Such visibility re<sup>fl</sup>ects the attention and interest of each side and motivates each other’s participation in crowdsourcing discussions.

Third, this study shows an attempt to incorporate knowledge acquired in social networking with knowledge of crowdsourcing. Studies of social networking suggest that participants’ awareness and perception of others’ knowledge distribution and social relationships on social platforms are often linked to their engagement [79], knowledge transfer [80], and collaboration [81] on the platforms. Although more and more <sup>fi</sup>rms integrate social networking functions within their crowdsourcing platforms (e.g., Threadless, Lego Mindstorms, and IBM Innovation Jam) [82–85], little attention has been paid to the impact of participants’ improved social knowledge, acquired from their exposure to others’ communications, on crowdsourcing collaboration and outcomes. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to <sup>fi</sup>lling this gap by illustrating that in the crowdsourcing process the awareness of others’ knowledge dis tribution and social relationships enables participants on one side (e.g., Mi fans) to more easily target the right people on the other side (e.g., employees). Such awareness also allows employees to more e<sup>fi</sup>ciently target conversations in their areas of expertise. We recommend that future research further incorporates the rich knowledge from social networking and crowdsourcing so as to advance research in both areas.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings demonstrate that crowd participants may engage in knowledge exploration and integrating behavior to overcome constraints on visibility in crowdsourcing processes. Although certain studies emphasize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of building a transparent communicative process [86–88], our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that it is almost impossible to develop a transparent crowdsourcing process because of privacy issues and the making of incorrect inferences regarding social information from abstract representations. Without doubt, constraints on visibility exist, which is why we adopt the concept of social translucence rather than social transparency. Although social translucence theory claims that individuals tend to utilize their learned social knowledge from their daily interactions to overcome constraints in a digitally translucent system [37], it has not explained what the constraints are and how individuals overcome them. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings contribute to social translucence theory by showing that constraints in crowdsourcing processes are manifested in the invisibility of and incorrect inferences regarding social information. To overcome the constraints, crowd participants may seek ways to explore hidden knowledge and integrate knowledge from di<sup>f</sup>erent sources to improve the accuracy of inferences.

## 5.3. Practical implications

From the practical perspective, our study highlights the necessity of promoting collaboration between external and internal participants to improve crowdsourcing outcomes. We recommend that host <sup>fi</sup>rms develop a strategic vision and action plan that adds social translucence to their crowdsourcing processes to foster such collaboration. In addition, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest the following practices for various relevant stakeholders in crowdsourcing processes.

First, we encourage host <sup>fi</sup>rms to build into their crowdsourcing platforms a shared translucent content production process that guides both internal and external participants to make expected contributions to crowd discussions. Crowdsourcing platforms such as Dell Ideastorm and Lego Mindstorms have already incorporated into their crowdsourcing processes certain functions that indicate the status of a discussion thread. However, most of the statuses are set by the internal side and only illustrate the expectations of the company to external users (i.e., collected, implemented, etc.). In addition, those statuses do not constitute a full path for content production in a discussion thread. Hence, we recommend crowdsourcing platform developers design functions (i.e., statuses of discussion threads) that form a shared sequential structure for the content production process of each discussion thread and allow participants of both sides to express their expectations for contents.

Second, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that participants from one side contribute more collaborative inputs to the discussion when they know that participants from the other side are interested in and paying attention to the same discussion. Hence, we advise host <sup>fi</sup>rms and platform devel opers to create abstract representations that visualize the involvements of both employees and users (i.e., number of participants who have contributed to a conversation, how much a participant has contributed to a conversation, number of participants who are currently involved in the conversation, etc.). In addition, managers and internal employees should realize that their involvement in the crowdsourcing processes will be perceived by external users and motivate them to actively participate in the crowdsourcing processes and create more innovative ideas. Thus, managers and internal employees are advised to spend time and e<sup>f</sup>orts in interacting with external users in the crowdsourcing processes, thereby improving the crowdsourcing outcomes.

Third, our study indicates that participants’ knowledge of social information, such as the knowledge distribution and relational networks of other participants, is important for collaboration between both sides. We suggest that host <sup>fi</sup>rms should incorporate social networking functions within their crowdsourcing platforms to illustrate the important relational information of crowd participants. Besides, participants of crowdsourcing platforms should not simply view the social networking function as an additional element of the platform. They are advised to use the social knowledge acquired from the functions to target the right people whom they want to seek help from and interact with during their participation in the crowdsourcing processes.

Fourth, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that both internal employees and external users might use what they have learnt from real-world interactions to overcome constraints in a digitally translucent crowdsourcing process. Therefore, we recommend that host <sup>fi</sup>rms create digitally translucent crowdsourcing processes closely resembling the communicative processes of the real world. Particularly, developers of crowdsourcing platform should incorporate more privacy functions into the crowdsourcing processes to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the real-world situation and provides platform users options to use and set those functions. Participants of crowdsourcing platforms should also realize that their awareness of social information from the platforms might be incorrect, and they are encouraged to explore and integrate di<sup>f</sup>erent sources of social information and utilize the social knowledge learned from daily interactions to overcome constraints on the visibility of online activities.

## 5.4. Limitations and future directions

This paper has several limitations that might a<sup>f</sup>ect its broader application. First, our focus on the collaboration between employees and users impels us to neglect the tension between competition and collaboration in the crowdsourcing process [89,90]. Competition between users might decrease their wiliness to collaborate with employees in discussions started by other users [91]. Hence, we advise future studies to explore the impact of social translucence on users’ awareness of competition and how translucence in<sup>fl</sup>uences the tension between competition and collaboration.

Second, although we have claimed that host <sup>fi</sup>rms should build socially “translucent” crowdsourcing processes, we have not provided explicit guidelines on “what should be represented” and “what should be hidden.” These guidelines are important because di<sup>f</sup>erent types of social information might be used for positive or negative ends, and their presence in various settings will likely structure interactions in di<sup>f</sup>erent manners [37]. Such a limitation may be attributed to the limited amount of data we collected from a single setting. Future research may further explore these guidelines to better understand the tradeo<sup>f</sup>s be tween visibility and constraint.

Third, as in any explorative study utilizing a single case, the generalizability of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings is limited [92]. For example, in this study, Mi fans show great enthusiasm toward Xiaomi and its products. Such a user characteristic is absent in many crowdsourcing settings; it may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the impact of translucence on collaboration in our case dif ferently from in other cases. Other factors such as user motivations, user challenges, and user bene<sup>fi</sup>ts [93,94] might also have similarly diverse e<sup>f</sup>ects. Therefore, we recommend that future research explore whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could be applied to other contexts.

Fourth, during our interviews, we guided the respondents to depict the representations and acquired knowledge <sup>fi</sup>rst. Following that, we asked them to recall their behavioral decisions based on the awareness of acquired knowledge. It is still arguable whether the respondents various behaviors (i.e., expected contribution, active participation, knowledge integration, etc.) are driven by their awareness of social information and constraints or by other factors such as external rewards and pursuit of reputation. Future research is suggested to incorporate more relevant control variables into the study to increase the rigor and cogency of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 6. Conclusion

In sum, our study extends prior research on crowdsourcing processes by focusing on the collaboration between internal employees and external users. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that host <sup>fi</sup>rms can promote collaboration between employees and users and achieve better crowd sourcing outcomes by building social translucence into their crowdsourcing processes. We recommend that future studies follow the preliminary <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this study to further explore practices that can help both scholars and practitioners to better understand how colla boration between employees and users is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by social translucence in crowdsourcing processes.
