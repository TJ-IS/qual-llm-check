# Policy for Sociotechnical Gen AI Assessment: Leveraging End Users

Ju Yeon Jung,<sup>1</sup> Susan J. Winter<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Notre Dame-IBM Technology Ethics Lab, USA, juyeon.jung@gmail.com <sup>2</sup> College of Information, University of Maryland, USA, sjwinter@umd.edu

## Abstract

Most organizations assessing Generative AI (Gen AI) rely on assessment materials produced by AI providers. These tend to be technical. To create a recommended sociotechnical assessment, the technical needs to be coupled with the social. This policy editorial suggests taking advantage of the knowledge of end users who are likely to use Gen AI in their work and provides an illustrative framework for doing so.

Keywords: AI Assessment, Sociotechnical, End User, AI Adoption

John King was the accepting senior editor. This editorial was submitted on July 24, 2024 and underwent five revisions.

## 1 Introduction

Advances in generative AI (Gen AI) that use large language models (LLMs) have spurred the development and release of new applications that promise improved productivity on the assumption that these new tools will change how tasks are performed. But promised improvements are proving elusive. An increasing chorus of commentators are arguing that improving productivity requires tackling the challenges of choosing, tailoring, and using Gen AI tools to fit particular work settings, and training workers to use AI productively for those tasks. Matching tools to work is a well-known tenet of the sociotechnical approach (Winter et al., 2014), but many organizations are assessing Gen AI without properly considering its fit with the work. They are influenced primarily by the technical knowledge of Gen AI provided by vendors and internal experts. Technical knowledge of Gen AI has a role to play in reaping productivity from work systems, but in-depth knowledge of the work from the end user is also integral to productivity gains from any Gen AI initiative. This policy editorial provides guidance for the cost-effective incorporation of end users into the assessment of Gen AI to help realize productivity.

## 2 The Gen AI Adoption Decision

Gen AI has garnered increased attention with the release of new tools that leverage statistical patterns in large language databases. The tools generate content (text, images, videos, audio, code, etc.) based on user-provided prompts. Many hope that Gen AI will change work practices and lead to productivity gains. Providers have poured billions of dollars into commercializing Gen AI capabilities and providing end users with access to Gen AI models. Overall investment in Gen AI has risen to over \$25 billion. The number of newly funded AI companies has increased, jumping to over 1,800 (40% growth) in a single year (Maslej et al., 2024). While nearly 9 out of 10 executives surveyed identified Gen AI as a top-three tech priority for 2024 (Apotheker et al., 2024), 9 out of 10 executives expressed hesitation because of uncertainty around benefits, lack of resources to invest, or lack of a clear strategy for its use. Even when adopted, Gen AI use has been limited to routine coding tasks, summarizing meetings or reports, and similar low-level tasks (Wiggers, 2024). Gen AI incorporation into companies’ workflows has remained low compared to the hopes surrounding this technology, and few Gen AI providers have turned a profit.

Hesitation makes sense, given the potential Gen AI risks. Providers are facing lawsuits over copyright infringements, and Gen AI outputs can be discriminatory, biased, or even factually incorrect (errors are often termed “hallucinations”). Adopters risk monetary and reputational losses from potential lawsuits and reduced goodwill. For example, Air Canada lost a 2024 lawsuit filed by a passenger who followed the advice given by a chatbot on the airline’s website, part of its AI initiative, to purchase a ticket and apply later for a bereavement refund. The airline denied the refund and argued that the chatbot was “a separate legal entity that is responsible for its actions.” The court held that the airline had engaged in “negligent misrepresentation” and ordered it to pay the passenger a partial refund, as well as damages and court fees (Garcia, 2024). Air Canada withdrew the chatbot.

Regulatory uncertainty has also increased the risk of adopting Gen AI tools. In 2023 alone, 150 state-level AIrelated bills were proposed in the US, with 38 of them passing. This was up from 61 proposed bills the previous year. The number of proposed federal-level AI bills in the US Congress went from 88 in 2022 to 181 in 2023 (Maslej et al., 2024). The European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) is wide-ranging and targets how companies develop and deploy AI models. Monitoring and ensuring compliance in an immature and rapidly evolving regulatory ecosystem increases cost and reputation risks.

Gen AI is a complicated landscape. Unless payoffs like productivity gains are significant and likely, why take the risk? Assessing Gen AI tools is often difficult. Most organizations start with assessments developed by technical experts (AI providers and in-house). These assessments tend to focus on technical issues: algorithms, models, and datasets. Payoffs that rely on the ability to match technology to end user work also require an adequate understanding of end users’ work and the integration of technical and work knowledge in a sociotechnical approach. This approach leverages the technical strengths of AI providers, which are often available and already paid for while including end user experts on the work itself. This makes end users partners in Gen AI adoption and use decisions.

This editorial presumes that the technical is readily available for Gen AI assessment. It offers policy guidance for navigating the Gen AI landscape based on the sociotechnical approach of integrating technology and work. Adoption decisions are based on the assessment of both technical expertise available from Gen AI providers and expertise obtained from end users. The strategy adds organizational context, goals, strategies, workflows, stakeholder relationships, resources, and work practices to technology. No single technology is effective across all industries and contexts. In this case, “one size fits all” will not work. By bringing organizational end users into assessment, complementary components of the sociotechnical approach can be included.

## 3 The Role of End Users in Gen AI Assessment

Gen AI’s potential to improve productivity can be assessed at multiple levels: AI algorithms, datasets, user-system interactions, and applications (Feuerriegel et al., 2024). But Gen AI assessment can be uneven. The focus is often mostly on the algorithm level, on technical issues such as “algorithmic opacity” (how or why algorithms reach results), auditability, and whether Gen AI tools are black-boxed (Buhmann et al., 2020). Technical aspects like training data used to develop Gen AI models are also often assessed because they can result in misrepresentation and bias. External Gen AI providers such as OpenAI and Microsoft attend to technical issues and have a sense of possible uses. Yet their interest in selling products means they might not align completely with customer expectations of payoffs, such as productivity gains. Experts in technical issues can assess such issues relevant to productivity but might leave important considerations out.

We encourage organizations to incorporate missing components of the sociotechnical approach to Gen AI assessment, such as productivity improvements expected from Gen AI tools that support end user work. Effective assessment requires organizations to combine knowledge of the technology with an understanding of the organizational and competitive context impacted by work practices (Kellogg et al., 2024). In-house experts on Gen AI with sufficient technical understanding might contribute to understanding the organizational and competitive context but seldom have in-depth knowledge of the work that end users do.

The technical is important in assessing Gen AI. The real-world impacts of Gen AI tools include how the tools are deployed and shaped by end users for work in a specific organizational setting. The sociotechnical approach focuses on how technologies are embedded in everyday work practices in a larger context understood by experienced end users. How end users should implement and manage Gen AI to include work practices has been the focus of a growing body of research but still lags in terms of transforming and implementing operational structures and work practices suitable for Gen AI (e.g., Iansiti & Lakhani, 2020) and specifying organizational requirements for Gen AI implementation (e.g., Sjödin et al., 2021). Gen AI adoption is often framed in abstract and positive terms that fail to provide practical guidance on how decision makers should engage end users to assess Gen AI in context.

End users are complementary to providers in Gen AI assessment. Integrating end users into Gen AI assessment can provide practical benefits for applying Gen AI in work contexts. Organizational leaders should include end users along with providers to develop a better understanding of the value of Gen AI to the firm.

End users can provide deep, context-specific assessment of Gen AI by highlighting needs and ways of lowering Gen AI risks and surfacing productivity opportunities. If end users can be held responsible for the negative consequences of Gen AI use, this will become an issue with increasing regulation.

## 4 Setting up a Sociotechnical Gen AI Assessment Process

Engaging end users successfully in Gen AI assessment requires attention to policy, thoughtful planning, and appropriate training. A sociotechnical assessment process for Gen AI tools requires systems-level and end user-oriented policies that bring together Gen AI providers, in-house experts, and the potential end users of these tools. Gen AI providers can provide technical documentation on Gen AI, though additional work might be needed to make documentation accessible to nontechnical end users. Providers could provide “datasheets” to summarize the motivation, composition, collection, and intended use case datasets for Gen AI end user training, especially when end users are consumers of these datasets (see Gebru et al., 2021). Datasheets or other means of conveying datasets might need to be made easier to read and interpret. End users might need to be trained in appropriate assessment processes. Providers could give “model cards” for detailing Gen AI model performance, intended use cases, and ethical considerations (see Mitchell et al., 2019). Providers could also make results available from internal Gen AI audits that combine requirements analysis, use case ethics reviews, social impact assessments, and failure mode analysis (Raji et al., 2020).

End users can be integrated into assessment processes, combining no-code or low-code exploration of the technical and allowing end users to explore and evaluate different types of bias in datasets and algorithms. However, there are limits to what can be done since many Gen AI datasets, models, and algorithms are proprietary and Gen AI providers might have better access than end users. Of course, there are still open questions about the integration of end users into the assessment process that remain to be explored. Future research is needed to determine what end users can really contribute, what organizations will actually allow them to do, how trade-offs will be managed, who will be involved in assessment, what kind of training is needed, how mistakes are handled, etc.

The challenge is to bring end users to the point where they can perform their own assessments of Gen AI focused on how these tools relate to their work, with end users providing data to Gen AI providers or auditors. To do so, end users should learn to use tools like “the sociotechnical matrix” (see Sloane et al., 2022) to assess Gen AI context, goals, data, functions, and the construct validity of assumptions used by Gen AI models with respect to specific organizational contexts. End users could also identify possible “abstraction traps,” where the abstract technical systems of Gen AI fail to account for interactions in particular social contexts (Selbst et al., 2019). Coordinative building blocks could facilitate a sociotechnical Gen AI assessment by end users. The organization could further support end users by placing such building blocks into a framework for systematic application.

## 5 A Sociotechnical Gen AI Assessment Framework for End Users

End users seldom create Gen AI tools because they usually do not fully understand the underlying technologies. However, training end users in assessment combining the social and the technical could enable them to contribute to Gen AI assessments. End users could be guided in assessing Gen AI tools. Table 1 shows a sample sociotechnical Gen AI assessment matrix that conceives of Gen AI as a technology (e.g., datasets and algorithms) that interacts with end users, with resulting impacts on the organization (Weidinger et al., 2023). This framework introduces four criteria (relevance, ethics, functionality, and feasibility)—(1) relevance: whether the intended use of the Gen AI provides value to the end user, whether that value outweighs risks and costs, and whether outputs are relevant to end user problems and practices; (2) ethics: whether end users can ensure that the Gen AI will be used ethically in terms of its fairness, explainability, uses that respect privacy, provide autonomy, and enable redressability; (3) functionality: whether the Gen AI tool functions as intended and is robust and safe to use; and (4) feasibility: whether the end user has what is required for the Gen AI to function as intended over time (e.g. data quality and model validity), and whether the Gen AI system can be used effectively as intended by end users as part of everyday practice (e.g. usability, infrastructure, human resources).

This framework is only one example of how to coordinate the sociotechnical assessment of Gen AI tools. It is illustrative of a strategy that integrates both the assessment of technical aspects (e.g., dataset features, model performance metrics) with the concerns of the nontechnical end users (e.g., dataset and AI model appropriateness for the particular organizational setting, tasks, and end user challenges). This integration allows end users to be more equal participants in the Gen AI assessment process as they think through how Gen AI might affect their work by generating specific scenarios for Gen AI use, which will help end users become more proficient in thinking through the potential impacts of Gen AI.

Drawing on this framework, end users can probe the appropriateness of Gen AI tools for their particular problems—for example, job performance. End users might use datasheets and model cards provided by the Gen AI provider and consider how “good” and “bad” performance is defined and measured by data quality, model validity, relevance of the data, and use of the model in job performance. End users might also assess the relevance of Gen AI outputs to improving job performance in their work practices and settings, given the organization’s vision and values and the system’s usability, or identify its potential impacts on the organization as a whole or on employees of different demographics or those who perform different tasks.

End users might also evaluate the ethical implications of Gen AI-based applications in their particular work context, such as whether a facial recognition system for time and attendance monitoring meets criteria for fairness and privacy or conflicts with organizational values. Such an analysis could identify potential negative consequences and help identify mitigation opportunities (Toffel & Aal, 2024).

This is not the only sociotechnical framework that can be used. Decision makers could work with end users to develop frameworks that fit their needs and resources, defining and scoping assessment and selecting and evaluating criteria, as appropriate. The resulting framework might require the involvement of both end users and Gen AI providers. Providers might be needed to evaluate technical or proprietary Gen AI features, even though technical assessments (e.g., functionality) are only a part of Gen AI assessment. The assessment of some elements might require a collaborative effort involving both end users and Gen AI providers. Some elements of the assessment may rely solely on end users or solely on providers, but this would likely be rare. A sociotechnical assessment usually provides more complete information and enables a more robust decision-making process. This policy editorial’s focus on end users in Gen AI assessment is intended to leverage domain expertise and knowledge of the context in which Gen AI might be used.

The deployment of an exploratory policy framework for assessing Gen AI tools could enable end users to identify and highlight Gen AI requirements, facilitating feedback between Gen AI providers and end users. Such frameworks might inform the customization of Gen AI tools, allowing modifications to better fit the work context. End user assessments might also provide feedback to inform Gen AI provider designs and increase the value of resulting Gen AI tools.

## 6 Conclusion

Fierce competition in the market might drive Gen AI providers toward bigger, more computationally powerful models. Gen AI regulations and industry guidelines are emergent and not yet stable. There is much to inform Gen AI assessment, and organizations are encouraged to get as much as they can from Gen AI providers. This policy editorial argues that organizations assessing the adoption of Gen AI should use a sociotechnical approach to assessment by empowering end users to assess Gen AI impacts on their own terms to clarify opportunities and mitigate risks. The goal is to help organizations make sound Gen AI decisions. End users are complementary to Gen AI providers, and user insights into context-specific user needs could help Gen AI providers build more valuable products and services.

A sociotechnical approach to Gen AI assessment that puts end users into a position that is complementary to Gen AI providers might require compensatory effort given the preference often given to Gen AI providers. End users might need to be front and center for a time. End users need to be cultivated into users that can “express themselves … independent of high-tech scribes” (Fischer et al., 2017, p.62). Such Gen AI assessment is grounded in the tradition of end user system development. This tradition enables end user design, modification, and adaptation of software systems to better align with domains of practice (Paternò, 2013). Allowing end users to customize Gen AI to meet their needs in context should increase Gen AI benefits. End user assessment of Gen AI provides a foundation for end user Gen AI development, and Gen AI advancements should empower rather than marginalize end users, for whom Gen AI purportedly provides value.
