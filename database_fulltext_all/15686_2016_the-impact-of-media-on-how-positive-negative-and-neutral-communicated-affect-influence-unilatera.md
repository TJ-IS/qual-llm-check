# The impact of media on how positive, negative, and neutral communicated affect influence unilateral concessions during negotiations

Norman A. Johnson<sup>1</sup>, Randolph B. Cooper<sup>1</sup> and Richard D. Holowczak<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, Houston, USA; <sup>2</sup>Zicklin School of Business, Baruch College, City University of New York, New York, USA

Correspondence: Norman A. Johnson, Decision and Information, Sciences & MIS, C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, 334 Melcher Hall, Room 280C, Houston, TX 77204-602, USA. Tel: +713-743-4164; Fax: +713-743-4940; E-mail: njohnson@bauer.uh.edu

## Abstract

In the online environment, audio and instant messaging (IM) media are quite commonly used by people to communicate with each other and make offers as they negotiate. While we know much about how IM and audio differ, we know very little about how offers that are favorable to the recipient (termed unilateral concessions) are affected by what and how people communicate over these media. The purpose of this study is twofold – (1) to examine how such concessions are influenced by communication that is either neutral, or positive, or negative in affect; and (2) to determine how the use of IM, relative to the use of audio, influences the effects of these types of communication on unilateral concessions. We develop a research model based on prosocial theory, which suggests that negotiators using audio are predisposed to interpret their partners’ motivations and behaviors in a positive (prosocial) light while negotiators using IM are predisposed to interpret their partners’ motivations and behaviors in a negative (competitive) light. We manipulate the use of IM and audio in an

experiment designed to test predictions based on this theory. Our work provides theoretical and empirical support for the idea that communications other than concessions (such as positive, neutral, and negative affect) can lead to more or less self-sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce depending on the medium employed, and thereby motivate negotiators to make greater or fewer unilateral concessions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we found that (1) positive affect comments can increase unilateral concession independent of the medium used by negotiators; (2) neutral affect comments can increase unilateral concession when negotiators use audio, but have little impact when they use IM; and (3) negative affect comments can decrease unilateral concession when negotiators use audio, but can increase unilateral concession when they use IM. These results provide insights to researchers and practical guidance for negotiators. European Journal of Information Systems advance online publication, 22 March 2016; doi:10.1057/ejis.2016.4

## Introduction

Over two decades ago, Holsapple et al (1991) pointed out that understanding social behavior is central to the design of negotiation support systems. A major component of such systems is the electronic linkage, which enables negotiators to communicate with each other (Lim and Benbasat, 1993). In this study, we focus on two kinds of this linkage, synchronous text-based instant messaging (IM, over which people communicate by text only) and audio (such as telephone or VoIP, over which people communicate orally only)

as these are commonly used in today’s online business settings (Johnson and Cooper, 2015). In some over-thecounter markets where U.S. monetary securities are traded, for example, estimates are that 80% of the time IM is used and 20% of the time audio is used for price negotiations (Duf<sup>fi</sup>e et al, 2005; Anonymous, 2011). Further, as an alternate marketplace to eBay, Bonanza provides an opportunity for buyers and sellers to engage in one-on-one price negotiations using IM in order to reach agreements (CrunchBase, 2012). In addition to negotiation sites, retail organizations are <sup>fi</sup>nding IM negotiations pro<sup>fi</sup>table. For example, WideStorm.com provides IM software that is implemented on organizations’ Websites and that enables consumers and vehicle dealers to negotiate the price of a vehicle entirely online (Gibson, 2010). More recently, Amazon added a new feature to its Website, termed ‘make an offer’, which allows individual customers to have private negotiations with individual sellers (Smith, 2014).

In negotiations, an individual and his or her partner (the terms we use to describe the opposing parties to a negotiation) have a common goal of reaching agreement and also have divergent goals in that they prefer different terms of agreement (Thompson and Hrebec, 1996). With negotiations over the price of an object (a type of what is known as distributive negotiations), for example, sellers typically prefer higher and buyers prefer lower agreement prices. However, as the negotiation process is under way, each price offer by an individual can include a concession, in which the individual changes his or her previous offer in support or in favor of the partner’s interests and which results in a reduction of bene<sup>fi</sup>t to the individual (Pruitt, 1981). One important type of concession is a unilateral concession, which is a favorable offer made by an individual without an explicit expectation of a subsequent concession by his or her partner, but typically with the hope that the partner will reciprocate with a concession (Lawler et al, 1999). These concessions are important for a number of reasons. For example: (1) they help to determine the type of agreement reached between negotiators, (2) they increase the likelihood of agreement, and (3) they determine how satis<sup>fi</sup>ed negotiators are with each other (Pruitt, 1981; Kwon and Weingart, 2004).

Researchers argue that getting unilateral concessions from the negotiating partner is an important goal for an individual (Pruitt, 1981; Kwon and Weingart, 2004). However, there is a lack of research on how to achieve the goal of encouraging negotiators to offer unilateral concessions during the negotiation process. In addition, we know that media differ on many dimensions and that these differences can have pervasive effects on communication processes and the goals of users (Putnam and Roloff, 1992; Topi et al, 2002). There is also evidence that communicated affect can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the negotiation process (Johnson et al, 2008). However, we do not know how the interaction of different media with the communication of positive, neutral, and negative affect can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the offering of unilateral concessions. The purpose of this study is, therefore, twofold – (1) to examine how unilateral concessions are encouraged or discouraged by communication that is either neutral, or positive, or negative in affect; and (2) to determine how the use of IM, relative to the use of audio, moderates the effects of these types of communication on such concessions.

We were able to <sup>fi</sup>nd only one article (see Kopelman et al, 2006) that examined the range of positive, negative, and neutral affect communicated in negotiations. Kopelman et al describe two experiments relevant to our paper. Their Experiment 1 examined the degree to which positive vs negative communicated affect in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the likelihood of reaching agreement. Their Experiment 3 examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of positive, neutral, and negative communicated affect on the amount of concession included in a single counteroffer. Our study differs from these in a number of ways. One of these ways is that rather than having a dependent variable that represents the end result of negotiations (their Experiment 1) or the result of a single counteroffer (their Experiment 3), we observe concession making as it occurs throughout a relatively unconstrained negotiation process. Another way is that we examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of positive, neutral, and negative affect in the contexts of audio vs IM communication. Our work also differs with respect to the theory used. Kopelman et al employ cooperation theory, which assumes that negotiators either have face-to-face contact or have imagined themselves to be in such contact (Johnson et al, 2009). In contrast, our study employs prosocial theory, which is not tied to face-to-face contexts, and, as described below, is more appropriate for typical negotiation contexts.

As it pertains to communicated affect and the moderation of such communication by IM and audio media, our work is similar to that of Johnson et al (2009) and Johnson and Cooper (2015). Johnson et al (2009) found no direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence of aggregated positive affect on total concessions. Our current study examines the in<sup>fl</sup>uences resulting from a disaggregated spectrum of positive, neutral, and negative communicated affect, and we hypothesized and found a direct in<sup>fl</sup>uence resulting from our disaggregated positive communicated affect. In addition, our current study’s dependent variable is unilateral concession. Though this is part of total concession, as described above, unilateral concession is very important in its own right. Johnson and Cooper (2015) found that an individual’s negative affect signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced his or her partner’s ending concessions. As ending concession can occur only once, at the end of negotiations, Johnson and Cooper (2015) were unable to examine the process by which concessions were offered. In contrast, our current examination of unilateral concessions enabled us to focus on the negotiation process itself exploring the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of communicated affect on unilateral concession as the negotiation process unfolds, rather than relating their totals after negotiations end.

## Theoretical foundation

Our work is in accord with the research philosophy of behavioral decision researchers, who focus on how negotiators actually make decisions (e.g., Thompson, 1998)

rather than on the philosophies of researchers who employ game theory and mathematical analyses, which assume fully rational negotiators. Acknowledging that negotiators do not intuitively act rationally, behavioral decision researchers encourage the use of descriptions of actual negotiations in order to prescribe negotiation strategies (Bazerman et al, 2000).

Trust is a key concept that has been used to explain negotiators’ behaviors (Lewicki et al, 1998; Thompson et al, 2010). Trust involves self-interest: an individual trusts the negotiation partner to the degree that the individual believes the partner will not take advantage of the individual. Further, it has been found that the individual is generally more likely to offer concessions when the individual trusts the partner (Pruitt, 1981; Thompson et al, 2010), and that this trust can be gained by the partner offering concessions to the individual (Pillutla et al, 2003). However, there are times when individuals come to negotiations with concerns other than, or in addition to, selfinterest. At times individuals behave in ways that bene<sup>fi</sup>t others while incurring costs to themselves, and this can be the essence of unilateral concession making, which is the focus of this study. Prosocial theory is relatively broad in scope and is able to account for such behavior, as well as for trust-related behavior. Thus, we use prosocial theory to help explain our predictions pertaining to behaviors we relate to unilateral concessions.

A major focus of our study is an examination of how the media over which negotiations occur can in<sup>fl</sup>uence an individual’s interpretations of his or her negotiation partner’s motivations and behavior. We argue below that, when using audio for negotiations, individuals tend to interpret the partners’ motivations and behavior from a more prosocial perspective (or mental model); when using IM for negotiations, individuals tend to interpret the partners’ motivations and behavior from a more competitive perspective (or mental model). The medium employed, thereby, systematically biases individuals’ understandings of, and reactions to, their partners’ communications.

In the sections that follow, we cover the ideas that are germane to our research model. We begin with a description of how unilateral concession <sup>fi</sup>ts within the negotiation process, followed by a description of prosocial behavior and how it relates to negotiations. We then focus on how different media can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree to which individuals have a more prosocial vs competitive bias when interpreting and reacting to partner communications. This is followed by a discussion on the communication of affect and how it can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the negotiation process. We end with a description of the negotiation context of interest, including the type of negotiation and the object of negotiation.

## Negotiation and unilateral concession

Negotiation entails back-and-forth communication involving a great deal of give and take with the objective of reaching agreement (Fisher and Ury, 1991). For example, in a negotiation over the price of an item, an agreement is reached when both negotiators’ offers are the same. Negotiation differs from the general notion of group decision making in terms of concession. Concessions occur when one negotiator makes an offer that bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the interest of his or her negotiating partner, while reducing his or her own bene<sup>fi</sup>t, and are typically required to reach agreement (Pruitt, 1981). For example, when negotiating the sale of an asset, the seller offers a price that he or she is willing to accept as payment. The buyer may then make a counteroffer lower than that of the seller’s offer. The seller may then make a counteroffer that is lower than his or her <sup>fi</sup>rst offer but greater than the buyer’s offer. In response to the seller’s second offer, the buyer may make another offer greater than his or her <sup>fi</sup>rst offer. The difference between the seller’s <sup>fi</sup>rst and second offers represents a concession by the seller; the difference between the buyer’s second and <sup>fi</sup>rst offers represents a concession by the buyer. In this way, concession is ritualized as an exchange of offers and counteroffers (Zartman, 1977).

Concessions can take three forms. As described earlier, unilateral concessions are the focus of this study, and are those made by an individual without an explicit expectation of a subsequent concession by the partner, but with the hope that the partner will reciprocate with a concession. Reciprocal concessions pay back unilateral concessions (Malhotra, 2004). Ending concessions are those that go beyond reciprocation and ensure that negotiations end in agreement (Pruitt, 1981). Examples of these types of concessions are illustrated in Table 1, where the seller initially offers 30 and the buyer counters with 16. The seller’s next offer is 27, and the difference of 3 (the amount that it is less than the seller’s initial offer) represents the unilateral concession by the seller. When the buyer then counters with the offer 20, the difference of 4 (the amount that it is more than his or her initial offer) represents two types of concessions by the buyer – a reciprocal concession of 3 and a unilateral concession of 1. When the seller then counters with the offer 25, the difference of 2 (the amount that it is less than the seller’s immediately previous offer) represents by the seller a reciprocal concession of 1 and a unilateral concession of 1. When the buyer agrees with an offer of 25, the difference of 5 (the amount that it is more than the buyer’s immediately previous offer) represents by the buyer a reciprocal concession of 1 and an ending concession of 4.

As illustrated in Table 1, unilateral concessions can be offered by both negotiating parties and can occur throughout the negotiation process. Though such concessions may seem counter to an individual’s best interests, there are many times when unilateral concessions are strategically appropriate (Pruitt, 1981). For example, it is typically unlikely that the buyer and seller will agree on a price with their <sup>fi</sup>rst offers; therefore, at least one unilateral concession is required in order to reach agreement. In addition, a unilateral concession may be the most effective way to keep negotiations from ending in an impasse, which would forego bene<sup>fi</sup>ts associated with agreement (Brett et al, 1998). Negotiated agreements, therefore, typically require unilateral concession making. We next describe the prosocial behavior literature that we will use to help identify how communication between negotiators can affect the likelihood of unilateral concessions and, thereby, can affect the likelihood of a negotiated agreement.

## Prosocial behavior

Prosocial behavior is that which is ‘intentionally bene<sup>fi</sup>cial to others (not necessarily without self-interest) and involving some (self) sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce’ (Lindenberg, 2006, p. 23). Recent research in such <sup>fi</sup>elds as sociology, economics, and social psychology is ‘all at least compatible with the position that [prosocial behavior] is part of human nature. [That is, individuals have] … other-regarding sentiments … [ and they sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce] … for others to whom they are not related’ (Piliavin and Charng, 1990, p. 29). Of speci<sup>fi</sup>c interest here are the widespread and consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings that, during economic transactions, individuals’ concerns can go beyond their own material gains to incorporate the material gains of others. These other-directed concerns re<sup>fl</sup>ect individuals’ internalized norms of good conduct, which include caring for the well-being of others (Smith, 1759 [1982]; Arrow, 1981; Fehr and Schmidt, 2006; Polidori and Teobaldelli, 2013). This occurs because individuals ‘derive positive utility from exhibiting behavior that corresponds to their ideas about what is right and what is wrong’ (Polidori and Teobaldelli, 2013, p. 286), and is ‘important for bilateral negotiations’ (Fehr and Schmidt, 2006, p. 683). Prosocial behavior occurs even between anonymous strangers in onetime interactions (McGuire, 2003).

A number of prosocial behavior theories exist in sociology, psychology, and economics, though they vary in terms of their foci on dependent and independent variables. We base our discussions on Lindenberg’s (2006) work, which integrates the various prosocial theories.

Prosocial behavior can manifest in different forms. For example, an individual will abstain ‘ … from the temptation to breach implicit or explicit agreements or promises … [and] will refrain from hurting others even at a cost to him- or herself’ (Lindenberg et al, 2006, p. 9). In addition, a person’s behavior can be fair to the degree that he or she ‘… will not seek to maximize his [or her] share of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and minimize his [or her] share of the costs, but take his [or her] “fair share” of both’ (Lindenberg et al, 2006, p. 9). Thus, an individual acts more prosocially to the degree that he or she is more concerned for the others’ interests (i.e., is more benevolent), as opposed to selfinterest. In this way, prosocial behavior can include, as well as go beyond, that associated with trusting behavior, in that it can also involve self-sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce.

There are three principle prosocial goals. Of the three goals, the gain goal focuses on resource growth, which is closely tied to the economic notion of self-interest. This goal is typical in contexts where negotiations over assets or resources, such as money, occur between anonymous strangers. (See Appendix A for a discussion of the other two goals and the rationale as to why we are focusing on the gain goal.) With a gain goal, negotiators’ mental models can be prosocial or competitive. The prosocial mental model links behaviors such as fairness to greater likelihoods of reaching a fair agreement, and predisposes negotiators to be fair and benevolent. The competitive mental model links behaviors such as threats and holding <sup>fi</sup>rm to greater likelihoods of getting greater concessions from the negotiating partner, and predisposes negotiators to be more sel<sup>fi</sup>sh. Thus, the likelihood and the degree to which an individual exhibits prosocial behaviors of fairness and benevolence vs sel<sup>fi</sup>shness are dependent on which mental model is dominant.

The dominant mental model also results in individuals becoming sensitive to contextual cues that are important to elements in that model, and interpreting them based on that model. For example, an individual with a dominant competitive mental model would be more aware of cues that help the individual take advantage of the negotiating partner, such as indications that the partner is not a skilled negotiator. In addition, those cues would be interpreted from a competitive perspective; for example, positive affect communication, such as friendliness, would be viewed as a sign of being a weak negotiator. In contrast, cues associated with the fair and benevolent distribution of resources would be more salient with a dominant prosocial mental model; communicated affect, such as friendliness, from this perspective would be viewed as a willingness to behave in a fair manner.

As described next, whether IM or audio media are used in negotiations plays a part in determining the degree to which an individual’s prosocial or a competitive mental model is more dominant.

## The in<sup>fl</sup>uence of media on mental models

Situational factors can affect perceptions regarding the social context and, thereby, in<sup>fl</sup>uence the dominance of an individual’s prosocial or competitive mental model (Lindenberg, 2006). These perceptions include an individual’s expectations regarding the relationship he or she will have with the negotiation partner (Flache and Macy, 2006; Lindengerg, 2006). For example, individuals that expect an egocentric relationship behave in ways that show more concern for themselves and less concern for others (Oesch and Murnighan, 2003). In the following discussions, we argue that the use of IM (audio) is a situational factor that results in individuals becoming more (less) egocentric and therefore having more dominant competitive (prosocial) mental models.

Our negotiation task is primarily one of convergence, which requires the parties to ‘reach a common understanding and to mutually agree that they have achieved this understanding’ (Dennis et al, 2008, p. 580). Negotiation therefore bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from media that support synchronicity (Dennis et al, 2008). Though media have differing abilities to support synchronicity based on their different sets of capabilities, in general, audio and IM channels have similar synchronicity support, in that they both are rated as medium (Dennis et al, 2008).

However, research conducted since the early 1970s has demonstrated many other ways in which audio and IM media can affect communication and, therefore, perceptions among individuals (Johnson and Cooper, 2015). This research stream has resulted in numerous theories, including social presence theory (Short et al, 1976), media richness theory (Daft and Lengel, 1986), the social identity model of deindividuation effects (Postmes and Spears, 1998), cuelessness theory (Rutter, 1987), and reduced social cues theory (Sproull and Kiesler, 1986), which suggest that the use of IM, as compared with audio, reduces individuals’ abilities to convey nonverbal cues and provide quick feedback, which signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces relationship and socially oriented communication (Hayne and Rice, 1997; Spears et al, 2002).

As a result, IM promotes perceptions of greater social isolation (Friedman and Currall, 2003) because, for example, communication is ‘received and written while the writer is in isolation, staring at a computer screen’, which makes IM ‘profoundly asocial’ (Friedman and Currall, 2003, p. 1329). This results in an individual being less aware that he or she is negotiating with another speci<sup>fi</sup>c individual (Weisband and Atwater, 1999) and focuses the individual’s attention on himself or herself in the here and now (Trope and Liberman, 2010). This self-interested, egocentric, focus can lead a negotiating individual to have a relatively dominant competitive mental model (Lindenberg, 2006). In general, an element of this and other kinds of mental models is co-orientation, which means that each individual assumes that his or her partner uses the same mental model (Lindenberg, 2006). Thus, individuals holding dominant competitive mental models perceive their negotiating partners also have relatively dominant competitive mental models. For example, individuals are more likely to interpret negotiation partners’ motivations and behaviors as opportunistic and unethical (Kelman and Hamilton, 1989; Valley et al, 1998), involving attempts to manipulate and exploit the individuals (Walther, 1996; Fortune and Brodt, 2000). This interpretation by individuals using IM has been termed sinister attribution by Thompson and Nadler (2002). For example, there is an increased likelihood that the individual will interpret concessions by the negotiation partner as attempts at manipulation and exploitation (Walther, 1996; Dirks and Ferrin, 2001; Fortune and Brodt, 2000).

In contrast, audio facilitates the communication of nonverbal cues and quick feedback by enabling negotiators to (1) hear the timing and intonation of speech, (2) send and receive messages at the same time (such as when a party who is speaking hears the other saying uh huh), (3) take turns and stay in sequence by, for example, allowing each party to know the exact moment when the other has <sup>fi</sup>nished communicating, and (4) reply just about when a message is received and understood, without delay (Short et al, 1976; Daft and Lengel, 1986; Clark and Brennan, 1991; Postmes et al, 1998; Friedman and Currall, 2003). Along with ‘… nonverbal and paralinguistic reminders of the social context, people’s attention turns [toward] … others and so does their concern with being positively evaluated [by others] or with liking the other’ (Kiesler and Sproull, 1992, p. 91). This increased awareness of, and concern for, others can result in individuals having a relatively more dominant prosocial mental models. Again, because of co-orientation (Lindenberg, 2006), the individual believes that the negotiating partner also has a relatively dominant prosocial mental model. Thus, as the individual is attempting to be fair, the individual believes that the partner is also attempting to be fair. For example, a partner who concedes little may not decrease the likelihood of reaching agreement because the individual believes the partner is acting out of good intentions, and the individual is, therefore, more willing to make greater concessions (Dirks and Ferrin, 2001).

Not all researchers agree with the notion that text-based computer communication signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly restricts the transfer of relationally and socially oriented information. Some researchers suggest that the degree of relationship-oriented communication that can be transmitted via a medium may be greater than what would be expected by an objective evaluation of the medium’s richness (DeSanctis and Poole, 1994). For example, employing emoticons as well as variations in font type, color, and case in order to convey voice in<sup>fl</sup>ection and pitch can enhance the ability of text-based media to communicate emotion (Rice and Love, 1987; Barry and Fulmer, 2004). Though this is true, such communication conveys ‘far fewer … clues about emotional states’ than do nonverbal cues via telephone (Friedman and Currall, 2003, p. 1334). In addition, the ability of a text-based medium to convey relationally and socially oriented information may depend on the relative familiarity of communicating individuals with each other, thereby enabling them to employ cues based on their shared experiences (Carlson and Zmud, 1999). As the focus of this paper is on agreements among strangers, individuals’ abilities to convey such information over text-based media are signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly diminished (Naquin and Paulson, 2003).

Walther (1996) and Tidwell and Walther (2002) also suggest that individuals can adapt their behavior to the medium to a degree that as much social and relational information can be communicated via text as via face-toface, though such social and relational communication may be at a slower rate. Their examples of individuals’ adaptation to the medium include instances of email romances, online social support communities, and discussions about sex, love, family, and religion. However, their arguments are less likely to apply to negotiations (Friedman and Currall, 2003), as the goals of such instances are to build relationships and provide support, not to manage con<sup>fl</sup>ict, that is, not to ‘assert one’s needs or wishes through differences of opinion’ (Friedman and Currall, 2003, p. 1334).

Therefore, whether an individual evaluates communication by the partner in light of a prosocial or a competitive mental model is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by whether the medium used is audio or IM. In addition, as we describe in detail below, whether an individual’s dominant mental model is prosocial or competitive in<sup>fl</sup>uences how the individual interprets positive, negative, and neutral affect comments communicated by the negotiation partner. For example, when viewed from a prosocial model perspective, positive affect communicated by the partner can increase the individual’s perception that the partner is fair. In contrast, the same communication when viewed from a competitive model perspective can be interpreted by the individual as the partner being insincere and manipulative.

## Affect and communicated affect.

Affect is a feeling and a basic aspect of social interaction (Anderson and Thompson, 2004; van Kleef et al, 2004b), and includes emotions (brief states directed at speci<sup>fi</sup>c stimuli), moods (states of longer duration than emotional experiences that are not directed at anything speci<sup>fi</sup>c), and traits (stable individual differences in the tendency to experience emotions and moods) (Anderson and Thompson, 2004; van Kleef et al, 2004b). An individual’s affect can be important in negotiations because it can motivate thoughts and actions. For example, it has been found that an individual experiencing positive affect (i.e., pleasant feelings such as excitement and happiness: Barry and Oliver, 1996) tends to negotiate more cooperatively because the individual perceives his or her negotiation partner as acting with good intentions (e.g., Frank, 1988; Fridlund, 1994; Anderson and Thompson, 2004). This increases the likelihood that the negotiators will reach an agreement (Olekalns and Druckman, 2014). The emotion aspect of an individual’s positive affect can be enhanced by the partner’s communication of positive affect (Gabarro, 1978; Barry and Stewart, 1997), such as the communication of solidarity, tension release, and acceptance (Bales, 1951).

It has also been found that an individual experiencing negative affect (i.e., unpleasant feelings such as unhappiness and hostility: Barry and Oliver, 1996) tends to negotiate more competitively because the individual perceives his or her negotiation partner as acting with malevolent intentions (e.g., Moore et al, 1999; Lewicki and Wiethoff, 2000; van Kleef et al, 2004a). The emotion aspect of an individual’s negative affect can be enhanced by the partner’s communication of negative affect, such as the communication of disagreement, tension, and anger (Bales, 1951). However, there is also research suggesting that under some circumstances the communication of negative affect, such as anger, by the partner can lead the individual to behave more cooperatively by lowering demands and making more concessions (Côté et al, 2013; Olekalns and Druckman, 2014). This may be because of the individual’s belief that the communicated anger indicates that the partner may be about to stop negotiations, and that a concession is necessary if there is to be any hope of reaching an agreement (Lelieveld et al, 2012; Johnson and Cooper, 2015).

Some popular lay notions of negotiation suggest that negotiators should ‘neither feel nor express emotion’ (Koppelman et al, 2006, p. 84). This neutral affect orientation has not been the focus of much recent research, though there is some literature suggesting that communicating only neutral affect can reduce the chance of gaining rapport and trust, and thereby the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts associated with the communication of positive affect (Drolet and Morris, 2000; Schneider et al, 2013). Examples of neutral affect communication can include the unemotional communication of facts (Bales, 1951).

There is a wide range of expressive affect, and one of the most well-established schemes for identifying affect is the Bales’ interaction process analysis coding scheme. Though the Bales system was developed in 1951, it is still quite useful, being regarded as a prototype of negotiation behavior analysis methods (Nieuwmeijer, 1992; Hopmann, 2002), and one of the most frequently used systems in negotiation studies (Angelmar and Stern, 1978). In addition, many schemes have been based on it directly (e.g., Hopmann, 2002) or indirectly (e.g., Putnam and Jones, 1982). Thus, we use the Bales’ system in our theoretical development and our empirical operationalizations. The system is a reasonable choice for us also because it enables us to distinguish among positive, neutral, and negative communicated affect. Bales’ system consists of 12 categories representing three major areas of communication: three categories of positive affect, three categories of negative affect, and six categories of neutral affect. Discussions below associated with our hypotheses provide rationales supporting our focus on one category of positive affect, one category of negative affect, and two categories of neutral affect.

An important way in which our work differs from prior research is in the consideration of how the mental model being employed by individuals in<sup>fl</sup>uences their interpretations of the different categories of affect communicated by their partners.

## Negotiation context

Our focus is on distributive price negotiation between two individuals (a dyad) who are strangers, because this is what most often occurs on websites such as Bonanza and retailers using Widestorm.com, and quite recently on Amazon. In negotiations, over such websites, individuals are driven to reduce uncertainty regarding the task process, the object of negotiation, and each other’s preferences, because doing so is necessary for smooth and understandable interactions and for each person to have a sense of control over his or her outcomes (Berger and Calabrese, 1975; Berger and Bradac, 1982). So that we can examine individuals’ attitudes and behaviors in a similar context, our object of negotiation is a computer-generated virtual lottery ticket, with opportunities to win its face value that are in units of points and determined by a virtual prize wheel. The kind of lottery tickets used in this study has been employed in many prior studies in order to examine individuals’ attitudes toward and behaviors resulting from uncertainty (e.g., Berg et al, 1995).

## Research model

We develop our hypotheses by examining how the communication of affect by the partner in<sup>fl</sup>uences the amount of unilateral concessions made by the individual. However, we propose and evaluate our hypotheses at the dyad level. This approach is reasonable because of the vast research stream demonstrating that the communication of affect within a group results in a convergence of affect among group members through a process of emotional contagion (Schoenewolf, 1990; Barsade, 2002). Thus, for example, when a dyad partner communicates positive affect to the individual, the individual will tend to adopt and mimic that positive affect, communicating it back to the partner.

In addition, an individual will make inferences by observing what his or her dyad partner does. These observations are used to formulate causal attributions such as the reason for a partner’s action, and in turn provide a basis for an individual’s responses (Kwon and Weingart, 2004). The inferences made based on these observations are informed by what researchers describe as negotiators implicit theories (Bazerman and Carroll, 1987; Thompson and Hastie, 1990). We advance the view that a prosocial theory is one such implicit theory, and we use it as a basis for what we predict. For example, hypotheses associated with audio are based on the argument that the individual’s dominant mental model tends to be prosocial, and through co-orientation, the individual assumes that the partner’s dominant mental model is also prosocial. The individual is thus biased toward identifying and evaluating cues that support this assumption. In addition, hypotheses associated with IM are based on the argument that the individual’s dominant mental model tends to be competitive, and through co-orientation, the individual assumes that the partner’s dominant mental model is also competitive. The individual is thus biased toward identifying and evaluating cues that support this assumption.

We also rely on established relationships found to exist among negotiators’ communications, prosocial behaviors, and concession making. We <sup>fi</sup>rst develop hypotheses (labeled with a subscript ‘a’) that depict how negotiators’ communications are perceived and acted upon when audio is used and the negotiators’ dominant mental models are prosocial (see Figure 1). We then determine how a change in the dominant mental models from prosocial to competitive, as a result of using IM instead of audio, moderates these relationships, and associated hypotheses are labeled with a subscript ‘im’. For example, when using audio, Figure 1 indicates that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of negative communicated affect on unilateral concession is negative and the change in in<sup>fl</sup>uence when IM is used instead of audio (Δ) is positive. Thus, when using IM instead of audio, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of negative communicated affect on unilateral concession is the sum of that negative− in<sup>fl</sup>uence plus the Δ associated with the use of IM. For example, if the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of negative affect with audio is 0.15 and the change in in<sup>fl</sup>uence when using IM (i.e., Δ) is 0.19, then the resultant in<sup>fl</sup>uence of negative affect when using IM is 0.04.

Positive affect communication and unilateral concession From a prosocial perspective (when using audio), a partner’s comments that convey positive affect are likely to increase the individual’s beliefs that the partner will be fair because they establish like-mindedness (Bickmore and Cassell, 2001), indicate the partner’s concern for the individual’s interests, and evoke pleasant feelings (Frank, 1988; Fridlund, 1994; Anderson and Thompson, 2004). Bales (1951) describes three types of positive communicated affect. (Though variants of these types have been developed since the work of Bales (e.g., Putnam and Jones, 1982), they remain faithful to Bales work). The <sup>fi</sup>rst shows solidarity in that it indicates commonality, raises status, or gives reward. The second typically involves humor and in that it includes joking and laughing. The third (Category 3) displays acceptance of counsel, and in that it shows accord, understanding, and concurrence. Of these, we focus on acceptance of counsel because it is the aspect of positive affect that tends to be more taskoriented.

On the basis of Category 3 of Bales (1951) interaction process categorization scheme, a partner’s communication includes accepting counsel positive affect if it shows acceptance, understanding, or concurrence with the individual’s prior communication. For example, in the experiment for our study, during a negotiation via audio, a person’s Category 3 comment was ‘Two points, alright, that sounds reasonable, Yeah’. A partner accepting the individual’s counsel supports the individual’s perception that the partner has a dominant prosocial mental model because it signals that the partner is willing to accept the individual’s in<sup>fl</sup>uence (Six et al, 2010), and therefore, cares about the individual (Mayer et al, 1995; Dunn and Schweitzer, 2003). This perception will then make the individual more willing to behave in a prosocial manner, which increases the likelihood of concession making because the individual is more motivated to engage in fair negotiations (Marwell and Schmitt, 1975; Pruitt, 1981) and is less worried about being taken advantage of (Epley et al, 2006). As a result, we expect the following.

<sup>H1</sup>a<sup>:</sup> When using audio, unilateral concessions will increase due to the communication of accepting counsel positive affect.

From a competitive perspective (when using IM), individuals are more likely to interpret positive affect comments from their partners in a negative light (Byron, 2008), for example, as being insincere (e.g., damning with faint praise) and as attempts at manipulation. In the experiment for our study, during a negotiation over IM a partner’s Category 3 comment likely to have been interpreted this way by the individual was ‘As a seller I am willing to accommodate you’. In order to avoid being manipulated, individuals thus reduce concession making (Epley et al, 2006). In addition, from the bias of their competitive mental models, individuals are looking for evidence that the partners are weak negotiators. The communication of positive affect by partners may be interpreted as evidence of their weakness, which can be taken advantage of by the individuals. This leads to the following prediction.

<sup>H1</sup>im<sup>:</sup> Compared to audio, IM will reduce the positive influence on unilateral concessions that results from the communication of accepting counsel positive affect.

Neutral affect communication and unilateral concession In contrast to positive and negative affect, Bales provides many categories of communication that convey neutral affect. We focus on seeking counsel in terms of questions of fact because it <sup>fi</sup>ts with our positive affect choice in that it has a task orientation. In general, people engage in behaviors to reduce uncertainty (Berger and Calabrese, 1975). In addition, as we noted earlier, individuals are driven to reduce uncertainty during negotiations (Berger and Calabrese, 1975; Berger and Bradac, 1982). One of the most widely noted uncertainty reduction behavior is seeking information by asking questions (Berger and Calabrese, 1975; Goldsmith and Baxter, 1996).

Making up and asking questions require cognitive effort (Clark and Brennan, 1991). As people tend to expend the least amount of cognitive effort (for a given level of decision accuracy) when seeking information (Hulland and Klienmuntz, 1994), these questions by partners are more likely in negotiations when the partner is willing to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by them. This willingness provides evidence that the partner’s dominant mental model is prosocial (Six et $^ { a l , }$ 2010). As the individual is examining this evidence from a prosocial perspective (because audio is being used) the individual is more willing to behave in a prosocial manner. As a result, the individual is more likely to make concessions because he or she is less worried about being taken advantage of (Epley et al, 2006).

Bales (1951) suggests that rather than examining only questions, a ratio of fact-related questions to the sum of fact-related questions and fact-related answers be used in order to normalize across contexts. Bales’ Category 7 pertains to fact-related questions in terms of orientation, information, repetition, clari<sup>fi</sup>cation, and/or con<sup>fi</sup>rmation. Category 6, then, pertains to answers to such questions. For example, one partner in our experiment when negotiating via audio asked ‘The probability that I’m going to play for 12 points is 50, right?’ and the individual answered ${ } ^ { \prime } \mathrm { Y e s } ,$ I understand what you are saying, but I’m asking for a higher price’. The question is Category 7 communication and the answer is Category 6 communication. We will refer to the ratio of fact-related questions to the sum of fact-related questions and fact-related answers more simply as fact-related questions. We thus hypothesize the following:

## ${ \bf H } 2 _ { \mathbf { a } } { \mathbf { : } }$ When using audio, unilateral concessions will increase due to the communication of fact-related questions.

From a competitive perspective (when using $\operatorname { I N } ,$ individuals are more likely to interpret their partner’s questions in a negative light (Byron, 2008), for example, as insincere attempts at ingratiation with the purpose of manipulating the individual into being more prosocial. From our present study, an example of a Category 7 communication by a partner using IM is: ‘the chances of anyone buy this ticket is low … the probability of getting 0 is more than 75%, why would anyone pay more $\ldots ? ^ { \prime }$ To which the individual responded with the following Category 6 communication: ‘I know I am looking at it.’ We thus hypothesize the following:

$\mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathrm { i m } } \mathrm { : }$ Compared to audio, IM will reduce the positive influence on unilateral concessions that results from the communication of fact-related questions.

## Negative affect communication and unilateral concession

Though using audio biases the individual toward positive interpretations of communication, we do not expect this bias to blind the individual to the communication of negative affect. Thus, we expect that the partner’s communication of negative affect will tend to make the individual wary as to the degree that the partner’s dominant mental model is prosocial (Forgas, 1998; Morrow et al, 2004).

Bales (1951) indicates that there are three types of negative communicated affect. The <sup>fi</sup>rst type shows disagreement without emotion (e.g., merely stating an unemotional ‘no’ in response to an offer). As described earlier, differences in the ability to convey nonverbal cues, such as voice tone and in<sup>fl</sup>ection, play a large part in distinguishing between IM as opposed to audio communication. Therefore, this lack of emotion reduces the potential to identify differences between IM and audio that might in<sup>fl</sup>uence unilateral concession making.

Bales’ second type of negative communicated affect displays disagreement with anger. This type of communication is also ignored here because it has received a great deal of coverage in the negotiation literature in general (e.g., Friedman et al, 2004; van Kleef et al, 2004; Kopelman et al, 2006) and more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in literature examining concession making during negotiations $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ , Johnson et $a l ,$ 2008; Johnson and Cooper, 2015).

We, therefore, focus on Bales’ third type of negative communicated affect (Category 11), which displays tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement. On the basis of Category 11 of Bales interaction process categorization scheme, a partner’s communication includes tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement negative affect when it shows tension, anxiety, or frustration with the individual’s behavior. An example is when a partner in our experiment, using audio, gasped and then said ‘That’s still too high, man’. By communicating tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement, the partner demonstrates a lack of willingness to be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the individual and thereby tends to refute the individual’s co-orientation assumption, indicating that the partner does not have the best interests of the individual in mind (Mayer et $^ { a l , }$ 1995; Dunn and Schweitzer, 2003). As a result, the individual reduces concession making (Friedman et al, 2004; Kopelman et al, 2006) because he or she does not want to be taken advantage of (Epley et $^ { a l , }$ 2006). We thus expect the following:

## $\mathbf { H } 3 _ { \mathbf { a } } \colon$ When using audio, unilateral concessions will decrease due to the communication of tension-filled disagreement negative affect.

From a competitive perspective (when using IM), the individual attempts to manipulate the partner to concede more. To this end, the individual tracks the partner’s reactions to these attempts (Pruitt, 1981). Expressions of tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement by a partner therefore can be interpreted by the individual in accord with the individual’s competitive mental model as evidence that the partner is close to his or her concession limits (Thompson et al, 1999), especially since, when using $\scriptstyle { \mathrm { I M } } ,$ negative affect communicated by the partner tends to be interpreted as being more intensely negative by the individual (Byron, 2008). An example is when a partner in our experiment, using IM, typed the comment ‘8 is too low!!!!!’ This suggests to the individual the possible consequences if he or she does not offer concessions, such as con<sup>fl</sup>ict escalation and an inability to reach agreement (Van Kleef et al, 2004). We therefore expect the following:

## Method

## Procedure.

One hundred and forty-eight freshmen through seniors (40% female) in a university with a population of 17,000 students participated in the experiment that took place over a couple of months. As participants arrived at the experiment, they were randomly assigned to different rooms: one for sellers and one for buyers. Sellers and buyers received instructions about the experimental task via PowerPoint, and completed three practice rounds of negotiation. In the practice rounds, they were told the following: (1) The experiment began when a lottery ticket, a prize wheel, a timer, and instructions appeared on their computer screen. (2) After negotiations ended, the appropriate prize wheel would be spun in order to determine the number of points the ticket holder had won. (3) Though dyad negotiation partners were in different locations, they would see the same information at the same time on their computer screens. (4) Anyone could initiate negotiation by contacting their dyad partner via the assigned medium, either by text using IM or orally using VoIP (without a visual component). (5) They had three and a halfmin with which to complete their negotiations. (This time limit was determined based on pilot studies.) (6) Before the time elapsed, the seller had to indicate via the PC what the parties agreed on in terms of the decision to sell or not to sell the ticket. If the seller did not make such an indication and the time elapsed, both the seller and buyer would receive no points.

After the practice rounds, sellers and buyers were randomly assigned to each other in order to form 74 negotiating dyads. The dyads were then randomly assigned to negotiate using either IM or audio. The dyads then performed the experimental negotiations. When they <sup>fi</sup>nished, dyads that used IM were switched to audio and vice versa, and the negotiations ensued using the new media. In addition, these dyads were randomly assigned to negotiate over one of two lottery tickets: one with a relatively high and one with a relatively low expected value. The dyads then performed the experimental negotiations. This resulted in a two factor randomized design. We did not provide any other experimental manipulations, which allows relatively natural interactive dynamics to occur, and makes our study more relevant to real-world negotiations (Druckman and Olekalns, 2008).

## Task

Our object of negotiation is a lottery ticket, with opportunities to win its face value depicted by a prize wheel (Figure 2). Participants in each dyad negotiated the price of the lottery ticket in terms of points. Each participant in a dyad was given US\$10 for participating in the experiment and was told that at the end of it, he or she had a chance to receive an extra \$10, depending on the number of points earned as a result of the outcome of the negotiation and the lottery draw for the ticket’s value.

At the beginning of the negotiations, participants saw a prize wheel on their PCs that displayed two potential pairs of lottery ticket values: 0 and 12 points, and 0 and 34 points (Figure 2). Participants were told that after negotiations ended, there was a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–12 value pair and a 50% chance that the prize wheel spun for them would be the 0–34 value pair. Participants were also told that after the value pair was randomly determined, the prize wheel representing that pair would be spun in order to determine if the holder of the lottery ticket received 0 or more points. For example, if the 0–12 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 74% chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 12 points (the shaded portion in Figure 2 prize wheel on the left) and a 26% chance that the result would be 0 points. If the 0–34 value pair prize wheel was randomly selected, there was a 98% chance that the result of spinning the wheel would be 34 points (the shaded portion in Figure 2 prize wheel on the right) and a 2% chance that the result would be 0 points.

The participants were not provided with these percentages; rather, they had to estimate them by examining the prize wheels. After negotiations ended, this resulted in the ticket holder having a 37% chance of winning 12 points (0.5\*0.74), a 49% chance of winning 34 points (0.5\*0.98), and a 14% chance of winning nothing (0.5\*0.26 +0.5\*0.02).

At the start of negotiation the seller owned the ticket. If the seller decided to keep the ticket and won the lottery (the prize wheel landed on the shaded area), he or she received points equal to the actual ticket value: 12 or 34 points; if the seller lost (the prize wheel landed on the unshaded area) he or she got zero points. In either case, the buyer received zero points. If the seller sold the ticket, he or she received points equal to the selling price, and the buyer received points equal to his or her lottery winnings (determined as described above for the seller) less the amount he or she used to purchase the ticket. Owing to different risk preferences, different perceptions of expected values, different feelings regarding luck, and so on, there are usually a number of prices at which the buyer and seller both believe exchange of the ticket is preferable to no exchange.

On the basis of the methodology developed and validated by Berg et al (1995), the generality of our experiment was increased by doing the following: Sellers in half of the dyads received the lottery ticket and associated prize wheel described above with an expected value of 21.1 points, while sellers in the other half received a lottery ticket and associated prize wheel with the same points (12 and 34) but with chances of winning reduced to result in a lower expected value of 2.95 points. This expected value is calculated as follows. The chance of winning 12 points was 1% and winning 34 points was 17%. With this second lottery, the ticket holder, therefore, had a 0.5% chance of winning 12 points (0.5\*0.01), an 8.5% chance of winning 34 points (0.5\*0.17), and a 91% chance of winning nothing (0.5\*0.99 +0.5\*0.83).

## Operationalizations

Media A single variable was employed with a value of 0 for audio and 1 for IM.

Bales categories Like Putnam and Jones (1982), and as Bales recommended, our coders coded ‘thought units’ within each complete ‘thought phrase’ that was expressed by subjects. An offer signaled the end of each thought phrase, and each phrase typically had more than onethought unit in it. Thought units could be very small, such as the single word ‘ah’ or changes in aural tone and/or pitch. Thus, a single thought phrase communicated from a partner to the individual would typically be coded with multiple Bales categories. Coders received instructions such as that they should take the role of the individual to whom the communication is directed, as well as regarding how to handle repetitions of the same thought, run-on multiple thoughts, interruptions, and so on. Table 4 shows the total number of thought units identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by our coders across each medium.

Negative affect Bales Category 11 tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled negative affect is required for H3. During negotiation, all communication for each dyad was recorded. Audio <sup>fi</sup>les and written logs of IM were then coded using the Bales scheme. A partner’s thought unit was Category 11 if it showed tension, anxiety, or frustration with the individual’s behavior.

Because of the number of thought phrases that were exchanged over both communication media, nine coders were trained and employed. Thought phrases for each medium were randomly divided into three sets consisting of thought phrases from about 25 dyads. Three coders were assigned to each set of data. All coders were blind to the research hypotheses and worked independently of each other. Average inter-coder reliability for Category 11 was 0.92.

Each coder assigned a value of 1 to a thought unit for each Bales category that they believed reasonably described the thought unit, and a 0 for all other categories. For example, if a coder believed a thought unit showed anxiety, he or she would code a 1 for Category 11. The coders’ evaluations for this thought unit were then averaged. For example, if only one coder perceived an instance of Category 11, then Category 11 would have a value of 0.33.

Positive affect Accepting counsel positive communicated affect re<sup>fl</sup>ects Bales Category 3, and displays agreement, passive acceptance, understanding, concurrence, and/or compliance; this category is required for H1. The procedure described above for Category 11 was used for Category 3. The average inter-coder reliability was 0.91 for Category 3.

Neutral affect H2 requires the coding of seeking counsel. This involves getting counts for Bales Categories 6 and 7 in a manner similar to Category 11 described above. As suggested by Bales, seeking counsel is then calculated by dividing Category 7 by the sum of Categories 6 and 7. The average inter-coder reliability for these Categories was 0.89.

Unilateral concessions Our dependent variable is unilateral concession making, which is calculated by totaling the unilateral concessions by both parties in a dyad. Table 1 illustrates this calculation under the label ‘Dyad Totals’ within the ‘Unilateral Concession’ column.

Controls Our controls are those factors that can in<sup>fl</sup>uence unilateral concessions but in which we are not interested. When the seller initial offer is lower and/or the buyer initial offer is higher, there is less negotiating room before agreement is reached, and thus unilateral concessions would tend to be smaller. The concession making process could also be sensitive to whether the lottery ticket had a high or low expected value. In addition, each dyad used IM and audio during separate negotiations. Therefore, the ordering of media for each dyad could affect their performance. Finally, though not part of our model, the main effect of the audio vs IM variable must be considered. Therefore, these controls were included in our statistical model.

## Results

An experiment was used to collect data to test our hypotheses. The data were of two types: (1) longitudinal within each dyad (one data point for each offer made) and (2) cross-sectional across dyads. Time is represented by an offer within each dyad, and as described above, each offer represents the end of a thought phrase for that dyad. For example, the dyad illustrated in Table 1 would have 6 longitudinal points in time. For each offer, unilateral concession is determined by accumulating all prior unilateral concessions for the dyad up to and including the speci<sup>fi</sup>c offer. In Table 1, the unilateral concession associated with the buyer offer of 20 would equal 4 ( = 3+1). The amounts of communicated negative, positive, and neutral affect are determined similarly, but with a onethought unit lag. For example, Category 11 negative affect that is associated with the buyer offer of 20 in the Table would consist of the sum of all Category 11 negative affect values from all messages up to but not including the thought unit that contained the buyer’s offer of 20. This allows the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of all Category 11 negative affect in thought units that are antecedent to each offer. Descriptive statistics for the data are provided in Table 2.

Time is not consistent within or across dyads. For example, two different dyads may make 6 offers but the time between each offer probably differs among offers within each dyad, as well as among offers with the same number (e.g., the second offer for each dyad) across dyads. In addition, the number of offers will typically differ across dyads; we found one having only one and one having 32. Chapter 5 in Singer and Willett (2003) offers a modeling approach that can handle such data using, for example, the mixed procedure with full maximum likelihood estimation that is provided in SPSS Version 22. (See Appendix B for statistical model details.) With unilateral concession as the dependent variable, the statistical model includes accepting counsel, seeking counsel, and tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement as <sup>fi</sup>xed effects independent variables, and media as a <sup>fi</sup>xed effect moderating variable. The in<sup>fl</sup>uences of these variables on unilateral concessions are estimated over time using data from within each dyad, as well as across all dyads.

Results of this analysis are depicted in Figure 3 with details in Table 3; <sup>fi</sup>ve of our six hypotheses are supported at P = 0.05, providing substantial support for our model. Two of our three media moderator hypotheses are supported, indicating a substantial in<sup>fl</sup>uence of media differences. Eighty-four of the 148 negotiations (74 dyads, each negotiating using IM and audio) resulted in agreement.

We have argued that unilateral concession is worthy of study because of its importance in reaching agreement. This was tested by logistically regressing agreement on total unilateral concession. The results support the importance of unilateral concession with P = 0.02 and an 82% correct prediction rate.

## Discussion

IM and audio are commonly used for negotiations in today’s online business settings. Though there is a large stream of research on how IM and audio differentially affect communication, little is known about how these media can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the abilities of negotiating dyads to promote unilateral concession making. The offering of unilateral concessions by both parties is important because it is one of the most effective ways to increase the likelihood of reaching agreement. To this end, we examined how the same categories of communication promote or inhibit unilateral concession making, depending on whether IM or audio is used. As described below, a better understanding of these differences can help negotiators choose the most effective communication medium, or when constrained to use a speci<sup>fi</sup>c medium, can help them develop more effective communication strategies.

The communication categories we examined involved neutral, positive, and negative communicated affect. We developed a model based on prosocial theory in order to relate these types of communication to the degree of unilateral concession making. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have several implications for research and practice, but also limitations. Next, we elaborate on these issues.

## Implications for research

Before we describe speci<sup>fi</sup>c insights regarding the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of communicated affect over IM and audio media, we address our use of prosocial theory. Negotiation researchers, such as Kopelman et al (2006), who focus on communicated affect typically base their research on cooperation theory. As described earlier, this theory is less appropriate for communications over IM or audio media because it assumes that negotiators have envisioned, or will <sup>fi</sup>nd it appropriate to envision, themselves in face-toface contact even when using these other media. However, the validity of this assumption is doubtful. Cooperation theory’s predictions are thus less applicable when negotiations occur over IM or audio because of differences in the communication process and the nature of the relationships between negotiating parties. This is because these factors are a part of what de<sup>fi</sup>nes a social structure, and changes in social structure can make signi<sup>fi</sup>cant changes to cooperation (Axelrod, 1984). For example, face-to-face contact promotes cooperation through the convergence of nonverbal expressive behavior (Drolet and Morris, 2000).

We, therefore, employ prosocial theory that can account for how IM predisposes a negotiating individual to have a relatively stronger competitive mental model while audio predisposes the same individual to have a relatively stronger prosocial mental model. These differences in mental model orientation help to shape the interaction between negotiators by biasing them to view their partners’ motivations and behaviors from a more competitive or prosocial perspective when using IM or audio, respectively. These biases became apparent in the following ways as revealed by our investigation.

The literature typically indicates that communication of negative affect reduces the likelihood of reaching agreement $( \mathrm { e . g . }$ , Kopelman et al, 2006). We found that the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement (Category 11) is in accord with this literature, demonstrating a reduction in unilateral concessions when audio is used $\mathbf { ( H 3 _ { a } ) }$ . However, when IM is used, this type of communication can increase unilateral concessions $\mathbf { ( H 3 _ { i m } ) }$ . We hypothesized that this may be because of tracking that occurs when individuals have more competitive mental models. In this case, the individual is trying to manipulate the partner into conceding as much as possible, and the individual, therefore, looks for cues that the partner is close to the point where he or she would not concede any further. Tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreement can provide such cues in a manner similar to the cues associated with angry disagreements (van Kleef et al, 2004). When confronted with this evidence, the individual must offer concessions in order to be able to reach agreement.

It is frequently stated, or implied, in the literature that a text-based medium like IM makes negative affect comments more likely (Thompson and Nadler, 2002; Johnson et al, 2008) and researchers, therefore, encourage users of such media to make their messages positive so as to build rapport (Morris et al, 2002). This recommendation is based on the assumption that positive messages have an invariant in<sup>fl</sup>uence over different media. Our study supports this guidance.

We also examined the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on unilateral concessions of neutral affect communication (Categories 7 and 6) in the form of fact-oriented questions. In accord with Kopelman et al (2006), we found that neutral affect comments increased concession $\left( \mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathbf { a } } \right)$ . However, we found that these comments had no effect on unilateral concessions when IM was employed $( \mathbf { H } 2 _ { \mathbf { i m } } ) .$ . We hypothesized that when using audio, asking fact-oriented questions provides evidence that the partner has a more prosocial model. However, when using IM individuals interpret cues via a more competitive model and, therefore, tend to discount seeking counsel as being insincere.

Our results also yield more general insights that are of theoretical value to scholars conducting research on the negotiation process. In the past four decades, one signi<sup>fi</sup>cant focus in negotiation research has been on how unilateral concessions made by an individual cause or result from a partner’s reciprocal concessions (e.g., Komorita and Esser, 1975; Pruitt, 1981; Kwon and Weingart, 2004). For example, the Graduated Reciprocation in Tension Reduction Theory (Osgood, 1962) prescribes that a small unilateral concession should be made initially, and if reciprocated, unilateral concessions should gradually be made larger. ‘Once their low risk trust $( \mathrm { e . g . \_ } \mathrm { \_ s . } )$ [a small unilateral concession]) is reciprocated, then players can gradually increase their level of trust in the other player, … through greater and greater unilateral concessions, while at the same time unconditionally cooperating by … [reciprocating the concessions]. [Thereby] mutual trust, and fully trustful cooperation, will eventually develop’ (Yamagishi et al, 2005, p. 303). We add to this literature by providing theoretical and empirical evidence that communications other than concessions, such as positive, neutral, and negative affect, can lead to more or less self-sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce depending on the medium employed, and thereby motivate negotiators to make greater or fewer unilateral concessions.

## Implications for practitioners

We encourage negotiators using IM or audio media to be aware of the potential consequences on unilateral concession (and thereby on agreement) of negative, positive, and neutral affect comments. We found that positive affect comments can increase unilateral concession independent of the media used. Negotiators should, therefore, consistently provide such comments. Neutral affect comments can increase unilateral concession when using audio, but have little impact when IM is used. Negotiators should, therefore, put extra effort into providing such comments only when negotiating over audio. Our result concerning neutral affect also implies that a negotiator should not become frustrated or annoyed when his or her partner asks a large number of questions. Rather, such questioning indicates an increasing dominance of the partner’s prosocial mental model that can be encouraged by providing appropriate responses.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings regarding negative communicated affect in the form of tension-<sup>fi</sup>lled disagreements indicate that such communication should not be employed when using audio because it can decrease unilateral concessions and, therefore, the likelihood of agreement. However, when using IM, this type of communication can be used to increase unilateral concession making and thereby increase the likelihood of reaching agreement. Thus, a negotiator who has trouble keeping his or her negative emotions under control would best be served to choose IM rather than audio for negotiations.

## Limitations

The contributions of this study should be viewed with the recognition that the study has some limitations. Potentially important limitations involve the amounts awarded in the negotiations, our use of students as participants, the lack of independent variable manipulation, the relatively brief time for negotiations concerning a single issue (price), and the external validity of our negotiations. These are addressed next.

Each subject was paid \$10 to participate in the study. In addition, one participant in each dyad won another \$10 if he or she had the greater number points resulting from the sale and lottery (as described earlier). The concession process that we found might be quite different than that for real-life con<sup>fl</sup>ict settings in which the stakes or rewards are likely to be much greater than in our game. However, similar negotiation strategies have been found to be prevalent in both low stake games, as well as circumstances with much higher stakes. For example, the matching strategy was found in a single-issue labor-management negotiation over compensation at the level of hourly wage rates between \$50 and \$70 per hour and annual wage rates between \$80,000 and \$112,000 per year (Ma et al, 2006).

The age of our students are involved in purchasing items on websites such as Bonanza and Amazon so the use of these students is not necessarily a threat to the validity of our study. In addition, though students are not necessarily good surrogates with regard to ethical dilemmas (Bean and D’Aquilla, 2003), they can to be reasonable surrogates with regard to <sup>fi</sup>nancial decisions (Liyanarachchi and Milne, 2005).

Our study design did not involve the manipulation of independent variables, and therefore, might make the support of causal hypotheses problematic. As described earlier, this lack of arti<sup>fi</sup>cial manipulation allowed us to examine the natural interactive dynamics of negotiation as it occurred, and thereby makes our study more relevant to real-world negotiations. However, this potential limitation is not as serious as it might appear because with our longitudinal data, our independent variables were measured with a one-thought unit lag behind the dependent variable. For example, when we examine the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of accepting in<sup>fl</sup>uence communication on unilateral concessions resulting from the 5th offer, we measure the accepting influence communication that occurred before but not including the communication of the 5th offer. This noncontemporaneous measurement ‘ameliorate[s] the possibility that the observed relationship is a result of reciprocal causation’ (Singer and Willett, 2003, p. 180). This enables us to make relatively strong statements of causal hypotheses support.

Our study was based on a relatively brief encounter between negotiators concerned with a single issue (price). This is typical because time limits are common in negotiation as negotiators desire an end to the uncertainty and emotional demands of the process and their constituents often press for quick resolution (Carnevale et al, 1993; De Dreu, 2003). Furthermore, although many negotiations are concerned with multiple issues, they tend to progress in phases and on an issue-by-issue basis (Holmes, 1992; Morris et al, 2002), with a single issue (such as price) often dominating while other substantive issues are considered tangential (Kwon and Weingart, 2004). Nevertheless, it is possible that if negotiators had more time for negotiation and were concerned with multiple issues, more complex media and communication effects on unilateral concession would occur. For example, there would be greater opportunities for negotiators to know each other via IM due to a tendency for people to be hyper-personal in their interactions over this medium as they compensate for its limitation (Tidwell and Walther, 2002). The bases for unilateral concessions could be altered as result of this occurrence.

We noted in the introduction that the ways people negotiate in practice motivate this study. They negotiate over a variety of assets, trying to agree on a wide range of prices. As part of this process, they have social interactions. Against this background, and as we simulate negotiation in the form of a game, one might ask why will our results generalize to practice. We contend that they will apply to practice because our game has the basic characteristics of what de<sup>fi</sup>nes negotiation in practice. These characteristics pertain to the properties of the object of negotiation and the concerns of negotiation partners.

Our object of negotiation is a lottery ticket with opportunities to win its face value depicted by a prize wheel. The ticket is an asset because it has a monetary value. However, there are uncertainties around this value because of its two potential winning amounts (12 or 34), as well as the chance of not winning (value of 0). Perceptions about these uncertainties vary with dyad partners, and partners will assign different utility valuations based on them. In practice, this also is the case with other objects. For example, one of the authors purchased an expensive radio on Ebay in order to be able to listen to a favorite AM station. The online description of the radio, as well as the associated reviews by individuals owning that type of radio, indicated that it was one of the best in terms of receiving weak signals. However, there was no guarantee that it would receive the sought after AM station. Given the description and reviews, the net expected utility (bene<sup>fi</sup>ts minus costs) was positive. The unit was purchased and received in working order. It did receive stations that were previously not available. However, it did not receive the sought after station. Its resulting utility ended up to be negative. An analogous outcome with respect to our game could be what resulted when a partner paid 15 for a ticket that yielded an outcome of 12.

Partners to the negotiation in our game had concerns about what action each other would choose. They had to choose an action from a set of feasible alternatives, such as to make, accept, or decline offers. Manski (2000) notes that the problem of inference on partners’ reaction functions in this kind of game has much the same structure as the problem of inference on supply and demand in markets. In accord with this idea, Rosenzweig and Wolpin (1994) have applied game theory to interpret data on intra-family monetary transfers. In a similar way, and with respect to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspect of concern about partners’ actions, our results apply to negotiation in practice.

## Detail regarding choice of gain goal

There are three principle goals that can result in prosocial behavior: hedonic, gain, and normative (Lindenberg, 2006). The hedonic goal focuses on a short-term desire to feel better ‘right now’, where feeling better can be experienced in terms of bodily states (e.g., excitement, hunger, thirst, pain) and psychic states (e.g., sense of loss, angst, affection, happiness, pleasure). Thus, an individual acts prosocially when these actions make the individual feel better in the short term. For example, when an individual likes the negotiation partner, being fair to the partner can make the individual feel good. Therefore, within negotiations an individual with a dominant hedonic goal and a mental model linking fairness to immediate positive feelings would not take advantage of the partner even though being fair comes at potential monetary cost to the individual.

The gain goal involves a longer-term perspective, with the aim to improve the individual’s resources (such as money, competence, contacts, and status), and is closely associated with the economic notion of self-interest (Lindenberg, 2006). As with the hedonic goal, this goal is directly tied to self, but it is only indirectly tied to emotions as the resources must be used at a later time in order to have any hedonic effect. Thus an individual acts prosocially when ‘it pays.’ For example, a seller may sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce monetary gains by offering a low price to the buyer if the seller’s mental model links the sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce to an increase in his or her status, and the emotional pleasure that can be garnered in the future as a result of this increased status is valued by the individual more than the current monetary sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce. However, if there is little monitoring of the negotiation by others, the degree to which others see this sacri<sup>fi</sup>ce decreases, which lowers the individual’s potential status gain. In such a negotiation context, prosocial behavior by the individual becomes less likely when the individual has a gain goal (Lindenberg, 2006).

The normative goal involves acting appropriately, with appropriate actions de<sup>fi</sup>ned by behavioral norms (Lindenberg, 2006). This goal is other-directed, toward a group or an individual as a member of that group. With a normative goal, an individual acts in accord with the norms ‘as a matter of course’, because it is what should be done, and there is no direct tie to emotions. (In contrast, if an individual behaves in accord with norms in order to receive immediate social approval, the individual is acting in line with a hedonic goal. Further, if an individual behaves in accord with norms in order to increase his or her status that can be used to further hedonic goals at a later date, the individual is acting in line with a gain goal.) With a normative goal, prosocial behavior is more likely when there is a clear mental model, which demands that individuals act prosocially (e.g., be cooperative, fair, and/or trustworthy) (Lindenberg et al, 2006).

There is a natural a priori ordering of goal strength based on the degree to which the goals are tied to an individual’s emotions and self, with the strongest goal being dominant (Lindenberg, 2006). As the normative goal is not directly tied either to emotions or self, it has the least strength and tends to be in the background. This natural ordering can change in speci<sup>fi</sup>c contexts. For example, the normative goal can be dominant when there are strong institutionalized (e.g., religious) beliefs and the context is one in which an individual’s mental model clearly relates the normative guidelines to speci<sup>fi</sup>c behaviors. However, participants in our experiment re<sup>fl</sup>ect the general Western population using electronic commerce, in that they do not have strong institutionalized beliefs (e.g., bordering on the religious) regarding negotiation behaviors. Therefore, further discussions will exclude the normative goal.

We are thus left with hedonic and gain goals. As the hedonic goal is tied to both an individual’s emotions and self, it tends to have the highest strength and thus tends to be dominant. The gain goal is tied to the self but is only indirectly tied to emotions (through the use of resources at a later date), and therefore, has less a priori strength. However, aspects of a negotiation context can alter this a priori hedonic goal dominance. As we are interested in anonymous strangers who voluntarily enter into negotiations with the express purpose of reaching agreement regarding the purchase and sale of the object of negotiation, we expect the gain goal to be dominant. That is, sellers and buyers are likely to be more interested in resource gain (the object of negotiation or money) that can be used later for hedonic purposes. We, therefore, limit our discussions to mental models and cognitive frames within the gain goal.
