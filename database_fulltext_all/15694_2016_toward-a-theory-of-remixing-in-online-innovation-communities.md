# Toward a Theory of Remixing in Online Innovation Communities

Michael A. Stanko

Poole College of Management, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, North Carolina 27695, mike\_stanko@ncsu.edu

W ithin online innovation communities, remixing (i.e., the community’s use of an existing innovation as source material or inspiration to aid in the development of further innovations) is an interesting form of knowledge collaboration. This study investigates an open theoretical question: Why are particular innovations remixed by online innovation communities? Some innovations languish, while others are widely remixed. Community members (even those unknown to the innovation’s creator) may remix, taking the source innovation in directions the original innovator may have never imagined. Within online innovation communities, remixing is not bound by some of the constraints to knowledge collaboration found in more traditional environments. To address our research question, we begin with variables constituent to innovation diffusion theory, essentially remixing this long-established theory to predict cumulative remixing in online innovation communities, using arguments grounded in the user innovation and learning literatures. We also consider two forms of communication that are relevant to knowledge sharing in online communities (online community interaction and front page presence). Regression analysis (using data pertaining to 498 3D printable innovations) shows that community interaction is highly influential in determining which innovations are remixed by the community. Conversely, the innovation having a presence on the community’s front page does not have a significant effect on remixing. Observability has an inverse-U-shaped relationship with remixing; this suggests the value placed on experiential learning. Fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is used as a supplementary analysis technique (with robustness testing), and the results are largely consistent with regression findings but offer interesting insight into innovation configurations that consistently result in remixing from the community. Within specific configurations, fsQCA results indicate a contingent effect of observability and complexity; that is, under certain conditions, complex innovations are more likely to be remixed by the community.

Keywords: online innovation communities; remixing; user-generated content; innovation diffusion theory; 3D printing

## 1. Introduction

This research endeavors to understand an online innovation community’s propensity to remix particular innovations. Online innovation communities devoted to, for instance, software (e.g., sourceforge.net), 3D printing (e.g., thingiverse.com), and multimedia (e.g., scratch.mit.edu), facilitate read/write culture (see Lessig 2008), in which members of online communities can consume innovations developed by other community members, but are also empowered to remix others’ ideas to develop new concepts. The term remix was originally applied narrowly to music and later video, though in recent years remix has become commonplace in contexts such as literature and photography, as well as within innovation communities (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012, Navas 2012). In online innovation communities, remixing is the community’s use of an existing innovation as source material or inspiration to aid in the development of further innovations (Lenhart et al. 2010, Lessig 2008). Remixes leverage “meaning created by the [source] reference to build something new” (Lessig 2008, p. 76). The shift toward user-generated content (UGC) and away from professionalism (i.e., the “cult of the amateur”) has drawn criticism (e.g., Keen 2008), though it is clear that read/write culture is gathering momentum. Improvements in information technology (IT) tools and their widespread availability (such as design software and tools easily learned online, infrastructure for widespread sharing of UGC, and the availability of lower cost hardware) fuel this momentum, enabling a shift from the traditional producer-to-consumer innovation model toward an ecosystem of user innovator collaboration (Rifkin 2014, Yoo et al. 2010). Against this backdrop, an important research question becomes, Why are particular innovations remixed by an online innovation community? Some innovations are remixed many times by the innovation community, taking the source material in directions the original innovator may not have dreamed of. Online communities enable knowledge collaboration without direct dialogue, even between members who do not know each other (Faraj et al. 2011). At the same time, many other innovations are ignored by the community, failing to generate remixes. We set out to explain this disparity.

Examining the determinants of remixing is necessary to developing a more complete understanding of online innovation communities, which is of interest to both managers and researchers. There is an acknowledged need to better theoretically understand knowledge collaboration in online communities (Faraj et al. 2011), as well as users’ roles as innovators and shapers of others’ contributions (Majchrzak et al. 2013, Nambisan et al. 1999). Importantly, there are benefits to harnessing the potential of user innovators by developing an innovation that is remixed. Online innovation communities generate tremendous value through remixing: improving innovations’ shortcomings, combining aspects of multiple innovations, or optimizing innovations for particular applications (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012). Innovating firms are aware of this; organizations such as Audi, Starbucks, Harley Davidson, and Nike are increasingly looking to harness the power of user innovators (Füller et al. 2008). Innovation tactics drawing on experts, customers, and critics, such as crowdsourcing and hackathons, have become much more mainstream (Dockser Marcus 2014, Poetz and Schreier 2012). Despite remixing becoming commonplace across contexts and the potential value of understanding its determinants, little is known about the factors making the remixing of an innovation likely (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012). Even in cases where remixing is not sought after, an understanding of the determinants of remixing may help discourage the unwanted behavior.

Why particular innovations are remixed by an online innovation community is an open theoretical question. To address this, we begin with Rogers’ (2003) diffusion factors as explanatory variables, altering Rogers’ arguments, and extending innovation diffusion theory to explain remixing. Given our interest in understanding remixing in innovation communities, it is perhaps natural that we, in essence, remix a long established theory for this purpose. Traditionally, the diffusion of innovations has been thought to center on the reduction of potential consumers’ uncertainty (Rogers 2003, Tornatzky and Klein 1982). Here, we argue that the community’s remixing of an existing source innovation can involve a different consideration: community members’ desire to learn. Learning is a primary incentive for user innovation (Lakhani and Wolf 2005, Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011). Remixing is one way to gain experiential learning. Personal experience adds meaning to previously abstract concepts (Kolb 1984). Remixing also results in a reference point (i.e., the remixed innovation) to test the validity of thinking during the learning process. The importance of learning and the context of remixing within online innovation communities necessitate extending, adapting, and updating Rogers’ (2003) theorization to address our research question. Online communities are flexible, complex settings that differ drastically from traditional organizational forms, as members (and their interests, identities, and efforts) constantly shift within and flow into or out of the community (Faraj et al. 2011). Community interaction and knowledge sharing are defining elements of online innovation communities (Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006, von Krogh et al. 2003), typically with publicly visible discourse with regard to specific innovations. Community interactions may include criticism or suggestions to improve innovations. We argue that online community interactions are important to stimulating remixing. Also, given that learning is a priority within these communities, we theorize that excessive observability can affect the community’s tendency to remix innovations. Given that much can be learned by observing these innovations, the value created for community members through remixing may be lessened in these cases.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. The next section introduces our theoretical foundation. In Section 3, hypotheses are formed, extending innovation diffusion theory to explain remixing in online innovation communities. The data used for this research (relating to 498 3D printable innovations) are described in Section 4. Two distinct analysis approaches are taken: First, hypotheses are formally tested using regression analysis. After this, fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is used to detect complex contextual effects. The article concludes with a discussion of the study’s contributions as well as limitations and future research directions.

## 2. Theoretical Background

The idea that users might customize a product is not a new one. Rogers (2003, p. 17) discusses the notion of reinvention: “the degree to which an innovation is changed or modified by a user in the process of its adoption and implementation.” This notion of reinvention includes users who opt to use several, but not all, components of an off-the-shelf software offering, or car enthusiasts who add after-market accessories after purchasing a car. The distinction for IT-enabled UGC is important: within online innovation communities, prior consumption is not required to modify an innovation. The ability to change the invention is not restricted to those who first choose to consume. Interested members of the community can remix an innovation (a form of knowledge collaboration between the creator of the original innovation and the community of remixers), regardless of consumption and even despite a lack of social relationship with the innovation’s creator or those who have consumed. This demonstrates the unprecedented fluidity of collaboration within online communities (see Faraj et al. 2011) not possible in traditional contexts, nor in the time of Rogers’ (2003) theorizing.

Given our research question (why are particular innovations remixed by an online innovation community?), we employ constituent constructs from innovation diffusion theory (Rogers 2003). Innovation diffusion theory’s identification of multiple innovation attributes makes it well suited to adaptation to predict community remixing. This is as opposed to other theoretical explanations of the propagation of technology not incorporating wide ranging sets of product attributes, and instead focusing on characteristics, attitudes, and intentions of users, such as the technology acceptance model (Davis 1989, Venkatesh 2000) and the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (Venkatesh et al. 2003).

Innovation diffusion theory has been widely applied to organizational IT innovations (such as laptops, computer-aided drafting, email, business productivity software, electronic record keeping; Brancheau and Wetherbe 1990, Oborn et al. 2011) as well as to behaviors such as shopping online (Pavlou and Fygenson 2006) or browsing the mobile Internet (Hsu et al. 2007). Throughout this paper, we use the term innovation in a broad sense, consistent with Rogers’ (2003) definition: an idea, practice, or object perceived as new by the individual considering it. In prior diffusion research, the typical assumption has been that adoption of a product is binary: either the product is adopted or not (Mahajan et al. 1995), with a general emphasis on total market response (i.e., sales volume). Note that adoption refers to an individual user’s decision, whereas diffusion refers to the collective response of all users. There has been some investigation into the diffusion of UGC; for instance, researchers have examined the diffusion of open source software (Bonaccorsi and Rossi 2003) and beta releases (Makinen et al. 2014), as well as the diffusion of YouTube videos (e.g., Liu-Thompkins and Rogerson 2012, Susarla et al. 2012) and multimedia content (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012). Here, we are concerned with functional innovations (i.e., not art forms). The notion that aspects of a functional innovation may impact its propensity to be remixed has yet to be examined.

Since innovation diffusion theory is well established (excellent reviews include Greenhalgh et al. 2004, Rogers 2003), our focus is on considering the challenges to applying it to explaining remixing within online innovation communities and extending it accordingly, rather than summarizing its theorized effects on consumption. Several meta-analyses have examined the effect of Rogers’ (2003) five factors on consumption (e.g., Arts et al. 2011, Tornatzky and Klein 1982). These meta-analyses show that relative advantage (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as better than the idea it supersedes) and compatibility (whether an innovation is congruent with existing understandings of members of a social system) are influential determinants of consumption. Empirical support related to the influence of trialability (the degree to which an innovation may be experimented with), observability (the degree to which an innovation is available and visible), and complexity (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and to use by members of a social system) on consumption has been mixed, with many nonsignificant findings reported for these theorized diffusion factors (Arts et al. 2011, Jeyaraj et al. 2006, Tornatzky and Klein 1982). In Section 3, we consider the peculiarities of remixing, such as the prioritization of learning, as well as the fluid, interactive, publicly visible nature of online communities in developing theoretical arguments extending Rogers’ (2003) theory to predict remixing by an online innovation community.

Communication channels also play a central role in innovation diffusion theory (Agarwal and Prasad 1998, Rogers 2003). The contemporary communication mechanisms that characterize online communities are critical in enabling collaboration and knowledge sharing, and have shaped the culture of these communities (Goh et al. 2016, von Krogh et al. 2012). Accordingly, we consider the level of online community interaction (the degree to which an innovation is extensively discussed online by members of the innovation community). We also consider front page presence (promotion of the innovation on the online community’s home page). Importantly, front page presence is an effort to promote the innovation, while observability is a trait of the innovation itself. To illustrate, Rogers (2003, p. 259) uses the examples of hardware innovations being more observable and software being less observable due to the nature of these products. Of course, it is possible for either type of innovation to be widely communicated through front page presence.

## 2.1. Remixing and Motivations for User Innovation

Though the notion of remixing has not yet received much research attention from innovation scholars (for an exception in the context of multimedia production, see Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012), the growing literature on user innovation, particularly in innovation communities, is relevant in developing hypotheses with regard to remixing. Note that we do not draw a strong distinction between various research streams related to user innovators in developing our hypotheses (e.g., open source software development, peer production communities, customer communities) given the commonalities that these groups share, such as (1) the importance community members place on learning and their need to make use of the innovations developed; (2) the centrality of remixing, known as code reuse, splintering, or tweaking in other contexts; and (3) members’ range of involvement in these communities, from an active core to more peripheral, passive members (Haefliger et al. 2008, Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012, Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006, Rullani and Haefliger 2013, Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011).

Research related to innovation communities identifies numerous motivations for community involvement (Shah 2006, von Krogh et al. 2012). Two of the most critical motivators for involvement in innovation communities are the desires to (1) learn (Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011) and (2) make use of the innovation being developed (Lakhani and Wolf 2005). Both these incentives are thought to be relevant to predicting the community’s collective remixing behavior and will inform our hypothesis development. First, interest-based learning is a substantial part of the answer to “why would community members invest time and energy to remix an existing innovation?,” just as learning has been found to be an important motivator for other types of contributions to innovation communities (Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011, von Krogh et al. 2012). Where traditional learning is hierarchical (teacher to student), remixing represents democratic learning by doing through building on the ideas of other community members. Because of the importance of learning in determining an online innovation community’s remixing behavior (Lessig 2008), we will draw on the theory of experiential learning (Kolb 1984, Straub 2009). The remix process begins with some uncertainty regarding the obstacles community members may encounter along the way, but with assurance that they will develop their skills, problem solve, and reflect on choices made once the remix is complete; this experiential learning process is central to remixing.

Own use is also thought to be an important motivation for remixing, just as it has been shown to be elsewhere in innovation communities (Lakhani and Wolf 2005, von Krogh et al. 2012). Community members self-servingly wish to develop the best possible innovations for their own use, which drives them to pursue remixing, just as the desire for superior innovations motivates members to contribute effort in other ways within innovation communities (Hertel et al. 2003, Shah 2006). Beyond learning and making use of the innovation, innovation community members have numerous other intrinsic (e.g., enjoyment, exposure to new innovations) and extrinsic (e.g., reputation, employment opportunities) motivations for participation (Fleming and Waguespack 2007, Hertel et al. 2003, Jeppesen and Frederiksen 2006, Roberts et al. 2006). The desire to feel creative during the coding process as well as the need for intellectual stimulation have also been shown to be very important motivators for contributions to open source software development (Lakhani and Wolf 2005). It is possible that motives for participation in an innovation community evolve over time (Shah 2006); community members may initially join to improve an innovation for their own use, but stay involved due to learning or enjoyment.

## 3. Hypotheses

Online innovation communities will tend to remix innovations with a high degree of relative advantage for several reasons. One important motivation in innovation communities is own use; that is, communities of user innovators are pragmatically motivated to develop the best possible innovations to meet their own needs (Hertel et al. 2003, Shah 2006, von Hippel 1988). Given this, efforts to produce innovations of superior quality (for community members’ use) will be aided by starting with excellent source material. Thus, the community is incented to locate relatively advantageous source innovations when remixing. Relatively advantageous source innovations offer a “credible promise” as to the viability of future related developments (Haefliger et al. 2008), attracting community members to promising source innovations. The higher ratio of benefits to costs of a relatively advantageous innovation encourages potential remixes, just as it is traditionally thought to foster consumption (Rogers 2003). Given the importance of reputation in innovation communities (Lerner and Tirole 2002), community members will prefer to associate themselves with a source innovation that is relatively advantageous, and conversely, may distance themselves from source innovations lacking relative advantage.

Learning is an important outcome from remixing (Franke and Hader 2014, Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012). Experiential learning theory stipulates that learning is a tension- and conflict-filled dialectic (i.e., truth finding) process of observation, action, and reflection (Kolb 1984). Real life experiences validate abstract concepts; personal experience is viewed as the focal point for learning within experiential learning theory. In the case of remixing, selecting source innovations with a high degree of relative advantage will result in better learning outcomes since the observation stage will include assessing and understanding superior source material. Furthermore, for remixers, the reflection phase will often include a learning comparison between the source innovation and the remix developed (this can be thought of as a competitive comparison to understand each innovation’s relative merits); comparison against high-quality source material again improves learning outcomes. Remixers collectively select source innovations with high levels of relative advantage as a means to direct their remix experience toward more optimal learning outcomes; purposefully directing learning is commonplace in self-regulated learning environments (Dabbagh and Kitsantas 2012). Thus, we argue the following:

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>1</sup> <sup>(H1).</sup> Relative advantage has a positive relationship with remixing.

The online innovation community will not respond to an innovation (through remixing or otherwise) unless the innovation in question can be placed within the context of existing community understandings (Hargadon and Douglas 2001). Source innovations that are more compatible with community expectations allow for the innovation to be mapped more easily to members’ existing cognitive schemas (Moreau et al. 2001). Generally, it is easier for innovations to generate a market response if they do not challenge consumers’ expectations of how a product should be used (Gourville 2006). In the same way, members of the innovation community will collectively be more likely to conceive of improvements and personalizations for source innovations that are compatible with their expectations. Also, innovations that are compatible with previously introduced ideas are more likely to be well received by the innovation community as they tap into established interest areas within the community.

On the other hand, source innovations that are not compatible with the expectations of the innovation community will tend to languish, failing to be remixed. In the words of Hargadon and Douglas (2001, p. 478), “Purely novel actions and ideas cannot register because no established logics exist to describe them.” Community members will not anticipate benefits from the use of a remixed innovation when they are unable to comprehend the source innovation itself. For community members to become entangled (in the parlance of sociomateriality; see Orlikowski 2007) with a source innovation through remixing, a basic understanding is first required. Put simply, community members will likely not be inspired to remix noncompatible innovations that they may not even understand how to use, just as consumers generally are slow to consume incompatible innovations (Arts et al. 2011, Straub 2009).

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>2</sup> <sup>(H2).</sup> Compatibility has a positive relationship with remixing.

Trialability (the degree to which an innovation may be experimented with) is thought to positively impact the community’s propensity to remix a source innovation. Recall that observation is the first stage in the experiential learning process (Kolb 1984). Community members are able to engage in a more participative observation phase for trialable innovations (without the concern of encountering restrictions), encouraging the community to begin and make continued progress on remixes (see Buijs 2003). Trialability allows experimentation in a low-risk setting, letting remixes gather momentum without the concern of complying with restrictions. Decreasing community members’ fear of risk taking is important to promote creative behaviors (Dewett 2006), such as remixing.

Trialability also allows for a greater spectrum of allowable outcomes through experimentation. Communities of user innovators are self-servingly motivated to develop innovations that are valuable to themselves (Hertel et al. 2003, von Hippel 1988). Thus, limitations on possible outcomes lessen the potential value derived from remixing, acting as a disincentive. In this context, limitations include restrictions on the type of remixes that can be developed from a particular source innovation. Limits imposed on potential outcomes will reduce community motivation and engagement (Zhang and Bartol 2010), lessening the community’s propensity to remix the source innovation.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>(H3).</sup> Trialability has a positive relationship with remixing.

Observability is posited here to have an inverse-U-shaped relationship with remixing. In cases where observability is very low, the community will be unlikely to remix the source innovation. Very low levels of observability may present a practical barrier to remixing—community members may not be able to remix an innovation without thoroughly observing it. Furthermore, creative activities (such as remixing) are fostered by seeing and understanding the ideas of others (Amabile 1996). In the context of the theory of experiential learning, the observation phase that begins the cycle of experiential learning (Kolb 1984) cannot be fertile when the source innovation is not easily observed. Unobservable innovations will not capture the collective imagination of community members and will fail to demonstrate that the source innovation is of enough substance to warrant remixing. Thus, innovation communities will be unlikely to remix innovations with very low observability.

Innovation diffusion theory holds that observability makes the benefits of a product more apparent and thus increases consumption (Arts et al. 2011). However, with respect to remixing, since part of the benefit in question is learning, high levels of observability reduce the incremental learning possible for community members and, thus, part of the value generated through remixing. While learning is a primary motive within innovation communities (Lakhani and Wolf 2005, Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011), community members may have little left to learn from remixing highly observable innovations, since all aspects of the innovation are apparent through observation. Therefore, we expect that those innovations with very high levels of observability will discourage remixing. With less incremental learning available, interested members of the innovation community will collectively tend not to remix these highly observable innovations. Very high observability acts to shift some community learning regarding the source innovation from remixing (a form of active learning) to simply examining (passive learning; see James et al. 2002).

In short, innovations with enough observability to demonstrate the innovation’s substance and spark the collective imagination of the online innovation community, but not so much observability as to diminish the incremental learning available through remixing, are the most likely to be remixed extensively by an innovation community.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>4</sup> <sup>(H4).</sup> Observability has an inverse-U-shaped relationship with remixing.

Innovations that are simple (i.e., not complex) are more likely to generate a remix response from an innovation community for several reasons. First, simple innovations are, by nature, easy for the community to understand. Simplicity allows a larger base of community members to envision ways that they could benefit from remixing the source innovation. Given that community members must be able to comprehend (at least generally) an innovation before they can remix it, some community members will find very complex innovations confusing and effortful to understand initially (Rogers 2003). These members may avoid overly complex innovations entirely. In short, complexity acts as a barrier to community engagement with an innovation (Arts et al. 2011).

Second, simple innovations are more straightforward to build on through remixing (and improvements made via remixing may be more apparent). Adding features to or modifying the initial design is less complicated when drawing on a straightforward source innovation (Snodgrass and Vanderwart 1980). Less complex source innovations provide community members with fewer constraints in reimagining the innovation, offering more open-ended possibilities for future engagement (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012). Because of this lack of constraints, simpler innovations may be more enjoyable to remix; enjoyment has been shown to be an important motivator in innovation communities (Ståhlbröst and Bergvall-Kåreborn 2011). The ease and joy of remixing simple source innovations will result in community members collectively remixing these innovations more frequently.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>5</sup> <sup>(H5).</sup> Complexity has a negative relationship with remixing.

The final two hypotheses concern contemporary forms of communication within online communities (front page presence and online community interaction). Members of online communities who are actively engaged in producing original innovations or remixing others’ innovations can be thought of as core members. Conversely, those that only consume others’ innovations (i.e., passive users) can be thought of as being on the periphery of the innovation community (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012, Faraj et al. 2011). Core members typically produce the vast majority of contributions within innovation communities (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012). Peripheral members contribute to the innovation community only sporadically, tending to lurk in the background as invisible observers (Rullani and Haefliger 2013). While peripheral members tend to not develop their own contributions, they are important as consumers of others’ innovations within the community (Setia et al. 2012). This is akin to the “onion-like” hierarchy observed in open source software development (Crowston and Howison 2006).

Core members of online communities prefer the opinions of others within the community, which generally comply with community norms, over communications tactics intended to reach a broad audience (Kozinets et al. 2010). Core members actively take control over their information consumption and resist efforts to direct their attention, such as promotion on the community’s home page (see Mangold and Faulds 2009). Because of the substantial investment in time that core members make within the community and the ease of online communication, core members tend to actively resist this type of online promotion (Cox 1998, Crowston and Howison 2006) as an “inevitable defensive position in the circumstances” (Cox 1998). This is one of the processes that shapes innovation communities into having the core/peripheral structure (Rullani and Haefliger 2013). Since the core members producing the vast majority of remixes do not respond to online promotional tactics such as an innovation having a presence on the community’s home page, this negates any potential impact of front page presence on the community’s remixing behavior.

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>6</sup> <sup>(H6).</sup> Front page presence has no relationship with remixing.

While we argue in H6 that front page presence does not relate with remixing, H7 posits that online community interaction has a positive relationship with remixing, just as traditional word of mouth is typically thought to have a positive relationship with consumption (Agarwal and Prasad 1998, Rogers 2003). Interactive knowledge sharing is germane to online innovation communities; commentary visible to all community members can quickly and easily be generated by any member of the community (von Krogh et al. 2003). This characteristic of online innovation communities may render electronic word of mouth even more impactful in determining community remixing than traditional word of mouth communication is in a typical innovation diffusion context.

Since interactions between members of online innovation communities often include discussion of how to achieve better or different solutions (Shah 2006), online community interaction regarding a source innovation is thought to be one of the most important stimulators of community remixing of that source innovation. Even where discussion among community members points out limitations or needs that are not addressed by a design, this communication may spur the community to address these concerns through remixing. Discourse regarding a new innovation can also be important in establishing the innovation’s legitimacy to the community (Barrett et al. 2013), particularly given the public visibility of community comments. Community members may view community interaction pertaining to a source innovation as an indicator that a remix of that source innovation might also generate conversation within the community, which is likely important to community members concerned with their reputations (see Lerner and Tirole 2002).

<sup>Hypothesis</sup> <sup>7</sup> <sup>(H7).</sup> Online community interaction has a positive relationship with remixing.

## 4. Empirical Study

To examine an online innovation community’s remixing behavior, we use data from Thingiverse (http:// www.thingiverse.com), thought to be the largest online community dedicated to 3D printable objects. Thingiverse allows users to post their objects (i.e., innovations) for other members of the community to print, comment on, and remix. Designers post one or more images of their objects as well as one or more files necessary to print or remix the object on the object’s Thingiverse page. Thingiverse designers generally choose from Creative Commons licenses (McCarthy 2012), which allow more or less restriction on the use and remixing of their objects. When community members print or remix an object, they are directed to report this activity.

Since time is required for community remixing once innovations are made public, we allow one year between the posting of objects and measurement of the frequency of remixing (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez

2012). The first portion of the data we use to test our hypotheses was drawn from a publicly available “snapshot” Thingiverse data set, made available by peerproduction.net. These data were webscraped (i.e., data were systematically harvested from the Internet) on November 5, 2013. Since a one year time lag is desired, we use only the objects publicly posted in October 2012. Of the 79,498 designs that had been publicly posted since the inception of Thingiverse in 2008, 1,150 of these were posted during October 2012. For these 1,150 objects, time-invariant variables (such as the category of the posting and the number of files necessary to print the design) needed for our analysis were added to the original data via manual webscraping. During this process, the original publicly available data were checked for accuracy and consistently found to be reliable.

Thingiverse is an ideal setting to test our hypotheses, given this community’s focus on individual objects. Several aspects of this context and the time period examined ensure that potential user-level determinants of remixing are not likely confounds. First, Thingiverse is a meritocratic space (Klein 2013), where objects (rather than designers) are evaluated. The site architecture is organized around objects, with comments only organized by object. During the time span investigated, Thingiverse users likely knew little about the designer of an object they were examining. Says Eric Mortensen, Thingiverse’s community and audience development manager, “Thingiverse isn’t a social network” (Mortensen 2015). Also, both 3D printing and remixing represent substantial commitments. Each print typically requires several decisions to be made (such as infill—the proportion of a solid object that should be filled in with material during printing), multiple hours of printing time, plus the cost of materials. Remixing also involves a substantial time commitment. This ensures that users are printing or remixing an object due to the merits of that object.

During the time span investigated, user-level features now common on UGC sites had not yet been established within Thingiverse. Currently, like many UGC communities, Thingiverse has a “follow” feature, allowing community members to easily monitor other members to keep up with their work. This feature was launched by Thingiverse in October 2012 (the same month objects in our sample were posted; Mortensen 2015), but was not widely used until much later: “It has taken a very long time for people to pick up ‘following’ on Thingiverse” (Mortensen 2015). Since the follower network was not established on Thingiverse during the time span we investigate, relational differences in users’ network placement cannot confound our results. Also, a “group” feature (letting Thingiverse members participate in discussions with others focused on a particular subject, software tool, or printer) was launched in 2014 (Mortensen 2015). Prior to this, designers’ individual interests were not easily discernible (other than through their designs), and the possibility of a shared group relationship between members did not exist.<sup>1</sup>

Several types of postings inappropriate for our analysis were screened out prior to analysis:

1. We removed postings that were incomprehensible or marked as a user testing his ability to post to the site.

2. We removed postings that contained no files to download or had no images of the object.

3. We removed postings by Makerbot Industries (the parent company of Thingiverse), since these are not UGC.

4. Since we are concerned with functional innovations (i.e., not art or purely aesthetic work), we removed several categories of objects (e.g., “art,” “sculptures,” “scans and replicas”). These categories include objects that are inconsistent with our focus on functional innovations and our application of innovation diffusion theory. Uncategorized posts were also removed.

5. Categories with fewer than 10 postings during the focal time span and those categories with no reported makes or remixes were removed. This allows us to control for category effects without encountering estimation concerns in our analyses.

6. Postings that did not use one of the standard Creative Commons licenses typically used on Thingiverse and postings with licenses that explicitly did not allow for remixing were removed.

After this screening process, our data set contained 498 designs from 22 categories, including 3D printer accessories (n = 87), 3D printer parts (n = 76), kitchen and dining (n = 36), 3D printing (n = 34), mobile phone (n = 32), electronics (n = 26), toy and game accessories (n = 21), and organization (n = 20).

## 4.1. Measurement

The measures (see Appendix A) for Rogers’ (2003) five diffusion factors relate to the object itself, or the object’s Thingiverse page (created by the object’s designer). We do not distinguish between the object and its page; it is not possible to view or access the object without going through the object’s page. Consistent with the total product concept (Levitt 1980), the page is considered an extension of the physical object. One preferred method researchers have used to measure relative advantage is to rely on potential adopters’ evaluations of the innovation (Tornatzky and Klein 1982). Consistent with this approach, we use the number of likes from members of the innovation community normed by the number of page views. Liking is a way to quickly and conspicuously show positivity toward an online object (Gerlitz and Helmond 2013), collectively providing an indicator of whether or not this meritocratic community (Klein 2013) sees a particular design as having advantages over existing innovations. Norming the number of likes by page views avoids bias in the measure due to some objects generating more awareness within the community. Compatibility (whether an innovation is congruent with existing understandings of members of a social system) is measured using an indicator variable indicating whether the source object was itself developed by remixing a previous design (1) or not (0). Being remixed from a previous design specifically addresses one aspect of compatibility identified by Rogers (2003): compatibility with previously introduced ideas. Designs that are remixed from a prior design are, by nature, more consistent with previously introduced ideas within the community than those that are not. The measure employed is consistent with primary measures of compatibility that, for instance, rate innovations on “how much different the innovation is from older ways of doing the job” (Tornatzky and Klein 1982, p. 34), and with secondary studies using patents’ citations to measure technological compatibility (for instance, the compatibility index developed by Maurseth and Verspagen 2002). Importantly, this measure does not capture all dimensions of compatibility; examining other dimensions of compatibility is discussed as a direction for future research. Trialability (the degree to which an innovation may be experimented with) is measured using the number of accommodations granted by the license chosen by the designer. The objects in our data set make use of four different Creative Commons licenses, which govern the following activities: (1) the ability to use the object commercially and (2) the ability to license a remix under different terms than the original object. Thus, our scale for trialability ranges from 0 to 2, indicating the number of allowances made for the particular innovation. Innovations with more accommodating licenses can be experimented with more widely and freely, and thus are more trialable. Both license restrictions measured represent meaningful nontechnical obstacles to experimentation for members of online innovation communities. “I think they [license restrictions] absolutely limit behavior—certainly within the Thingiverse community” (Mortensen 2015). Consider that commercial use of 3D printed objects is very common (for instance, there were over 18,000 3D printed objects for sale on Etsy.com as of February 24, 2016). The first restriction limits commercial experimentation with the object. In cases where this license restriction is absent, commercial use of the object is unconstrained. The second restriction limits experimentation with future remixes, which is a particularly relevant form of experimentation given our research question. We do not claim that this measure exhaustively captures all forms of experimentation with an innovation (i.e., all forms of trialability); other possibilities are discussed as future research directions. Also, recall that we have removed objects from our sample that use licenses explicitly prohibiting remixing. (Since they cannot be remixed, these objects are not informative in addressing our research question.) Accordingly, it can be fairly said that all objects in the sample have at least some degree of trialability. Observability (the degree to which an innovation is available and visible) is measured as the number of images posted by the creator on the innovation’s Thingiverse page. An innovation that has many images posted is considered more observable than an innovation with a single image. Innovations with only one posted image may not allow community members to easily view all aspects of the innovation (which may provoke uncertainty; Rogers 2003). Other objects have 10 or more images posted; we consider these to be more available and visible to the community. Complexity (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and to use by members of a social system) is measured using the number of downloadable files posted within the design’s Thingiverse page that are necessary to print the design. Files provided in alternate formats, resolutions, or sizes are not counted in this measure (as they are not strictly necessary to print the design). While an object requiring only one file tends to be simpler, more complex designs can require a large number of files to be printed for consumption or to be manipulated for a remix. Multiple files are commonly used for designs that contain subcomponents (each of which needs to be printed separately). The requirement of multiple time-consuming prints followed by assembly represents additional difficulty in use (part of the definition of complexity). Consistent with this study’s focus on individual objects, this measure focuses on the “detail complexity” of the object, rather than other forms of complexity (such as interdependence or dynamic complexity), similar to operations management researchers who have used the number of components as an indicator of detail complexity (e.g., Bozarth et al. 2009).

Front page presence (promotion of the innovation on the online community’s home page) is measured as an indicator variable: whether or not the design was featured on Thingiverse’s home page (www.thingiverse .com). “The front page obviously has a much broader audience than the rest of Thingiverse” (Mortensen 2015). In terms of driving community awareness, featuring leads to an innovation getting “a massive boost, probably 0 0 0 several orders of magnitude” (Mortensen 2015). This increase in traffic is essentially the goal of online promotional tactics such as display ads. Once a design is featured (generally within 48 hours of being publicly posted), a badge indicating the design’s featured status is permanently displayed on the design’s Thingiverse page. Note the distinction in measurement between front page presence and observability. While front page presence impacts how often the innovation is seen, observability refers to how much of the innovation can be seen—a trait of the underlying innovation (including the page it resides on). Online community interaction (the degree to which an innovation is extensively discussed online by members of the innovation community) is measured using the number of online comments posted pertaining to the innovation by community members. All community members (including those who have or are considering printing or remixing the object as well as the original designer) may comment. It is possible for back and forth discussion to occur between the object’s creator and a community member interested in the specific object, or between two or more community members without the creator participating. Discussion often relates to requests for help printing the object and settings for various 3D printers, and also includes suggestions, praise, and criticism. A count of the comments on innovations’ discussion boards indicates which objects are being discussed within the community (and conversely, which are not).

Remixing (the innovation community’s use of an existing innovation as source material or inspiration to aid in the development of further innovations) is measured using the cumulative number of members of the community reporting that they have “remixed” the source object into another design (Hill and Monroy-Hernandez 2012).<sup>2</sup> Remixing is captured based on community members reporting their remixing of an object in response to prompts from Thingiverse. The structure of the online community as well as cultural norms within the community appear to support the practice of self-reporting. Remixing correlates closely with downloads $\bar { ( p } < 0 . \bar { 0 } 0 0 1 )$ , indicating that self-reporting is working effectively. Consistent with our earlier discussion of the possibility of remixing without initial consumption in online innovation communities, we note that 35.2% of the objects in our sample that are remixed by the community have zero reported makes (i.e., 3D prints). Community members are remixing without consuming in at least this portion of cases.<sup>3</sup>

Several control variables are used in our regression analyses. Consumption (the innovation community’s use of an existing innovation in its current form) is measured using the cumulative number of members of the online innovation community reporting that they have “made” (i.e., 3D printed) the object (a form of digital consumption; see Belk and Llamas 2013). Note that consumption is used as a control variable, since consumption of an innovation may prompt remixing (for instance, if users are dissatisfied with consumption of the source innovation; Rogers 2003). Date posted allows us to control for the time that has elapsed since the design was posted, using the day of the month (1 to 31); recall that all innovations in our sample were publicly posted in the same month. Twenty-one category indicator variables are used, with 3D printing accessories as the default, to control for potential category effects.

Online community interaction, relative advantage, and compatibility also positively $( p < 0 . 0 1 )$ impact consumption, with nonsignificant findings for the other variables of interest (including the linear and nonlinear effects of observability). These results are consistent with examinations of Rogers’ (2003) diffusion factors in traditional settings; for instance, Tornatzky and Klein’s (1982) meta-analysis also finds that relative advantage and compatibility are significantly related to consumption, with nonsignificant effects of trialability, observability, and complexity.

In models 1 and 2, consumption is shown to be a significant determinant of remixing. The use of an explanatory variable that can itself be predicted by other predictor variables raises concerns for potential bias from endogeneity (Baum et al. 2003, Neter et al. 1996). To address this, we reestimate models 1 and 2, fitting models accounting for endogenous regressors (similar to Han and Mithas 2013). Here, Poisson regression with endogenous regressors (two-step generalized method of moments) is used; the results are shown in Table 2 (models 1b and 2b). Two instrumental variables (variables that are not in the explanatory equation but are related with the endogenous explanatory variable) are specified: description length (the character count of the description of the object written by the designer) and the total number of page views for the focal object. These are appropriate instrumental variables since they only affect remixing indirectly $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ through consumption). First, since the object’s description is typically written by the designer to convince community members of the value of possessing the physical object, we expect that the length of the description will only directly influence consumption. Next, page views are an indicator of community members’ awareness of the object. Many page views are generated by peripheral community members, who are only consuming ideas of others and not adding their own contributions (Crowston and Howison 2006). While a small number of community members typically generate the vast majority of contributions to innovation communities (Dahlander and Frederiksen 2012), it is the large number of peripheral community members that are able to generate substantial page views. The awareness indicated by page views (alongside the set of explanatory variables hypothesized) is not sufficient to impact remixing other than through its effect on consumption. Instrumental variables must also correlate with the endogenous variable and be independent from the error process (Baum et al. 2003). Both instrumental variables significantly correlate with consumption (both $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . A small Hansen’s J statistic indicates that instruments are uncorrelated with the error term. For both models 1b and 2b, the J statistics (Table 2) are very small (both $p < 0 . 1 0 )$ , indicating the validity of the instruments. Since this estimation approach does not substantively change our results (i.e., no coefficient is significantly different using this estimation based on the 95% confidence intervals of the negative binomial coefficients), we can conclude that endogeneity is not a serious threat in interpreting these results.

4.2.2. Fuzzy Set Qualitative Comparative Analysis. To supplement our regression analyses and uncover contextual effects that may be impactful in determining remixing by an online innovation community, we conduct fsQCA using the data set described above. fsQCA’s emphasis is on identifying sets of conditions that lead to the outcome in question (El Sawy et al. 2010, Fiss 2011, Ordanini et al. 2014). Though it is outside the scope of this research to thoroughly introduce fsQCA, several outstanding reviews are available (e.g., Ragin 2008a, Ragin and Rubinson 2011). fsQCA and regression are dissimilar but complementary techniques—while regression’s emphasis is on the discrete effect from each independent variable, fsQCA uses Boolean algebra to identify configurations of conditions that consistently lead to the outcome (Vis 2012). This focus on configurations allows for the detection of complex causal mechanisms. For instance, fsQCA may detect that within one configuration, the presence of a condition leads to an outcome, but within a second configuration, the absence of the same condition is consistently associated with the outcome. fsQCA is an exploratory technique—it investigates the relationships between the specified outcome and all possible combinations of the conditions specified. The ability to uncover combinations of causal conditions that lead to the outcome can produce findings that offer valuable nuance to complement regression-based analysis $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ Amenta et al. 2005, Ordanini et al. 2014, Stanko and Olleros 2013). fsQCA should not be seen as a test of the hypotheses put forward here, but rather, as a way to uncover additional configural nuances that cannot be inferred from the regression analyses. Note that fsQCA has no counterpart to regression’s notion of nonlinear effects.

To begin fsQCA, the data used for the regression analysis must be transformed into fuzzy set scores, representing the level of membership in each set (from 0 to 1). To calculate fuzzy set scores (using fsQCA 2.5), three anchors must be specified for determining membership in each condition: the thresholds for (1) full membership, (2) nonmembership, and (3) the crossover point—the point at which there is maximum ambiguity about a case’s membership status (Kent 2008, Ragin and Rubinson 2011). For instance, a score of 0.75 indicates that an innovation is mostly in the set under consideration, whereas a score of 0.25 indi cates that the innovation is mostly outside the set. Anchors should be set using meaningful theoretical or interpretive rationale, not through the use of sample statistics (Ragin 2007). Appendix B reports the calibration points used in this study, with detailed results and robustness tests. Since there is no way to multi plicatively control for the effect of the date posted in fsQCA, this control variable is not used in our fsQCA. Since use of the category control variables specified in the regression analyses would lead to an uninterpretable number of configurations (there are 22 categories), these are also not used in our fsQCA. Next, we inspect all possible configurations to observe the distribution of innovations in our sample. To ensure that identified solutions are adequately populated, eliminating unpopulated and sparsely populated configurations is necessary (Ragin 2008b). In our case, we remove all configurations where $n \leq 1 .$ , which results in maintaining over 80% of our cumulative sample, consistent with Ragin’s (2008a) benchmark. Since relatively few innovations in our sample garner front page presence (4.2%), removing sparsely populated configurations results in no configurations that include front page presence. Accordingly, we remove the front page presence variable from the fsQCA.

Table B.2 reports fsQCA results. In fsQCA, consistency is the degree to which cases sharing a combination of conditions demonstrate the outcome in question (Kent 2008, Ragin 2008b). For all analyses, a consistency cutoff of 0.75 is used (Ragin 2008a). In fsQCA, coverage is a measure of the proportion of cases of interest explained by the causal expression. It is analogous to regression’s $R ^ { 2 } ,$ the degree to which the solution predicts the outcome (Ragin 2008b). Two explanatory configurations consistently lead to community remixing. Consistency scores for the two configurations are 0.79 and 0.78; these configurations usually, but not always, result in remixing. First, innovations that are relatively advantageous, compatible, trialable, nonobservable, noncomplex, characterized by high levels of online community interaction, and consumed tend to be remixed by the community. Second, innovations that are characterized by all explanatory conditions specified (see Table B.2) are also likely to be remixed. In fsQCA notation, the midlevel dot represents “and,” with the symbol ∼ representing “not.” Thus, the configurations associated with remixing can be denoted as follows:

1. Relative advantage  compatibility  trialability  ∼observability  ∼complexity  online community interaction  consumption;

2. Relative advantage  compatibility  trialability observability  complexity  online community interaction  consumption.

Many of the findings from this analysis are consistent with our regression findings. Relative advantage, online community interaction, and consumption are consistently associated with remixing, just as they were found to be in the regression analyses. There are also several novel insights generated. Within configuration 2, complexity promotes remixing, indicating that high levels of complexity may prompt remixing under specific circumstances. Within configuration 1, a low level of complexity is associated with remixing; it is noteworthy that this configuration also is characterized by nonobservability. Interestingly, within these configurations, nonobservable, noncomplex innovations are likely to be remixed, just as observable and complex innovations are. Simple, nonobservable innovations may allow for some learning through remixing that is not otherwise possible (due to the lack of observability) within this configuration. fsQCA refines our thinking and shows this relationship to be complexly contingent: for innovations that are members of the other sets indicated, an appropriate alignment of observability and complexity promotes community remixing. Finally, while our regression analyses did not detect significant effects of compatibility or trialability, fsQCA indicates that within these two configurations, both are important to ensuring remixing.

Comparison of the two fsQCA solutions (see Table B.2) shows that the observability and complexity conditions change in tandem, with no other differences between configurations. Accordingly, we ran a regression model (otherwise identical to model 2) that includes the interaction of these two terms (observability × complexity). The interaction term is nonsignificant (p < 0010), with other terms materially unchanged. This indicates that the contingent effect fsQCA identifies is only impactful within these specific configurations— there is no significant interaction of observability and complexity for the sample as a whole. fsQCA’s ability to identify this contextual, contingent effect that is not detectable by regression analysis across the entire sample illustrates the complementary nature of fsQCA and regression (Ragin 2008a).

## 5. Discussion

This study develops and tests arguments theorizing an online innovation community’s propensity to remix innovations (i.e., the community’s use of an existing innovation as source material or inspiration to aid in the development of further innovations), drawing on variables constituent to innovation diffusion theory. Theorizing and empirically demonstrating the effects of these “remix factors” with a large sample of innovations is a needed step toward understanding this type of knowledge collaboration in online innovation communities, which is not bound by some of the constraints typical in traditional environments. For instance, knowledge collaboration between the creator of a source innovation and the community of remixers can happen without members knowing each other and without consumption of the source innovation. Within online innovation communities, the nature of what it means for an innovation to diffuse is changing (Lessig 2008). In addition to passively consuming innovations developed by others, community members expect to actively engage with innovations, remixing to further innovate and make them their own. By understanding these remix factors, our findings empower innovators to put forward work that is most likely to generate an energetic remix response from an innovation community.

Online innovation communities are interactive in nature. Members can easily take part in publicly visible discourse regarding an innovation; these online communities give user innovators a platform for knowledge sharing. We find that this community interaction is very influential in determining whether an innovation will be remixed extensively by an online community. Publicly visible online discussion pertaining to an innovation may contain criticism of the innovation, which encourages community members to address shortcomings of the innovation by remixing it. Discussion among community members may also pertain to alternative uses or customizations for the innovation, which again prompts community remixing. In reputation-conscious online communities (Lerner and Tirole 2002), community interaction serves to bring attention to particular interest areas, which likely encourages further related developments (such as remixes). While community interaction is influential in determining an innovation’s propensity to be remixed by the community, front page presence is not an effective tool to stimulate remixing. The core users generating the vast majority of remixes within innovation communities actively resist this type of promotional effort (Crowston and Howison 2006). Our findings suggest that untargeted online advertising will not be efficient in instances where encouraging remixing is the goal. It appears that front page presence allows an innovation to achieve a level of awareness within the community (which is necessary for consumption), but remixing requires greater impetus (potentially need- or learning-based), which can be fostered by extensive interaction between community members.

The inverse-U-shaped relationship between observability and remixing points to the value placed on learning by community members. While some observability is necessary for innovations to spark interest and for community members to begin experiential learning, too much observability dampens the community’s tendency to remix an innovation. Overly observable innovations may act to push some potential remixers toward passive learning (i.e., observation) instead of active learning (i.e., remixing).

Consumption’s role in determining remixing within online innovation communities has not yet been thoroughly examined. Here, consumption is shown to be an important determinant of community remixing, but is also shown to not be strictly necessary to remixing within these communities. The necessity of prior consumption, which restricts user innovation in traditional diffusion environments, does not constrain remixing within online innovation communities. Remixing without prior consumption is commonplace within online innovation communities. That said, the consumption of an innovation does prompt remixing, as members of an innovation community conceive of improvements and personalizations based on their consumption experiences with the source innovation.

fsQCA results provide additional configural nuance in our understanding of the determinants of remixing that could have been only very partially intuited based on the regression analyses. For innovations that are characterized by relative advantage, compatibility, and trialability as well as high degrees of online community interaction and consumption, those innovations that are nonobservable and noncomplex are consistently remixed, as are innovations that are observable and complex. Within these configurations, proper alignment between observability and complexity is impactful on the propensity to remix. Noncomplex innovations with lower levels of observability will be accessible to innovation community members of all skill and knowledge levels while allowing for learning (even from relatively simple innovations that require less observability to be understood; Labay and Kinnear 1981). For observable, complex innovations, it seems plausible that learning may still be possible through remixing due to the source’s complexity, while observability allows community members’ experiential learning to begin. Consider also the two alignments of observability and complexity that are not associated with remixing within these configurations: complex, nonobservable innovations may prove too difficult for community members to understand and creatively remix. Finally, for noncomplex innovations that are highly observable, remixers’ learning is limited.

## 5.1. Managerial Implications

This research has meaningful implications for innovators and managers concerned with the remixing of their innovations. First, in instances where remixing is sought, promoting the innovation on an online community’s home page will not be efficient. Where remixing is desired, promotional efforts should be focused on tactics generating interaction between community members, such as blogger outreach or otherwise encouraging community engagement (Füller et al. 2008). Innovators wishing to foster remixing would also be well served to avoid very high levels of observability. It is conceivable that a staged approach, in which community members could see some source innovation detail immediately with more information becoming available as learning occurs (for instance, in the form of a user toolkit; Franke and Hader 2014, Jeppesen 2005), would foster remixing.

## 5.2. Limitations and Future Research Directions

Based on our goal to understand the determinants of an innovation being remixed by an online innovation community, we have investigated constructs constituent to innovation diffusion theory alongside two forms of contemporary communication that characterize these online communities. We do not claim this to be an exhaustive set of the determinants of remixing; other meaningful predictors of remixing likely exist. Variables congruent to learning may be a good starting point. Some categories of online community interaction (see Kudaravalli and Faraj 2008, von Krogh et al. 2003) may prove more impactful on remixing; for instance, it is conceivable that the posting of particular types of questions (e.g., requests for technical resources) may signal the community’s further learning interest that may manifest through remixing. Commentary containing feedback on the innovation may prompt remixes due to heightened motivation to improve the existing innovation. It is not entirely clear whether positive or negative comments regarding the innovation have a greater effect on community remixing.

It will likely prove worthwhile to apply user-centric (rather than product-centric) theoretical lenses to predict remixing. Importantly, this study did not attempt to investigate differences between users. Given that users have different interests as well as levels of expertise and influence, these factors may be important in determining remixing in other contexts (e.g., Susarla et al. 2012). It is also possible that in UGC contexts with conspicuous user-level differences, these differences may mediate or moderate the innovation level effects found here. On the other hand, the identity ambiguity inherent to online communities (Faraj et al. 2011) may have interesting creativity effects and social implications. Investigation of possible nuances in determining remixing within a team setting is also warranted. While we have drawn implications for innovating firms, further investigation into firm-sponsored activity is needed (West and O’Mahony 2008).

As Rogers (2003, p. 225) acknowledges, the diffusion factors will necessarily have slightly different meanings across innovation contexts. For instance, there may be other valuable perspectives on complexity; the innovation’s interdependence (i.e., standalone versus part of a larger system) may impact its propensity to be remixed. While this study’s measure of compatibility focuses on compatibility with previously introduced ideas, other dimensions (such as compatibility with values and beliefs) may prove impactful on community remixing. The measure of trialability used here focuses on nontechnical aspects of experimentation; other measurement perspectives on trialability are warranted. Finally, the measures of consumption and remixing employed here rely on members of the innovation community to collectively report these activities when prompted. Replication of this study in a UGC context in which consumption and remixing are monitored by investigators, such as controlled experiments, is worthwhile.

By identifying factors influential in determining the innovations that are likely to be extensively remixed by online innovation communities, this research represents a step forward in understanding this type of knowledge collaboration. There is ample room for further research in this area, and we hope that researchers will build on these findings in the future.

While the methodological triangulation used in this study represents one form of robustness check, it is also possible to specifically test the calibration thresholds used for fsQCA. We follow the approach of Skaaning (2011) to examine calibration thresholds for robustness. Alternative thresholds are set close to the original thresholds, making “only minor changes 0 0 0 to ensure that similar theoretical justifications could apply to the original as well as the new anchors” (Skaaning 2011, p. 395). It is not practical to set alternative thresholds for compatibility (an indicator), trialability, complexity, consumption, or remixing (these are all integers lacking the range to meaningfully lower the thresholds, with a high number of zero values for consumption and remixing). Thus, we establish alternative thresholds for relative advantage, observability, and online community interaction (see Table B.1) to detect whether the configurations reported are robust to small changes in the calibration of these sets.

Next, 26 replication analyses are manually conducted. Systematic combinations of the original calibration thresholds and the alternatives are specified to observe the impact of changes to the calibration thresholds. As with the original analysis, all configurations in which $n \leq 1$ are removed, and a consistency cutoff of 0.75 is used. Following Skaaning (2011), additional configurations are disregarded.
