# Assessing the impact of knowledge management strategies announcements on the market value of firms

Byounggu Choi <sup>a,</sup>\*, Ana Maria Jong <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> College of Business Administration, Kookmin University, 861-1 Jungnung-dong Sungbuk-gu, Seoul 136-702, Republic of Korea <sup>b</sup> Objective Corporation Limited, Australia

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Article history: Received 25 January 2007 Received in revised form 31 August 2009 Accepted 27 September 2009 Available online 12 October 2009

Keywords: Knowledge management strategy Event study methodology Stock market reaction Market value Knowledge-based view

## A B S T R A C T

Although there is much literature on the relationship between KM strategies and organizational performance, the benefit of KM strategies is not well understood. We addressed this issue by exploring how KM strategies influence a firm’s market value using event study methodology. We evaluated the cumulative abnormal returns for KM strategies announced by U. S. firms from 1998 to 2003. Our findings supported the hypothesis that firms’ announcements about their KM strategies provoked positive reactions in the market. More specifically, strategies that focus on either (i) knowledge reusability through IT or (ii) knowledge sharing through informal discussions among employees contributed to higher performance than strategies that emphasized both. This outcome empirically supported our argument that the emphasis on either tacit or explicit knowledge results in a better market value of the firm. Furthermore, the market’s reaction to the announcement is dependent on the firm’s industry classification. The findings of this study offer insights that may help managers maximize the market impact of their KM strategies.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations are increasingly undertaking KM initiatives and making significant investments in them. Furthermore, investors are paying more attention, when valuing a firm, to announcements concerning KM activities. For example, Lockheed Martin’s announcement of their effort provided its employees with a centralized base that allows them to access the expertise of seasoned employees without constantly having to ask for help; this affected investors’ evaluation of the firm, resulting in a positive cumulative abnormal return (CAR) assessed at about 4%.

Most organizations continue to make efforts to realize benefits using KM. However, despite their best efforts, it is not clear how KM enablers can be employed strategically. KM strategies are high-level plans that focus on providing the organization with the knowledge and capabilities needed to achieve its visions and goals. They provide the framework within which the organization can manage new ways of using KM. Furthermore, KM strategies outline KM processes required for managing knowledge effectively [25]. KM strategies have become a fundamental issue for many companies [1].

Several studies have categorized the different types of KM strategies that organizations may adopt. The emphasis of these studies has generally been on how KM strategies affect organizational performance. With a codification strategy, knowledge is stored in repositories systematically, while a personalization strategy implies person-to-person communication. We believe that companies undertaking either of the two strategies in isolation produce higher performance than companies adopting both strategies.

Although the concept of KM strategy is receiving increased attention, the literature primarily relies on case studies, anecdotes, and conceptual frameworks. Empirical studies remain few and far between. Moreover, among the few empirical studies that have explored KM strategies, the majority have focused on the relationship between KM strategies and non-financial performance. These studies have not addressed the effects of KM strategies on financial performance. Understanding what performance gains firms can receive from KM strategies is essential before they can implement KM strategies. However, previous studies have not provided a way for executives to understand the true benefits that can be achieved from KM investments.

We examined the impact of KM on a firm’s perceived market value. This was pursued by employing an event study to assess the impact of KM strategies announced by firms on the market value of the firms. Event study methodology uses change in market value to measure the market’s response to a variety of announcements, such as KM system implementation. Positive fluctuations in the stock market as the result of firm’s announcements reflect the benefits that the market expects the implementation to contribute to the organization’s value.

## 2. Literature review

A review of prior KM research uncovered key enablers for successful KM in organizations; these included human, technical, and organizational resources. The studies have shown how KM enablers affect organizational performance. For example, Sabherwal and Sabherwal [21] examined the effects of contextual factors on CAR associatedwith IT-based KM announcements by adopting event study methodology. The results of their study highlighted the need for managers to consider the specific circumstances of their firms in deciding whether IT-based KM efforts would be appropriate.

However, only a few researchers paid attention to understanding the relationship between KM strategies and organizational performance empirically, owing to the difficulty inherent to the assessment of KM strategies and their effects on organizational performance. They can be classified into three categories: (i) financial, (ii) non-financial, and (iii) both. Illustrative studies in each of these categories are listed in Table 1.

The studies in the first category measure the impact of KM strategies on organizational performance by using financial indices likereturnonsales(ROS),returnonassets(ROA),andreturnonequity (ROE). Pai [18] empirically investigated 20 Taiwanese integrated circuit (IC)design firmsfrom1997 to2002,and categorized them into discoverers, discretionists, external learners, internal exploiters, and overall creationists – emphasizing both learning from external sources and exploitation from internal sources. It was found that the internal exploiters were the best performers, while the discoverers were the worst in terms of ROA and ROE.

The second category examined the relationship between KM strategies and non-financial performance such as effectiveness, efficiency, and innovativeness. Choi and Lee [4] examined 54 Korean companies and categorized their KM strategies into passive, system-oriented, human-oriented, and dynamic. The dynamic strategy – focusing on both knowledge reusability through IT and knowledge sharing through informal discussions among employees – was found to result in higher performance. Keskin [13] divided KM strategies into explicit- and tacit-oriented and investigated the relationship between KM strategies and organizational performance. He found that KM strategies had a positive effect on organizational performance, and that the impact on performance was higher with the explicit-oriented strategy than with the other. Schulz and Jobe [24] developed four categories of KM strategies – codification, tacit, focused, and unfocused – used by multinational corporations in the U.S. and Denmark. Empirical analyses suggested that the focused strategy was superior to the others. They also indicated that different kinds of organizational knowledge required matching forms of codification to increase subunit performance.

The third category compared and analyzed organizational performance depending on KM strategies involving both financial and non-financial performance. Ordonez de Pablos [17] attempted to identify how KM strategies affect organizational performance. She applied Bierly and Chakrabarti’s typology of generic KM strategies to analyze a cross-section of Spanish firms. A significant relationship between KM strategies and non-financial performance was found. However, the ROA used to measure the financial performance of the firms demonstrated that the firms’ performance was not influenced by the KM strategy adopted. Table 1 presents these studies in terms of KM strategy categories, organizational performance, suggested KM strategy, and the research findings.

From these studies, some interesting inferences can be drawn. First, empirical researchers agree that tacit- and explicit-oriented strategies are central to the KM strategy of an organization. Even when some studies have investigated other factors, they described KM strategies basically along tacit and explicit dimensions, reflecting their KM focus.

Second, guidelines for choosing the right KM strategies are unclear. Although many conceptual studies have shed light on guidelines for employing explicit- or tacit-oriented strategies, they are not consistent. Some attempt to strike a balance between them, while others focus on only one. Such inconsistent results can also be found in the empirical studies. Some suggest equal emphasis on the two strategies, while others have argued that companies should pursue one strategy but use the other to support it, and that straddling indicates a lack of focus, and should thus be avoided. Therefore, a significant challenge is to clarify the relationship between KM strategies and organizational performance.

## 3. Development of the research hypotheses

## 3.1. KM strategies

Many KM strategies have been developed to help an organization manage its knowledge. Among them, we tried to identify the relationship between KM strategies and financial performance on the basis of Choi and Lee’s work, developed on the basis of the knowledge-based view (KBV), which holds that knowledge assets can be a unique resource that may lead to a long-term sustainable competitive advantage [23], and provide a strong theoretical underpinning. The KBV identifies two types of knowledge: tacit and explicit, which are a parsimonious yet powerful pair of KM strategy types. They also empirically examined the relationship between KM strategies and organizational performance using companies from various industries, analyzing the relationship between KM strategies and industry types.

KM strategies can be split into dynamic, system-, humanoriented, and passive, according to their level of explicit- or tacitoriented KM, as shown in Fig. 1.

Companies using a passive strategy are ineffective in integrating different streams of knowledge and implementing new knowledge into the organization. They do not truly exploit their knowledge, do not manage their knowledge systematically, nor recognize the importance of sharing their knowledge within the firm.

Companies using the system-oriented strategy explicitly attempt to increase organizational efficiencies by codifying and reusing knowledge through advanced IT, eliminating the threat of knowledge assets leaving the company when an employee resigns and eliminating the need for further communication and coordination among organizational members. Economies of scale and organizational effectiveness occurs. This strategy is appropriate for knowledge-routinized organizations who emphasize knowledge embedded in technologies, rules, and procedures

The human-oriented strategy takes a personalization approach, where tacit knowledge is communicated through person-to-person contact. The emphasis of this strategy is on acquiring and sharing tacit knowledge and interpersonal experience. Organization members learn from each other; but intimacy and trust among members is essential. Firms following this strategy rely on strategic communities, mentoring, and storytelling for effective KM. This strategy is appropriate for expert-dependent organizations who focus on the competencies of key members or analyst-dependent organizations that emphasize the skills of key members.

The dynamic strategy focuses on firms aligning their strategies with knowledge characteristics; it takes an aggressive role on both system- and human-oriented strategies with unbounded knowledge sources. Firms employing this strategy stay ahead of the competition by aggressively seeking existing knowledge as well as creating new knowledge faster than their competitors. This strategy is suited for communication-intensive organizations who vigorously exploit knowledge while exploring new prospects.

## 3.2. Hypotheses

Firms with a passive strategy do not announce any strategic KM events and thus could not be investigated. However, the moderating effect of the industry was considered in order to examine the relationships between KM strategies and organizational performance for different industry types.

## 3.2.1. KM strategies announcement and market value

The systems-oriented strategy focuses on codifying and storing organizational knowledge. Some studies have warned that relying on such a strategy could reduce the firm’s performance, because it leads its employees to depend on decontextualized and encoded knowledge to guide their behaviors [11], resulting in the loss of ambiguity of the knowledge; which renders the duplication or imitation of knowledge easy, resulting in erosion of the firm’s performance advantage. However, many studies have shown that this strategy provides fast and reliable access to organizational knowledge across geographical, social, and organizational boundaries, increasing the firm’s profits by providing economic reuse of knowledge [27], enabling firms to enjoy competitive advantage. Firms adopting this strategy can facilitate knowledge flow by using IT (Intranets, shared databases, expert systems, and e-mail) [26]. Furthermore, importance of the system-oriented strategy is increasing due to the globalized business environment requiring knowledge sharing and transfer between geographically dispersed subunits. This strategy allows companies to retain their best practices and identify and exploit new business opportunities. Therefore, investors should react positively to a system-oriented strategy announcement, creating a positive abnormal stock market return around the date of such an announcement. Thus:

Hypothesis 1. Announcements of a system-oriented strategy will have a positive impact on a firm’s abnormal returns.

The human-oriented strategy can enhance organizational performance by activating organizational members’ tacit knowledge. Embedded tacit knowledge retained by the organization is the most secure and strategically significant since it may be difficult for other organizations to understand, imitate, and duplicate it. Organizations improve their competitive advantage on the basis of this immobility and inimitability source of sustainable advantage. It also helps companies create new tacit knowledge, such as a shared mental model or technical skills within its communities, due to informal training and one-on-one mentoring [10]. Created tacit knowledge allows organizations to change and provide customized service, ultimately resulting in improved competitiveness [7]. Moreover, since knowledge is shared and exchanged through informal, non-routine, and personal contacts, this strategy prevents involuntary transfers of organizational knowledge. Thus, this strategy can be expected to generate significant benefits for companies, and result in increased organizational performance. Thus, markets, including individual and institutional investors, etc. are expected to react positively to such strategy announcements.

Hypothesis 2. Announcements of a human-oriented strategy will have a positive impact on a firm’s abnormal returns.

The relationships between dynamic strategy and organizational performance have been investigated from two different perspectives. One pointed out the danger of a strategy which emphasized both system- and human-oriented strategies and insisted that companies should pursue mainly one strategy [9]. The other argued that neither human- nor system-oriented strategy alone was sufficient to manage organizational knowledge effectively and recommended a symbiotic approach [12]; over-emphasizing one at the expense of the other may result in the organization losing its competitive advantage. By following on both strategies, companies may improve their organizational efficiency by codifying knowledge, thus can enhance their effectiveness by providing customized knowledge to their customers; i.e., an organization should seek an integrated approach.

We attempted to identify the relationship between a dynamic strategy and organizational performance for the latter view, because the benefits of the symbiotic approach have been supported by empirical as well as conceptual studies; e.g., [8]. Therefore, we anticipated a positive relationship between the dynamic strategy and firm value, and that organizations who adopted the dynamic strategy would gain a higher return on market value.

Hypothesis 3. Announcements of the dynamic strategy have a positive impact on a firm’s abnormal returns.

Hypothesis 4. A firm’s abnormal returns will be significantly higher for announcements of the dynamic strategy than for announcements of either the system-oriented strategy or the human-oriented strategy.

## 3.2.2. KM strategies and industry types

The effect of a firm’s industry type has also been considered in KM strategy-related literature. King and Zeithaml [14] believed that organizations should use KM strategies that fit their industry types. Therefore, we also tried to identify the effects of industry type on the relationship between KM strategies and firm performance. The underlying assumption was that the KM strategies adopted by a firm should be influenced by industry types classified as manufacturing, financing, or service.

KM strategies of manufacturing companies are believed to have a higher corporate performance if they adopt a human-oriented strategy over the system-oriented strategy. Product development in manufacturing firms requires the integration of a broad range of knowledge about R&D, marketing, and finance. It is necessary to access the functional knowledge pertinent to the product, and then to integrate and synthesize that knowledge [20]. Thus, manufacturing firms require frequent interaction between employees in different departments to coordinate their ideas and knowledge [5], and require interaction with external experts and customers to determine which new products are closer to their customers’ needs. Such dynamic interactions enable firms to combine knowledge about their environments in unique ways, and to transform this knowledge into new designs and manufacturing processes. Therefore, manufacturing firms need to adopt a humanoriented strategy to support their dynamic interactions.

Similarly, service firms may achieve higher performance by adopting a human-oriented strategy because they should provide knowledge-based services [16]. Although a system-oriented strategy may improve its ability to service customers more rapidly and reduce service costs by reusing formalized prior knowledge, customer satisfaction and loyalty may suffer because this strategy may provide little discretion in fulfilling customer needs. A human-oriented strategy helps firms create new tacit knowledge that serves as a source of creativity, enabling firms to change innovatively, and provide customized services, which results in improved performance.

Financial firms are believed to achieve higher performance by adopting a system-oriented approach because a large portion of their business processes can be formalized. Although they do offer some less codifiable services, such as advice to customers, their major services are easily codified. In addition, the products or services provided by financial firms are better supported by IT because they are information intense. Therefore, the relationships between KM strategies and organizational performance are expected to differ depending on the industry types.

Hypothesis 5. A firm’s abnormal returns attributable to KM strategies differ depending on the industry type to which it belongs.

## 4. Research methodology

Assessing the impact of KM on performance improvements is difficult due to the difficulty in measuring knowledge. However, one purpose of managing knowledge is to increase the returns to the organization, and thus measuring its benefits is essential for KM [3]. Without measurable success, support for KM is not likely to improve, or even continue. Previous studies used ROA, ROS, ROE, or ROI. However, these accounting-based measures have been criticized because they limit the capability to penetrate into the underlying process in which knowledge is stored. Organizational knowledge may reside beyond the financial surface [31]. Moreover, since accounting-based measures are mainly concentrated on monitoring historical results, they may be inappropriate in assessing a firm’s future performance.

In an attempt to overcome these deficiencies of accountingbased measures, we used an event study methodology. It captures the overall assessment of a large number of investors of the discounted value of current and future firm performance due to individual events, which affect the market value of the firm [29]. In addition, it depends on stock market reactions that can be easily observed. However, it has several disadvantages. For example, if its assumptions are violated, the empirical results can be biased or inaccurate, and thus may prove problems (see [28] for a detailed review). However, the current market value of the firm reflects the present value of all future benefits to the firm, both short- and long-term. Thus, this methodology provides a powerful technique for assessing the effectiveness of the firm in foreseeing and adapting to its changing environment.

The event study methodology can be used by researchers to assess organizational performance by examining the stock market’s response to an announcement [19], assuming that the market prices already reflect all prior information, all changes in the firm’s earnings is rapidly reflected in the stock price. Thus, any abnormal stock price change that results represents the expected future benefit as perceived by investors [30]. Thus, when the firm’s stock is revaluated after an announcement, providing an abnormal return, then the announcement affected the market value of the firm.

## 4.1. Data

The objective of our study was to analyze the stock price behavior of firms announcing plans to adopt a particular KM strategy. In order to accomplish this, a sample of KM strategyrelated announcements was constructed for the period from January 1, 1998 to December 31, 2003 using two leading news sources: Business Wire and PR Newswire. The Lexis/Nexis online search feature was first used to identify 15,769 articles with headline and lead paragraphs containing the following words: (i) KM, KM strategies, KM initiatives, KM systems, knowledge creation, knowledge acquisition, knowledge sharing, knowledge transfer, knowledge integration, knowledge synthesis; and (ii) elearning, center of excellence, virtual community, strategic community, community of knowledge, community of practice, learning organization, and organizational learning. After examining the articles to remove unrelated announcements, 295 articles remained; we excluded announcements containing information about general trends in KM, a partnerships between two or more KM vendors, sales or performance of specific KM products, KM conferences, the performance of a particular KM product, and KM awards. After the full text of the 295 articles had been investigated, only 121 were retained, excluding announcements on nonprofit or government organizations, or firms not listed on major U.S. security exchanges (NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ).

The sample was reduced further by discarding announcements: (i) that occurred during the period from 2 days before the KM announcement to 2 days after it, thereby possibly confounding the issue of the KM announcement; those removed include announcements of dividends, earnings, etc. for the same firm, (ii) more than one KM announcement for the same firm such that the earlier announcement was within the period starting 6 months before the second announcement (in such a situation only the earlier announcement was included), and (iii) announcements by firms which were listed on stock exchanges within 122 days, because these firms were new listings and did not have a trading history of 120 days prior to the first day of the event window. These steps reduced the final sample to 79: 7 for 1998, 8 for 1999, 10 for 2000, 18 for 2001, 20 for 2002, and 16 for 2003. The final sample is provided in Appendix A.

## 4.2. Measures

Content analysis (see [6] for a detailed review) was used to extract KM strategies from the text of the announcements. It applies quantitative analysis to examine the documents and provide tools to assess the impact on the market value of KM strategies announced by firms. In addition, it is nonintrusive because it analyzes existing texts. Thus, it does not suffer from researcher demand bias and is relevant to the study of KM strategies, where access to informants is often a serious problem.

In order to analyze KM announcements objectively, we used several criteria proposed by earlier studies. The criteria and related literature used in our study are reported in Appendix B. Examples of abbreviated announcements are shown in Appendix C.

Two raters, including one of the authors and a KM specialist in a global consulting company, independently categorized each announcement into either a system-, human- or dynamic-oriented strategy. When a KM announcement was classified differently, the two raters discussed it and then the author-rater decided its classification. The interrater reliability assessed by the Cohen’s Kappa was 0.78 which was deemed acceptable.

The interaction effect of KM strategies and industry types on organizational performance was also an objectives of our experiment. For examining industry effects, the announcements were classified into one of three industries: manufacturing, financial, or service. Two-digit level standard industrial classification (SIC) codes of the U.S. Department of Commerce were adopted and modified slightly in order to classify the industry types of the sample firms.

## 4.3. Data analysis

Determining whether KM strategies announcements represented new information to investors and affected the stock price required an estimate of the return of the stock would have been if the announcement had not taken place. To do this, we calculated the abnormal returns, which are assumed to reflect the stock market’s reaction to the new announcements, using a market model for each firm with an estimation window. CAR will be positive if most market participants expect that the announcement will result in a positive cash flows in future.

Using procedures employed in prior KM event studies, the model to compute CAR was:

$$
R _ {i, t} = \alpha_ {i} + \beta_ {i} R _ {m, t} + \varepsilon_ {i, t}\tag{1}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l} R _ {i, t} = \text { rate   of   return   of   stock   for   the   firm } i \text { on   day } t; \\ R _ {m, t} = \text { rate   of   return   on   the   market   portfolio   on   day } t; \\ \alpha_ {i}, \beta_ {i} = \text { are   market   model   intercept   and   slope   parameters   for   firm } i; \\ \varepsilon_ {i, t} = \text { disturbance   term   for   stock } i \text { on   day } t. \end{array}
$$

We used the S&P 500 as the index of market portfolio. The S&P is a capitalization-weighted index which indicates the price trend movements based on a broad cross-section of the market. For estimating the market model, a 120-day estimation period was employed. The length of the period used in our study was consistent with prior studies of capital market response [2].

To calculate CAR, a 5-day event window $\left( t = - 2 \tan \tt { + } 2 \right)$ was used. The crucial design issue in an event study is the length of the event window; it should depend on the event being studied. Sabherwal and Sabherwal [22] argued that a short period should be included prior to a KM event in the event window because there may be a leak of information about a firm’s planned activities. Furthermore, they insisted that the period of a day or more after the KM announcement should be considered in order to capture any reaction that may occur in the first few days after the event. Following these suggestions, we used a 5-day event window.

In order to predict the expected return over the t = [-2, +2] event window, we used the coefficient found from regression (1). The abnormal return for stock i on event day t was computed as:

$$
A R _ {i, t} = R _ {i, t} - (\hat {\alpha} _ {i} + \hat {\beta} _ {i} R _ {m, t})\tag{2}
$$

The estimated abnormal returns were unbiased estimates of changes in the market value of firm i during the event period, that can be attributed to investors’ reactions to the announcement. The variance for abnormal returns was calculated as:

$$
\operatorname{var} \left(A R _ {i, t}\right) = \left(s _ {i} ^ {2} \left[ 1 + \frac {1}{T _ {i}} + \frac {\left(R _ {m , t} - \bar {R} _ {m}\right) ^ {2}}{\sum_ {\tau} ^ {T _ {i}} \left(R _ {m , \tau} - \bar {R} _ {m}\right) ^ {2}} \right]\right)\tag{3}
$$

where

$s _ { i } ^ { 2 } =$ residual return variance from the estimation of market model;

$$
\begin{array}{l} \bar {R} _ {m} = \text { mean   market   return   over   the   prediction   interval }; \\ T _ {i} = \text { number   of   days   in   estimation   interval   (in   our   case,   120) }; \\ \tau_ {i} = \text { observation   within   the   event   window }; \\ t _ {i} = \text { the   day   in   the   estimation   interval }. \end{array}
$$

The abnormal returns were cumulated over the event window (t = 2) in order to calculate CAR and var(CAR):

$$
C A R _ {i, t} = \sum_ {j = - t} ^ {t} A R _ {i, j}\tag{4}
$$

$$
\operatorname{var} \left(C A R _ {i, t}\right) = \sum_ {j = - t} ^ {t} \operatorname{var} \left(A R _ {i, t}\right)\tag{5}
$$

CAR depending on KM strategies and industry types are summarized in Table 7. Since two firms belonged to the oil and gas extraction industry, they were dropped when analyzing the industry effect on the relationships between KM strategies and CAR.

Table 8 shows that the interaction effect was significant $( F = 3 8 . 4 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ . Industry types moderate the association between KM strategies and CAR.

The analysis can be summarized as follows. First, the stock market positively reacts to a firm’s KM announcements of a system- or human-oriented strategy, thus supporting Hypotheses 1 and 2. Second, the stock market shows no significant reaction to a dynamic strategy. Therefore, Hypothesis 3 was not supported. Third, announcements related to the dynamic strategy resulted in lower CAR than those for the system- and human-oriented strategy. Therefore, Hypothesis 4 was not supported. Finally, the relationship between KM strategies and CAR depending on industry types was shown in Fig. 5. As expected, the humanoriented strategy showed the highest CAR in the manufacturing and service industry, while the system-oriented strategy exhibited the highest CAR in the financial industry and the lowest CAR in the manufacturing industry. Therefore, Hypothesis 5 was supported.

## 6.2. Implications and conclusions

Our study adopted a new approach for investigating the relationship between KM strategies and firm value. We used event study methodology which appears to be able to capture both tangible and intangible benefits, with the current and future benefits of a KM investment since it focuses on the effect on the equity price.

Our study extended previous work by showing an empirical relationship between KM strategy announcements and stock prices. We found that investors expected future benefits to a firm’s performance as a result of system- and human-oriented strategy implementation. Our results enable managers to measure the effects of KM, and help them make decisions on the issue of KM strategies and KM-related investment priorities.

Obviously, IT is the impetus for the initial interest in KM. Furthermore, ITs enable firms to acquire or access explicit and simple knowledge from documented and computerized sources in a relatively efficient and timely manner. Thus, the effective development or adoption of IT is a key enabler for the implementation of successful KM strategies. Well-constructed IT, however, does not guarantee successful KM. An organization may have difficulties in establishing its knowledge creating environment due to the lack of an appropriate culture despite its well-constructed IT. In addition, initiating KM only through IT faces high risk of failure [15]. In order to avoid the risk, companies should integrate system- and humanoriented strategies by paying more attention to enhancing tacit knowledge by a variety of methods such as community of practices.

As expected, industry type appears to affect the relationship between KM strategies and a firm’s market value. It appears that investors consider a variety of KM-related factors when they assess the future benefit of KM announcements. Our result implies that managers should consider both the firm’s external and internal factors simultaneously in formulating KM strategies.
