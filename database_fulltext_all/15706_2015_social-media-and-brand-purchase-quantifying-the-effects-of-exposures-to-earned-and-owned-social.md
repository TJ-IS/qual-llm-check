# Social Media and Brand Purchase: Quantifying the Effects of Exposures to Earned and Owned Social Media Activities in a Two-Stage Decision Making Model

KAREN XIE AND YOUNG-JIN LEE

KAREN XIE is an assistant professor at the Daniels College of Business, University of Denver. She holds a Ph.D. from Temple University’s Fox School of Business and Management. Her research interests include hospitality technology and marketing, and business information and analytics. She is a recipient of Wharton Customer Analytics Initiative Research Opportunities (2012 and 2014), Hospitality Technology Research Award (2013), and Best Paper Award of Caesars Hospitality Research Summit (2011). She was previously an information analyst at Accenture Management Consulting and an event marketer at UBS. Her work has appeared in journals such as International Journal of Hospitality Management, International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management, and Journal of Hospitality Marketing and Management.

YOUNG-JIN LEE is an assistant professor in the Department of Business Information and Analytics at Daniels College of Business, University of Denver. He received a master’s degree in information systems management from Carnegie Mellon University, and a Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Washington. His research interests include economic and marketing aspects of online social media, mobile IT markets, and adoption and diffusion of IT innovations. His work has appeared in journals such as Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, and in conference proceedings of the International Conference of Information Systems, Workshop on Information Technology and Systems, Workshop on Information Systems and Economics, and others.

ABSTRACT: This study investigates the effects of exposures to earned and owned social media activities and their interaction on brand purchase in a two-stage decision model (i.e., likelihood to purchase and the amount purchased offline). Our study is instantiated on a unique single-source dataset of 12-month homescanned brand purchase records of a group of fast-moving consumer good brands and Facebook brand Fan Page messages related to the brands. We first find that exposures to earned and owned social media activities for brands have significant and positive impacts on consumers’ likelihood to purchase the brands. Their effects are, surprisingly, suppressive on each other. Second, exposures to earned and owned social media activities have almost no impact on the amount purchased offline with presence of in-store promotions. Our study contributes to our knowledge body of social media marketing by demonstrating that social media activities for a brand can foster the consumer base of the brand, but that effort is not necessarily salesoriented. In addition, our study is conducive to guiding marketers onto the strategic allocation of advertising dollars to online social channels featuring a mixture of earned and owned social media.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: brand community, brand purchase, Facebook Fan Page, multilevel modeling, social media, social media marketing.

Social media are now generally considered to have entered the mainstream media landscape and cover a broad demographic spectrum with 75 percent of Internetusing adults in the United States using social media regularly [5]. The large number of users has produced extensive online user-generated content and hence attracted firms’ attention [31, 51, 52]. For example, more than 1.5 million businesses have set up Facebook Fan Pages for brand communication purposes [28]. Firms have strongly increased spending related to social media over the past few years with growth forecast to \$8.22 billion by 2015 [62]. The popularity of social media and the increased emphasis placed by firms on social approaches have made it more important to understand how social media exposures affect consumer brand purchase.

Social-media-based advertising differs from traditional media such as TV commercials, e-mail communications, and online banners in that it enables interaction between firm and consumers, as well as among consumers. Given the source of social media activity (i.e., who creates the social media message), we can divide social media messages into two categories, earned social media and owned social media. Formally, Stephen and Galak [68] provide a definition of earned social media referring to social media activity related to a brand that is not directly generated by the brand owner or its agents. Marketing actions from the brand owner can help generate earned social media activity but the owner does not directly generate the activity [15]. In contrast, owned social media is social media activity that is generated by the brand owner (or his/her agents) in social networking services (e.g., Facebook) that it can control (see examples of earned and owned social media activities in a Facebook Fan Page in Figure 1).

Although the interactive nature of social media featuring earned and owned social activities for a brand scales up online buzz of the brand tremendously to help increase publicity around the brand [19], it is not clear what the relative marketing effectiveness of exposure to earned and owned social media activities in one single social networking platform on consumer decisions in the path to purchase. Furthermore, the interrelated effect of the two forms of social media activity on the decisions is not well understood. Consumers receiving brand-related information from social sources may be more likely to trigger purchase behavior than those who receiving brand-related information from traditional media [57, 69]. Although it may seem logical to expect that increasing exposures to brand social media activities will have a positive impact on consumers purchasing decisions regardless of their sources, it is not clear how the two forms of social media activity (earned and owned) are dependent on each other to affect the consumer decisions. As such, knowing the effects will be conducive to guiding marketers onto the strategic allocation of advertising dollars to online social channels featuring a mixture of earned and owned social media.

The objective of our study is to assess the relative and the interrelated effect of exposures to earned and owned social media activities in a two-stage decisionmaking model featuring likelihood to purchase and the amount purchased offline. Our study is instantiated on a unique data set of time-series purchase and social media marketing exposures of local households for a specific fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) category,<sup>1</sup> over a continuous period of twelve months in 2011.<sup>2</sup> The

FMCG industry is important in terms of revenue creation and consumer attention. According to U.S. Department of Labor Statistics [14], the average household spends about \$312 a month on FMCG or groceries. In addition, consumers tend to actively go through the online research process for FMCG products. “It’s a process they’ve learned from researching the \$40,000 SUV” [44]. This data set covers the weekly earned and owned media activities within social media brand communities (i.e., Facebook Fan Pages), complemented by brand purchases of participating household panelists on a weekly basis. Our data set allows us to look into the economic value of exposures to social media activities for brands at highly disaggregated individual household and brand levels. Using our data set, we exploit a blend of mixed model and probit model to estimate the household likelihood to purchase, controlling for the variation of baseline tendency at the household level and differences across brands. We also use a blend of mixed model and Heckman model to estimate the amount purchased offline affected by earned and owned brand exposures in Facebook while accounting for situational factors such as in-store promotions.

By estimating our two-stage models of households’ likelihood to purchase and their amount purchased offline with disaggregated data of social media activities and brand purchases, we first found that exposures to earned and owned social media activities have significant and positive impacts on households’ likelihood to purchase. Surprisingly, we found their effects can be suppressive on each other in a way that an increase in exposure to owned social media activity can actually lower the effect of exposure to earned social media activity on the likelihood to purchase, or vice versa. However, this suppressive effect on one type of social media activity may become substantial only when the level of exposure to the other type of social media activity is relatively high. Second, exposures to earned and owned social media activities have almost no impact on the amount purchased offline when we control for in-store promotions. In other words, households tend no longer to rely on information from social media to decide the amount purchased offline but are driven by in-store promotions. Our study provides a rigorous quantification of the economic impact of a social media brand community’s exposures on consumers’ likelihood to purchase and their amount purchased offline at the individual household level. It serves as the first attempt to contextualize the two-stage decision making process where brand purchase is driven by social media exposures and situational factors such as in-store promotion.

We believe that our work has a number of important implications for both academics and practicing marketers. First, our study unveils the intricate roles of earned and owned social media activities on consumer likelihood to purchase and complements prior social media studies that have overlooked this area (e.g., [3, 22, 68]). Consumer-generated content and brand-controlled messages can mitigate the effect of each other on consumers’ likelihood to purchase, underscoring a balanced use of earned and owned social media in fostering customer base. Second, our research documents the limitation of social media exposures in driving the amount purchased offline with presence of situational factors such as in-store promotions. This finding provides a view that differs from prior studies that may have overestimated the effect of social media on product purchase because of their ignorance of other situational factors that can influence consumer decisions (e.g., [17, 18]). As such, social media exposures to a brand can foster the consumer base of the brand but that effort is not necessarily sales-oriented. Finally, we advocate an integrated marketing perspective that emphasizes a coordinated use of social media activity and other marketing and sales techniques (i.e., in-store promotions, discounts) in different stages of consumer decision making to maximize sales revenue. Our findings on how consumers respond to social media exposures as they convert from intention (likelihood) to purchase to actual offline purchase behavior address the void of consumer conversion in prior social media studies (e.g., [13, 69]) and add to the knowledge body of the social media marketing practice. By incorporating social media advertising and in-store promotion into its marketing matrix, the company can effectively convert likelihood to purchase to in-store purchase that is specific to the brand.

## Related Literature

This study is closest to the multichannel marketing literature in which scholars have examined how different advertising channels and marketing mix work with each other. Research is only now emerging to help marketers decide how to best coordinate their multichannel marketing and engage highly digitally empowered customers. Table 1 provides a summary of existing literature on multichannel marketing. However, empirical studies investigating social media effectiveness and the sources of brand purchase still lag in three important aspects that motivate our study.

First, most literature analyzes the effectiveness of consumer-generated earned social media or online word of mouth in an isolated fashion without accounting for the effect of firm-generated owned social media [16, 52, 68, 69]. How earned and owned social media interact with each other is not well known, particularly in the current social media platforms where earned and owned media coexist. In addition, despite the industry trend toward using integrated marketing mix elements including advertising and in-store promotion, how to coordinate earned and owned social media activities, as well as in-store promotion that consumers might be also exposed to at the time of purchase, to maximize brand purchase remains a knowledge gap due to the sparse research. Without accounting for the interplay of social media exposures from both earned and owned social media channels as well as in-store promotion, the estimated effects can be biased [65]. Adding to prior literature, our study focuses on the direct interplay between earned and owned channels within one self-contained platform (i.e., Facebook Fan Page), preventing the influence of confounding factors from separate platforms that have been used in most previous research [19, 67]. By accounting for situational factors such as in-store promotion at the time of purchase, this study mitigates the estimation bias of prior research (e.g., [29]) which overlooks important marketing factors such as in-store promotion that may influence the effect of earned and owned social media channels.

Second, prior studies focus mainly on one-time-purchase items such as law services [32] or short life cycle information goods such as books [29] and software [47], likely due to the availability of data in these categories. However, information goods are unique in that they have product life cycles that are short and also follow predictable exponential patterns [55, 63]. These products experience the greatest level of sales (and marketing activity) immediately after launch. Very quickly after that, sales (and marketing activity) taper off dramatically. The danger of using such product categories is that results can be very sensitive to the stage of the product life cycle during which the researcher collects the data. As a result, the sales changes observed can be more easily attributed to changes due to the natural progression of the product life cycle and are less likely to be a result of changes in the marketing environment. In contrast to prior literature, our study provides rigor in quantifying the effect of social media exposures in relation to repeated purchase and mundane consumer products, that is, fast-moving consumer goods, that have a relatively stable product life cycle and recurring marketing exposures on social media platforms.

Last but most important, prior social media studies have focused on the direct purchase outcomes of various aspects of social media [4, 16, 33], shedding little light on consumer conversion from likelihood to purchase to actual offline purchase behavior with respect to social media exposures within each stage. Not acknowledging how consumers respond to social media exposures as they convert from likelihood to purchase to actual offline purchase would result in a biased quantification of the effect of social media exposures [19, 67]. This is because consumers may respond differently to social media exposures as they convert on the path to offline purchase. For example, a consumer may walk into a local store after receiving social media exposures for a brand but he/she may be likely to purchase the brand because of the in-store promotion rather than the social media exposures he/she has received. In addition, focusing on direct purchase outcome rather than the consumer conversion may disable marketers’ ability to obtain early warning about the effectiveness of brand social media exposures and adjust their marketing practices before the occurrence of actual purchase or failure to purchase. Our study explicitly delineates the effect of social media exposures during the consumer conversion, which has been overlooked in prior literature that focuses solely on the direct outcomes. In addition, we account for the potential trade-off between social media advertising and in-store promotion in the offline purchasing context. Finally, we provide early warning to marketers in terms of maximizing consumer conversion using social media exposures on the path to purchase.

## Conceptual Framework

Previous research shows that effective marketing requires a complex interplay among marketing metrics [45, 65] and advertising on media channels should increase effectiveness of promotions on consumer purchase (or other performance metrics such as sales, diffusion, and customer acquisition). However, most literature has tended to examine the isolated impact of either advertising [17, 18, 50] or promotion [11]. These studies overlook the interplay of advertising and promotion on brand purchase in different consumer decision making stages. Our study differs from these prior studies in research questions and applications. First, we seek to directly compare the effectiveness of social media exposures for a brand with that of promotion efforts. Second, our empirical application is to both the likelihood to purchase stage and the amount purchased offline stage, which provides an integrated setting in which we are able to illustrate the relative effectiveness of multimetric marketing efforts including social media exposures and in-store promotions.

Our framework builds on the long-standing theory of planned behavior (TPB) [2]. The theory of planned behavior holds that people, in responding to information signals, proceed through a series of stages beginning with awareness and knowledge-building (cognition or learn) to conviction and action (conation or do) [39]. Similarly, we propose that uninformed customers, in responding to information signals (i.e., social media advertising), proceed through the stage of likelihood to purchase (Stage 1: Being informed) to the amount purchased offline (Stage 2: Purchasing). Specifically, awareness or learning about the brand are traced through the levels of exposures to owned social media activity (e.g., brand page newsfeeds and manager response) and earned social media activity (e.g., brand “like,” “share,” brand-related comment and status update), and the interaction in the form of earned and owned social media exposures. The behavioral or conative components are traced through the amount purchased offline. Figure 2 presents our theoretical framework.

The rationale for our framework follows. Exposures of advertising content are often triggered by an overt (and traceable) consumer decision [67]. This event provides an opportunity to examine metrics that reflect the extent to which consumers are active in seeking knowledge (awareness or learning). The overt actions required by consumers receiving Facebook brand page newsfeeds allow consumers to exchange information with each other. Such interaction reflects awareness and knowledge building and accordingly indicates a cognitive process at work. As consumers pursue learning about a brand, they may take advantage of multiple sources of ad-based information (owned social media) and peer opinions (earned social media) [67]. The exposures to brand information communicated from firms (brand page newsfeeds) and between consumers (brand-related “likes,” share, comments, and status updates) are overt expressions that present brand learning and awareness. In this process, brand knowledge is cocreated in a way that owned and earned channels interact, complement, and reinforce each other. Lastly, brand purchase reflects the aggregate actions of consumers in their behavior. As consumers purchase products in the conventional physical retail store, they are very likely to take advantages of multiple in-store promotions from the brand or its competing brands. Our framework explicitly allows for consumers to cycle among multiple onsite discounts and in-store promotions in the purchasing stage.

Our framework provides a potential weapon in the arsenal of advertisers, as it seeks to show the effectiveness of marketing metrics (advertising and promotion) in nudging consumers toward the likelihood to purchase and the amount purchased offline. The pathways proposed in our framework can serve as early signals of brand performance successes and problems for brands. For example, if marketing actions move customers closer to the buying decision in a series of mental steps, then tracking and interpreting the pathway can provide early evaluation signals. Specific actions that strengthen the competitive position of the brand in customers’ hearts and minds may not translate into sales immediately, but the pathway can verify that marketing moves customers in the right direction. In the case of performance problems, the consumer may not react immediately by switching to another brand, but tracking customer attitudes may diagnose declining interest and offer a chance for remedial action before sales are affected. In this sense, our framework is able to help managers adjust their marketing actions for the future as they observe the performance of previous marketing campaigns.

## Hypotheses Development

Whereas traditional advertising effects on sales and related outcomes have received extensive attention, less research has examined the marketing and financially relevant consequences of owned social media. Owned media creates brand awareness [40]. As consumers research brands, they may take advantage of multiple sources of ad-based information [67]. Studies imply that traditional advertising is likely to influence consumer purchase behaviors because such media offers informative messages that may increase consumers’ knowledge of brand features and benefits or alter how much a consumer thinks he knows (i.e., perceived knowledge) [23]. The perceived knowledge may influence how the consumer responds to the brand. Therefore, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: The level of exposure to owned social media activity for a brand is positively associated with likelihood to purchase the brand.

There has been a growing interest in understanding how word of mouth (WOM), particularly online WOM, impacts sales, diffusion, and other marketing performance measures [8, 13, 49, 69, 71]. The impact of earned social media on marketing outcomes has received disproportionately less attention in marketing literature than that of owned media (television, online banner ads, newspaper, print, radio, etc.). Although the owned media is the information source of brands and products in the social media context, it seems logical to expect that earned social media exposure will also impact brand purchase. First, because consumers appear to view online WOM from fellow independent consumers as credible information sources the earned social media tend to substitute other forms of business-to-consumer [35] and offline WOM communication [48]. Receiving information from an earned social source may be more influential in shifting consumers’ opinions, and, ultimately, triggering purchasing behavior. Second, because WOM is often topic-specific and socially interactive, earned social media is more likely to snowball high-involvement consumers who want to discuss topics of mutual interest with like-minded others. Thus, earned social media may effectively reach high-involvement consumers who want to socially interact and who are inclined to purchase. We propose:

Hypothesis 2: The level of exposure to earned social media activity for a brand is positively associated with likelihood to purchase the brand.

Earned and owned social media, as brand-specific marketing channels, are playing similar roles of producing a large amount of brand mentions, online buzz, and expressions about a brand. Several previous studies have shown empirical evidence of marketing channel cannibalization along with the problem of information overload grounded in cognitive psychology [66]. For example, Gopal et al. [34] study keyword-based ads channels, including the search channel and the content channel, and find significant cannibalization across the two channels as well as significant diminishing returns to impressions within each channel. Similarly, Fan et al. [26] find that pricing and advertising may cannibalize each other to harm the digital product distribution online. In these cases, when media exposures from one channel are presented, additional media exposures from another channel do not necessarily offer new information or opinions about the brand, and meanwhile may contribute to the problem of information overload and the consequent cognitive overload [9]. That is, decision makers responding to media exposures from both brand owner and peer consumers have little if any excess cognitive capacity. Narrowing one’s attention as a result of the interruption is likely to result in the loss of information cues. Chen et al. [12] explain that this decreasing effect is due to information substitution dynamics that the information from the brand and market replaces the need for information from online product reviews. Under this circumstance, although an increased amount of earned and owned social media exposures both seem to contribute to brand purchase, their effects are likely to be discounted by consumers cognitive capacity [9], and the growth of one channel is likely to cannibalize the effect of the other channel to some degree [24].

On one hand, consumers usually become informed of the product characteristics and features through product newsfeeds from owned social media channels. Given that there are already a large number of informative owned brand messages, consumers are very likely to be receptive to and persuaded of the brand. Cannibalization is likely to happen when peer-generated discussions (often with mixed opinions) keep influencing the decision making to the point where the consumers divide their attention to the detriment of advertising messaging of a brand. Consequently, consumers have a difficult time determining which earned social media exposure is most relevant for brand evaluation and thus might not necessarily increase the likelihood to purchase from the brand. In this case, with an additional level of exposure to earned social media activity, the effect of exposure to owned social media activity on brand purchase is likely to diminish.

On the other hand, consumers tend to rely on word-of-mouth to make purchase decisions because they are more user-oriented and credible than content posted by business providers in owned channels [6]. If there is a considerable peer-to-peer discussion about a brand, consumers tend to become fully aware of the product. Additional owned social media exposures, in this case, might be less successful at increasing likelihood to purchase and encouraging virality [70]. A plausible explanation might be that consumers who are receptive to the brand will be persuaded quickly, while those who are not receptive will not be won over by repeated owned advertising exposures [7]. As a result, the effect of earned social media exposure is likely to be discounted given an increased amount of owned social media exposure.

We therefore combine the two perspectives discussed above to suggest that the saturation of different exposure weight in either channel can create the impression of advertising wear-out or fade-out, which result in a decline in the likelihood to purchase. We propose:

Hypothesis 3: The level of exposures to owned social media activity negatively moderates the effect of the level of exposures to earned social media activity on likelihood to purchase and vice versa.

Marketers often take the position that marketing metrics such as advertising and promotions lead to increased sales [25]. Advertising can enhance the brand’s nonmonetary attractiveness, and promotions provide a monetary incentive to buy the brand [64]. In terms of the relative impact, advertising and promotion differ in different product life cycles [10]. Advertising is used to inform consumers about features and benefits, to strategically position a brand, and to build awareness. Previous research finds that advertising spending was positively related to the introduction and growth stages of the product life cycle, and negatively related to the maturity stage [27]. During the mature phase of the product life cycle, intense competition can also lead managers to shift funds away from advertising and into promotions as they attempt to take market share from competitors [64]. Hence, previous studies find that for a mature commodity such as fast-moving consumer goods, advertising has a small effect on brand sales compared to promotions such as price differences, discounts, and coupons [36, 41]. In particular, studies focusing on an in-store environment have found that customers are much more responsive to instore promotions (deals, preprinted cents-off promotions, displays, newspaper features) than to the number of advertising exposures [36, 41]. Therefore, social media advertising would impact likelihood to purchase and drive consumers to visit a store, but the amount purchased offline is likely to be driven by situational factors such as in-store promotions. We propose:

Hypothesis 4: The level of social media exposures has less impact than in-store promotions on the amount purchased offline.

## Data

Our research context is business fan page communities on Facebook for a group of FMCG companies. The products of these companies can be found in all major grocery chains, and their brands have a strong presence on social media channels. The FMCG companies set up their Facebook communities to serve as a platform to engage and interact with their consumers. Facebook is the only social media platform the companies use to engage consumers. This offers us a thorough, unambiguous setting to examine the impact of earned and owned social media exposures on brand purchases. The company tracked Facebook exposures presented to each participating household panelist and the brand purchase of these households for six brands (see Table 2 for distributions of Facebook messages and brand purchases among these six brands). All six brands are presented on the company’s Facebook Fan Page. The product category of the brands is similar (e.g., soft drinks or candy) but under different brand names (e.g., Pepsi Next and Pepsi Max for PepsiCo, M&Ms and My M&Ms for Mars).

Specifically, the data sponsor company uses network card data capture devices to track event log Facebook messages with a brand mention that a panelist sees in his/ her newsfeeds, timeline/wall, ticker, and others’ timelines/walls. In addition, the company used in-home purchase scanners to record the purchase value of participating households. Because of the tremendous impacts of online social networking services on both online and offline businesses, traditional advertising channels such as TV, radio, magazines, and newspapers no longer dominate marketing strategies and firms have been seeking innovative ways to leverage social networking platforms through which firms can more effectively penetrate consumer networks. Therefore, our modeling effort focuses on unfolding the various effects of online product marketing within a social networking platform on consumer offline purchase behavior. Following Moe and Schweidel [56] and Lee et al. [46], our model for consumer offline purchase behavior has two stages in order to fully investigate offline sales impacts of exposures to earned and owned brand messages in a social network platform. Our initial model first specifies a potential consumer’s latent likelihood to purchase as a function of consumer heterogeneous valuation, consumer characteristics, and the influence of exposures to social media brand message in Facebook. Second, the latent likelihood to purchase model is linked with the model of the amount purchased offline, specifying a consumer’s decision on the units of brand purchases at offline stores. This modeling effort enables us not only to account for a potential bias stemming from self-selection purchase decisions of potential consumers but also to separately analyze different processes of consumer purchase behavior under the social media influence of earned and owned brand messages in Facebook.

## Household likelihood to purchase (Stage 1)

A household may make a decision to purchase a specific brand of product based on its baseline brand valuation. Therefore, we first assume that a household i’s purchase decision is driven in a large part by its brand j valuation $\nu _ { i j } .$

$$
\begin{array}{c} v _ {i j} = \delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1: n _ {1}} X _ {i} + u _ {j 0}, \\ \delta_ {i 0} \sim N \Big (\alpha_ {1}, \sigma_ {\delta_ {i 0}} ^ {2} \Big) \\ u _ {j 0} \sim N \Big (\alpha_ {2}, \sigma_ {u _ {j 0}} ^ {2} \Big) \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $\delta _ { i 0 }$ is a household $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ level of baseline preference for product category and $u _ { j 0 }$ allows for variation across brands. We further assume that $\delta _ { i 0 }$ can vary across households and has a normal distribution with a mean of ${ \bf a } _ { 1 }$ and a standard deviation of $\sigma _ { \delta i 0 }$ . Likewise, $u _ { j 0 }$ has a normal distribution with a mean of ${ \bf a } _ { 2 }$ and a standard deviation of ${ \sigma } _ { u j 0 } . X _ { i }$ is an $n _ { 1 } \times 1$ vector of household specific covariates that describe the household’s characteristics. The household may adjust its brand evaluation with its exposure to social media activities with the brand. As such, we model the latent brand response $V _ { i j t } ^ { * }$ as a household’s expected brand evaluation for the brand j by the household i at time t:

$$
V _ {i j t} ^ {*} = v _ {i j} + \gamma_ {1: n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \varepsilon_ {i j t},\tag{2}
$$

where $Z _ { j , t ( i ) }$ is an $n _ { 2 } \times 1$ vector of social media influential covariates including the numbers of earned messages (Earned) and owned brand messages (Owned) with the brand and their lagged and interaction terms; $u _ { i j t }$ is the idiosyncratic error with a mean of 0 and assumed to follow a standard normal distribution. As such, the $1 \times n _ { 1 }$ vector $\delta _ { 1 : n 1 }$ captures the effects of household characteristics on a household’s brand evaluation and the $1 \times n _ { 2 }$ vector of $\gamma _ { 1 : n _ { 2 } }$ captures the impacts that Earned, Owned, their lags and interaction may have on a household’s brand purchase decision.

Based on our conceptualization of the latent component of brand purchase decision, a household i purchases the brand j at time t if:

$$
V _ {i j t} ^ {*} = \delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1: n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {1: n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + u _ {i j t} > p _ {j},\tag{3}
$$

where $p _ { j }$ is the representative price of brand $j .$ Then, the probability that i purchases the brand $j$ at time t is given by the following probit model:

$$
\operatorname * {P r} \left(z _ {i j t} = 1\right) = \Phi \left(V _ {i j t} ^ {*} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j}\right),\tag{4}
$$

where $\Phi ( \cdot )$ denotes the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { n } } _ { 1 }$ captures the effect of the price; $z _ { i j t } = 1$ indicates that the household made a decision to purchase the brand (and $z _ { i j t } = 0$ otherwise). The vectors $\delta _ { 1 : n 1 }$ and $\gamma _ { 1 : n _ { 2 } }$ capture the effects of covariates characterizing the household’s characteristics and the social media exposures for brand $j$ on the purchase decision, respectively. Finally, our model can be fit using maximization of the log-likelihood function of (4) for the entire data set.

In our modeling, mixed models have been chosen in favor of panel data models such as fixed effects and random effects models because of our observations nested within households and brands [30, 61]. For example, one household panelist can be exposed to online brand social media of multiple brands and also the panelist can buy more than one brand offline in our data. Therefore, observations for different brands but by the same household panelist (the same cluster) are correlated because the observations share common household-level random effects, $\delta _ { i 0 }$ . Note that including the random effects $\delta _ { i 0 }$ in Equation (1) is also our effort to control for the difference among households in consuming social media related to the product category, which cannot be captured in household specific covariatesX . We later include this kind of household-level random effect as well in our second-stage model of the amount purchase offline. Similarly, one brand can be also exposed to multiple household panelists and therefore observations for multiple panelists in this brand cluster share common random effects of the $\mathrm { b r a n d } , u _ { j 0 }$ , as well. Mixed effects modeling can allow us to take into account these intracluster correlations [60] while panel data models are restricted in explaining this. In this regard, our two-level model (household panelist level and brand level) can better deal with heterogeneities in households and brand levels than panel data models.

## The amount purchased offline (Stage 2)

Whereas our probability model (4) can be used to explain a household’s likelihood to purchase a brand given the level of exposures to social media activities with the brand, the household’s decision of how many units to buy (the amount purchased offline) is a separate process [20]. Also, the decision process for the amount purchased offline is not independent of the household’s likelihood to purchase the brand (i.e., consumers who have a higher likelihood to purchase may buy more units than consumers who have a lower likelihood to purchase). As such, a two-stage purchase decision-making process can be represented based on whether to buy a brand in (4) and how many units of the brand to buy. Thus, the expected amount purchased offline is:

$$
\begin{array}{c} E \big [ Q _ {i j t} | z _ {i j t} = 1 \big ] = E \Big [ Q _ {i j t} | \left(V _ {i j t} ^ {*} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j}\right) > 0 \Big ] \\ = E \big [ Q _ {i j t} | \delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1: n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {1: n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + u _ {i j t} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j} > 0 \big ], \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

where $Q _ { i j t }$ is the units of the brand j purchased offline by a household i at time t. We then model the purchased units:

$$
\begin{array}{c} Q _ {i j t} = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1: n _ {3}} X _ {i} + \pi_ {1: n _ {4}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \psi_ {1: n _ {5}} M _ {j t (i)} + \varepsilon_ {i j t}, \\ \beta_ {0 i} \sim N \Big (\alpha_ {3}, \sigma_ {\beta_ {0 i}} ^ {2} \Big) \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

$\beta _ { 0 i }$ has a normal distribution with a mean of ${ \bf a } _ { 3 }$ and a standard deviation of ${ \sigma } _ { \delta 0 i }$ which allows unexplained effects of household heterogeneity in the amount purchased offline model (6). $\beta _ { 1 : n _ { 3 } }$ captures the effects of observed households characteristics. Similar to the model of the likelihood to purchase in (4), estimation of $\pi _ { 1 : n _ { 4 } }$ can help us investigate whether earned and owned social media exposures can still influence a household’s amount purchased offline. Because any purchase amount significantly depends on any direct in-store promotion such as discount or coupon at the time of purchase, we include an $n _ { 5 } \times 1$ vector of $M _ { j t ( i ) }$ including any in-store promotion at the time of purchase. In our data, in-store promotions tendered at the time of purchase are time-invariant and we cannot observe when the panelist obtained the in-store promotion information. Therefore, we cannot test the impact of the social media exposures on the amount of purchase along with the effect of in-store promotions if we use the fixed effects estimation in panel data models, which cancel out all the fixed effects including in-store promotions. By using mixed modeling and keeping promotion variables, we can still estimate the effects of both in-store promotion and social media variables and also minimize a potential bias stemming from an endogenized effect of in-store promotion in the absence of the in-store promotion variable in the model specification.

Finally, we assume $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ is an idiosyncratic error and has a standard normal distribution. If the errors $u _ { i j t }$ in (4) and $\varepsilon _ { i j t }$ in (6) are correlated with each other, our estimates in Equation (6) alone are spurious. Therefore, we need to account for the household participation in brand purchases when we estimate (6). Following the Heckman correction [37]:

$$
\begin{array}{c} E \big [ \varepsilon_ {i j t} | u _ {i j t} > - \big (\delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1: n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {1: n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j} \big) \big ] = \rho \sigma_ {\varepsilon} \lambda_ {i j t}, \\ \text { where } \rho = c o r r \big (\varepsilon_ {i j t}, u _ {i j t} \big) \text { and } \\ \lambda_ {i j t} = \frac {\phi \big (\delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1 : n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {1 : n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j} \big)}{\Phi \big (\delta_ {i 0} + \delta_ {1 : n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {1 : n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \eta_ {1} p _ {j} \big)}. \end{array}\tag{7}
$$

First, for each purchase observation in our data set, we can now compute:

$$
\hat {\lambda} _ {i j t} = \frac {\phi \left(\hat {\delta} _ {i 0} + \hat {\delta} _ {1 : n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \hat {\gamma} _ {1 : n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \hat {\eta} _ {1} p _ {j}\right)}{\Phi \left(\hat {\delta} _ {i 0} + \hat {\delta} _ {1 : n _ {1}} X _ {i} + \hat {\gamma} _ {1 : n _ {2}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \hat {\eta} _ {1} p _ {j}\right)},
$$

(the inverse Mill’s ratio) by estimating likelihood to purchase in (4). Second, we fit the proposed model of the amount purchased offline in (6) with the revised equation in (8) with log-transformed quantity,<sup>3</sup> $Q _ { i j t }$ as:

$$
\log (Q _ {i j t}) = \beta_ {0 i} + \beta_ {1: n _ {3}} X _ {i} + \pi_ {1: n _ {4}} Z _ {j t (i)} + \psi_ {1: n _ {5}} M _ {j t (i)} + \rho \hat {\lambda} _ {i j t} + \varepsilon_ {i j t},\tag{8}
$$

to take into account the selection effect in the amount purchased offline.

As we hypothesized, in general, households with a greater exposure to earned brand social media activity in the current week or previous weeks are more likely to purchase (the estimates of $\gamma _ { 1 } , \gamma _ { 4 }$ , and $\gamma _ { 7 } > 0$ and statistically significant at the 5 percent level). The effect of exposure to owned brand social media activity on a household’s likelihood to purchase is also positive and significant (the estimates of $\gamma _ { 2 } , \ \gamma _ { 5 } ,$ , and $\gamma _ { 8 } > 0$ and statistically significant at the 5 percent level). Hence, an increase in exposure to either type of brand social media activity can promote households’ in-store purchases and these positive effects may last at least a couple of weeks. In other words, companies’ efforts to send their brand-related information through a Facebook Fan Page has the potential to persuade consumers to buy their brand items. Also, such company-generated messages including online interaction with consumers about the brand can act as informative messages that traditional advertising features to increase brand awareness. Similarly, organically generated messages related to the brand within a company’s Facebook Fan Page play a role similar to the company-generated messages in persuading consumers and increasing brand awareness because these kinds of messages can directly reveal consumers sentimental attitude to the brand as a form of word of mouth.

Although both effects of exposures to earned and owned social media activities are positive on the likelihood to purchase, the effect of one type of social media activity seems not independent of the other type of social media activity $( \gamma _ { 3 }$ is negative and statistically significant at the 5 percent level in Table 6). This suggests that more exposure to owned social media activity may negatively moderate the positive effect of exposure to an earned social media message on likelihood to purchase. That is, the effects of earned and owned social media activities can be, surprisingly, suppressive on each other. This suggests that for every additional brand message generated by the brand’s company that a household is exposed to in the company’s

Facebook Fan Page, a brand message generated by the crowd in the company’s Facebook Fan Page is on average 0.02 percent less persuasive. While this result would imply that the firms should stick with one channel to display social media exposures, this decision is complicated by our further finding on the presence of increasing returns to exposures cross-channel: with an increase in the number of exposures in either channel, the likelihood to purchase the brand would increase. Specifically, we find the negative moderating effect is not very substantial from a practical point of view because of the positive net effect gained from an additional owned social media message (about 0.2 percent increase in likelihood to purchase). Also, in our data, households have on average 6.7 owned Facebook messages and 4.8 earned Facebook messages in a week when social media exposure occurred in that week (no exposure in most weeks). This implies that the negative moderating effect by either type of social media activity may become substantial only in an extreme situation in which a household is exposed to a very large volume of either type of social media activity. Given the presence of these effects, how channels should be optimally utilized becomes a nontrivial question.

## The impacts of brand social media activities on the amount purchased offline

By estimating our model of the amount purchased offline, we examine whether exposures to social media activities can still have a direct impact on the amount purchased in-store while accounting for situational factors such as in-store promotions. Mixed models (1) and (2) in Table 7 show the results of estimating model (8) using multilevel mixed-effects linear regression with the household random effects as specified in (6). Mixed model (1) uses one promotion variable (Promotions) counting the weekly number of in-store promotions at the times of purchases while Mixed model (2) specifies the details of the in-store promotions. Heckman (1), (2), and (3) in Table 7 show the results using Heckman’s maximum likelihood estimation without the random effects specified in (6) [37]. Heckman (1) uses the promotion sums (Promotions) while Heckman (2) specifies the details of Promotions. To test this more robustly, Heckman (3) uses the subsample of the households having observed household size and income level to fit the data with the same model specification of Heckman (2). Therefore, Table 7 also demonstrates that our results are quite robust with different model specifications. In addition, the estimates of $\lambda _ { i j t }$ in Table 7 consistently suggest that the different level of likelihood to purchase driven by exposures to social media activities is significantly related to the household’s amount purchased offline. As such, there exist indirect effects of earned and owned social media activities through the selection effect, and the amount purchased offline model should take into account the likelihood to purchase.

In Table 7, the effects of in-store promotions are consistently positive and statistically very significant $( \phi _ { 1 } ~ > ~ 0 )$ across all different model specifications. The estimated effects of different types of in-store promotions in Mixed (2), Heckman (2), and Heckman (3) are positive and statistically very significant as well. This result confirms our intuition that households tend to buy more units of a brand product when in-store promotions are available. However, most estimated effects of social media activity variables are not statistically significant in Table 7 when we control for in-store promotions. Interestingly, we observe the estimated effects of Earned and the second-order lag of Earned are negative and statistically significant in all models except Heckman (1), but the magnitudes of the estimates are quite small. This occurrence may show that the increased level of exposure to Earned social media activity can somewhat reduce the amount purchased offline. However, the actual impact of Earned is quite minimal because the amount purchased offline may decrease by only about 0.1 percent with one additional exposure to earned social media activity.

As such, households’ exposures to social media activities with a brand of FMCG indeed have almost no direct impact on households’ consumption whereas a variety of in-store promotions can actually lift the sales by affecting consumers to buy more and consume the FMCG products faster [1]. The amount purchased offline is driven mainly by households’ desire to keep higher inventory levels, which results in the flexible usage rate phenomenon [54]. Because the products of interest in our study are also FMCG, in-store promotion would be a main purchase accelerator and non– price-oriented social media activities are likely to work like advertisements [43]. That is, compared to sales-driven in-store promotions, social media exposures tend mainly to educate customers about brands, foster the customer base, and enhance brand awareness.

## Discussion and Conclusions

This paper studies how different types of online social media activities affect consumer offline brand purchase of fast-moving consumable goods (FMCG). Our analysis suggests that consumers’ increased exposures to earned and owned social media activities for a brand can elevate consumers’ likelihood to purchase the brand and these positive influences can last for at least a couple of weeks. Therefore, firms may be tempt to seek cost-effective ways to increase consumers’ exposures to both types of social media activities. However, our results also demonstrate that the effect of one type of social media activity is not independent of the other type, and thus earned social media activity and owned social media activity actually have a suppressive impact on each other in increasing consumer likelihood to purchase. Interestingly, after taking into account the influence of situational factors such as instore promotions, income level, and family size in purchasing in order to further analyze the sales impact of exposures to social media activities, earned and owned social media activities do not play a significant role in driving a higher level of brand purchases at the times of purchases.

This study provides important implications for practicing managers. First, we demonstrate that increases in consumers’ exposures to their brand messages through brand pages or consumers’ social circles in Facebook can increase consumers’ tendency to buy the brands of those FMCG companies. On a firm-sponsored virtual community such as the brand’s Facebook Fan Page, consumers are willing to learn about the community sponsor and extend their relationship with the sponsor, via a future transaction in our case, or via direct contact with the firm [58]. We suggest that firms actively initiate sponsored online communities such as Facebook Fan Pages to create direct relational bonds with customers [59] and facilitate peer supports and services [53]. Indeed, the social capital accumulated in the sponsored online communities is being used in the creation of value. Therefore, firms can commercially exploit the value created from relationships in the sponsored subsocial network [72]. However, the companies should be cautious about their strategy of social media type because increases in one type may diminish the benefit of the other type that may already exist. For example, in the situation where an FMCG has been taking great advantage of network effects for buzzword creation for its brand by consumers, their new marketing effort to increase brand page exposures may offset the existing benefit of “shared” or “transmitted” viral messages for its brand. In addition, FMCG firms should correctly identify their need for social media given that the social media strategy is most effective in producing increases in brand awareness, but not in product sales. Therefore, an FMCG company’s successful social media strategy can expand its consumer base for its brand but that effort cannot help its existing consumer base (loyal consumers) buy more of its brand.

Second, this study underscores the limitation of social media advertising in driving the amount purchased offline with the presence of in-store promotions. The fact that the social media advertising has little effect on brand purchase is alarming because of the sheer number of owned advertising events. These failed attempts introduce the possibility that consumers could be averse to an overload of social media marketing. The primary reason most members join a brand on Facebook is most likely not to learn about products. While “earned” marketing is typically out of the hands of a business, “owned” marketing is controllable. In order for a brand’s Facebook presence to add value, the companies must be very tactful and subtle in their marketing attempts. Taking into consideration the two-stage decision-making process, marketing managers should utilize earned and owned social media strategies in conjunction with in-store promotions in order to maximize sales revenue. For example, a company could use its Facebook brand page to inform consumers of its in-store promotions. By incorporating advertising and in-store promotion into its marketing matrix, the company can effectively convert likelihood to purchase to instore purchase that is specific to the brand. Thus we recommend that marketers become aware of the relative effect of various marketing metrics and adopt a selective marketing strategy that focuses on a coordinated use of in-store promotion and earned and owned advertising exposures.

Finally, our research reveals the importance of unobtrusive Facebook marketing. Based on our findings, social networking service providers such as Facebook can provide more customized marketing plans to FMCG companies planning to advertise their new brands in social network platforms. For example, instead of distributing resources to both earned and owned social media strategies, Facebook initially sets up sequential plans to promote the new brand page. First, Facebook guides the FMCG company to initially invest and focus on its new brand page to intensify users’ exposures to the messages from the new brand page. Then, by tracking the progression of brand page messages dissemination within their platform (or in subpotential consumer networks), Facebook can identify cohesive groups of potential consumers who can best trigger viral process for the new brand. Then, promoting the brand messages to the identified groups would be the next step to enhance viral marketing for the new brand.

This study also adds to the social media literature by enriching our body of knowledge on social media marketing. First, with the growth of firm-sponsored communities such as Facebook brand Fan Pages, it is important to examine the firm-sponsored cocreation activities conducted by the participation of consumers or any engaging action at the behest of a sponsor (firm) [72]. However, extant research focuses mostly on consumer-generated content and activities (e.g., electronic word of mouth or consumer reviews) without paying enough attention to the jointly actualized activities of firms and customers. Our study is on the frontier of examining sponsored cocreation activities by specifically looking at the interplay of owned and earned social media activities of firms and consumers. In this effort, we have also responded to Zwass [72], which calls for scholars to distinguish customer-initiated virtual communities from those that are firm-sponsored because it helps in appropriately contextualizing research findings. Second, the majority of existing research on online social media has focused on its sales impacts in various settings selling information goods in a stand-alone fashion without weighing different sources of social media. An emerging research stream has started to look at direct and indirect purchase outcomes of various aspects of social media by distinguishing what types of social media are manageable or what types are not. We contribute to this later stream by investigating the purchase impact of organically earned social media within consumer networks and intentionally owned social media generated by firms. Further, we separately quantify the influences in two different stages of consumer brand purchase (i.e., likelihood to purchase and the amount purchased offline).

Our study paves the way for future research. First, future research can explore the effect that different subtypes of owned and earned social media have on brand purchase [21]. WOM has varying forms and origins. In this study, earned social media include two different events: status updates and comments. These two social media mechanisms can come from the panelists themselves or their friends. Similarly, owned social media comes in seven different forms: brand like on profile, brand like on newsfeed, brand like on timeline, brand like on ticker, brand message, liked message, and shared message. Future studies could break down earned and owned social media into different subtypes and measure the effect of each on likelihood to purchase and the amount purchased offline. Such analysis would reveal which specific categories of earned and owned social media are most effective.

Second, future scholars can use the concept of subcategorizing in analyzing instore promotion effects. In our study, in-store promotions in fact came in four types: special price, free items, coupons, and additional volume (through which a consumer gets a larger size for the price of a smaller size). Future research should look into the separate categories of in-store promotions to determine which in-store promotion types are most effective. Future research could aim to determine the optimal combination of earned social media, owned social media, and in-store promotions. Breaking down earned social media, owned social media, and in-store promotions into more categories will provide marketing managers with the knowledge to even more specifically fine-tune their marketing approaches.

Finally, data analytics, such as we employed in our study, are imperative in the FMCG industry. As “data is a strategic asset,” data analytics facilitates optimal decision making regarding product development, product and market strategy, supply chain strategy and planning, and corporate strategy [38]. Essentially, data analytics takes out a lot of the “guesswork” involved in corporate planning and strategizing. Our study provides managers with the knowledge that social media is characterized by advertising channel interplay and a potential trade-off with in-store promotion. Such insight would not be possible without data analytics. Those managers who are concerned with relative performance measures and market share can utilize our study and take caution when employing competitor-benefiting social media exposures. As an increasing percentage of FMCG firms are expanding their data analytics capabilities, future competitiveness within the industry will rely on the discovery and analysis of data. Thus, for FMCG marketers who want to thrive not just survive in the evolving world of data analytics, we recommend that they conduct extensive data-rich research, understand emerging applications of analytics, and explore new ways to harness real-time data to better understand market competition and improve decision making. We call for future research that can provide up-to-theminute perspectives by integrating data analytics into a corporate strategy in the FMCG industry.
