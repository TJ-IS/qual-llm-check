# OPERATIONAL IT FAILURES, IT VALUE DESTRUCTION,AND BOARD-LEVEL IT GOVERNANCE CHANGES<sup>1</sup>

Michel Benaroch Department of Accounting and IS, Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University Syracuse, NY 13244 U.S.A. {mbenaroc@syr.edu}

Anna Chernobai

Department of Finance, Whitman School of Management, Syracuse University Syracuse, NY 13244 U.S.A. {annac@syr.edu}

This paper presents an empirical study of changes that firms implement in their board-level IT governance (ITG) upon experiencing operational IT failures. Consistent with the separation of oversight from management decisions, board-level ITG is responsible for monitoring managerial IT decisions and policies for controlling IT resources. We expect that operational IT failures indicating inadequacies in board monitoring of control over IT resources would result in a negative stock market reaction and, in turn, induce firms to improve their board-level ITG. Our expectation is confirmed based on a sample of 110 operational IT failures from U.S. public financial firms. Specifically, our results demonstrate that subsequent to experiencing operational IT failures, firms make improvements to the IT competency level of their boards, and the improvements are proportional to the degree of negative market reaction. However, those improvements are only on the executive side of the board, namely: an increase in the IT experience of internal (executive) directors and an increased turnover rate of CIOs serving on the board. Furthermore, the likelihood of CIO turnover is lower in ITintensive firms where such turnover could be more disruptive. Our results contribute to understanding the critical connection between operational IT failures and board-level ITG.

Keywords: Operational IT failure, board-level IT governance, IT governance monitoring, board IT competency, IT governance mechanisms, board of directors, financial services

## Introduction

On December 19, 2013, Target Corp., the second-largest discount retailer in the United States, disclosed a breach of 40 million credit and debit card accounts over a nearly threeweek period before Christmas. Then on January 10, 2014, the company said hackers also stole personal information from as many as 70 million customers. As of early March 2014, Target shares are down 5.8 percent since the breach was disclosed. Moreover, on March 6, 2014, Target’s CIO resigned, reflecting the pressure that the retailer faces as it tries to restore its reputation. Many analysts say the CIO “was a scapegoat.” On May 5, 2014, Target’s CEO and Chairman resigned amid the fallout from the data breach, showing how responsibility for computer security now reaches right to the top. (D’Innocenzio, March 6, 2014; May 5, 2014.)

Operational IT failures, or failures of operational IT systems, “can paralyze a company’s ability to make its products, deliver its services, and connect with its customers, not to mention foul its reputation” (Carr 2003, p. 11). Examples include technical glitches, service outages, and security breaches. Generally, operational IT failures compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of data assets or functional IT assets (software, hardware, networks, users, system operators, etc.) responsible for the creation, storage, processing, transport, and safeguarding of data assets (Benaroch et al. 2012).

The literature on operational IT failures is sparse and focused on their value relevance to the firm. Extant studies document the adverse impact of such failures on firms’ equity value (Benaroch et al. 2012; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Goldstein et al. 2011; Hovav and D’Arcy 2003; Kannan et al. 2007; Ko and Dorantes 2006) and financial reporting quality (Anthony et al. 2006; Bolster et al. 2010; Campbell et al. 2003; Gatzlaff and McCullough 2010; Zhou 2004). Beyond work on impact, we are aware of only two studies on other facets of IT failures. Goldstein et al. (2011) use the resource-based view of the firm to propose the notion of IT resource weaknesses as a theoretical lens for understanding the impact of operational IT failures. They propose an IT resource weaknesses–IT valuedestruction perspective to complement the IT resource strengths–IT value-creation perspective studied extensively in the IT literature. Moreover, they empirically find that failures compromising the availability and integrity of functional IT assets are more harmful to the firm than failures compromising the confidentiality of data assets. Amiri et al. (2014) more recently proposed a conceptual model for understanding when IT failures compromising the availability of IT services create a strategic risk requiring special attention by board members and senior executives. Their work touches upon an important gap in the literature: the connection between operational IT failures and board-level IT governance.

Our interest is in changes in board-level IT governance (ITG) in firms that experience operational IT failures. This paper adopts the following widely cited definition of ITG:

IT governance is the responsibility of the board of directors and executive management. It is an integral part of enterprise governance and consists of the leadership and organizational structures and processes that ensure that the organization’s IT sustains and extends the organization’s strategies and objectives (ITGI 2003, p. 10).

Implied by this definition is the critical role that the board of directors plays in ITG. Accordingly, and consistent with broad governance literature separating oversight from management decisions (Fama and Jensen 1983), recent IT research treats board-level ITG apart from managerial-level ITG (Jewer and McKay 2012). Managerial-level ITG is concerned with the design and implementation of IT decisionmaking structures. It covers arrangements or frameworks delineating who makes which IT decisions as well as organizational factor contingencies that underlie these arrangements (Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Weill and Ross 2005). It also covers mechanisms firms use to implement and enact ITG, including structure, processes, and relational mechanisms (De Haes and Van Grembergen 2009). In turn, board-level ITG is concerned with monitoring managerial IT decisions as well as policies and procedures for controlling IT resources (Andriole 2009; Buckby et al. 2005; Jewer and McKay 2012; Kambil and Lucas 2002; Mähring 2006; Nolan and McFarlan 2005). The board’s monitoring function aims to reduce managerial inefficiencies and to avoid failures that affect the business (Hillman and Dalziel 2003). Carrying out this function effectively hinges on the board’s level of IT competency, derived from the ITG mechanisms the board employs and their traits. These mechanisms include the directors on the board and their IT capital (knowledge, experience, skills, education, and so on), presence of a CIO or another senior IT executive on the board, and board-level IT committees. As determinants of board IT competency, market investors perceive these mechanisms to be beneficial to firm performance. A positive market reaction is reported for the appointment to boards of Internet firms directors who possess IT and e-commerce experience (Richardson and Zmud 2001), and upon the creation of IT strategy committees (Lunardi et al. 2014). Other studies find a positive connection of the IT knowledge-level of board directors with IT budget levels (Kambil and Lucas 2002), and, in turn, with subsequent shareholder returns (Kobelsky et al. 2008) and with future firm performance (Henderson et al. 2010). This research stream emphasizes that boards’ level of IT competency contributes to IT value-creation through better alignment of investments in IT resource strengths with business priorities. However, it is silent on the connection between board IT competency level and IT value-destruction arising from IT investment failures and operational IT failures (Bharadwaj et al. 2009).

This paper studies changes that firms implement to improve their boards’ IT competency level in response to suffering operational IT failures. More precisely, we study changes to determinants of board IT competency, including creating board-level IT committees, replacing a (presumably underperforming) CIO as part of the board exercising its monitoring responsibility on executive IT management, and increasing the ratio of board directors with IT experience. For brevity we will hereafter refer to any one or more changes in these determinants as changes in board IT competency. With this in mind, and knowing that operational IT failures result in IT value-destruction (e.g., Benaroch et al. 2012; Goldstein et al. 2011), our goal is to address two questions:

(1) Is the negative impact of operational IT failures on firms market value a predictor of post-failure changes in the level of board IT competency?

(2) If so, which specific determinants of board IT competency are these changes associated with?

To examine these questions, we draw on agency and resource dependence theoretic perspectives on the monitoring and service (resource provision) functions of the board (Hillman and Dalziel 2003). We theorize on how the impact of operational IT failures on the firm, as a consequence of inadequate board monitoring of IT matters, would affect the board’s level of IT competency, an antecedent or precursor to the board’s ability to carry out its monitoring function effectively. We measure impact of operational IT failures on the firm using the event study methodology, following ITG research on public events that arrive discretely in time (Dehning and Richardson 2002). We estimate the market reaction to, or abnormal stock returns observed when investors become aware of, operational IT failures. As to board IT competency level, we draw on broad corporate governance research (Hillman and Dalziel 2003) and board ITG research (Jewer and McKay 2011; Li et al. 2007; Nolan and McFarlan 2005) and measure it in terms of three determinants, or ITG mechanisms, namely: the ratio of directors on the board having IT capital (experience), the existence of a CIO/CTO role on the board, and the existence of board-level IT committees.

To test our theoretical predictions, we use a sample of 110 operational IT failures that occurred in publicly traded U.S. financial services firms between 1992 and 2009. We examine changes in firms’ board IT competency level in the two years following announcements of IT failures from the year prior to the IT failures. We have three main findings. First, changes in the level of board IT competency are significantly associated with the magnitude of (negative) abnormal equity returns measured around IT failures. Second, changes in the level of board IT competency are lopsided and driven by changes on the side of internal (executive) directors; they are dominated by an increase in the ratio of internal directors with IT capital and by the turnover of (presumably underperforming) CIOs/CTOs serving on the board. The lack of changes relating to independent directors despite them being the chief oversight body has several possible explanations we discuss later. Third, turnover of CIOs serving on the board is less likely in more IT intensive firms, perhaps because it is more disruptive to these firms’ business. Additionally, we follow Goldstein et al. and control for the effect of IT failure types but find no evidence that changes in board IT competency levels are driven by a particular type of IT failures. We also test for various potential sources of endogeneity and find our results to be robust. Overall, these findings validate our predictions about implications of IT failures on changes in board-level ITG mechanisms determining the board IT competency level.

This paper makes several novel contributions. One contribu tion is to the literature on operational IT failures. We are first to study operational IT failures in connection with board ITG. We find that firms institute IT-related changes in their boards upon suffering operational IT failures that impact their market value. This result suggests that IT failures are comparable in caliber to adverse accounting and finance events (e.g., fraud and earnings misstatements) known to result in corporate governance changes (Agrawal and Cooper 2017; Desai et al. 2006; Johnstone et al. 2011; Srinivasan 2005). In sum, this study draws attention to operational IT failures being an area that deserves greater attention in the IT literature. Our other contributions are to the board ITG literature. First, we provide evidence that market investors consider the board to be a principal ITG body, at least relative to IT value destruction resulting from operational IT failures. We document significant changes in board ITG mechanisms that firms institute to improve their boards’ level of IT competency, consistent with normative board ITG literature (Nolan and McFarlan 2005; Parent and Reich 2009) and descriptive board ITG literature documenting an IT knowledge and attention deficit at the board level (Huff et al. 2006; Jordan and Musson 2004; PWC 2006). More precisely, our results suggest that the IT competency level of boards improves only as a result of increasing turnover of (presumably underperforming) CIOs who serve on the board and of bringing to the board more executive directors with IT experience. Our results also broaden recent work on board accountability for IT-related deficiencies, and specifically on CEO/CFO turnover in firms which by law disclose IT-related deficiencies that affect financial reporting (Masli et al. 2016). Our work goes further by showing that higher turnover rates of board members are also associated with an improvement in boards’ IT competency level, and that they extend to CIOs serving on the board. In addition, our results apply not only to IT deficiencies that could affect financial reporting quality, but also to IT deficiencies at the root of operational IT failures that in fact impact business operations and compliance. Second, we expand Jewer and McKay’s (2012) model of antecedents and consequences of board ITG. To begin with, our model posits and empirically validates the existence of an additional link that loops back from consequences of ineffective board ITG monitoring (i.e., IT failures) to antecedents of board ITG monitoring effectiveness (i.e., board IT competency), where the link is moderated by firm IT intensity. Moreover, while Jewer and McKay’s proposed board ITG model rests on strategic choice and institutional theories, our model is based on the most prominent theoretical lenses used in corporate governance research, namely, agency theory and resource dependence theory (Baysinger and Butler 1985; Hillman and Dalziel 2003). Both of these contributions help to close a gap in the board ITG literature; they broaden theoretical and empirical insights on the link between board ITG and IT value creation to the understudied link between board ITG and IT value-destruction. Overall, our contributions carry important practical and research implications, to which we will return.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we link operational IT failures to the responsibilities of board-level ITG vis-à-vis oversight and control over IT resources. We then lay out our theoretical foundations and research hypotheses. In the subsequent section, we present the data and methods used to test the research hypotheses, along with the analysis results. Finally, we discuss the main results, their implications for research and practice, and directions for future research.

## Theoretical Background

This section sets the theoretical grounding of this study. We introduce the link between operational IT failures and the existence of IT resource weaknesses, discuss the role of ITG in monitoring and control over IT resources, and then identify specific ITG mechanisms and their monitoring responsibilities.

## Operational IT Failures and IT Governance

The occurrence of operational IT failures stems from actual failures of operational IT systems (software, hardware, networks, users, etc.) and/or the data assets that these systems record, process, transport, and safeguard. Such failures signal the presence of IT resource weaknesses in the firms experiencing the failures (Goldstein et al. 2011).

Based on an expanded version of the resource-based view of the firm, firms compete on two axes: resource strengths and resource weaknesses (Powell and Arregle 2007). The resource strengths axis pertains to the creation and use of distinctive inimitable resources that may result in a competitive advantage and value creation. Much literature has focused on this axis in relation to IT resources (e.g., Bharadwaj 2000; Melville et al. 2004; Wade and Hulland 2004). The resource weaknesses axis, by contrast, pertains to failures to attend to existing resources that may result in erosion of competitive advantage and in value destruction. Resource weaknesses arise from failures to make adequate investments in the acquisition, enhancement, and upkeep of resources. They may develop concurrently with resource strengths (West and DeCastro 2001): for example, an IT system that endows an organization with a competitive advantage but is poorly implemented may also have deficiencies that could lead to operational IT failures. Moreover, weaknesses may exist in resources that are neither rare nor inimitable but are necessary to maintain competitive parity (Arend 2004; West and DeCastro 2001). In either case, resource weaknesses “destroy value in a firm rather than simply fail to add any” (Arend 2003, p. 280), a view supported empirically by event studies of operational IT failures (e.g., Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Goldstein et al. 2011; Kannan et al. 2007).

The existence of IT resource weaknesses can be linked to ITG, since a primary goal of ITG is to ensure the effective acquisition and utilization of IT resources within the firm. As we stated earlier, IT research distinguishes between management-level ITG and board-level ITG (Jewer and McKay 2012). Management-level ITG research focuses on ITG arrangements or modes delineating the distribution of IT authorities, rights, and accountabilities (e.g., centralized versus decentralized), organizational contingencies associated with different ITG modes, and ITG frameworks combining the first two (Brown and Grant 2005; Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999; Weill and Ross 2005). This stream also explores mechanisms for enacting ITG. Weill’s (2004) survey of CIOs identifies 15 common ITG mechanisms, including IT steering committees, IT executive committees, and IT architecture committees. De Haes and Grembergen’s (2009) Delphi study with senior IT professionals identifies 33 mechanisms they classify as ITG structures, ITG processes, and ITG relational mechanisms. Board-level ITG research focuses on functions of the board in ITG and respective ITG mechanisms (Buckby et al. 2005; Jewer and McKay 2012; Mähring 2006; Nolan and McFarlan 2005). One central board ITG function is monitoring, or oversight, of IT management actions for the purpose of avoiding ineffective IT investment behavior and holding management accountable for IT decisions and their implementation (Grover et al. 2007; Wilkin and Chenhall 2010). Another central board ITG function is service, or provision of resources ranging from IT expertise of board directors to preferential access to external IT providers. IT expertise of board directors and board IT committees are among the ITG mechanisms identified by De Haes and Grembergen’s Delphi study.

Of greater relevance to our study context of operational IT failures is board-level ITG. In what follows we elaborate on the board ITG monitoring and service functions and on board ITG mechanisms.

## Board ITG Functions and Theoretical Roots

The board ITG monitoring and service functions mirror the central board functions in corporate governance. Solid theoretical groundings for these functions of the board come from agency theory and resource dependence theory (Hillman and Dalziel 2003).

The ITG monitoring function of the board has its theoretical roots in agency theory (Fama and Jensen 1983). Assuming a firm owner (principal) that hires a manager (agent) for her ability to increase the firm value and transfers to the agent decision rights, agency theory studies problems likely to arise when the principal’s interests and the agent’s interests are not aligned. When the owner appoints a board as an oversight body with decision control authority, the board is presumed to monitor managers’ decisions and actions in order to prevent inefficiencies and business failures. Monitoring is a costly obligation “met by scrutiny, evaluation, and regulation of the actions of top management” (Hillman and Dalziel 2003, p. 385). The agency problem exists in the IT context as well (Ho et al. 2011, p. 598):

According to the principal/agent literature, managers may … make excessive IT investments because a larger IT department brings higher power and salaries. … [that] may intentionally imitate others investment decisions to enhance their professional reputations, with little or no regard to their firms’ IT needs. … making a substantial IT investment may merely represent managers’ attempt to comply with institutional pressures, and thus may not necessarily lead to improved firm performance.

ITG monitoring, too, is a board function that involves two activity sets (Buckby et al. 2005; Jewer and McKay 2012; Mähring 2006; Nolan and McFarlan 2005; Parent and Reich 2009). One set centers on observing that IT contributes to a competitive advantage and delivers against the strategy. These activities support value creation via investment in IT resource strengths. Another set includes identifying IT threats and opportunities critical to the firm’s future, ensuring adherence to the firm risk profile of IT, and monitoring compliance of IT with regulations and contractual commitments. These activities support value protection via avoidance and remediation of IT resource weaknesses.

The ITG service function of the board, also termed the provision of resources function, is rooted in resource dependence theory. This theory views the board as a mechanism that provides the firm with environmental linkages and access to resources that help to limit the firm’s dependence on external entities, to lower uncertainty for the firm, and to reduce transaction costs associated with environmental interdependency (Hillman and Dalziel 2003). In the ITG context, the board service function would cover a range of activities, including reinforcing the public image of the firm’s IT capability, providing IT expertise and IT counsel to management, enabling preferential access to external IT providers and other third parties, and aiding in the formulation of IT strategy. All of these activities focus on the board as a provider of resources, not a monitor and evaluator of management.

The board monitoring and service functions are theoretically related. Arguably, these functions cannot be distinguished in practice; they are “subtly intertwined” and can “operate concurrently” (Mintzberg 1983, p. 86). Hillman and Dalziel (2003) integrate the agency and resource dependence perspectives on these functions, yielding a model in which board capital (directors’ knowledge, expertise, experience, skills, relations, reputation, etc.) is a primary basis for the board’s ability to perform these functions to affect firm performance. Their model provides the foundation for the theoretical model we present in the next section.

## Board ITG Mechanisms

The board carries out its ITG monitoring and service functions using a number of ITG mechanisms that parallel board mechanisms studied in the corporate governance literature. These ITG mechanisms include the directors on the board, function-specific roles (e.g., CFO or CIO), and board-level committees. Table 1 summarizes these mechanisms’ main responsibilities.

The first board ITG mechanism comprises the directors on the board (Jewer and McKay 2012; Mähring 2006; Nolan and McFarlan 2005). Based on agency theory, directors’ monitoring responsibilities include guidance on the design, implementation, and enforcement of a system of internal controls over IT resources, as well as review of the audit process and findings about IT controls (Li et al. 2007; Parent and Reich 2009). Based on resource dependence theory, their service responsibilities include providing IT advice and counsel to management, hiring competent IT executives, and providing access to capital and other resources (Richards et al. 2005; Nolan and McFarlan 2005; Wilkin and Chenhall 2010). Some responsibilities are split between independent directors and internal (executive) directors (Baysinger and Butler 1985). Independent directors hold a direct responsibility for monitoring, and they provide such resources as expertise in IT and how IT is used in the firm’s industry as well as access to external IT players. Internal directors inform the design of controls over IT resources by identifying risks of most concern to the firm’s business (Richards et al. 2005). They also set the “control environment,” or “tone at the top,” which influences and incentivizes the control consciousness of employees (COSO 2004). Internal directors also set priorities for allocating IT budgets, IT personnel, and other resources toward implementing and remediating controls over IT resources.

Other IT committees include technology and operational risk committees, and information security committees, among others.

The second board ITG mechanism is the role of a CIO or another IT executive on the board (Jewer and McKay 2012; Weill and Ross 2005). In many firms that depend on IT, CIOs are members of the senior executive management and the board, also termed the top management team (TMT) (Chatterjee et al. 2001; Earl and Feeny 1995; Hamdan 2011; Preston et al. 2008; Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1989). Such CIOs are an important source of information for independent and internal directors to monitor IT matters effectively. They inform the design of controls over IT resources by virtue of grasping the bigger picture, knowing what IT resources put the business at risk, and having the responsibility of delivering smooth and secure IT services to the business. CIOs also ensure that controls over IT resources are implemented and adhered to in practice. Under Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) Section 404, CIOs have to sub-certify to CEOs and CFOs the adequacy of IT controls over financial reporting (CIO Insight/Gartner 2004).

The third board ITG mechanism comprises board IT committees. Appointed by the board, these committees assist in monitoring IT matters, auditing IT controls, implementing the IT strategy, and driving IT decisions (Weill and Ross 2005). Based on our review of multiple financial proxy statements filed with the SEC by publicly traded companies in our data sample, board IT committees include the IT steering or strategy committee, the IS auditing committee, and other narrowly scoped committees. The IT steering committee may include the CEO, CFO, business unit executives, CIO or another IT executive, and representatives from risk management, auditing, and operations (ITGI 2006). It monitors the overall IT performance (Weill and Ross 2005) and creates firm-wide policies and procedures for implementing controls over the acquisition and use of IT resources (Earl 1989). The IS audit committee, which is often a subcommittee of the audit and control committee, tracks the effectiveness of IT controls and IT risk management processes (Wilkin and Chenhall 2010) and communicates its assessments and recommendations to executive management, external auditors, and major stakeholders. Other board IT committees, such as the information security committee or the technology and operational risk committee, typically oversee more narrowly focused areas of control over IT resources.

## Theory and Research Hypotheses

We next present our theoretical model and then use the model to develop research hypotheses on the link between the economic impact of operational IT failures on the firm and postfailure changes in ITG mechanisms that determine the board level of IT competency.

## Theoretical Model

Our theoretical model in Figure 1 adapts and expands the models of Hillman and Dalziel (2003) and Jewer and McKay (2012) to our study context. As we noted previously, Hillman and Dalziel integrate the agency and the resource dependence theoretical perspectives on the main corporate governance functions of the board, offering a model where the board monitoring and service functions have board capital (directors’ expertise, experience, skills, reputation, and so on) as a primary antecedent and firm performance as a consequence. We augment their model by adding a link that loops back from the consequence to the antecedent. In our model, the consequence is a change in firm market value observed upon the occurrence of operational IT failures. It parallels the contribution of IT to firm performance in the board ITG model of Jewer and McKay. The antecedent in our model is board IT competency, a precursor to the board’s ability to perform its ITG monitoring and service functions. Following Jewer and McKay, board IT competency derives from (1) directors’ IT capital (akin to board capital in corporate governance), (2) a CIO or another senior IT executive serving on the board, and (3) other ITG mechanisms such as board IT committees. Finally, moderating the relationship that closes the loop in our model is firm IT intensity, which parallels the role of IT in the firm in Jewer and McKay. This moderating effect is rooted in Nolan and McFarlan’s (2005) normative IT strategic impact grid, which suggests that the role of IT in the firm influences the level of IT competency the board needs to carry out its ITG functions.<sup>2</sup> This moderating effect is also factored in the interpretation of direct precursors to the board’s ability to fulfill its functions in Hillman and Dalziel’s model. Namely, IT intensity cannot independently make for effective ITG monitoring; rather, it influences the board’s decision to monitor, contingent on the board having the requisite IT competency. On this basis, our model takes firm IT intensity to amplify or constrain changes in determinants of board IT competency that occur following IT-related changes in firm market value.

Before we proceed to formulate our hypotheses, let us define the constructs in our model. Change in firm market value is a market-based measure reflecting how the arrival of relevant unexpected news results in investors adjusting their expectations about firm performance (in terms of future cash flows, uncertainty of cash flows, cost of capital, growth potential, and so on). In this study, we focus on unexpected changes in firm equity value, or abnormal returns (ARs), observed when operational IT failures come to light at discrete time points.<sup>3</sup> Board IT competency is a precursor to the board’s ability to carry out its ITG monitoring and service functions. Its determinants are:

(1) Directors’ IT capital parallels board capital in corporate governance (Forbes and Milliken 1999; Hillman and Dalziel 2003). It indicates directors’ knowledge, expertise, experience, and industry and educational background in the specific area of IT. It has been used in multiple board ITG studies. Firms whose boards have more directors with IT experience report fewer weaknesses in IT controls (Li et al. 2007). Directors’ IT capital is argued to improve ITG effectiveness (Nolan and McFarlan 2005; Weill 2004) and, in fact, is shown to be an antecedent of board ITG effectiveness (Jewer and McKay 2012). It has been also studied in connection with appointing directors to boards of Internet firms (Richardson and Zmud 2001). Kambil and Lucas (2002) find that independent directors’ IT capital is correlated with firms’ IT budget levels, with having a CIO on the senior management team, and with the frequency of IT reviews by the board; and internal directors’ IT capital is correlated with the size of IT investments requiring board review.

(2) CIO on the board indicates the CIO’s structural power and potential influence on the board (Preston et al. 2008; Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1989). A CIO who is at peer level with the firm’s TMT is likely to be accepted into the firm’s inner circle and, hence, is expected to have greater authority and contribution to the board’s involvement in ITG (Chatterjee et al. 2001; Earl and Feeny 1995).

(3) Board IT committees aid the board to carry out its ITG functions. They help with IT value-creation by keeping costly IT investments under control and on track to providing a competitive advantage (Nolan and McFarlan 2005). They also help with IT value protection by ensuring smooth IT service delivery to daily business operations (Parent and Reich 2009). The IT strategy committee and (IT) audit committee are ITG structures identified in the Delphi study of De Haes and Van Gremberge (2009).

## Hypotheses Development

We expect the occurrence of operational IT failures to result in a negative change in firm market value and, subsequently, in a positive change in the level of board IT competency. We focus on the latter part of this relationship, as the former part has been validated empirically (Benaroch et al. 2012; Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Goldstein et al. 2011; Hovav and D’Arcy 2003; Kannan et al. 2007; Ko and Dorantes 2006).<sup>4</sup>

A negative change in firm market value, or in firm equity prices, is an indication of IT value destruction resulting from investors losing faith in the firm’s control of its IT resources. Investors would associate operational IT failures with an increased likelihood that (1) the firm’s IT resources have deficiencies, and (2) the IT deficiencies have been going undetected and their implications underappreciated. From an agency theory perspective, regaining investors’ confidence would require firms to signal a commitment to improving the board monitoring of IT matters, in support of preventing future IT failures. One signaling strategy is to improve the board IT competency level, and we expect the improvement to be commensurate with the magnitude of change in firm market value around IT failures. This expectation has empirical support in accounting and finance research documenting a positive link between adverse corporate events, such as fraud and earnings restatements, and changes in corporate governance, such as CFO turnover and the addition to the board of directors with financial expertise (Agrawal and Cooper 2017; Desai et al. 2006; Li et al. 2007; Johnstone et al. 2011). Greater board IT competency is also beneficial from the resource dependence perspective. It would improve the board’s ability to carry out its service function, particularly by offering IT expertise and IT counsel to management in support of remediation and prevention of IT resource deficiencies at the root of IT failures. In this light, we formulate our first hypothesis as follows:

## H1: The market value drop around recent operational IT failures is positively associated with a change in the board IT competency level.

As our study context involves a multilayered agency problem, a change in board IT competency level post IT failures would involve all determinants of IT competency. Investors would trace negative impacts of operational IT failures primarily to independent directors on the board—the chief oversight body investors have expected to be responsible for ITG monitoring. Independent directors, in turn, have likely expected executive directors on the board to institute appropriate ITG practices to ensure that controls over IT resources are effective and efficient. And, executive directors may have delegated much of the responsibility for enterprise IT management to the CIO and her subordinates.

We expect CIOs on the board to be most strongly affected by operational IT failures, in line with the agency theoretic principle that mutual monitoring by the board members would act to curb individual directors (Fama and Jensen 1983). The board is given monitoring and sanctioning rights it can use to align agents’ effort with stakeholders’ interests. In particular, if value-reducing events under the charge of one board member negatively impact other members’ value, as judged by the managerial labor market, the other members have an incentive to monitor and sanction activities of the former (Rediker and Seth 1995).<sup>5</sup> Accordingly, acting in concert and based on observable performance measures of a board member, the remaining board members can sanction a poorly performing member or even replace her/him. In fact, when facing serious performance issues, superiors may be successful in placating shareholders by dismissing a subordinate associated with the poor performance (Boeker 1992). In this sense, CIOs may bear the greatest culpability for IT failures and become candidates for such superior-initiated scapegoating, given that IT failures are linked to their core responsibilities and that the impact of IT failures on firm market value is observable. On this basis we expect turnover of CIOs who serve on boards of firms that suffer negative changes in market value around IT failures (Feng 2015; Higgs et al. 2014).<sup>6</sup> It would signal to investors the board’s effort to improve monitoring and control of IT resources. (A similar expectation exists for CIOs not serving on the board, but it is not considered here because data limitations make it difficult to test.)

We also expect the board to intensify ITG monitoring by investing in means that improve its capacity to acquire, interpret, and act upon relevant information. The board would be willing to increase the agency monitoring costs to signal to investors an emphasis on IT oversight as well as to lower the residual loss directors may experience when firm performance suffers; residual loss is an agency cost often linked to penalties imposed on directors by the labor market for management talent (Fama and Jensen 1983). One means for intensifying ITG monitoring is the addition of board IT committees. These committees limit IT-related decision rights of executive directors and IT management by virtue of the board delegating to committees decision initiation rights and de facto decision ratification rights on various IT issues. These committees signal to investors the board’s heightened attention to IT oversight and to involvement in ITG (Higgs et al. 2014; ITGI 2006; Weill and Ross 2005).<sup>7</sup> Adding IT committees would also increase the need for board directors with IT experience (capital). Members of board IT committees must include IT competent directors, as implied by the agency theoretic principle of aligning decision rights with specific knowledge useful to competently exercise those rights (Fama and Jensen 1983). Moreover, the board would need to retain IT competent directors outside committees to be able to curtail committee-born agency costs by means of critically questioning the work of IT committees and monitoring their decisions (Moldoveanu and Martin 2001).<sup>8</sup>

There are other reasons to expect boards aiming to intensify ITG monitoring to have more directors with IT capital. In agency theory, monitoring effectiveness hinges on the monitor knowing as much as the monitored (Fama and Jensen 1983; Jensen and Meckling 1992). Thus, to be effective at ITG monitoring, independent board directors need a requisite IT absorptive capacity, or prior IT knowledge. “Prior related knowledge confers an ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it” (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, p. 128). This becomes more important in highly technical settings such as IT (Michael 2009). Having general knowledge of IT allows independent directors to ask executive management competent questions to uncover information for monitoring IT matters. Having firm-specific IT knowledge (about the firm’s IT capabilities, IT suppliers, IT organizations, and so on) allows them to independently verify the information provided by executive management and to form their own opinions on critical IT matters. Independent directors’ ability to ask questions and form independent opinions is crucial in light of their insulation from relevant information about the company that is available to executive directors (Hooghienstra and van Manen 2004; Nowak and

McCabe 2003).<sup>9</sup> Agency theory warns of such an information asymmetry (Moldoveanu and Martin 2001). The asymmetry could be perceived to hinge on the integrity of executive management, which may have an incentive to withhold or color information that would increase independent directors’ ability to exercise their monitoring duties and to sanction management actions (Hooghienstra and van Manen 2004). In the IT context, such information asymmetry is typically greater (Michael 2009). Aside from integrity concerns, executive directors themselves may be subject to an information asymmetry relative to the CIO, IT managers, and their subordinates and third parties. To be able to provide independent directors with adequate information for ITG monitoring, executive directors themselves need the IT competence and absorptive capacity to understand and appreciate internal IT issues and to meaningfully convey the issues to independent directors. All of these arguments lead us to expect boards of firms that suffer IT failures to have more independent and executive directors with IT capital. More directors with IT capital send the right signal to investors, who according to agency theory “would expect monitoring activities to become specialized to those … individuals who possess comparative advantages in these activities” (Jensen and Meckling 1976, p. 354). It should eventually translate into better firm-wide controls over IT resources.

The changes we expect in all determinants of board IT competency are beneficial also from the resource dependence perspective on the board ITG service function. More directors with IT capital provide greater assurance for effective remediation of IT deficiencies at the root of IT failures. A well-supported hypothesis in the corporate governance literature is that board capital is positively associated with the provision of resources critical to firm performance (Hillman and Dalziel 2003). This has support in board ITG research as well (Jewer and McKay 2012; Kambil and Lucas 2002). More independent directors with IT capital increase the board’s ability to offer IT advice and counsel to management, to advocate for more IT budgets, to hire competent IT management, and to provide preferential access to IT vendors and other third parties. More internal directors with IT capital enhances the board’s understanding of the business costs of IT risks, and facilitates prompt allocation of resources and setting priorities to remediate IT deficiencies. Firms that have disclosed weaknesses in IT controls over financial reporting and replaced their CEO with a more IT competent person have been shown to remediate IT control weaknesses more promptly (Haislip et al. 2016). Moreover, since “senior managers often employ industry average IT spending as a percent of sales as a benchmark for setting their own IT budget levels” (Kobelsky et al. 2008, p. 991), more IT competent executive directors would allow better alignment of IT budget levels with the firm’s idiosyncratic IT needs.

On the basis of the preceding discussion, we expect firms that suffer operational IT failures to make changes in all determinants of board IT competency. This leads to our next hypothesis:

H2: The market value drop around recent operational IT failures is positively associated with the following changes in determinants of board IT competency:

(a) Increase in the ratio of independent directors with IT experience;

(b) Increase in the ratio of executive directors with IT experience;

(c) Turnover of a CIO serving on the board; and,

(d) Establishment of board IT committees.

Changes in board IT competency levels following IT failures will not happen if they might result in costly disruptions to the firms’ business.<sup>10</sup> Out of the changes considered in hypothesis 2, turnover of a CIO serving on the board is likely to be the costliest or most disruptive to the firm. This can be explained in terms of the service function of the board. While boards might seek to dismiss a (presumably underperforming) director to protect their legitimacy, Cowen and Marcel (2011) argues based on resource dependence theory, that boards would be concerned about the repercussions of dismissing a director who is crucial to meeting resource provision needs or obligations. This is particularly true if relationships with internal and external entities are an important input to firm success, or if the potential disruption of those relationships threatens future competitiveness. In such cases, boards may be less inclined to tolerate the disruption and distress of director dismissal; they may either not consider dismissal seriously or have difficulty building adequate support within the board. This logic extends to a CIO on the board being arguably the only director whose dismissal could jeopardize access to relational IT resources, ongoing relationships the

CIO has forged with IT vendors, handling of legacy issues that impact IT resources needed for day-to-day business, and so on. Thus, while the benefits of replacing a CIO with the “right” CIO might suggest a greater likelihood of CIO turnover, the reality is that replacing an incumbent CIO is disruptive and creates a host of challenges (Chan et al. 1997; Chowa 2010; Gerth and Peppard 2014; McGillicuddy 2007; Sabherwal and Chan 2001; Smith et al. 2013).<sup>11</sup>

CIO turnover is more disruptive and challenging in firms that are IT intensive or heavily dependent on IT (Luftman and Ben-Zvi 2010). First, IT intensive firms typically face high growth and environmental uncertainty (Dewan et al. 1998; Xue et al. 2011), may have greater exposure to IT risks (Tanriverdi and Du 2009), and may therefore be more prone to operational IT failures. Incumbent CIOs have a comparative advantage in managing their company’s IT risks. They understand better the weak aspects of their firms’ IT resources based on knowledge of their firms’ business and, therefore, can more effectively respond so as to correct and prevent IT resource weaknesses. Second, since IT intensive firms invest more heavily in developing stronger firm-specific IT capabilities over time, incumbent CIOs can better leverage their firms’ superior IT capabilities by combining them with non-IT capabilities in ways that promote better firm performance in the longer run. Hence, it is more difficult and risky for IT intensive firms to find the right CIO to replace an existing CIO. In fact, CIO turnover could be detrimental to these firms’ ability to maintain and tap into the organizational IT capabilities as well as to focus on the long-term quality of IT resources (personnel, systems, etc.) and their alignment with business (Luftman and Ben-Zvi 2010). For these same reasons Hacker (2003, p. 14) termed the IT turnover problem since the dot-com era “a silent but significant profit killer.” In sum, avoiding CIO turnover carries intrinsic benefits in IT intensive firms.

This entire discussion does not extend to other determinants of board IT competency. There is no reason or evidence to suggest that replacing non-CIO directors that lack IT capital or creating board IT committees is nearly as disruptive even in IT intensive firms. Accordingly, we hypothesize that:

H3: The market value drop following operational IT failures and firm IT intensity interact negatively in their effect on turnover of CIOs on the board, such that the effect of market value drop on CIO turnover is weaker in firms with greater IT intensity.

## Data, Analysis, and Results

## Data Sample

Our data source for operational IT failures is a commercial operational risk events database, called Financial Institutions Risk Scenario Trends (FIRST), marketed by IBM. FIRST contains data on adverse operational risk events that occurred globally over the past decades, with the bulk of events happening after 1980 and in financial services firms. FIRST collects event data from public sources such as The Wall Street Journal and Reuters, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) filings or reports of investigations, Wells Notices, court rulings, and company news wires.

We focus on incidents announced after 1985 in publicly traded U.S. financial services firms. First, following Goldstein et al. (2011), using an electronic auto-search we screened candidate IT failures for the presence of one or more of the following keywords: data, computer, electronic, information, system, technology/technical, security, software, hack, phishing, access, code, password, data, network, transaction, error/erroneous, hard drive, outage, volume, internet, interrupt, breach, cyber, virus, attack, glitch, steal/stole, confidential, process, e-mail, private account/information/ record, and privacy. Then, each author independently reviewed the detailed narrative for every candidate incident to determine whether the incident was indeed an operational IT failure. The guiding principle was determining whether data and/or IT assets were at the root of the incident or whether they simply played a by-product use role. Agreement was reached on almost all instances and the authors were able to reconcile all remaining differences. This step yielded 195 IT failures. These IT failures were then classified by each author as data- or function-related failures, following the categories in Goldstein et al., and as confidentiality, integrity, or availability failures, following the categories in Benaroch et al. (2012). Appendix A elaborates on these types of IT failures.

We excluded from the identified IT failures those that met one or more of the following criteria, ending up with a sample of 110 IT failures. First, we excluded IT failures that had no exact first announcement date or had an announcement date overlapping with confounding events that occurred within a window of plus or minus three days (e.g., earnings announcements, merger and acquisition announcements, change in management, and major lawsuits and fines) as identified through I/B/E/S (for earnings announcement dates), Dow Jones Factiva, and LexisNexis business news databases.<sup>12</sup> Second, to avoid contamination by economy-wide events and periods of excessive equity market volatility, we eliminated 20 IT failures that occurred in the six months following the September 11, 2001 terrorist attacks and the six months following the August 2008 financial market meltdown. Third, we dropped IT failures in firms with unavailable market data in the CRSP database or unavailable IT and corporate governance data in the SEC’s EDGAR database for the years surrounding the operational IT failure (e.g., due to merger activity).<sup>13</sup> Applying these criteria resulted in a final sample of 110 IT failure announcements; illustrative examples are provided in Appendix B. Table 2 tallies the distribution of IT failures across the 1992–2009 period in six 3-year intervals. Tables 3 and 4 summarize the frequency of our data sample by failure type and institution type.

Our IT and corporate governance data were collected primarily from proxy statements, DEF 14A, that publicly traded firms file annually with the SEC. These proxy statements are available through the EDGAR database. Of relevance to this study, these filings contain the titles and biographies of board members and top executives in the firm, as well as information on board-level committees (see examples in Appendix C). We record CIOs/CTOs and other IT executive titles only if they are named executive officers in proxy statements. This approach favors focusing on CIOs that serve on the board because these CIOs have greater authority and influence and because CIO data collected from proxy statements is more complete in nature (Chatterjee et al. 2001; Earl and Feeny 1995; Hamdan 2011 Preston et al. 2008; Raghunathan and Raghunathan 1989).<sup>14</sup> Likewise, we record a board-level IT committee only if it is reported in the proxy statements (see examples in Appendix C). In our sample, the majority are IT steering committees and technology leadership councils. Other reported committees are labeled technology and operations, technology and operational risk, IS audit and control, and technology and information security. Our data are collected beginning with the most recent year prior to the announcement of an IT failure (T = 0) and ending two years after (T = +2); hereafter, upper case “T” denotes calendar year and lower case “t” denotes day. The data contain about 2,400 board directors a year (7,095 over three years), 79 CIOs serving on boards in a year (236 over three years),<sup>15</sup> and 18 board IT committees a year (57 over three years).

## Measuring Market Reaction to IT Failures

We use a short-term market-based measure for the market reaction to operational IT failures, namely, the relative change in the firm’s market value measured around the announcement of an IT failure. The relatively discrete nature of how IT operational failures come to light allows us to make use of the instantaneous impact of such events on firms’ equity value, consistent with ITG studies of events that are publicly announced at identifiable dates (e.g., Chatterjee et al. 2001; Richardson and Zmud 2001). Short-term market-based measures can isolate the impact of an event that market investors perceive and reflect in their reaction to the event, in contrast with longer-term measures based on periodic (annual) financial reports conflating information on other events beside the target event.<sup>16</sup>

We apply the event study methodology using Eventus<sup>®</sup> (Cowan Research 2007) to test the impact of operational IT failures on firms’ market valuations. Generally, when an unexpected event brings new information to the market about a company, the stock price quickly adjusts and nonzero abnormal returns (AR) accrue around the date of announcement for that firm (Brown and Warner 1985; McWilliams and Siegel 1997). Daily ARs are the differences between the observed and the expected daily returns. Daily returns are based on daily closing prices. Expected returns were computed based on a single-factor market model estimated over a window ranging 301 days to 46 days prior to the event date. The benchmark market index was constructed using a diversified market portfolio of equally weighted or value-weighted equity returns. We also tried Fama-French three-factor model, Fama-French-momentum four-factor model, and market excess returns model as alternative specifications. ARs were computed for an event window of several days overlapping the event’s first announcement date (t = 0). ARs were then summed for each event window to form cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) and then averaged across all firm events to produce the mean CAR.

Following the convention, we tried various event windows starting up to 3 days before day 0, to account for a possible leakage of information prior to an IT failure revelation, and extending up to 3 days after day 0, to capture more fully the adjustment of stock prices to the revelation of the news. Finally, since the a priori expectation is that IT operational risk events generate negative ARs, we carried out a one-tail (left-tail) test to check whether negative ARs and CARs are statistically significant. We used Patell’s standardized version of the test statistic that is commonly used to account for the fact that events are not clustered around a particular date (Brown and Warner 1985).

Table 6 depicts the event study results. For all 110 IT failures, mean daily ARs are positive until day t = -2, then drop below zero on day t = -1 in most model specifications, and remain negative throughout the event window. Mean CARs in all event windows are negative and statistically significant, supporting the expectation that operational IT failures result in a significant drop in the market value of firms experiencing the events. Figure 2 further illustrates that the mean CARs (based on one-factor model with equal-weighted market index) remain negative beyond 10 days past the announced IT failure day. In all subsequent econometric models we use CARs for the event window t = [-1,+2], denoted as CAR[-1,+2] and obtained using the equal-weighted index. This window yields the highest magnitude of CAR and statistical significance, and there is negligible difference between CARs obtained using equal-weighted and valueweighted market index. In robustness checks, we also employed CARs for the [-1,0] and [-1,+1] windows, both of which yielded qualitatively similar results.

The implication of these findings is that IT failures are indeed costly to the firm. The drop in equity prices leads to the jump in the cost of equity capital, a key driver of the discount factor that the market applies to future cash flows. Upon IT failures, a higher discount factor becomes necessary to compensate investors for the additional risk that they face (Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2009). We will revisit this issue in the “Discussion” section.

## Measuring Board IT Competency Level

Building on the definition of constructs in our theoretical model, we proceed to derive a measure for this construct based on a number of indicators of IT competency (directors’ IT capital, a CIO/CTO on the board, and board IT committees.) The first two are independent directors’ IT capital and internal (executive) directors’ IT capital. Bassellier et al. (2003) have developed IT expertise scales for line business managers, but these scales measure granular technology details not typically reported for individual directors in proxy statements (e.g., familiarity with personal computers, with IT project management, and with specific IT applications). Instead, a measure that counts dichotomous indicators has been used by board ITG researchers to measure directors’ IT capital (Boritz and Lim 2007; Jewer and McKay 2012) and by finance researchers to construct board governance and risk management indices (Ellul and Yerramilli 2003; Gompers et al. 2003; Larcker et al. 2007). Following the same approach, we read through the biography of each board member, as reported in SEC proxy filings, and used similar criteria to Jewer and McKay’s (2012) to establish IT capital: (1) Has the director held an IT management position in the past? (2) Has the director previously worked in a public IT firm? (3) Does the director hold IT-related college degrees or certifications? Meeting any of these criteria earned a director a point of IT capital. Using this coding approach we constructed two variables: Board\_ITCapital, the percentage of independent directors with IT capital points, and ExecMgmt\_ ITCapital, the percentage of internal (executives) with IT capital points (excluding the CIO on the board). This coding was carried out independently by one of the authors and two research assistants. The percentage inter-rater agreement was around 95%, and the three raters were able to reconcile differences and arrived at a consensus. The third indicator of board IT competency is the presence of a CIO/CTO role on the board (CIO\_CTO\_OnBoard), coded as binary-valued. A fourth indicator is the number of board IT committees documented in the SEC proxy filings in EDGAR (IT Committee). In effect, this indicator is coded as binary-valued because firms in our data set have at most one board-level IT committee listed in their proxy statements.

Based on the above indicators, we constructed two measures of aggregate board IT competency level. One measure, Board\_IT\_Competency\_Score, is computed as a simple sum of four IT competency indicators: Board\_ITCapital, ExecMgmt\_ITCapital, CIO\_CTO\_OnBoard, and IT\_ Committee. The first two are taken in decimal form and the last two are dichotomous. As such, Board\_IT\_Competency\_ Score can range from 0 to 4, with higher values indicating greater board IT competency. This simple additive score recognizes that the four indicators pertain to ITG monitoring mechanisms with complementary responsibilities (Table 1), while weighing the indicators equally. For robustness testing we created another version of this variable that makes no judgment about the relative importance or weight of the four indicators. Following Ellul and Yerramilli (2013), we use principal component analysis (PCA) to construct a Board\_ IT\_Competency\_PCA variable as the first principal component of the four factors; it is obtained by conducting a singular value decomposition of the correlation matrix of the four indicators and extracting the eigenvector with the highest eigenvalue.

We also defined change versions for each of the variables measuring board IT competency level. For continuous variables, ΔBoard\_IT\_Competency\_Score, ΔBoard\_ITCapital, and ΔExecMgmt\_ITCapital, change is measured as the level of the variable one or two years post an IT failure less the level of that variable in the year immediately prior to the IT failure. For dichotomous variables, ΔCIO\_CTO\_OnBoard takes a value of 1 if a CIO/CTO serving on the board was replaced, and 0 otherwise, and ΔIT\_Committee takes a value of 1 if the firm has established a new board-level IT committee and 0 otherwise. (Recall that firms in our data sample have at most one board-level IT committee listed in their proxy statements.) One advantage of using the aggregate ΔBoard\_IT\_Competency\_Score measure is that it is sensitive to the possibility that changes in individual determinants of board IT competency may cancel each other out.

## Measuring IT Intensity

IT intensity (IT\_Intensity) is computed by, first, deriving an industry-level IT intensity score as the share of (dollar) IT capital in total capital for an industry segment according to Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) data (Stiroh 2002; Han et al. 2011),<sup>17</sup> and, then, scaling this score by the firm’s per centage of sales generated from a segment as weight (Tanriverdi and Du 2009). Our IT intensity measure varies for each firm and IT failure as a function of: the firm’s segment among the seven segments into which BEA classifies the financial industry, the firm’s sales share within its industry segment, and the year in which an IT failure occurred.<sup>18</sup>

## Control Variables

We use four sets of control variables. The first covers corporate governance characteristics commonly used in corporate governance research (Agrawal and Chadha 2005; Hoitash et al. 2009; Johnstone et al. 2011; Rechner and Dalton 1991). Measured in the most recent year prior to the announcement of an IT failure, these variables are: board size (BoardSize), duality of CEO as the board chairman (CEO\_Chair), proportion of independent directors (IndepDirectors), and existence of a risk committee (RiskCommittee).

A second set of controls covers common firm characteristics. One is the firm’s growth prospect, measured by Tobin’s q ratio (FirmGrowth) (Bharadwaj et al. 1999). Tobin’s q tends to be high when a firm’s valuable intangible assets (e.g., IT capabilities and human capital) are high relative to the replacement cost of the firm’s tangible assets. Growth firms have been shown to suffer greater operational losses (Cummins et al. 2006). Another control is firm size (FirmSize), measured by total liabilities, a firm characteristic found to influence the reaction to operational IT failures (Cavusoglu et al. 2004; Kannan et al. 2007). The third is equity volatility (FirmRisk), taken as a proxy for firm idiosyncratic risk (Ettredge and Richardson 2001). The remaining control variables in this set are used in robustness checks: (1) beta (FirmBeta), a proxy for firm systematic risk, computed from the single-factor CAPM model; (2) leverage ratio (Firm Leverage) computed by dividing total liabilities by total assets; (3) return on assets (ROA) computed as EBITDA to total assets ratio; and (4) the numeric version of long-term debt S&P credit rating (Rating), with higher values referring to higher ratings.

A third set consists of industry-level controls. One is a variable that captures any otherwise uncaptured heterogeneity in the industry affiliations of the firms in our sample. We use a dummy variable for each of the two-digit SIC codes to capture such fixed effects. Others are measures of industry concentration used to control for competition as part of robustness testing: the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, fourfirm industry concentration ratio, and weighted average R&D expenditures (Li et al. 2006; Li et al. 2013).

A fourth set consists of IT failure attributes used as controls in robustness tests. Following Goldstein et al. (2011), we separate data- from function-related IT failures by using dummy variables D\_DATA and D\_FUNCTION. Likewise, following Benaroch et al. (2012), we distinguish between confidentiality, integrity, and availability IT failures by using dummy variables D\_CONFIDENTIALITY, D\_INTEGRITY, and D\_AVAILABILITY, respectively.<sup>19</sup>

Board IT Competency score is highly positively correlated with each of its components by construction. ## Analysis and Results: Changes in Board IT Competency Post IT Failures

We proceed to examine whether firms experience changes in their board IT competency level upon the occurrence of operational IT failures. Descriptive statistics in the top half of Table 7 provide some preliminary evidence for strengthening of board IT competency in the one or two years post an IT failure. The average board IT competency score for our sample rose from 1.14 at year T = 0 to 1.16 at year T = +2. Among its components, the average ratio of independent directors’ with IT expertise rose from 9.26% at T = 0 to 10.06% at $T = + 1$ , the same ratio of executive management rose from 16.53% at T = 0 to 17.62% at T = +2, and the proportion of firms with an IT committee increased from 16.36% at $T = 0$ to 17.27% at T = +2. For most of these increases t-tests of mean differences were not statistically significant. This is not a concern given the univariate nature of these statistics and the diverse nature of firms in our sample (Table 7).

## Econometric Framework and Multivariate Results

To understand the link with the impact of IT failures, we use multivariate analysis to examine whether greater market penalty contributes to greater changes (improvements) in board IT competency level. For each determinant of board IT competency, we compute the change in its level in years $T =$ +1 and $T = + 2$ following the announcement of an IT failure from its level in the most recent year prior to the announcement, $T = 0 .$ . For each metric $j = 1 , . . . , J$ and event $i = 1 , . . . , N ,$ the general form of our econometric models is as follows:

ΔBoard IT Competency metric<sub>ij</sub>\_ \_ =

$$
\begin{array}{l} \alpha_ {j} + \beta_ {j} C A R _ {i} + \gamma_ {j} I T \_ I n t e n s i t y _ {i} + \delta_ {j} C A R _ {j} \times \\ I T \_ I n t e n s i t y _ {i} + \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} n _ {j k} C o r p o r a t e G o v e r n a n c e _ {k i} \\ + \sum_ {m = 1} ^ {M} \tau_ {j m} F i r m C h a r a c t e r i s t i c _ {m i} + \varepsilon_ {i} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

The first covariate, CAR, is included to test whether the magnitude of adverse market reaction to IT failures influences changes (improvements) in board IT competency. We add the interaction term CAR × IT\_Intensity to capture any potentially moderating effect for IT intensive firms, as per our hypothesis H3. Note that, although IT\_Committee was a component of the ΔBoard\_IT\_Competency\_Score variable, we were unable to perform the regression model for the change in $I T _ { - }$ Committee variable since only a very small subsample of firms established such a committee during the two years following IT failures. We used OLS models for ΔBoard\_IT\_ Competency\_Score, ΔBoard\_ITCapital, and ΔExecMgmt\_ ITCapital and a Logit model for ΔCIO\_CTO\_OnBoard.<sup>20</sup>

Models 2(d)–(f) and 3(d)–(f) show no conditional effect on change in IT capital of independent directors or of internal (executive) directors, respectively. This means that hypothesis H1 is not supported; a change in overall board IT competency is observed only when IT intensity is accounted for.

We can summarize the main results as follows. First, there is no increase in IT capital of independent directors on boards as a function of the market reaction to operational IT failures or firms’ IT intensity level. Second, there is an increase in IT capital of internal (executive) directors as a function of the market reaction to IT failures, independent of firms’ IT intensity. Third, on average there is a weak increase in turnover of CIOs on the board as a function of the market reaction alone, and the increase is greater for firms with low to moderate IT intensity. Fourth, an increase in the aggregate board IT competency score is seen only for firms with low to moderate IT intensity.

## Endogeneity and Other Robustness Tests

We next check for endogeneity, a common concern in corporate governance research (Wintoki et al. 2012), and run additional robustness tests. For brevity, we report in Table 10 results only for the change in aggregate board IT competency level (Board\_IT\_Competency\_Score); results for changes in individual determinants of board IT competency level are similar to those in Table 9.

Endogeneity generally applies when an independent variable in Equation (1) is a choice variable that is correlated with unobservable variables relegated to the error term. One source of endogeneity is the possibility of reverse causality (simultaneity), where an explanatory variable and the dependent variable influence one another. A common approach to ameliorating this concern is to use lagged explanatory variables (Reed 2015). We already do this in all our models (Table 9) since all independent variables are lagged by one or two years. It is not possible that time t (current) changes in the level of board IT competency influence time t-1 or t-2 (past) values of abnormal returns or levels of control variables.

Another source of endogeneity is an omitted variable that affects both CAR and changes in board IT competency and is, therefore, highly correlated with both CAR and the error term in Equation (1). We address this concern using a two-stage least squares (2SLS) procedure, and specifically use the control function approach since our model is nonlinear due to the interaction term with CAR (Heckman and Robb 1985; Wooldridge 2002). The instrument variable (IV) we use for CAR is the median CAR for each year in our sample period. This choice of IV follows studies that use annual variable medians to instrument a time-dependent variable (e.g., Laeven and Levine 2009; Luo et al. 2014).<sup>23</sup> The premise is that time may be another factor influencing the magnitude of investors reaction to IT failures, in line with studies that observe an increase over time in the magnitude of market reaction to announcements of IT investments (Im et al. 2001) and appointments of outside directors to boards of internet firms (Richardson and Zmud 2001). This choice of IV is supported by a high correlation between the IV and CAR in our data (correlation = 0.45, p-value = 0), satisfying the relevance of IV condition. At the same time, the annual CAR medians is an exogenous IV. In capturing the time-dependent component of CAR that varies across years, this IV is highly correlated with CAR and hopefully not so with changes in board IT competency level (i.e., it is correlated with the latter only through CAR itself). We followed the next procedure to develop the 2SLS estimator with the control function approach. In the first stage, we develop an OLS estimator of CAR using all control variables in Equation (1) and the annual median CAR (IV), and save the residuals. The IV enters this model with a statistically significant coefficient of +0.864 (p-value < 0.000), and the excluded instruments have a partial R-squared of 0.1733 and F-test statistic of 20.96 (pvalue = 0). The validity of the IV for CAR is supported by the Anderson canonical correlation likelihood ratio test (Anderson 1951) and the Cragg-Donald Wald test rejecting the under-identification null hypothesis (p-value < 0.01) (Cragg and Donald 1993). In the second stage, we add to Equation (1) the first-stage residual term as a covariate. The first-stage residual absorbs the correlation between the (noninstrumented) CAR and the second-stage residuals, such that the second-stage residuals are not correlated with CAR and other covariates and have zero correlation with the IV (not shown here). This satisfies the exogeneity of IV condition. in Model 7 in Table 10 shows that the first-stage residual term enters the second-stage model with a positive but insignificant sign, and all other coefficients hold in sign and significance. This implies that endogeneity in the original models in Table 9 is not a concern.

A related source of endogeneity is unobserved heterogeneity, whereby unobserved factors that are common to firms with some similarities affect both CAR and changes in board IT competency level. This concern is commonly addressed by including firm fixed effects and additional controls that may be driving the key results (Wintoki et al. 2012). Due to small sample size, we opted out of including the fixed effects at the firm level and instead used 2-digit SIC code fixed effects (Model 1). This should control, at least partially, for unobserved cross-industry heterogeneity that may exist between firms (Wooldridge 2002). We also include measures of industry concentration in Models 2–4. All earlier results remain qualitatively the same with coefficients of industry concentration entering our models with insignificant signs. Models 5–6 include additional IT failure type control variables that could potentially explain our results, to see if the significance of the CAR variable drops. As seen in Table 10, our earlier results from Table 9 still hold in the presence of these additional controls.

An additional source of endogeneity is changes in the board IT competency variable that are a function of past levels of this same variable. Following Wintoki et al. (2012) we treat for this possibility by adding to the model a lagged variable of board IT competency score for the year preceding the IT failure. The lagged Board\_IT\_Competency\_Score enters the model (Model 9, Table 10) with a negative sign (p-value = 0.068), suggesting that firms with an already high board IT competency level are predicted to make smaller changes in their board IT competency. Most importantly, the signs and significance of CAR, the interaction variable, and all controls remain robust to this model specification, thus confirming our earlier findings.

We also control for potential sample selection bias that arises when the dependent variable is observed only for a restricted, nonrandom sample; it could be that our sample may be selfselected in the sense that the probability of experiencing IT failures is not random.<sup>24</sup> Therefore, we use Heckman’s (1979) two-stage estimation. In the first stage, a Probit model is produced for the universe of all publicly traded firms with the same 2-digit SIC codes as our sample firms and for which all data needed to estimate the likelihood of IT failure are available (a total of 60,254 firm-month observations during 1989–2010). In the Probit model, we use the common control variables in Equation (1) and four additional variables: systematic risk (FirmBeta) and leverage (FirmLeverage) (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2003), as well as profitability (ROA) and credit rating (Rating) (Chernobai et al. 2011). We find that the probability of IT failure is positively linked to beta and negatively linked to leverage, ROA (insignificant), and credit rating, with an R-square of 19%. This step produces a bias correction factor, termed inverse Mills ratio. In the second stage, the inverse Mills ratio is included as an additional control in the main model (Equation (1)). The inverse Mills ratio enters our main model in the second stage with a positive but insignificant sign (Model 8, Table 10), and all remaining coefficients have the same magnitude and significance as in the original specification (Table 9). This result alleviates concerns over endogeneity due to selection bias.

Additional robustness tests were performed to further validate our main results. We experimented with PCA-based measure of change in board IT competency level (Model 10, Table 10). We also ran models with additional controls, including firm age, variables differentiating between internal and external IT failures (results unreported). All the tests performed do not reject the validity of our main findings in Table 9.

## Discussion

We proceed to interpret the main results of our study, discuss their implications for research and practice, and summarize their key limitations and directions for future research.

## Main Results

In light of the gap we identified in the operational IT failures and ITG literatures, this paper postulates and empirically tests the existence of a relationship between the stock market reaction to operational IT failures and changes in the level of board IT competency.

The results of our study build on prior studies on the effects of operational IT failures on shareholder wealth (Benaroch et al. 2012; Bharadwaj et al. 2009; Goldstein et al. 2011). For an average firm in our sample, the announcement of an IT failure translates into an average market value loss of about \$1 billion during a 4-day period surrounding the press-cutting date, an economically substantial figure.<sup>25</sup> This dramatic loss in shareholder wealth suggests that market investors do not perceive IT failures to be temporary glitches but rather manifestations of underlying IT deficiencies that jeopardize the firm’s long-run earnings capacity (Bharadwaj et al. 2009). Some evidence for such long-lasting effects on firms is offered in Figure 3: (1) the average cumulative abnormal stock returns for our sample of 110 IT failure announcements remains negative for over three months post announcement, implying a long-lasting increase in the cost of equity capital, and (2) the cost of debt increases and stays higher for over four months, as seen by daily abnormal changes in credit default swap spreads for a subsample of 60 IT failure announcements.<sup>26</sup> Of course, since both these long-term measures may conflate the impact of other events that occurred post IT failure announcements, future research should study these measures more carefully.

Our overall results show that, in firms that experience operational IT failures, the level of board IT competency undergoes changes proportional to the negative market reaction to operational IT failures. This finding validates suggestions of how firms should respond to IT failures (Bharadwaj et al. 2009, p. 75):

The negative market reaction to IT failures should help managers make the case for organizational changes needed to improve the reliability of existing IT systems … [and] should also provide motivation for boards and executives to ensure that corporate IS/IT strategies and related IT governance practices … are appropriate, proactive and effective in minimizing the occurrence of IT failures.

If boards of directors truly aim at maximizing shareholder wealth, then, when shareholders’ trust is eroded by operational IT failures, the changes in board IT competency level should aim at regaining this trust and should be commensurate with the drop in market value. Our results specifically show that firms with operational IT failures experience improvements in select determinants of board IT competency. The improvements are dominated by increases in the ratio of internal (executive) directors with IT experience (capital) and by turnover of (presumably underperforming) CIO/CTOs on the board. This result is consistent with the agency problem and the managerial entrenchment theory (Fama and Jensen 1983; Shleifer and Vishny 1997), which postulate that executives are the first to be dismissed or see their compensation packages changed when faced with their firms’ failures. This result also reinforces Bharadwaj et al.’s (2009) call for execu tives to be more aware of investors’ weariness of unexpected IT failures and to take a more proactive stance in business continuity risk analysis and disaster recovery procedures necessary for minimizing the (re)occurrence of IT failures.

With this said, a surprising aspect of our results is the lack of increase in IT capital of independent directors. We can think of two plausible explanations. One explanation is that turnover of independent directors is constrained by board size. An important role of these directors is to offer expertise and counsel in specific functional areas. For example, accounting research finds that adverse financial reporting events are followed by appointments of independent directors with the financial expertise necessary for remediation (Farber 2005; Johnstone et al. 2011; Li et al. 2010). However, because boards are limited in size, changes in the board composition following operational IT failures may be constrained by a higher demand for independent directors with expertise in other areas than IT. Another plausible explanation relates to the notion of “scapegoating.” As alluded to in our development of the second hypothesis: “[because] the directors have the ability to make changes in the management team …, the managers may become scapegoats” (Walsh and Seward 1990, p. 426). This phenomenon has support in studies reporting that, following financial restatements, firms are more likely to dismiss their CEO and senior executives than independent directors (Arthaud-Day et al. 2006; Gangloff et al. 2014). Of course, this possibility raises a serious concern about the efficacy of independent directors as an ultimate oversight and ITG monitoring body. We leave the examination of this interesting observation to future empirical research.

Also surprising in our results is the lack of change in the number of board-level IT committees; only one company in our sample added a single committee after experiencing an IT failure. It could be that boards sensitize their existing committees to IT issues rather than add new board-level IT committees. That is, some companies may simply expand the mandate of existing board-level committees and/or assign to those committees more directors with IT experience (capital). Accounting for this possibility is another interesting issue for future research to explore.

In addition, we find that the propensity to replace CIO/CTOs serving on the board as a function of the adverse wealth effect of IT failures is negatively moderated by the firm’s IT intensity, in line with our hypothesis that top management turnover is disruptive to the business. Interestingly, however, for firms of high IT intensity, a more negative wealth effect of IT failures is actually associated with a smaller likelihood of replacing CIO/CTOs on the board. This latter result may suggest that, beyond the arguments we put forth in support of hypothesis H3, the lower likelihood of CIO/CTO turnover in more IT intensive firms is also a product of avoiding a perception of scapegoating. Scapegoating is said to “placate external demands for action without challenging the strategic status quo,” to “communicate to those demanding action (i.e., shareholders) that fault for prior wrongdoing resided squarely with a single individual and did not permeate the rest of the organization,” and to also “send a signal to investors that the firm is unwilling to admit the presence of systemic problems and is content with maintaining established procedures” (Gangloff et al. 2016, pp. 1617-1618). In effect, though, this is exactly the perception that IT intensive firms may choose to avoid. More IT intensive firms may instead want to communicate that CIO/CTOs serving on the board are retained since their familiarity with firm-specific IT resources is crucial to efforts to remediate problems at the root of IT failures. It is worth noting that in our sample the average tenure of CIOs in IT intensive (above median values) firms is approximately 30 percent longer (significant at the 5% level)

than that in less IT intensive (below median values) firms.<sup>27</sup> We hope that future research would shed more light on the connection between operational IT failures and CIO turnover.

## Implications for Practice and Research

The links that our study finds between operational IT failures and changes in the board IT competency level have notable implications for research and practice. Perhaps the most basic implication is the attention that should be devoted to operational IT failures. Our findings echo accounting research findings on the types of adverse events that firms and equity markets are known to care about, including frauds and financial restatements. The impact of operational IT failures we study here appears to be of a comparable caliber. Another implication for practice is the need to react strategically to the occurrence of IT failures. Upon experiencing IT failures, firms ought to improve their board IT competency level as a way to communicate to the market their serious intent to improve monitoring and control over IT resources, to remediate underlying IT deficiencies, and to prevent future operational IT failures. Accounting research indicates that this signaling strategy is beneficial for regaining investors’ trust: the market views governance changes aimed at remediation as positive signals, as evidenced by improved stock performance (Farber 2005) and decreased cost of equity (Ashbaugh-Skaife et al. 2009). Our data supports this view, at least for the short term. Specifically, we run an event study for a subsample of 31 IT failures for which we found dated public announcements of replacing CIOs on the board and of appointing board directors with IT experience in the two years post IT failures. We found a strong positive increase in stock returns: on day 0, CAR = +1.28%, significant at 1% level.

For research, a central implication is on the study of operational IT failures, an area that deserves more attention in the IT literature. The connection we find with board IT competency level highlights another important dimension along which to study IT failures. Our results present evidence that market investors consider the board of directors to be a principal ITG monitoring body, consistent with the view expressed by leading IT voices (Nolan and McFarlan 2005). This is significant, given the documented IT knowledge and attention deficit at the board level (Huff et al. 2006; Jordan and Musson 2004; PWC 2006); even our data shows that some firms do not have a single board member with IT background or experience (Table 7). However, while the changes in board IT competency level that we documented post IT failures aim to combat this IT knowledge and attention deficit, it is still difficult to tell whether or not such changes have the intended effect of reestablishing firms’ long-term reputation in the eyes of market investors. In this respect, studying the short- and long-term benefits and costs of specific improvements in determinants of board IT competency is another important issue for future research to consider.

The plausibility of a connection between board IT competency level prior to the occurrence of operational IT failures and the severity of market reaction to these failures is another important implication for both research and practice. Could it be that market investors perceive a higher likelihood of IT failures in firms with low board IT competency levels and, therefore, build this expectation into equity values? If so, would the market be more surprised by IT failures in firms with more IT competent boards and, therefore, adjust more negatively the ex ante expectations built into equity prices? Future research is needed to spell out the rationale, and examine whether variations in firms’ ex ante board IT competency levels explain cross-sectional variations in abnormal stock returns surrounding IT failures. Such research could provide the basis for a holistic theory of a reciprocal relationship between operational IT failures and boards’ IT competency level.

## Limitations and Future Research

Other implications for research relate to limitations of our study. One limitation is that our study does not examine whether changes to the board that improve its IT competency are associated with an improvement in firms’ future stock returns. In other words, it is not clear if firms that improve their boards’ IT competency level experience a reversal of the decrease in stock returns observed upon the occurrence of IT operational failures. As we said above, our preliminary examination of a small subsample of IT failures, for which we found dated public announcements of post-failure board changes, finds an eventual increase in stock returns. While answering this question fully is beyond the scope of our study, it is central to the study of board-level ITG in connection with operational IT failures and more broadly with IT value-destruction. We call on future research to address this question.

Other limitations of this study relate to our IT failures sample. First, since existing regulations do not mandate firms to report their operational IT failures, our data sample contains only IT failures that have been publicly disclosed due to their larger magnitude and/or their occurrence in companies that are subject to greater media scrutiny (e.g., larger firms). Not having data also on nonpublicly disclosed IT failures that firms experienced precludes us from performing a matched sample analysis of firms with and without operational IT failures, and thus limits our ability to examine the likelihood of improvements in board IT competency level subsequent to the occurrence of IT failures. Ideally, the matched samples methodology would have allowed us to include in our analysis firms that have not experienced operational IT failures and, therefore, to also effectively control for changes in board IT competency level that are not linked to the occurrence of IT failures. Future research seeking to address this limitation could explore additional methods for collecting and analyzing data on operational IT failures.

A second limitation of our sample is its focus on IT failures in financial services firms. Due to heavy government regulations, boards of directors may have different, more limited roles in financial firms than in non-financial firms (Yermack 1996). For example, Demsetz and Lehn (1985) showed that the ownership structure is less concentrated in financial, utilities, and other regulated sectors. Minton et al. (2014) found that the ratio of independent directors with financial expertise is positively and significantly related to firm risk, a result not supported for firms outside of the financial sector. Consistent with such unique characteristics of financial firms, our focus on such firms might explain why changes in board IT committees, as a board ITG mechanism, do not seem to have a link with operational IT failures in our sample. It has been suggested that financial firms are frequently perceived to engage in new risk management initiatives (e.g., establishing a risk committee) merely to satisfy regulatory compliance, without giving them real power (Ellul and Yerramilli 2013). In this light, future research ought to generalize our results using data spanning a wider range of industries.

Finally, our study does not control for the possibility that firms make changes in board IT competency not only in response to their own operational IT failures but also to rival firms’ IT failures. Rival firms’ IT failures could potentially force other firms to reexamine and change their boards, if necessary, thus creating an industry-wide domino effect. Some limited research has shown that adverse events, such as cybersecurity breaches, that occur in some firms could have an intra-industry spillover effect to rival firms (Aytes et al. 2006; Ettredge and Richardson 2003; Jorion and Zhang 2007).

In conclusion, our study is first to explore the link between operational IT failures and subsequent changes in the IT competency level of boards. We are hopeful that our theoretical framing and empirical findings would jump-start scholarly and industry dialogues and foster follow-up research on the interplay between ITG at the board level and IT valuedestruction due to operational IT failures.

## Appendix A

## Operational IT Failures Classification

Following Goldstein et al. (2011), we classify operational IT failures into data- and function-related IT failures. Data-related IT failures are those that result in disclosure of confidential data assets to unauthorized parties, misuse of data assets, or destruction of data assets. Functionrelated IT failures are those that result in loss of availability, or are the result of the mis-operation, of functional IT assets responsible for the handling of data assets.

Consistent with Benaroch et al. (2012), we define operational IT failure as any threat to the integrity, confidentiality, or availability of data assets or IT assets (software, hardware, networks, users, system operators, etc.) responsible for the creation, storage, processing, transport, and safeguarding of data assets

Confidentiality events are violations of the assurance that data and IT assets are shared only among authorized persons, systems, or organizations. These events include intentional incidents, such as phishing and hacker attacks on sensitive data (e.g., trade secrets, customer data), misuse of access codes, emailing of confidential data by unauthorized internal personnel, theft of proprietary source code; and unintentional data leakage incidents, such as loss of notebooks with sensitive data by an employee, erroneous posting of customer data on the firm’s website, and vendor loss of data in transport.

Integrity events are violations of the assurance that data and IT assets, including information flows, are authentic (i.e., genuine and trustworthy) and correct (i.e., preserved without corruption). Examples of Integrity events include erroneous updates of customer accounts by buggy software, user mistyping of a social security number, execution of unauthorized trades by external hackers, transactions settled at incorrect prices because of an erroneous data feed, ATM network malfunction due to a software bug, executing an incorrect trade due to a trader’s keystroke error, accidental or malicious deletion or modification of important data or programs by computer viruses or worms, and defacing of a company’s website by hackers.

Availability events are violations of the assurance that data and IT assets are delivered on a timely basis to those who need them. Examples include denial-of-service attacks and viruses that reproduce to overwhelm network bandwidth and email servers); unforeseen or accidental causes such as technical problems (e.g., hardware malfunctions, network outages, power failures, system crashes, and ISP problems that prevent a website from receiving customer e-orders); and, natural phenomena (e.g., floods and earthquakes), and human errors (e.g., operator errors).
