# Technological Frames, Organizational Capabilities, and IT Use: An Empirical Investigation of Electronic Procurement

Abhay Nath Mishra

J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, Georgia 30303, amishra@gsu.edu

Ritu Agarwal

Robert H. Smith School of Business, University of Maryland, College Park, Maryland 20742, ragarwal@rhsmith.umd.edu

The process by which organizations incorporate technological innovations into existing routines and use them on a regular basis persists as a central concern in the literature. Although we now have a fairly robust understanding of the drivers of innovation adoption, the use of innovations is less understood. In this paper, we draw on two streams of literature, managerial and organizational sensemaking, and organizational capabilities that have hitherto been used independently, to investigate organizational use of information technology (IT)-based innovations. Building on and extending prior work, we posit that organizational capabilities serve as complements to managers' technological frames related to an innovation. We focus on the use of an important technological innovation—business-to-business (B2B) electronic markets for procurement. We examine interactions between three technological frames—benefits frame, threat frame, and adjustment frame, and two organizational capabilities—technological opportunism and technological sophistication, and their relationship with the use of B2B electronic markets in firms. We test our research model using survey data collected from 292 firms. Results largely support the proposed conceptualization and shed new light on the key factors associated with firms' use of B2B electronic markets. Theoretical and practical implications of the findings are discussed.

<sup>Key</sup> <sup>words</sup>: electronic procurement; B2B electronic markets; technological frames; benefits frame; threat frame; adjustment frame; organizational capabilities; technological opportunism; technological sophistication; sensemaking

## 1. Introduction

The significance of innovation adoption for firms is well documented and the process by which innovations are assimilated into organizations persists as a central concern in the information systems (IS) and strategic management literatures (Damanpour 1991, Lewis et al. 2003, Malhotra et al. 2001, Rogers 1995, Swanson 1994). This literature acknowledges that information technology (IT)-based innovations offer the potential to substantially change and improve existing firm processes and routines, thereby conferring significant advantage on firms that can exploit them. Therefore, not surprisingly, considerable scholarly research has focused on understanding the adoption of technological innovations in organizations (see Fichman 2000 for a detailed review of the literature).

Although we now have a reasonably robust understanding of the determinants of organizational adoption of innovations, the decision to acquire a new technology is only the first step in a complex process related to firms' sustained use of innovations. There is increasing realization among researchers that the extent and nature of post-adoption use of IS influences their organizational impacts (Barua et al. 2004, Gallivan 2001). It has been noted that although firms adopt IT-based innovations, they often fail to successfully assimilate them (Chatterjee et al. 2002,

Fichman and Kemerer 1997, Liang et al. 2007). Surprisingly, compared to the breadth and depth of literature examining IT adoption, research related to IT use is sparse (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Gallivan 2001, Jasperson et al. 2005, Zhu and Kraemer 2005). To the extent that the value creation potential of innovations is realized only when firms can progress beyond the initial adoption stage, it is clearly useful to examine post-adoption stages of the innovation cycle.

In response to recent calls in the literature for researchers to focus their investigations on the <sup>use</sup> of IT innovations (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Barki et al. 2007, Jasperson et al. 2005), we extend the limited research that has specifically paid attention to later stages of the innovation cycle by drawing upon two streams of literature. One stream, related to managerial and organizational sensemaking, argues for the salience of cognitive schemas or “technological frames” (Davidson 2002, Orlikowski and Gash 1994) in behaviors with new information technologies. These frames represent individuals’ “definitions of organizational reality that serve as vehicles for understanding and action” (Gioia 1986, p. 50). Thus, frames symbolize what the innovation represents for organizational members and drive their use behaviors.

An alternate stream of work investigating technological innovations posits that firm capabilities are an important influence on use behaviors (Cohen and Levinthal 1990, Srinivasan et al. 2002, Zahra and George 2002). Recently, the set of capabilities relevant to innovation has been extended to include technological opportunism, which reflects a firm's propensity to respond with agility to signals from the environment (Srinivasan et al. 2002). Firms high in this capability scan their environment for emerging technologies and respond proactively to exploit, avoid, or ignore potential opportunities and threats. A second firm capability that is frequently related to innovation behaviors is technological sophistication. It reflects the extent to which a firm is already using IT in value-adding ways. To the degree that there is path dependence in a firm's use of IT-based innovations and such innovations require significant knowledge barriers to be overcome (Attewell 1992), technological sophistication plays a key role in subsequent innovation behaviors.

Building on prior research that underscores the role of complementary assets (Tripsas 1997), we theorize that firm capabilities in the form of technological opportunism and technological sophistication serve as complements to managers' technological frames related to an innovation. We examine the moderating influence of these capabilities on the relationship between three technological frames, benefits, threat and adjustment, and the use behavior of firms. The empirical context for our work is firms’ use of <sup>pub-</sup> lic business-to-business (B2B) electronic markets1 in the procurement process, and we test our model using firm-level data from 292 manufacturing firms in the United States.

It has been suggested that by enabling global connectivity, comparisons of offers across multiple suppliers, and reduced transaction costs, public B2B markets can engender radical changes such as altering internal processes, modifying industry structure, and creating hitherto untapped economic value. Because of the technological differences of these markets in comparison to both non-Internet-based and other Internet-based procurement technologies, the scope of changes necessitated by them, and their potential impact on market structure and buyer-supplier relationships, these markets epitomize a significant innovation in the procurement process, and the task of developing them to integrate and assimilate in organizational procurement process continues even today (Yoo et al. 2007). While there are anecdotal accounts of the emergence, rapid growth, relative decline, and resurgence of B2B markets, these markets have still not become a routine part of organizations, and constitute an innovation for the procurement process several years after their introduction. Indeed, the extant research has a rich tradition of treating electronic marketplaces as novel and innovative (Driedonks et al 2005, Kambil and Van Heck 2002). A white paper by Forrester Research (Bartels 2007) reports that as recently as September 2006, 23% of North American large enterprises (1,000 to 4,999 employees), 29% of very large enterprises (5,000 to 19,999 employees), and 36% of North American Global 2000 enterprises (20,000 and more employees) were <sup>not</sup> using eProcurement, yet, in the research literature, a deep understanding of organizational use of B2B markets is lacking. Much of the prior work on B2B markets has focused on developing conceptual analyses (Malone et al. 1987), reporting findings from case studies (Lee and Clark 1996, Driedonks et al. 2005), and developing stylized economic models (Yoo et al. 2007, Zhu 2004).

The research reported here makes four key theoretical contributions to our understanding of the innovation process. First, our focus is on an important post-adoption behavior, innovation <sup>use</sup>, which has been less studied in the existing literature. Second we extend the discourse on technological frames and their impact on IT use. Extant research on frames has focused almost exclusively on establishing frame incongruence—a lack of alignment in the frames held by different constituents—and demonstrating its adverse impacts on IT use (Barrett 1999, Lin and Cornford 2000, Orlikowski and Gash 1994). However, Davidson and Pai (2004) note that merely establishing that different groups think differently about IT and that such differences may cause problems may not be insightful anymore, and urge researchers to advance to other topics of theoretical interest. Third, we theorize about and test for moderation effects, illustrating that the nature of relationship between technological frames and firm capabilities, and IT innovation use is more nuanced than what researchers have explored till now. The need for theorizing and testing interactions between organizational belief structures and firm capabilities has been suggested in the literature but rarely attempted (Chattopadhyay et al. 2001, Langlois 1994). Finally, we introduce the construct of technological opportunism to the IS literature. We believe that this construct can enrich many models of firm IT use

## 2. Theoretical Background and Literature Review

## 2.1. IT Use in Organizations

In spite of more than two decades of research examining IT innovations, literature investigating organizational use of IT remains sparse (Zhu and Kraemer 2005) and our collective understanding of IT use is still limited (Barki et al. 2007, Liang et al. 2007). Prior literature has frequently conceptualized the innovation process as a series of stages and identified the sequences of events that occur during these stages. A number of stage models have been proposed and discussed to obtain a fine-grained understanding of innovation behavior (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Cooper and Zmud 1990, Rogers 1995). The extant research reiterates that the innovation process does not stop after adoption, but rather that adoption is the first stage in the process. In recent work, Jasperson et al. (2005) identify three high-level stages in the IT innovation process—pre-adoption activities, the adoption decision, and post-adoption activities such as IT use. Following this conceptualization, we examine organizational <sup>use</sup> of B2B markets as a key post-adoption activity.

IT innovation use has been conceptualized in multiple ways in the literature. A comprehensive review and synthesis of this literature suggests that while researchers’ views of IT use differ in definition and scope, there are some similarities in the myriad of conceptualizations proposed (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, DeLone and McLean 1992, Lassila and Brancheau 1999, Purvis et al. 2001, Rai et al. 2002). A defining feature of IT innovation use is that it must <sub>reflect</sub> <sub>(and</sub> <sub>measure)</sub> significant application of a technology to a business process to accomplish a task ingly, we adopt this conceptualization of IT use in our research.

## 2.2. Sensemaking, Technological Frames, and Organizational Action

A rich literature in managerial and organizational cognition and sensemaking suggests that cognitive frameworks, also referred to as mental models, knowledge structures, scripts, and frames are closely tied to how individuals make sense of and act within their environment (Abelson 1981, Gioia 1986). These frameworks provide the template for understanding and problem solving, and affect the stimuli noticed by individuals, their interpretation and subsequent actions (Elsbach et al. 2005, Priem 1994, Walsh 1995). Furthermore, the cognitive structures developed by key managers about a given concept, type of stimulus, or an issue affect the subsequent organizational action related to it (Chattopadhyay et al. 2001, Elsbach et al. 2005).

Technological frames refer to a subset of these structures that concern the assumptions, expectations, and knowledge that managers use to understand technology use in their organization (Orlikowski and Gash 1991, 1994). Orlikowski and Gash (1994) identify three frames, derived through interviews in a specific context—the nature of technology, technology strategy, and technology in use, and find that these significantly influence the way managers make sense of and use IT. While Orlikowski and Gash and others use qualitative techniques to elicit technological frames (Barrett 1999, Davidson 2002, Lin and Cornford 2000), mixed methods, such as multidimensional scaling and cognitive mapping, have been used to assess cognitive knowledge structures, of which frames are a subset (see Elsbach et al. 2005, Tan and Hunter 2002, Walsh 1995). In fact, Weick (1990) argues that methods such as systematic coding of documents, coding of verbatim transcripts of private meetings, and questionnaires and interviews can be used to assess cognitive structures. Some prior work has used a quantitative approach to assess such structures (Priem 1994), while others have used a combined qualitative and quantitative approach (Ramiller and Swanson 2003, Sahay et al. 1994).

Within the past decade, the notion of individual cognitive structure has been extended to collectives such as groups, firms, networks, and industries (Davidson and Pai 2004, Orlikowski and Gash 1994, Walsh 1995, Weick 1995). The existence of such supraindividual cognitive structures or schemas is predicated on the belief that groups of individuals can have knowledge about issues in a way that transcends the cognitive facilities of any one of them, and that collective ways of thinking and acting have a reality outside of the individual. Given our interest in understanding organizational (as opposed to individual) behavior, we focus our review on frames and sensemaking at the organizational level.

Weick (1995) suggests that sensemaking is a critical organizational activity. He further asserts that an organization can be interpreted as a social system that invents and interprets its environment through explanations and accounts of the sensed features. In other words, organizations are organized through acts of sensemaking. The process of organizational sensemaking entails iterative sensegiving and sensemaking (Gioia and Chittipeddi 1991); however, our focus is not on how cognitive structures evolve. Rather, we examine their effects on downstream outcomes in the innovation process.

Although Weick’s (1995) research on sensemaking has been very influential, one of the criticisms leveled against it is that it has focused almost exclusively on retrospective sensemaking (Gioia and Mehra 1996). However, the assumption that interpretations are made after situations are experienced and managers have had time to reflect may not always be justified. Gioia et al. (2002, p. 622) see making sense of the future as possible and as a task that requires an ability to envision the future as if it has already occurred. Theoretical development around prospective sensemaking constitutes a significant opportunity to extend the literature in sensemaking (Wright 2005). The notion of prospective sensemaking is also implied in the literature that suggests that managers and firms form cognitive structures in response to events in the environment and use these structures to take subsequent actions (Elsbach et al. 2005, Orlikowski and Gash 1994, Priem 1994).

## 2.3. Organizational Capabilities and Innovation

A stream of literature that has emerged parallel to research in sensemaking, and hitherto not been integrated with it, is the research examining innovation use using an organizational capability lens. Capabilities are a complex bundle of a firm's abilities, skills, and accumulated knowledge that enable it to repeatedly perform tasks that create value (Day 1994, Grant 1996). These capabilities influence innovation significantly; enable firms to identify new ideas, products, processes and opportunities; assimilate them widely and successfully; and obtain value from them. Capabilities are firm specific and deeply embedded within a firm, unlike many resources that may be more transferable (Barua et al. 2004). Thus, firms are likely to have heterogeneous endowments of capabilities to combine, coordinate, and exploit resources needed to innovate.

A number of firm-level capabilities have been shown to impact innovation, including innovativeness, technological orientation, and market orientation. These capabilities influence innovation, respectively, by enabling firms to develop products and services frequently; facilitating the use of advanced technologies in product development; and gathering and exploiting market intelligence about customer needs (Gatignon and Xuereb 1997, Kohli et al. 1993). Furthermore, Srinivasan et al. (2002) have identified technological opportunism—a sense and respond capability of firms with respect to new technologies—as a significant determinant of innovation adoption in firms. They argue that this capability allows firms to collect information about technological changes in the environment and respond to these changes quickly. Armed with better information, such firms can identify opportunities and threats associated with technologies. Srinivasan et al. (2002) show that technological opportunism is conceptually different from other organizational innovation traits and that it provides a significant incremental explanation, over and above other variables, of the adoption of e-business.

Additionally, researchers in IS posit that the resources, skills, and capabilities of a firm to leverage innovative IT influence their use (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999). Given its inherent complexity, successful assimilation of IT places significant demands on firms to understand technological nuances and to make necessary adjustments, so that IT may be deployed productively in a business context. Firms with sophisticated IT infrastructure and prior IT experience can build on preexisting resources and capabilities and meet these demands more easily. These firms can leverage their infrastructure and experience, and develop higher levels of technical knowledge, which aids IT innovation and assimilation (Damanpour 1991).

## 2.4. Synopsis of the Literature

As the review above illustrates, the literature on sensemaking and technological frames and organizational capabilities have each contributed significantly to our understanding of organizational adoption and IT use. However, researchers have drawn upon these literature streams largely independently, and findings obtained in one stream have been ignored in the other We suggest that significant new insights may be available about IT use through a synthesis of both streams, and offer such an integrated lens.

Theoretical justification for such a synthesis is implicit in prior work. For instance, Langlois (1994) notes that a firm’s repertoire of routines not only determines what tasks it can perform well but also conditions how it will recognize and interpret messages and stimuli. Likewise, building on the concept of organizational routines, Teece et al. (1997) suggest that capabilities influence a firm’s perceptual ability to recognize ways to improve competences and to identify opportunities to apply them. Thus, organizational innovation should be guided by both cognitive structures and self-assessed capabilities. Researchers have examined the separate effects of executives’ interpretation of the environment and organizational capabilities on firm actions, but better understanding of models that combine these factors is needed (Chattopadhyay et al. 2001, Langlois 1994). Yet, the relationship between a firm’s capabilities, the cognitive structures it develops, and their joint impact on innovative IT use has not been examined in detail. In this paper, we attempt to fill these gaps.

Two approaches have been suggested for identifying frames germane to a specific context: (1) researchers can establish a certain number of categories of frames and examine if there is support for their categorization empirically or (2) they can let these categories emerge in situ (Tan and Hunter 2002). While the first approach has the advantage that theoretical arguments can be invoked to establish frames (Tan and Hunter 2002), the latter approach has been argued to be more grounded in reality and managerial perceptions (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). However, both approaches have been used in the literature.

We chose the first approach in our study, and performed an additional qualitative validation step with knowledgeable managers in the domain. Prior theorizing and early identification of study constructs has been identified as an important step (Eisenhardt 1989). The literature has theorized about two key frames through which managers assess innovations and explore their choice landscape—opportunity or benefits and threats (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005, Chattopadhyay et al. 2001, Dutton and Jackson 1987). The former suggests a situation where there is a high potential for gain, while the latter represents a situation where there is a high potential for loss. Perceived threat causes wishful thinking, reliance on fate and restriction of the amount of information attended to, and the solutions considered (Jackson and Dutton 1988). Furthermore, to be used productively, innovations often necessitate significant changes in the embedding context comprised of firm processes and routines (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000), and perceptions about the magnitude of these changes can have an impact on the use of IT innovations (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). To verify the existence and salience of these frames, we conducted five semi-structured interviews with procurement managers in a large state in southwest United States. All interviews were conducted on-site, lasted between one and two hours, and were focused on understanding the frames firms employ to use B2B markets. We sought to both corroborate the existence of the benefits, threat, and adjustment frames, and to ascertain if any other coherent cognitive structure was evident in the managers’ views about B2B markets. Interviewees were not willing to have the conversations recorded, but extensive field notes were taken. The analysis of field notes confirmed that these frames collectively captured the gamut of views expressed, and did not reveal other significant cognitions guiding these managers.<sup>2</sup> As suggested by Orlikowski and Gash (1994), it is likely that the frames held by these managers were different from those of the CIO or managers in the IT division, however, because of their close association with and proximity to the procurement process, the frames held by procurement professionals are likely to be most closely related to actual use.

Because the technological frames literature has a spirit of interpretative research, it is important to note that we adopt a positivist epistemology. Earlier studies used qualitative methods almost exclusively to elicit frames. However, as noted before, a plurality of approaches is not only possible, but also desirable to extend the literature. This epistemological shift is consistent with the suggestions of scholars who underscore the importance of using a plurality of methods such as variance and process strategies, qualitative and quantitative techniques and positivistic and interpretive methods (Lee 1991, Weber 2004 Mingers 2001). In a similar spirit, our approach complements and extends existing interpretive work in technological frames.

As shown in Figure 1, we assert that two capabilities are likely to moderate the relationship between technological frames and the extent of B2B markets use. The first, technological opportunism, enables organizations to notice opportunities in the environment and exploit, avoid, or ignore them. The second, technological sophistication, enables organizations to exploit internal skills and resources to assimilate information technologies toward productive ends. ## 3.1. The Benefits Frame

The benefits frame represents the degree to which a firm perceives the potential of B2B markets to add value to the procurement process and expand the repertoire of organizational action. Perceptions about potential benefits from an innovation provide the motivation and impetus to use it (Gatignon and Robertson 1989). These perceptions also lower uncertainties related to the innovation (Rogers 1995), and increase the possibility of allocation of managerial financial, and technological resources necessary for its use. Firms that understand and realize the potential of the technology also realize the need to use it fully and integrate it with existing applications to realize the benefits (Premkumar et al. 1994). Firms that perceive higher benefits are also more likely to experiment with the technology and find innovative applications for it (Iacovou et al. 1995). Such beliefs, reflecting the instrumental outcomes that an innovation has the potential to offer, have been found to be among the most robust predictors of the adoption and use of technological innovations in organizations (Chwelos et al. 2001, Iacovou et al. 1995).

In the context of procurement, our interviews revealed that the use of B2B markets can provide extensive operational and strategic benefits. Operational benefits include possible savings from reduced product and supplier search, lower material costs, and reduced paperwork and manual interventions. B2B markets also enable access to improved information that firms can use to make better decisions in the procurement process, increasing the efficiency of procurement operations. In addition, B2B markets can provide strategic benefits by allowing firms to reengineer and streamline business processes. Sensing such benefits, a large number of firms such as Corning, Apple, 3M, Ford, and Northrop Grumman have used B2B markets to purchase products (Schonfeld and Morrison 2007). We expect the benefits frame to be positively related to the extent of B2B markets use in firms.

## 3.2. The Threat Frame

Threats exemplify the vulnerabilities and potential losses to which a firm might be exposed as a result of using an innovation. In our context, the threat frame assesses the degree to which firms believe that the use of B2B markets may have negative consequences for the procurement process. Threat perceptions are directly related to potential losses. In fact, Jackson and Dutton (1988, p. 375) suggest that threats may involve the likelihood of loss without gain. To circumvent the possibility of incurring loss, firms that perceive an innovation as a threat are likely to eschew its adoption and use. Indeed, prior research in IS has found support for such arguments. Riggins and Mukhopadhyay (1994) argue that adoption and implementation risks associated with interorganizational systems can negatively influence electronic data interchange (EDI) adoption. Lee and Clark (1996) suggest that transaction risk caused by bounded rationality may prevent firms from using electronic markets.

Scholars have hinted at the possibility of a variety of threats for firms that procure from B2B markets (Jap and Mohr 2002, Peleg et al. 2002). Differences in objectives among buyers and suppliers, information asymmetries between different parties, and the difficulties in enforcing agreements online may result in vendors supplying lower-quality products and misusing information provided by the firm (Clemons et al. 1993), and may also affect suppliers’ delivery performance. In addition, there may be concerns about the impact of firm participation in B2B markets on its relationships with existing suppliers, that might consider the move to participate in B2B markets as threatening, and stop cooperating. The open nature of these markets also raises concerns about the safety and security of information shared between firms suppliers, and the market. The threat frame has been responsible for firms such as Dell and Wal-Mart not participating in public B2B markets. Thus we expect the threat frame to be negatively related to the extent of B2B markets use in firms.

## 3.3. The Adjustment Frame

By their very nature, innovations create disruptions in organizations. Such disruptions can range from minor adjustments to radical restructuring of work processes, governance arrangements, reward systems, and the like (Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000, Chatterjee et al. 2002). Several scholars have suggested that firms and technologies need to continuously adapt to each other (Griffith 1999, Leonard-Barton 1988). Rogers (1995) argues that such adaptations take place during the implementation stage, but others note that these adaptations are an integral part of the entire cycle of innovation adoption (Leonard-Barton 1988). In other words, regardless of the stage of the innovation process, changes to the complementary sustaining environment for the technological innovation are warranted. Indeed, such changes are inevitable when firms adopt and implement IT (Barua et al. 2004, Brynjolfsson and Hitt 2000).

Managers develop subjective assessments about the scale and scope of transformations necessary to use innovations in a sophisticated and complete manner. The adjustment frame captures such assessments. In essence, it is an evaluation of the level of adjustment required for the firm to “use” the technology effectively. For instance, managers may believe that introducing a new technology will require a realignment of incentives such that individuals are motivated to use it (Beaudry and Pinsonneault 2005). Furthermore, they may believe that IT will entail considerable redesign in terms of reporting relationships and workflow. Relatedly, they construct evaluations of the expertise required for productive use of the technology. For instance, they may perceive the innovation as complex and believe that significant instruction and training is necessary before individuals can use it (Orlikowski and Gash 1994). Thus, if the adjustment frame indicates that the firm needs to make substantial changes to use the innovation, the extent of use will be low, i.e., the adjustment frame is expected to be negatively related to the extent of B2B electronic markets use.

## 3.4. The Moderating Effects of Capabilities

We examine two key organizational capabilities: technological opportunism and technological sophistication, and their interactions with managers’ technological frames.

## 3.5. Technological Opportunism

Although opportunism is typically viewed as a negative trait in a business partner, some prior literature has identified a benign form of opportunism (Moorman and Miner 1998). Srinivasan et al. (2002) use this interpretation and apply it in the context of the adoption of electronic business. They suggest that technological opportunism is an organizational capability with two components—technology-sensing and technology-response. Technology-sensing capability is a firm’s ability to acquire knowledge about and understand technological developments in its business environment. Technology-response capability is an organization’s willingness and ability to respond to technological changes in its environment. As a capability, technological opportunism enables firms to track and be cognizant of technological innovations and the opportunities and threats they exemplify, and to respond proactively. Technologically opportunistic firms can respond in multiple ways to a technological innovation: they may choose to reengineer business processes and strategies to exploit the opportunities; ignore the technology; or simply monitor it (Srinivasan et al. 2002, p. 49). Similar to prospector firms, firms with this capability derive value from being responsive to the opportunities (or threats) in the environment and to capitalize on (or counter) them (Srinivasan et al. 2002).

Firms high in technological opportunism have the ability, skills, and accumulated knowledge to acquire information about technological opportunities, to invest resources to exploit them, and to act proactively to innovate on a consistent basis. The entrepreneurial nature of technologically opportunistic firms makes it more likely that they wil experiment with B2B markets, invest resources to exploit their potential when such markets are perceived as beneficial, and use them to a large extent to enable efficient procurement. Simply put, technological opportunism will reinforce the expectations resulting from perceived benefits to guide the choice of plausible interpretations and actions. Thus the positive relationship between the benefits frame and B2B markets use is likely to be amplified in the presence of technological opportunism.

Hypothesis 1A (H1A). Technological opportunism will reinforce the positive relationship between the benefits frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

Technological opportunism may intensify the concerns about loss for firms that perceive threats from the use of B2B markets. Firms high in this capability are fundamentally information seekers and perform significant environmental scanning, which can reveal important negative information about an innovation. For instance, in the process of acquiring knowledge about and understanding technology developments in the environment, such firms may discover failed efforts by organizations to use B2B markets in the procurement process. Incomplete functionalities offered by and the collapse of several B2B markets may exacerbate threat perceptions because technological opportunistic firms are more attuned to such signals. Such signals are likely to trigger avoidance or a countering response, suggesting that the firm refrain from adopting and using the innovation. Thus, for the same level of perceived threat, an organization that is technologically more opportunistic is more likely to encounter and act on negative information: it will strategically choose to resist the use of B2B markets.

Hypothesis 1B (H1B). Technological opportunism will exacerbate the negative relationship between the threat frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

Technological opportunism will mitigate concerns about the changes needed in the organizational practices to use B2B markets effectively. As discussed by Srinivasan et al. (2002), technological opportunism also reflects the ability of a firm to reengineer its business strategies and processes. This capability enables firms to adapt and adjust rapidly in turbulent environments to exploit conditions that are perceived to be favorable (Srinivasan et al. 2002). The ability to sense and respond to the changes successfully over a period of time results in an environment where managers can steward the process of organizational change successfully. Hypothesis 1C (H1C). Technological opportunism will counteract the negative relationship between the adjustment frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

## 3.6. Technological Sophistication

A second capability salient in the context of ITbased innovation behaviors is technological sophistication. This construct refers to the extent to which a firm is using IT in value-adding ways. Similar constructs, called IT infrastructure sophistication,

IT sophistication, and IT management sophistication, have been used by Armstrong and Sambamurthy (1999), Chwelos et al. (2001), and Karimi et al. (2001). Chwelos et al. (2001) and Karimi et al. (2001) emphasize the dimension of management understanding and support of IT, and Armstrong and Sambamurthy (1999) focus on IT infrastructure. Our focus is neither on management understanding and support of IT nor on just IT infrastructure. We view technology sophistication as a capability that enables firms to use IT productively in business processes. Our conceptualization includes functional sophistication, which refers to the proportion and diversity of processes supported by IT-based applications and information integration sophistication, which refers to the degree to which ITbased applications are integrated both internally and externally. Firms with high technological sophistication can draw upon their existing skills, abilities, and resources to integrate B2B markets with the procurement operations, benefit from ease of learning and use, and incur lower costs of technology use (Attewell 1992). Thus we expect technological sophistication to be positively related to the extent of B2B markets use in firms. However, as argued below, we theorize that this capability interacts with managers' technological frames in its effects on the extent of B2B markets use.

High technological sophistication, a reflection of prior experience with related IT, enables firms to identify new linkages and uses of existing features in B2B markets in innovative ways (Cohen and Levinthal 1990). The skills and abilities accumulated during the use of related technologies in other contexts amplifies managers’ beliefs that they can successfully appropriate the opportunities that B2B markets offer to improve the efficiency of the procurement process and reduce costs. In other words, prior knowledge enhances the ability of firms to exploit options created by new IT applications that firms with low IT sophistication may be unable to identify or exploit. Thus, technological sophistication will strengthen the motivation and impetus to use the B2B markets for firms that perceive the innovation as offering greater opportunities.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Technological sophistication will reinforce the positive relationship between the benefits frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

In contrast, technological sophistication is expected to mitigate the negative impacts of threat and adjustment frames on B2B markets use. Prior experience with and more extensive knowledge of IT enables firms to take the necessary steps to counter any adverse effects the IT-enabled innovation is perceived as having. For example, threats posed by the potential for security compromises because of electronic connections with business partners are likely to be mitigated if managers have experience with projects that have used security applications to prevent the unauthorized use of sensitive information and digital signatures to enforce agreements. Successful execution of similar projects in the past may also suggest to managers that although risks are inherent in innovation contexts, they can be managed successfully. Likewise, a more sophisticated extant IT capability that supports the electronic sharing of information with business partners is likely to diminish the negative influence of required adaptations to complementary processes on innovation use. That is, managers may believe that their advanced IT capabilities will allow them to more easily overcome the hurdles imposed by appropriation of the technology in practice. Based on the above arguments we propose:

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). Technological sophistication will counteract the negative relationship between the threat frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

Hypothesis 2C (H2C). Technological sophistication will counteract the negative relationship between the adjustment frame and the extent of B2B markets use.

In addition to the constructs discussed above, two control variables that represent structural characteristics of firms are included in the research model: the size of the firm and the industry to which it belongs. Prior literature in innovation has suggested that firm size may have a significant impact on innovation (Cooper and Zmud 1990, Damanpour 1991, Rogers 1995). Thus, it is important to control for the impact of firm size on B2B markets use. Furthermore procurement practices and buyer-supplier dynamics differ considerably across different industries, potentially influencing the extent to which firms use B2B markets. Thus we control for the impact of industry on the use of B2B markets.

## 4. Methodology

The unit of analysis for studying the extent of B2B public markets use is the firm. The data for this study were collected through a mail survey of key informants within firms. Manufacturing firms within four industries (two-digit SIC codes 35, 36, 37, and 38) in the United States served as the setting for data collection. These firms manufacture industrial machinery and equipment, electrical and electronic machinery, transportation equipment, and measuring, analyzing, and controlling instruments. We selected four related industries purposively for two reasons. One, restricting our focus to similar industries allows us to lower extraneous sources of variation that may confound B2B markets use, and to develop grounded measures. Two, including more than one SIC code, ensures sufficient variation in constructs of interest that may not have been achieved with a single industry.

The survey instrument was developed after a thorough literature search and interviews with procurement managers in several cities in the southwest United States. The preliminary survey was reviewed for wording, content, and understandability by faculty members and doctoral students involved in ecommerce and supply-chain management research, and modified based on feedback obtained. These steps ensured face and content validity of the items. The modified survey was pilot tested with the members of the Institute of Supply Management (ISM)<sup>3</sup> in a large metropolitan area. The survey was modified based on preliminary analyses, and pilot tested a second time with members of another chapter of ISM. Statistical analyses conducted on 54 usable responses obtained for the second pilot test led to further improvements.

## 4.2. Operationalization of Variables

To the extent possible, we used existing measures and reworded them to relate specifically to our context. In the absence of existing scales, theoretically grounded new scales were developed. All the antecedent variables are measured with multiple indicators coded on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree; 7 = strongly agree). The benefits frame is assessed using six items that focus on the gains that may be possible from procuring on B2B markets (Kleindorfer and Wu 2003, Mahadevan 2003, Zhu 2004). The threat frame is operationalized using four items that relate to potential negative implications of using B2B markets (Day et al. 2003, Jap and Mohr 2002, Peleg et al. 2002). The adjustment frame is measured using four items that signify the process and context changes that firms may need to make to use B2B markets effectively. <sup>Technological</sup> <sup>opportunism</sup> is assessed using five items that relate to firmwide sensing and response capabilities. These items were adapted from Srinivasan et al. (2002) and reworded. technological sophistication using four items that measure the extent to which the firm is using IT in value-adding ways. Related constructs have been developed by several scholars (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999, Chwelos et al. 2001) and we suitably modified the items used in these constructs to fit our context.

The extent of B2B markets use is measured by the percentage of the total purchases firms make on B2B markets. This measure concisely captures and is an apt reflection of B2B markets use for procurement. A single-item dependent variable has been used in several recent IS studies. For instance, Purvis et al. (2001) measure assimilation as the proportion (in other words, percentage) of a firm's system development projects where the technology is used to support systems development work. Rai et al. (2002) use a single-item variable to measure IS use in their study. Furthermore, Carte and Russell (2003) recommend that the dependent variable be measured at a fine-grained level to detect moderation effects. In fact they recommend that the dependent variable should have at least as many levels as the product of the number of levels of the variables that constitute the interaction terms. The percentage scale accommodates this requirement, and allows for a fine-grained measurement of the extent of use. Finally, Wanous et al. (1997) note that single-item measures are adequate when the item is unambiguous for respondents—which is the case with extent of B2B markets use.5 The size of a firm is operationalized as the natural logarithm of the number of employees working in the firm; a common measure used in the literature. We created three dummy variables to operationalize the four industries that were surveyed; industry 38 served as the base industry.

## 4.3. Instrument Validation

Exploratory factor analysis performed to validate the scales yielded good results. All the items loaded high on their expected construct and low on other constructs (loading < 0.4). The reliability of constructs as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, varied from 0.77 to 0.91. After establishing the reliability and validity within the exploratory analytic framework, we evaluated the psychometric properties of the scale within the confirmatory approach using AMOS 4.0. The measurement model estimated for this purpose provided a good fit to the data (Relative chi square $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } ) = 2 . 0 7 ,$ Tucker-Lewis index = 0.983, Comparative fit ${ \mathrm { i n d e x } } = 0 . 9 8 6 ,$ ,Normed fit ${ \mathrm { i n d e x } } = 0 . 9 7 4 ,$ $\mathrm { R M S E A } = 0 . 0 6 )$ . The relative chi-square index was 2.07, below 3.0, which is the acceptable limit (Kline 1998). The goodness of fit indices, Tucker-Lewis index, Comparative fit index and Normed fit index were well above 0.9, which is the acceptable limit. At 0.06, RMSEA was below the recommended 0.08 limit (Hu and Bentler 1999). Based on all these values, we concluded that the measurement model provides a good fit to the data. Convergent validity was assessed by reviewing indicator loadings. The loadings varied from 0.5 to 0.92 and were significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , establishing the convergent validity of the scale (Anderson and Gerbing 1988). Discriminant validity was assessed by performing the confidence interval test. For each pair of constructs, a confidence interval was calculated using the estimated correlation plus or minus twice the standard error. None of the intervals included 1.0 (see Table 3), supporting discriminant validity (Anderson and Gerbing 1988). The comparison of variance extracted for each construct and the square of correlation between that construct and every other construct further indicated discriminant validity (Fornell and Larcker 1981).

Because we used the key respondent approach for data collection, we attempted to reduce the common method bias, and also estimated its impact on our results. First, as suggested by Podsakoff et al. (2003), we allowed responses to be anonymous and assured respondents that there are no right or wrong answers. Second, through iterative pilot testing, we attempted to have simpler and more direct questions. Finally, we used different scale end points for the predictor and criterion measures. These steps help control common method bias. Furthermore, we conducted Harman's one-factor test to estimate the extent of bias (Podsakoff and Organ 1986). Principal components analysis resulted in five components, which accounted for 62.6% of the total variance and the first component accounted for only 20.9% of the variance, hence there was no general factor accounting for more than 50% of the variation. We also added a first-order factor with all the measures as a control variable in the empirical model. Its addition did not change the variance explained significantly. Based on these tests, we conclude that common method bias is not a significant threat in our study.

## 6. Discussion

Our goal in this paper was to examine the moderating role of firm capabilities on the relationship between managers' technological frames and IT use. While a number of practitioner-oriented articles document the rise and fall of B2B markets and offer normative suggestions, academic research investigating the use of these markets has been sparse. From a theoretical perspective, a nuanced understanding of this phenomenon is necessary because research on postadoption use of IT is generally lacking in the literature (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005, Jasperson et al. 2005). Furthermore, the interaction effects of firm capabilities and managerial cognition on different stages of IT adoption have not been explored. Our research model extends current theorizing that has focused in isolation on the effects of firm capabilities and managerial cognition on IT innovation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first inquiry in the field of IS to conceptualize and test for the interaction between technological frames and organizational capabilities on the extent of IT innovation use.

As theorized, if IT-enabled innovation use indeed entails interactions between cognition and firm capabilities, researchers need to address a wide range of theoretical and methodological issues. To capture the rich context of IT use and explain it more thoroughly, researchers will need to consider both cognitions and capabilities, and theorize about possible interactions between them by drawing upon multiple streams of literature. It is our belief that despite the complexities associated with synthesizing research from multiple streams of literature, such efforts will be useful in that they may provide significant new theoretical insights into the use of IT applications. Additionally, researchers will need to be careful about interpreting main effects of cognition or capabilities in the presence of significant interaction effects (Carte and Russell 2003).

Three of the proposed moderating relationships are supported by the data, reinforcing the findings of Srinivasan et al. (2002) and establishing technological opportunism as an important variable in the context of IT-enabled innovation. Technological opportunism moderates both opportunity and threat frames and their relationship with the extent of B2B markets use. This suggests that a firm's sense and respond capability is significantly related to organizational decision making about IT use. It reinforces the positive impact of the opportunity frame and the negative impact of the threat frame on IT innovation use. More acute and detailed observations undertaken by technologically opportunistic firms enable them to be better aware of both opportunities and threats related to innovations. Such firms are likely to ponder both the aspects in crafting their response. In other words, technologically opportunistic firms are more astute observers of and responders to the new technologies. They respond to innovations in a balanced way. This is a significant insight because prior research has linked technological opportunism to opportunities only. Our findings also corroborate prior research that shows that experience with and extensive knowledge about related IT applications enables firms to notice novel opportunities to use new technologies in business processes.

To obtain a more fine-grained understanding of the nature of the moderation effect, we graphed the interaction between the three technological frames and two organizational capabilities (for parsimony, we only show the interaction graphs for significant interactions). The three levels of moderator variables are obtained by calculating the mean level of the variables (medium) and adding one standard deviation to the mean (high) and subtracting one standard deviation from the mean (low). The graphs in Figures 2 and 3 show positive slopes for all the three levels of technological opportunism and technological sophistication, respectively, suggesting a positive moderating impact. Moreover, the slope and hence the impact is highest for the high level and lowest for the low level. The graph in Figure 4 shows negative slopes for all the three levels of technological opportunism, reinforcing that it exacerbates the negative relationship between the threat frame and B2B markets use. Additionally, the slope and hence the impact is highest for the high level and lowest for the low level.

While three moderation hypotheses were supported, the other three were not. Our analysis did not reveal a significant moderating impact of (1) technological opportunism on the relationship between adjustment frame and B2B markets use, (2) technological sophistication on the relationship between threat frame and B2B markets use, and (3) technological sophistication on the relationship between adjustment frame and B2B markets use. However, there are strong theoretical reasons to believe that such impacts should be significant. Thus we examined these moderating effects through a more comprehensive post hoc dummy variable analysis (Bharadwaj et al. 2007, Johnson et al. 2004). We created three dummy variables each for moderator variables technological opportunism and technological sophistication.6 Using these dummy variables, we also created 18 interaction terms. While using these variables in the regression analysis, we eliminated the constant term and deleted the main effects of the three technological frame variables (Johnson et al. 2004). We found that the nine interaction terms representing the three non-significant moderation effects were all insignificant. In other words, at no level of the moderator variable is the interaction significant. The results obtained from moderated regression analysis and the post hoc analysis are at once surprising and interesting.

The most intriguing non-significant result relates to the adjustment frame. Although the direct impact of the adjustment frame on B2B markets use is predictably negative, this relationship is not moderated by technological opportunism or technological sophistication. This suggests that perceptions about significant changes necessary in organizational processes to use an IT innovation are negatively related to its use, and firm capabilities may be unable to attenuate the strength of the relationship. Furthermore, at no level of technological opportunism or technological sophistication—low, medium, and high—are the above relationships influenced. There are two plausible reasons for the non-significant result. First, technological opportunism entails sensing and responding to technological changes in the environment, and the abilities and skills relevant to manage such changes may not be transferable to managing internal process changes necessary to use B2B markets in the procurement process. These abilities and skills may not allay perceptions of significant internal organizational changes associated with B2B markets use.

Second, managers may perceive B2B markets as a procurement process innovation that is significantly different from other IT applications they have used in the past. Thus prior experience with and more extensive knowledge of IT (as captured in technological sophistication) may be unable to mitigate the negative impact of the adjustment frame. Indeed, the use of B2B markets is as much about process changes as it is about technology. With both the moderation effects insignificant, the main effect of adjustment frame can be interpreted. The main effect suggests that managers' beliefs that the changes required to the sustaining processes and incentive structures are significant will be negatively related to B2B markets use.

The insignificant effect of technological sophistication on the relationship between B2B markets use and threat frame suggests that technological proficiency may be insufficient to modify the relationship between perceived losses and B2B markets use. It is possible that although concerns related to information safety and security may be assuaged, considerable functional uncertainties related to product delivery and relationship with suppliers persist.

Both the control variables included in the study have insignificant effects on B2B markets use. Because similar manufacturing industries were chosen for this study, the non-significant impact of industry is not surprising. The insignificant impact of size suggests that B2B markets are a viable procurement innovation for firms of all sizes. Our finding also corroborates results from prior literature that have found size to have an insignificant effect on the adoption and use of IT applications (Armstrong and Sambamurthy 1999).

## 6.1. Limitations

Prior to discussing the implications of this research, we discuss its limitations. Our data were collected from one key respondent in each company, which could potentially create a common method bias problem. We tested for this problem and found that it was not an issue in our study. Our model posits moderation effects of organizational capabilities on B2B markets use and such effects are not straightforward for respondents to guess. Our results suggest that respondents from organizations that used B2B markets to a large extent as well from those of other organizations were unable to deduce the nature of interaction effects. Nevertheless, future research can circumvent this issue by surveying more than one key respondent. In addition, researchers can supplement primary data collection with data from secondary sources. We studied four related industries in an effort to control for extraneous variance. Our findings must be interpreted with this limitation in mind. The study is cross-sectional in nature and causality cannot be inferred.

Furthermore, we interviewed only one manager in every firm to assess the existence and salience of technological frames. We note, however, that our respondents were uniquely qualified to act as key informants for this study. Nonetheless, researchers may wish to interview multiple managers to assess these frames and to examine if there is convergence among the frames held by managers. It is also important to point out that firms may commence the use of B2B markets at different points in time and the elapsed time since adoption may influence extent of use. It is possible that we may have obtained different results had we controlled for time elapsed since adoption explicitly in our model.

Our objective in this paper was to theorize about and empirically examine the moderating effects of firm capabilities on the relationship between technological frames and IT use, rather than to establish a typology of frames. Furthermore, because technological frames are context specific, and have been derived by researchers for the circumstances specific to their studies, general frame domains have not emerged in the literature. In the absence of a strong typology of frames in the literature, the accumulation and interpretation of results across different studies becomes challenging and theoretical development suffers as a result. Hence, an important direction for future research is the development of a classification system for frames. Finally, our work builds on general theories of innovation but tests them in a specific empirical context. The generalizability of the findings beyond B2B markets would need to be empirically verified, although, to the degree that benefits, threats and adjustments exist for all new technologies, we believe the results would be similar in other innovation contexts as well.

## 6.2. Implications for Research

Our findings yield several avenues for future research. We discuss a few next. First, prior research has suggested that technological frames and organizational capabilities evolve temporally (Nelson and Winter 1982, Orlikowski and Gash 1994). A longitudinal examination that studies the evolution of technolog ical frames, organizational capabilities, interactions between the two and their effects on different stages of IT innovation should provide important new insights into the process aspects of IT innovativeness in firms.

The research model can be enriched by the inclusion of other frames and capabilities. For example, absorptive capacity has been linked to innovation in the prior literature, but we do not investigate its impact in our model. Furthermore, other cognitive frames about the technology such as the nature of technical change (incremental versus radical) and about the environment such as the relevant customer set or competition may also influence innovation. Organizational variables such as the degrees of centralization and formalization and top management support that have been found to be significant for innovation in prior literature can be used to extend the model. These additions may provide significant predictive improvement over the current model.

Finally, the frameworks of technological frames and sensemaking are ripe for further advancement and application in the field of information systems. Prior research using technological frames in IS has focused almost exclusively on using interpretive methods to elicit frames, establishing frame incongruence, and examining the impact of incongruence on IT use. This stream of research can be extended in two ways: (1) researchers can use both qualitative and quantitative methods to obtain the frames that individuals use to understand technological innovations. Such efforts have been highly recommended by scholars. (2) Technological frames can be used to examine a wide variety of research issues that have the potential to extend our understanding of the process of IT innovation. For instance, how do technological frames impact IT exploration and exploitation? Do organizational capabilities impact technological frames related to different information technologies differently? Will the joint impact of organizational capabilities and technological frames differ for different technologies? If yes, can researchers theorize about these effects?

## 6.3. Implications for Practice

This research has significant implications for managers from the perspective of managing technological innovations, in general, and B2B markets, in particular. Our results indicate that although both organizational capabilities and managerial perceptions of technologies individually are related to IT innovation use in processes, managers need to consider their joint impacts. The capabilities of an organization condition the effects of perceptual filters on the use of

IT-based innovations. In particular, levers of firm capabilities can be used effectively to manage opportunity and threat frames. The significant moderating effect of technological opportunism points to the need to develop an organizational culture that views environmental scanning as an important activity and creates incentives to support it. Likewise, the significant moderating effect of technological sophistication underscores the "virtuous" cycle of IT innovation. As firms become progressively more sophisticated in their use and application of IT, the “next” innovation becomes easier to use.

To the extent that the adjustment frame is negatively related to use, our results underscore the need to manage perceptions related to the difficulty of instituting organizational change. If process-related, structural, and incentive design changes are made <sup>prior</sup> to the introduction of an innovation, the likelihood of it being assimilated more extensively is higher (Zmud and Apple 1992). This observation reflects conventional wisdom that has argued for the need for process redesign prior to the introduction of enterprise systems. Executives need to understand that procurement through B2B markets is more than simply a technological innovation because it causes fundamental changes to the entire procurement activity.

Our findings also have implications for B2B public markets such as www.chemconnect.com and www. partminer.com. Certainly, these markets need to convey the operational and strategic opportunities that may be available to firms from using B2B markets, persuade firms about the safety, security, and robustness of the exchange platform, and suggest that participating suppliers are capable and willing to meet firms’ requirements. In addition, B2B markets need to tailor their messages contingent on the capabilities of the targeted organizations. These markets should provide evidence for why such capabilities may enable firms to use B2B markets effectively and efficiently B2B markets should not underplay the role of complementary changes and should prepare organizations, so that they can make these changes painlessly and are able to use them conveniently.

## 7. Conclusion

This study has contributed to a better understanding of post-adoption IT use in firms by drawing on and synthesizing two important streams of literature. We examine IT innovation use by proposing and testing the moderating effects of organizational capabilities on the relationship between technological frames and use. To the extent that using technological innovations will remain an enduring organizational challenge, we suggest that it is clearly a worthwhile endeavor for researchers to seek richer models that help explain and manage innovation-related activities.

Empirical testing of such models would contribute significantly to advancing knowledge and understanding of this important phenomenon. We hope that this study will stimulate researchers to investigate IT innovations in a wide variety of settings using multiple streams of literature. Such work is necessary to build a cumulative body of evidence that can advance knowledge about post-adoption use of IT innovations in organizations.
