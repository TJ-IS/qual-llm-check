# Experience Curves in Reward-Based Crowdfunding: An Empirical Study of Serial Creators

Nianxin Wang,<sup>1</sup> Qingxiang Li,<sup>2</sup> Huigang Liang,<sup>3</sup> Hao Hu<sup>4</sup>, Yi Wang<sup>5</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Jiangsu University of Science and Technology, China, nianxin.wang@gmail.com <sup>2</sup>School of Economics & Management, Jiangsu University of Science and Technology, China, lqx1075853568@163.com <sup>3</sup>Fogelman College of Business and Economics, University of Memphis, USA, huigang.liang@gmail.com <sup>4</sup>School of Digital Economics and Management, Wuxi University, China, huhao\_just@163.com <sup>5</sup>School of Business Administration, Southwestern University of Finance and Economics, China, wangyi63@swufe.edu.cn

## Abstract

Previous studies have reported a linear relationship between creator experience and crowdfunding success. However, creator experience does not guarantee a high likelihood of crowdfunding success. Drawing from experience curve theory and entrepreneurial learning research, this study investigates experience curves in reward-based crowdfunding by focusing on the characteristics of creators’ prior campaign experience. We argue that the positive experience-performance relationship applies only to veteran serial creators, while novice serial creators cannot effectively apply their experiential knowledge to new projects. We further posit that two cross-project characteristics (interproject diversity and interproject interval) moderate the shape of the experience curve. Using a dataset of 6,469 projects initiated by 2,452 serial creators from Indiegogo, we found that creator experience has a U-shaped relationship with crowdfunding success and that this curve is flattened by interproject diversity and interproject interval. Our work provides both theoretical and practical implications for crowdfunding.

Keywords: Crowdfunding, Serial Creator, Experience Curve, Entrepreneurial Learning, Learning Curve

Daniel Veit was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on June 26, 2023, and underwent three revisions. Huigang Liang and Nianxin Wang are the corresponding authors.

## 1 Introduction

Crowdfunding intended to raise a small amount of funding from many individuals via digital platforms has attracted much attention from industry practitioners and academic researchers. Because crowdfunding enables low-cost, centralized advertising of project ideas, secure and trustworthy contributions to those projects, and simultaneous solicitation by multiple projects, it has grown rapidly. The global crowdfunding market was valued at \$10.2 billion in 2018 and is expected to reach \$28.77 billion by 2025 (Marketstudyreport.com, 2019).

The entrepreneurial finance market includes four types of crowdfunding: reward-based, donation-based, lending-based, and equity-based (Mollick, 2014). In this paper, we seek to understand what leads to reward-based crowdfunding success for serial creators. We focus on reward-based crowdfunding for two reasons. First, unlike equity-based crowdfunding, where entrepreneurs sell equity to raise finance, reward-based crowdfunding allows creators to offer a product or a service to the market. Hence, reward-based crowdfunding provides creators with the opportunity to test the market and receive feedback. Consequently, serial entrepreneurs’ learning is more measurable and direct (Huang et al., 2022). Second, reward-based crowdfunding has become the dominant online model for entrepreneurs and small businesses to raise funds (Dushnitsky et al., 2016; Li & Wang, 2019; Mollick, 2014). Many creative ideas have been funded successfully through this type of crowdfunding. For example, Indiegogo, one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms, has raised more than \$1 billion for over 800,000 innovative projects since 2008 (Indiegogo, 2022).

Serial creators in crowdfunding are those who have launched multiple campaigns. While prior studies have shown empirically that creator experience has a significant effect on the likelihood of crowdfunding success (Butticè et al., 2017; Courtney et al., 2017; Mollick, 2014; Yang & Hahn, 2015), this relationship is not fully understood. While prior studies have proposed and studied linear experience-success relationships, the findings are inconsistent (Maier et al., 2021; Sewaid et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2021). Some researchers have found the relationship to be positive (Maier et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017), while others have found it to be negative (Peterson & Wu, 2021; Sewaid et al., 2021) or insignificant (Tornikoski & Newbert, 2007). Against this backdrop, our research attempts to reconcile the mixed findings by drawing from theories on the experience curve and entrepreneurial learning.

Existing studies implicitly assume that projects initiated by the same creator are independent and neglect the dynamic connection between the projects by the creator. However, serial creators can learn from their experience and apply the learned knowledge to new projects. Such dynamics of entrepreneurial experience have not been explored. Furthermore, the likelihood of crowdfunding success varies significantly across serial creators. Previous success does not guarantee future success, and previous failures do not absolutely preclude future success (Briggman, 2014). Our observation reveals that some serial creators perform more poorly over time, while others steadily achieve better results, indicating that the learning process and mechanisms for serial creators are complex. Given the uncertain trajectory and outcomes of their learning (Dahlin et al., 2018), it is important to understand how serial creators develop knowledge through experience to achieve crowdfunding success (Yang & Hahn, 2016).

In addition, prior experience alone cannot fully explain how entrepreneurial learning happens (Politis, 2005). Prior research suggests that the learning process of crowdfunding creators is context dependent (Levitt & March, 1988) and that the shape of the experience curve varies across different contexts (Argote & Epple, 1990). Based on the literature, we posit that interproject diversity and interproject interval are two possible contextual factors that determine the outcome of entrepreneurial learning (Morris et al., 2012).

We collected 6,469 projects initiated by 2,452 serial creators from Indiegogo to test our hypotheses. Our econometric analysis confirms that creator experience has a U-shaped relationship with crowdfunding success. Moreover, the experience curve is flattened by interproject diversity and interproject interval, which reduce both the uphill and downhill slopes of the curve.

Our work makes multiple contributions. First, it contributes to the crowdfunding literature by explaining the experience curve in reward-based crowdfunding and empirically examining the curvilinear relationship between creator experience and crowdfunding success. Several studies have investigated serial creators (Courtney et al., 2017; Sewaid et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017; Zvilichovsky et al., 2015), but they have focused mainly on the linear relationship between experience and performance, and their findings are inconsistent. The U-shaped experience-performance relationship uncovered in this paper helps to reconcile some of the inconsistencies in prior studies. Second, while previous studies have most often applied social capital (Buttice et al., 2017; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017) and signaling theory (Courtney et al., 2017) to explain creators’ experience accumulation, we draw on experience curve and entrepreneurial learning theory to treat the dynamics of serial creators’ experience as an entrepreneurial learning process, thereby providing novel theoretical insights into serial crowdfunding campaigns. Experience curves can more accurately depict how serial creators’ experience is applied to new campaigns and why experience does not always improve fundraising outcomes. Third, we propose two contextual factors (interproject diversity and interproject interval) and confirm their effects in shaping the experience curve of serial creators in reward-based crowdfunding. With such theoretical contextualization, our work provides a more comprehensive understanding of how serial creators learn from prior experience and alter their subsequent behaviors in pursuing crowdfunding success.

## 2 Theoretical Background

## 2.1 Entrepreneurial Learning and the Experience Curve

In the entrepreneurship literature, entrepreneurial experience refers to past involvement in founding and managing a venture (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). Entrepreneurial learning is often described as a dynamic and continuous process of gaining knowledge from the experience of relaunching and managing ventures (Politis, 2005). The ability of entrepreneurs to transform prior experience into knowledge determines the success of their new ventures. Entrepreneurship researchers have examined the effect of entrepreneurs’ experience on venture performance but have produced contradictory findings. The effect has been found to be positive (Eesley & Roberts, 2012), negative (Oe & Mitsuhashi, 2013), or insignificant (Tornikoski & Newbert, 2007). Some researchers have speculated that entrepreneurial learning is a nonlinear growth process (Cope, 2005) and applied experience curve theory for clarification.

Experience curve theory, first developed by Henderson in the 1960s to analyze the relationship between production cost and quantity, explains how cost benefits can be achieved through performing repetitive tasks. Experience curve theory can be used to model, track, and predict the performance of learners over time, based on cumulative volume. Experience curves have been used to explain how the cumulative experience of serial entrepreneurs affects their subsequent venture performance. Research has found that organizational learning outcomes improve with experience early in experience accumulation but the improvement rate declines in later stages (Anand et al., 2016; Yelle, 1979). In addition, recent experience has been found to be more valuable for organizational learning than old experience (Argote & Epple, 1990). The concept of the experience curve has been applied widely in contexts such as manufacturing (Yelle, 1979), new product introduction (Anand et al., 2016), and corporate acquisition (Barkema & Schijven, 2008) at individual, group, organization, and industry levels (Argote, 2012).

While the challenges of learning from limited experience have attracted considerable attention in the literature, the shape of experience curves may depend on their specific contexts. Some scholars have indicated that reliable learning from experience is possible only when entrepreneurs understand the causality between past campaign strategies and outcomes and that limited experience can lead to a misunderstanding of such causality and pose a significant risk to learning (Dahlin et al., 2018; Zhao, 2011).

Furthermore, researchers have proposed that limited experience creates barriers to learning and leads to spurious (or superstitious) learning. Barriers to learning refer to “obstacles encountered by entrepreneurs that prevent them from extracting appropriate knowledge from their prior venturing or from applying their existing knowledge appropriately to new ventures” (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). Spurious learning is “a false sense of understanding of the current situation from prior experience” (Musaji et al., 2020; Zollo, 2009). As a result, previous experience does not necessarily ensure better performance in new ventures, suggesting the possible existence of an experience curve (Musaji et al., 2020).

## 2.2 Experience in Crowdfunding

The number of serial creators who launch several crowdfunding campaigns in sequence has grown rapidly. Prior studies have explained serial creators’ high performance using social capital theory (Buttice et al., 2017; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017) and signaling theory (Courtney et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2022). Although many studies have examined the role of creator experience in influencing crowdfunding success, their findings are inconsistent. For example, the literature on serial creators has identified a positive relationship between prior experience and performance (Maier et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017). However, some research has revealed that a serial creator’s highly successful crowdfunding experience may lead to a negative outcome for their next project (Sewaid et al., 2021). These conflicting findings imply that entrepreneurial learning in crowdfunding is complex and warrants further investigation.

On crowdfunding platforms, creators acquire experience from their past projects. During the process, they receive feedback from the crowdfunding market. These experiences and feedback can be transformed into knowledge, which, in turn, will guide creators’ subsequent activities. This process fits basic entrepreneurial learning (Yang & Hahn, 2015). Based on the experience curve and entrepreneurial learning literature, we argue that serial creators’ learning in crowdfunding resembles serial entrepreneurs’ complex knowledge-based or analytical learning.

According to the entrepreneurial learning theory, considerable experience is needed before reliable learning can occur because limited experience may increase the risk of spurious learning (Zollo, 2009). Novice serial creators with low to moderate experience may draw incorrect inferences from previous experience. Hence, early in experience accumulation, experience may not necessarily increase fundraising performance. Instead, only veteran serial creators can achieve a positive experience-performance relationship. This finding suggests that the experience-performance relationship is nonlinear, which may explain contradictions in prior studies.

The experience curve varies across creators because each creator has a unique learning process characterized by various attributes, such as interproject diversity and the interproject interval of experience. Entrepreneurs can learn faster when events are frequent (Edmondson et al., 2001); however, being faster is not always better, especially when high frequency leads to information overload and limited learning (Hayward, 2002). Moreover, the shape of experience curves depends on variations across events. In the crowdfunding literature, only the direct effect of interproject diversity on performance has been reported (Yang & Hahn, 2015), and its role as a contextual factor has largely been ignored. To understand the learning process, we attempt to examine how interproject interval and interproject diversity shape the experience curve of serial creators in crowdfunding.

## 3 Hypothesis Development

## 3.1 Experience Curve in Crowdfunding

We argue that there is a curvilinear relationship between experience and crowdfunding success based on two key considerations. First, novice serial creators cannot overcome spurious learning due to their limited experience, and this obstacle negatively impacts their crowdfunding success. Second, veteran serial creators are more likely to succeed in crowdfunding because of their general awareness and greater likelihood of making effective inferences. Our study explores entrepreneurial learning as a continuous process that facilitates the development of knowledge necessary for the effective management of new ventures (Politis, 2005). Experience is anticipated to impact individuals’ future performance by facilitating learning. This process serves as a crucial source of applied knowledge that encompasses formal facts, established rules, and procedures. Additionally, experience enhances skills, particularly information-processing abilities acquired through practice, and enables the creation of new procedures and conclusions (Nass, 1994).

Entrepreneurs may acquire knowledge or skills by learning from experience, but if their experience is limited, what they learn from previous ventures may not translate into reliable knowledge because they do not understand how to generalize their new knowledge between ventures. In addition, novice serial creators may generalize their knowledge incorrectly and apply lessons learned from prior ventures to a new venture they wrongly believe is similar. Thus, novice serial creators may encounter difficulties in both extracting appropriate knowledge from prior experience and applying that knowledge appropriately to new ventures (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014).

The risk of spurious learning may increase with limited experience (March et al., 1991). Spurious learning may confound inference. Spurious failures, for instance, increase ambiguity and cause actors to modify routines and processes incorrectly (Dahlin et al., 2018). Successful outcomes attributed to spurious learning, on the other hand, can cause decision-makers to hold onto flawed beliefs (Kale et al., 2002) or attribute errors to violating rules and incorrectly executed routines (Rerup, 2006). Spurious learning may also increase the risk of latent errors and reduce creators’ ability to diagnose them. In summary, when experience is low, creators may lack the ability to create knowledge inductively, leading to spurious learning and increasing the probability of errors (March et al., 1991).

The learning process from limited experience also has some inherent limitations. For instance, entrepreneurial learning tends to be local, i.e., closely related to existing routines (Prencipe & Tell, 2001). This limitation is most salient among novice serial creators who are usually not able to see the whole picture (Benner, 1984). Thus, novice serial creators learn passively through single-loop learning only (Argyris & Schön, 1978; Prencipe & Tell, 2001). Single-loop learning involves changes in the entrepreneur’s knowledge but does not trigger adaptation to changing realities and objectives, which could give novice serial creators a false sense of understanding. Novice serial creators, without full awareness of a wider body of challenges and accompanying solutions, tend not to translate prior experience into appropriate solutions for problems of new ventures (Crossan et al., 1999). These conditions suggest the experience-performance relationship initially may have a negative trend because decisions are drawn from a limited pool of options bounded by current knowledge constraints (Musaji et al., 2020).

Plentiful experience provides some benefits. Notably, veteran serial creators can apply their prior knowledge to new businesses and cope with new difficulties for each endeavor. Greater familiarity with the business domain means that highly experienced creators are more likely to possess domain-related knowledge, skills, and expertise that result in a better aptitude for finding solutions to problems or meeting new challenges. Such expertise can improve their ability to manage marketing principles and related intelligence (Chua & Iyengar, 2008).

Even when veteran serial creators lack direct experience in dealing with these specific challenges, they may rely on the experience and expertise of others to exploit additional resources and make effective connections. With feedback from others, creators can reach some understanding of how and to what extent specific factors may influence funding performance, allowing them to adjust their strategies as needed. Veteran serial creators also typically demonstrate the entrepreneurial capability to process information effectively (Zollo & Winter, 2002). Veteran serial creators can draw more accurate conclusions and generalizations from their previous business endeavors and apply them to new ventures (Eesley & Roberts, 2012), thus avoiding cognitive and practical rigidity. Plentiful experience can also foster the development of new entrepreneurial routines that increase entrepreneurs’ ability to innovate and expand their operations (Frese, 2009).

Whether experiencing a period of failure or success, veteran serial creators are better equipped to avoid the negative influence of superstitious learning due to knowledge collection and validation of activities from previous ventures. Consistent with prior research (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014), we argue that novice serial creators’ performance may grow increasingly worse because of their superstitious learning and cognitive rigidity, but the positive experience-performance relationship will emerge eventually when they become veteran serial creators. Succinctly put, the probability of crowdfunding success declines at low to moderate experience but becomes positive for creators with moderate to high experience. Therefore, we propose:

H1: The relationship between accumulated experience and crowdfunding success is U-shaped, such that the relationship is negative at low experience levels and becomes positive at high experience levels.

## 3.2 Experience Curve in Different Contexts

According to organizational learning theory, the learning process of serial creators is context dependent (Levitt & March, 1988), where the experience curve’s shape varies depending on interproject diversity and interproject interval (Argote & Miron-Spektor, 2011; Musaji et al., 2020; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). Acknowledging the innate limitations of entrepreneurial learning that become salient depending on contextual factors, we examine the differential benefits and cost of interproject diversity and interproject interval for both novice serial creators and veteran serial creators.

## 3.2.1 Interproject Diversity and Learning Curve

We first explore the moderating effect of interproject diversity on the U-shaped relationship between entrepreneurial experience and crowdfunding success. We posit that at low to moderate levels of experience, a diverse range of experiences enriches an entrepreneur’s skill set and adaptability and reduces the negative impacts of limited experience on crowdfunding success. Conversely, at moderate to high levels of experience, excessive diversity can produce cognitive overload and causal ambiguity that flatten the upward relationship.

It is generally desirable for entrepreneurs to have a basic knowledge of many business domains (Lazear, 2004). Since entrepreneurs must perform many different tasks, they need diverse knowledge and skills (Spanjer & van Witteloostuijn, 2017). These diverse tasks force serial entrepreneurs to wrestle with a variety of fundamental issues that differ for each new venture. Because serial entrepreneurs are likely to encounter new challenges in each venture, the application of prior knowledge is rarely straightforward (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014).

Experience can be transformed into knowledge through two primary processes: exploitation and exploration (Politis, 2005). Exploitation concerns the application of what is already known, implying that individuals learn from experience through the implementation, refinement, and routinization of knowledge. However, crowdfunding’s focus on transforming innovative ideas into viable products demands an exploratory approach from creators (Yang & Hahn, 2015). Since the crowdfunding market is dynamic, creators whose experience is limited may struggle when relying solely on exploitation. Furthermore, effective exploitation may occur only after a significant accumulation of experience. If serial creators exploit knowledge and skills from a single type of experience, they may simply replicate the successful practices in that specific area and then suffer from success replication bias (Musaji et al., 2020). Relying heavily on one source of experience may increase the likelihood of drawing wrong conclusions and making inaccurate generalizations, which then may exacerbate the negative connection between experience and performance when experience is limited.

However, a creator whose projects are few but diversified could initiate novel projects that differ from previous attempts. Such diversification allows for a richer learning experience through exploration, including deviating from conventional approaches and overcoming novel challenges (Politis, 2005). The knowledge gained from exposure to a variety of tasks and a broader schema of events and conditions enables individuals to tackle problems more effectively (Yang & Hahn, 2016). As learning continues, creators may substitute or refine existing knowledge. Presumably, creators who continually seek and explore fresh wisdom are better equipped to generate progressive openings to new and novel campaigns than those who heavily rely on more homogeneous experiences. Therefore, in the crowdfunding industry, having limited but diverse experience may mitigate the negative relationship between experience and performance.

In addition, entrepreneurs’ prior experience contributes to the formation of “knowledge corridors,” which facilitate searching for and exploiting accumulated knowledge. Knowledge corridors exist as structures (routines, procedures, values, etc.), enabling entrepreneurs to navigate and leverage new opportunities effectively. Diverse experience can broaden an individual’s knowledge corridor, facilitating the exploration of a broader range of possibilities (Marvel et al., 2020). This expansion enhances the ability to identify and seize advantageous business opportunities. Also, research shows that entrepreneurs who can discern the best options among multiple opportunities produce superior results compared to those who face fewer options. Thus, when experience is at the low-to-moderate level, highly varied experience can nullify the adverse effects of limited experience by enabling the pursuit of more innovative opportunities (Marvel et al., 2020).

Importantly, diverse experience can lead to more connections across industries and in challenging markets. A diverse entrepreneurial background stimulates creativity via more connections which can provide “a more robust basis for learning and development in new uncertain situations” (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990, p. 131). Crowdfunding creators may initiate innovative projects across multiple product categories. When engaging in such projects, they may acquire diverse skills, such as formulating suitable framing strategies, designing appropriate rewards, and communicating with potential backers from a variety of backgrounds. When experience is limited, creators can derive greater benefits from diverse experience than from homogeneous experience because high experience diversity can encourage creators to identify associations between actions and newly considered feedback. In such cases, highly diverse experience can facilitate a better understanding of the interrelations between different tasks, including those connections and influences that may otherwise be overlooked. Therefore, it can be argued that novice serial creators with greater experience diversity gain new skill sets and connections, increasing their ability to recognize and act on entrepreneurial opportunities. Experience diversity thereby weakens the negative relationship between experience and performance.

Although high diversity has benefits, it is not always advantageous. When entrepreneurial experience accumulates to a certain level, greater diversity of experience may lead to overly complex relationships between prior experiences. This complexity can flatten the learning curve at a moderate to high level of experience because individual cognitive capacity can be exceeded when presented with more information than the learner is able to process. This concept, known as knowledge overload (Baron, 1998), can potentially inhibit entrepreneurs from fully exploiting the learning opportunities offered by their experience (Spanjer & van Witteloostuijn, 2017).

When an individual’s cognitive capacity is overwhelmed by the volume and diversity of knowledge from past projects, comparing and integrating these varied experiences becomes challenging. This complexity makes it harder to draw meaningful inferences from prior experience. If entrepreneurs cannot understand the causal relationship between experience and specific outcomes, they cannot fully utilize the gained capabilities. With causal ambiguity, a wrong inference may reduce performance. Hence, entrepreneurs with diverse and plentiful experience may have lower entrepreneurial performance. This result implies that interproject diversity may reduce the positive experience-performance relationship for veteran serial entrepreneurs. Taken together, we propose:

H2: Interproject diversity flattens the U-shaped relationship between experience and crowdfunding success such that it reduces both the negative relationship at low to moderate levels of experience and the positive relationship at moderate to high levels of experience.

## 3.2.2 Interproject Interval and Learning Curve

The temporal dimension of entrepreneurial learning, particularly the interproject interval, significantly modulates the transformation of raw experience into actionable, experientially acquired knowledge (Politis, 2005). This transformative process necessitates deliberate reflection and cogitation; such consideration is essential for the generation and assimilation of new insights. The duration of the interproject interval, therefore, plays a pivotal role in shaping the experienceperformance curve in entrepreneurial settings including crowdfunding (Musaji et al., 2020).

In early experience accumulation, short interproject intervals can be counterproductive because potential information overload can hamper effective learning (Musaji et al., 2020). When entrepreneurs embark on new crowdfunding projects in rapid succession, they often lack the requisite temporal buffer to thoroughly assimilate and evaluate the outcomes and lessons from previous ventures. This hastened pace can precipitate superficial learning processes and impair problemsolving responsiveness (Joseph & Gaba, 2015). Additionally, in such compressed time frames, creators are more prone to relying on entrenched routines and cognitive heuristics, even when these are misaligned with the current context. This phenomenon, known as anchoring bias, can skew novice serial creators’ perceptions and decision-making processes and exacerbate the negative slope of the experience curve when experience is scarce. Conversely, longer interproject intervals offer the necessary temporal space for in-depth processing, evaluation, and reflection on accumulated experience and can foster a more robust, effective learning trajectory. This extended duration mitigates the risk of superficial or superstitious learning, enabling entrepreneurs to develop a more nuanced understanding of their experience and apply this knowledge more effectively in subsequent ventures.

However, the benefits of extended interproject intervals are not linear across the experience spectrum. For veteran serial creators with moderate to high experience levels, prolonged interproject intervals may weaken specific memories and dilute knowledge. As the vividness and granularity of past experiences diminish over time, the feedback from these experiences becomes increasingly ambiguous (Joseph & Gaba, 2015). This ambiguity can hinder effective problem-solving and delay proactive responses to emerging challenges. In contrast, shorter intervals at this experience level can be advantageous, as they ensure the recency and salience of information, thereby enhancing the clarity and applicability of the learning outcomes (Haunschild et al., 2015).

In summary, the interproject interval’s impact on the experience-performance curve in crowdfunding is complex and multifaceted. Longer intervals may facilitate deeper learning and reflection in the early stages of experience accumulation. Conversely, they may impede the application and retention of knowledge at higher levels of experience. Thus, the interproject interval can interact with the creator experience stage to influence how well creators leverage their experience to achieve crowdfunding success. We therefore posit:

H3: Interproject interval moderates the U-shaped relationship between experience and crowdfunding success such that it reduces both the negative effect at low to moderate levels of experience and the positive relationship at moderate to high levels of experience.

## 4 Data and Method

## 4.1 Context of the Study and Sample

We collected data from Indiegogo.com, one of the earliest and largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms worldwide. Indiegogo launches campaigns in three major categories with 28 subcategories: Tech & Innovation (e.g., Audio, Camera Gear, Education, Energy & Green Tech, Fashion & Wearables, Food & Beverages, Health & Fitness, Home, Phone & Accessories, Productivity, Transportation, and Travel & Outdoors), Creative Works (e.g., Art, Comics, Dance & Theater, Film, Music, Photography, Podcasts, Blogs & Vlogs, Tabletop Games, Video Games, Web Series & TV Shows, and Writing & Publishing), and Community Projects (e.g., Culture, Environment, Human Rights, Local Business, and Wellness). Using the web data extraction method, we collected campaigns from all three major categories and 28 subcategories to ensure that our sample was representative of Indiegogo’s project population. Our initial sample comprised 50,888 projects launched on Indiegogo from October 8, 2008, to August 12, 2017. We targeted serial creators who initiated at least two projects. Creators who launched only one project were excluded. This filtering exercise resulted in a final sample of 6,469 projects initiated by 2,452 serial creators who, on average, launched 2.64 projects.

## 4.2 Measures

Crowdfunding success: The dependent variable was a binary variable indicating whether the campaign reached its funding goal. Crowdfunding success scored 1 when the project creator’s funding goal was reached or exceeded and 0 otherwise. There were two funding models on the Indiegogo platform, take-it-all and allor-nothing. For the take-it-all model, the creator keeps the entire amount raised regardless of whether the pledge meets the funding goal. For the all-or-nothing model, the creator receives nothing unless the funding goal is achieved. In crowdfunding, the funding goal represents the minimum amount required to complete the project. Regardless of the funding model, a funding goal must be set. Reaching or surpassing the funding goal by the campaign’s end signals that there is enough financial support to implement the project (Cumming et al., 2020). Thus, reaching the goal is the standard of project success. In the take-it-all model, although the creator can keep all the pledges, the project’s successful implementation is uncertain when the funding goal is unmet. In the all-or-nothing model, the goal should represent the minimum amount the creator needs to complete the project. Although some creators may set the goal artificially low, the funding should be determined by careful evaluation. Setting a goal significantly lower than what the project needs is very unwise. Therefore, it is reasonable to use crowdfunding success as our dependent variable for both models. Additionally, to enhance the results’ credibility, we also chose two common dependent variables, the funding ratio and the fundraising amount, in the subsequent robustness check.

Experience as creator: We measured creator experience based on the cumulative number of prior projects in all categories before a given project initiated by a creator.

Interproject diversity: We used the Simpson Diversity Index (Magurran, 1988; Rosenzweig, 1995) to measure the interproject diversity of serial creators using the following formula:

$$
\text {   Simpson   Index(D)   } = \frac {1}{\sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{s}} p _ {\mathrm{i}} ^ {2}}
$$

where p is the proportion (n/N) of crowdfunding projects of a particular subcategory (n) initiated by a creator divided by the total number of projects (N) initiated by a creator, and s is the number of subcategories. The Simpson Index (Simpson, 1949) measures the number of species and the relative abundance of each species, where the degree of their concentration is classified into types.

Table 1 shows an example of calculating Simpson diversity for a serial creator who launched four projects in three Indiegogo categories (A1-A3), where interproject diversity is calculated in line with the project launch sequence: if only one project is launched, the diversity equals 1. If two or three launched projects belong to different categories, p =1/2 or 1/3 for both projects and the interproject diversity equals 2 or 3, respectively. If a fourth project is launched (in the same category as the first project in this case), p for each category is 2/4, 1/4, and 1/4, respectively. The Simpson diversity thus equals 2.6667.

Interproject interval: Interproject interval was measured by the average number of days between the starting days of two adjacent projects initiated by the same creator. For example, for a serial creator launching four projects, with 79 days between Projects 2 and 1, 52 days between Projects 3 and 2, and 187 days between Projects 4 and 3, the average intervals would be 106 for Project 4, 65.5 for Project 3, and 79 for Project 2.

## 5.2 Curvilinear Relationships Between Experience and Crowdfunding Success

With a dichotomous dependent variable (crowdfunding success), we used binary logistic analysis to test our hypotheses. Multiplicative terms were created to examine the moderation effects. To minimize multicollinearity, we mean-centered the independent variables and moderators before computing the multiplicative terms (Aiken et al., 1991). Table 4 presents the logistic regression results. As shown in Model 1, the coefficient associated with experience squared is significant and positive (0.029, p 0.01). This suggests a U-shaped relationship between experience and crowdfunding success, providing initial support for H1.

Haans et al. (2016) suggest that a significant coefficient alone is necessary but not sufficient to establish a curvilinear relationship. To confirm the Usharped relationship between experience and crowdfunding success, we followed the three-step procedure proposed by Lind and Mehlum (2010). First, the coefficient of $\bar { X ^ { 2 } }$ needs to be significant and positive. Second, the slope must be sufficiently steep at both ends of the data range. As shown in Table $^ { 5 , }$ the slope at the low end of the experience is negative and significant, and the slope at the high end of the EC is positive and significant. Third, the turning point needs to be located within the valid range of the independent variable, creator experience. As shown in Table 5, the turning point (5.2818, $9 5 \% \mathrm { C I } = [ 4 . 4 8 0 $ 6.069]) falls within the data range of experience (min $\begin{array} { r l } { \mathbf { \Psi } } & { { } = \mathbf { \Psi } 1 . } \end{array}$ $\mathrm { m a x } \ = \ 1 6 )$ . Figure 1 shows the estimated relationship, the confidence interval for the maximum, and the two lower bounds on the slopes in each endpoint of experience. Therefore, our results confirm that there is a U-shaped relationship between experience and crowdfunding success, supporting H1.

## 5.5 Robustness Check

The creator’s entrepreneurial learning may be complicated, and some characteristics or learning processes are not observable. Hence, we conducted several robustness tests to increase the credibility and reliability of our results. First, our data included those projects that failed the first time and then were relaunched. The serial creators of the relaunched projects may have had specific learning that is applied to the relaunched projects. This implies that the learning curve would be different between the relaunched and brand-new projects. To control for the potential interference of relaunched projects on learning outcomes, we removed those projects that failed the first time and then were relaunched.

We manually checked all project titles to find and remove relaunched projects. If two or more projects from the same creator had highly similar titles but different sequence numbers and all failed or only the last one succeeded, we identified them as relaunched projects. In this case, we kept only the first project and deleted the rest. After excluding 378 relaunched projects (5.8%) and 116 serial creators (4.7%), we created a dataset containing 6,091 projects by 2,336 serial creators. Using the new dataset, we ran the logistic regression and found that all the results remained consistent (Tables 6 and 7). Only the turning point and the slope have slight differences: the turning point changed from 5.281 to 6.264, and the slope from -0.247 to -0.772 and 0.619 to 1.42. The U-shaped relationship and the moderate effect still exist, suggesting the robustness of our results.

We performed several additional tests to assess our results’ robustness. First, we controlled for experience as backer. As a two-sided platform, crowdfunding allows anybody to participate in both sides of the market, creating campaigns of their own as well as backing others’ projects. Both behaviors can create crowdfunding experience through different learning mechanisms, entrepreneurial learning, or observational learning (Wang et al., 2021). To exclude the effects of observational learning, we controlled for experience as backer and validated the effects of entrepreneurial learning, manifested as experience as creator, on crowdfunding success. As shown in Table 8, after controlling for experience as backer, the results remain unchanged in terms of magnitude and significance, confirming the robustness of our main findings.

Second, we selected the Shannon Index (also known as the Shannon-Wiener Index), a popular diversity index in the ecology literature (Begon et al., 1996; Magurran, 1988; Rosenzweig, 1995), to measure interproject diversity. It measures the entropy of all project categories and has been used previously to measure interproject diversity (Yang & Hahn, 2016). The formula for the Shannon index is:

$$
\text {   Shannon   Index(H)   } = - \sum_ {\mathrm{i} = 1} ^ {\mathrm{s}} p _ {\mathrm{i}} \ln p _ {\mathrm{i}}
$$

where $p _ { i }$ is the proportion (n/N) of crowdfunding projects in a particular subcategory (n) initiated by a creator divided by the total number of projects (N) initiated by a creator, and ln is the natural log (Begon et al., 1996; Magurran, 1988; Rosenzweig, 1995). We replaced the Simpson index with the Shannon index and conducted the regression. As Table 10 shows, the results are similar to the Simpson index results in terms of magnitude and significance, indicating the robustness of our results.

Third, we controlled for the funding model of projects (all-or-nothing or take-it-all). As stated earlier, different funding models require different strategies, which may generate different learning processes for serial creators. We constructed a dummy variable and coded it as 0 for all-or-nothing and 1 for take-it-all. As shown in Table 10, after adding this control variable and redoing the regression, the results remain consistent.

Fourth, we changed our dependent variable to fundraising amount, which refers to the total amount of money a project has achieved during the whole funding period. Fundraising amount is also the common dependent variable in the crowdfunding literature (Sewaid et al., 2021; Soublière & Gehman, 2020).

Our study draws on the theories of entrepreneurial learning and experience curves to investigate the experience curves of serial creators in the rewardbased crowdfunding context. Using a sample of 6,469 projects initiated by 2,452 serial creators from the Indiegogo crowdfunding platform, we examined the relationship between creator experience and crowdfunding success. We found that creator experience has a U-shaped relationship with crowdfunding success. It declines initially and, once sufficient experience accumulates, a positive learning trend emerges. We also found that the relationship between creator experience and crowdfunding success was moderated by two contextual factors: interproject diversity and interproject interval. Both moderators flattened the experience curve.

## 6 Discussion

research related to the lean startups approach suggests that in a rapidly developing environment with great uncertainty and complexity, like reward-based crowdfunding, success comes from speed (Ghezzi & Cavallo, 2020; Yang et al., 2019). Entrepreneurs can benefit from a short interval because it helps them conduct tests and experiments, learn, and evolve from this model. Particularly, the U-shaped curve becomes a linear relationship when interproject intervals are long. Long interproject intervals mean that the stimulus-performance feedback cycle allows for sufficient information processing and reflection, leading to a better understanding of prior experience and better subsequent decision-making. As a result, superstitious learning can be avoided. These results advance our understanding of experience curves in the context of reward-based crowdfunding, shedding light on the conditions under which experience translates into an improved likelihood of crowdfunding success.

## 6.1 Implications for Research

Our work offers several important contributions to the crowdfunding and entrepreneurial learning literatures. First, this study advances the understanding of the experiences of serial creators in reward-based crowdfunding. Our finding indicates that a U-shaped experience curve exists in the crowdfunding context. It declines at low to moderate levels of creator experience but becomes positive for moderate to high levels of experience. These results uncover the constraints of limited experience wherein novice serial creators cannot accurately gain knowledge and apply it to new projects. Novice serial creators need to run several projects to become effective creators. This finding is important because of the mixed evidence reported in the prior literature on serial creators (Courtney et al., 2017; Sewaid et al., 2021; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017; Zvilichovsky et al., 2015). Our work helps to reconcile conflicting findings and reveals the dynamic effects of creator experience over time.

Second, this paper adds a new theoretical perspective to the growing body of serial creators by applying the experience curve theory to the nascent crowdfunding research. Although the crowdfunding literature has emphasized the dynamic of serial creators and their fundraising performance, prior studies have mainly viewed experience as a type of social capital (Buttice et al., 2017; Skirnevskiy et al., 2017) or explored it through the lens of signaling theory (Courtney et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2022). However, serial creators are special entrepreneurs in the digital market, and their learning across campaigns is inherently an entrepreneurial learning behavior. Past studies have investigated creators’ entrepreneurial learning from experience but ignored the problems associated with limited experience and posited a linear experience-performance relationship (Yang & Hahn, 2015). Based on experience curve theory, we elaborate on why limited experience lowers crowdfunding success whereas enhanced performance occurs only after a given level of experience is accumulated. Our results empirically confirm the U-shaped experience curve with serial creators, challenging prior assumptions about serial creators’ entrepreneurial learning. Our research suggests that researchers should be cautious when looking into how serial creators’ learning processes can affect crowdfunding success.

Third, this paper enriches the understanding of the experience curve in entrepreneurial learning literature. Our results are consistent with the entrepreneurship literature, which proposes that the positive effect of entrepreneurial experience occurs only when experience has accumulated to a certain extent (Kim et al., 2009; Musaji et al., 2020; Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). This finding extends experience curve theory to the crowdfunding market context. Although we observe some consistency between our research and current knowledge on the topic, we suggest subtle differences that are worthy of further study.

Fourth, our results reveal that interproject diversity and interproject intervals moderate the experience curve in the crowdfunding market, which provides new insight into experience curve theory. Intuitively, interproject diversity may not be good for novice serial entrepreneurs due to their lack of experience. Previous entrepreneurial learning research has pointed out that interproject diversity can worsen the negative effect of limited experience (Toft-Kehler et al., 2014). However, in the crowdfunding context, interproject diversity may be beneficial for novice serial creators because crowdfunding is an innovation-based platform. The exploration of various project categories could enrich creators’ knowledge base and help them avoid superstitious learning. These results contribute to entrepreneurial learning literature by demonstrating the differences between traditional entrepreneurial learning and creator learning in the crowdfunding market.

## 6.2 Implications for Practice

This research has important implications for both project creators and platform designers in reward-based crowdfunding. For creators, our findings provide cautious notes on how experience can affect the likelihood of crowdfunding success in both directions. While our results indicate that learning from experience is difficult, our research generates some useful insights. We suggest that novice serial creators should launch and explore several different project categories because diversity brings more valid reference points and can compensate for limited experience. In addition, long intervals between projects are beneficial when the creator’s experience is limited. In such cases, creators may wish to spend time contemplating lessons learned from prior campaigns rather than rushing into the next campaign.

Even though veteran serial creators can generate accurate knowledge and causality between ventures, they should control their interproject diversity and interproject intervals. Veteran serial creators should refrain from launching too many new projects that are drastically different from past projects because knowledge derived from similar past experiences can help them deal with challenges and achieve crowdfunding success. Furthermore, launching projects quickly is an effective strategy for veteran serial creators. Thus, veteran serial creators are more likely to succeed if they keep interproject intervals short and focus on similar projects.

For platform owners, our findings provide guidance on optimizing platform functions to improve the likelihood of crowdfunding success. Experience has a curvilinear relationship with crowdfunding success. A deeper understanding of the importance of the creator’s experience would allow platform designers to optimize the information displayed on the project’s main page to better accommodate the backers’ needs and facilitate their investment decision-making. Furthermore, the moderating effects of contextual differences suggest that platform designers should display the number of campaign categories and campaign frequency on the creator’s homepage to help potential backers make informed investment decisions.

## 6.3 Limitations and Future Research Directions

This research has several limitations. First, the unique study context may limit the generalizability of our results. Data collection was conducted in one specific reward-based crowdfunding platform, Indiegogo.

Reward-based crowdfunding differs from other crowdfunding mechanisms in terms of the return on investment. The relationship between creators’ experience and crowdfunding success will thus differ in other types of crowdfunding.

Second, we selected a binary measurement, crowdfunding success, as the dependent variable to measure the effect of learning. While it is an appropriate variable theoretically, scholars have called for a renewed focus on the quality of learning outcomes (Musaji et al., 2020) because learning is an iterative and dynamic process with varying positive effects. Future research could select other performance measures, such as the pledge ratio or the number of backers, to investigate the curvilinear relationships between experience and crowdfunding performance.

Third, we used a cross-sectional research design to investigate experience curves in reward-based crowdfunding. However, because creator experience is gradually accumulated through learning over time, it would be interesting to examine how the changing volume of experience influences crowdfunding success. Future studies could use panel data to generate more insights into this effect.

## 7 Conclusion

Past research on serial creators has treated their experience mainly as a quality signal or as social capital to reveal the linear experience-performance relationship. However, the relationship between creator experience and crowdfunding success has not been fully understood, and the findings of prior research are inconclusive. We argue that serial creators’ experience helps to generalize knowledge from one venture and apply it effectively to a new situation. But according to entrepreneurial learning, experience may not improve performance because of superstitious learning when experience is limited. Building on the experience curve literature, we suggest that creator experience is gradually accumulated through learning over time and influences crowdfunding success in a nonlinear manner. Our findings suggest that the creator’s experience has a U-shaped relationship with crowdfunding success, confirming the existence of an experience curve in reward-based crowdfunding. Furthermore, we show that interproject diversity and interproject interval both moderate the curvilinear experience-performance relationship in the crowdfunding market. Our results advance the current understanding of serial creators’ experience in the reward-based crowdfunding context and provide practical guidelines for both creators and platform owners.
