# The Relative Efect of the Convergence of Product Recommendations from Various Online Sources

Jingjun (David) Xu <sup>a</sup>, Izak Benbasat<sup>b</sup>, and Ronald T. Cenfetelli<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Information Systems, College of Business, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong; <sup>b</sup>Accounting and Information Systems Division, Sauder School of Business, The University of British Columbia, Vancouver, Canada

## ABSTRACT

Most previous studies about online product recommendation sources (recommendation agents [RAs], consumers, and experts) have been limited to the evaluation by a single source on a website. Thus, the relative influence of convergent recommendations from diferent sources on consumers’ acceptance of the advice remains largely unknown. We draw upon and extend the product uncertainty model to theorize how the convergence of recommendations from various sources diferentially influences customers’ acceptance of recommendations. Our experiments show that the recommendation convergence between RAs and experts leads to the greater recommendation acceptance of the jointly recommended products than the convergence between experts and consumers or convergence between RAs and consumers. The rationale is that RAs best reduce fit uncertainty, and experts best reduce description and performance uncertainties. Experts and RAs complement each other by reducing all three dimensions of product uncertainty. Online merchants are advised to incorporate multiple sources into their websites, including sources (i.e., RAs and experts) that play complementary roles in reducing product uncertainty.

## Keywords

Online product uncertainty; description uncertainty; fit uncertainty; performance uncertainty; recommendation sources; online recommenders; convergent recommendations

## Introduction

E-commerce has continued to proliferate, and a large number of product choices are available online. Websites are increasingly providing product recommendations from multiple sources to help their customers reduce product uncertainty (ProU) and choose a suitable product from a wide variety of alternatives [70]. Examples of recommendation sources include fellow consumers [11, 89], product experts who possess recognized product knowledge and provide objective product opinions [76], and online recommendation agents (RAs) [1, 3, 6, 28, 84]. RAs assist online shoppers by eliciting their product preferences and making product recommendations that satisfy such preferences [84]. Prior literature suggests that information sources (e.g., experts and consumers) are important because they can afect customers’ attitude toward the provided information and can be more important than the information content [70]. Prior studies have extended our knowledge of the efects of various recommendation sources [4, 68, 76, 78,

79, 85-87]. However, subjects in these studies are exposed only to a single source on a website, and prior research is silent on the efects of recommendation convergence from multiple sources. The integration of multiple sources into one website ofers several benefits. First, online shoppers make an informed decision by having diferent points of view that provide them a broad understanding of the diferent choices and options available. Second, it provides a good overview of various recommendations found on the Internet so that shoppers can save the trouble of searching on multiple external websites. Given that comparing and cross-referencing among the three sources is easy, shoppers can appreciate the groundwork that website owners do for online shoppers to facilitate their product choice selection. Third, the integration gives users a second opinion and reduces the use of only one source that can be biased toward a certain brand or company. However, excessive information may confuse, overwhelm, and frustrate people. In this case, convergent recommendations from multiple sources help narrow down choices among products that one may be considering and enable shoppers to be certain and confident that they have made the right decision.

However, the relative efects of diferent combinations of multiple sources remain unknown, and the relative efect of multiple sources must be understood theoretically. Websites can readily provide multiple recommendation sources. For example, BestBuy, a well-recognized multinational retailer of technology products and services, provides RAs and consumer ratings on its website. Bedbathandbeyond.com and Bhphotovideo.com provide expert and consumer ratings to assist customers in choosing from a wide variety of electronic products. Tirerack.com ofers RAs, store experts, and consumer recommendations for its array of tire products. Amazon.com explores the use of expert reviews on top of its use of RA and consumer reviews<sup>1</sup>. Researchers hence must consider a highly realistic situation where multiple and diverse sources are available simultaneously, and investigate how the possible convergence of these sources influences recommendation acceptance. We define recommendation acceptance of a product as the extent to which a customer’s product choice matches the same product recommended by more than one source. Therefore, an important research question is: do convergent recommendations provided by diferent combinations of sources (e.g., RAs and experts, or experts and consumers) diferentially influence recommendation acceptance?

As the theory base to investigate the relative efect of multiple sources, we draw upon the ProU model [18, 34]. These previous studies have facilitated the identification of three dimensions of ProU that when reduced can increase the price premium, buyer satisfaction, and repurchase intention, among others [18, 34]. First, product description uncertainty (DeU) is due to the seller being unable to represent completely the characteristics of a product online. Second, product performance uncertainty (PerU) arises from a buyer’s concerns on a product’s long-term performance. Third, product fit uncertainty (FitU) originates from a buyer’s concerns of whether a product (or its characteristics) matches his/her needs.

Based on the tripartite conceptualization of ProU, we propose that users more likely accept convergent recommendations from two or more particular sources that can efectively reduce three distinct ProU dimensions than those convergent recommendations that reduce the same uncertainty dimensions to a lesser extent or reduce a lesser number of ProU dimensions. We theorize that the convergence of recommendations from RAs and experts (i.e., RAs ∩ experts)<sup>2</sup> can lead to greater recommendation acceptance than that of recommendations from any other two sources (i.e., experts ∩ consumers or RAs ∩ consumers). We attribute this expectation to the fact that experts and RAs complement each other by reducing all three dimensions of ProU. RAs best reduce FitU, and experts best reduce DeU and PerU. Consumers may also reduce DeU and PerU. However, their efect will not be additive on top of the presence of the experts, given that experts have a deeper and broader knowledge base than the consumers. To facilitate tractability, we focus on search products that can be identified with relevant attribute information (e.g., price, screen size, and weight) that a customer must assess before its use. Many content-based ${ \mathrm { R A } } s ^ { 3 }$ allow search products to be assessed adequately [84]. In addition, search products have a set of objective product attributes that can be observed with less subjectivity, therefore creating a shared reality (e.g., convergence) among the multiple recommendation sources.

This paper makes the following contributions. First, we integrate the three dimensions of ProU together in one study and advance the ProU model (used to identify the individual efects of ProU dimension) by proposing the efects of reducing the multiple dimensions of ProU simultaneously. Although each of these dimensions leads to describable outcomes for e-commerce merchants, the combined efect of the simultaneous reduction of multiple uncertainty dimensions has yet to be explored fully. Researchers should address this important theoretical void for the following reasons: (i) if the complementary efects of reducing the uncertainty of multiple product dimensions are overlooked, reduction of the additional dimension might be considered as incremental and ignored, and, thus, e-commerce merchants are placed at high risk of reducing such uncertainty partially or unsuccessfully; and (ii) when complementary capabilities are mistaken as substitutes, e-commerce merchants might focus on improving one dimension and overlooking the two other dimensions, leading to suboptimal performance. Second, we respond to the call of Dimoka et al. [18] for future research that focuses on reducing ProU in online markets with additional types of uncertainty mitigators. We do so by proposing RAs, experts, and consumers as joint alternative mitigators. Thus, we extend the ProU model in the context of multiple recommendation sources available on a single website, thereby marking a departure from prior research that has predominantly focused on websites that only provide a single recommendation source to their customers (e.g., [29, 38, 85]). Third, a relatively minor contribution is that we examine the efect of reducing uncertainty on recommendation acceptance, a consequence not previously examined in the ProU literature. Such a consequence is a more immediate outcome of ProU than distal outcomes, such as sales performance.

## Literature review and theoretical foundations

E-commerce recommendation sources have been used to help online shoppers increase product awareness, reduce information overload, and narrow down their product consideration set. However, reducing information overload does not necessarily indicate that the actual product choice decision in the subsequent decision stage becomes easy. Users still need to make a choice decision after learning about new products and facing information overload. We believe ProU will play a role in product selection. Therefore, we are going a step further and investigate even in cases where information overload is the same (all subjects facing three sources) and how ProU makes a diference.

## Product uncertainty model and signaling theory

The ProU model delineates the nature, dimensions, antecedents, and consequences of the ProU problem [18, 34]. The ProU model predicts that a reduction in ProU increases the price premiums in markets with asymmetric information, increases the satisfaction and repurchase-intention of buyers, and produces fewer product returns. The efects of ProU on these outcomes can be explained by information asymmetry literature [60], which shows that poorly informed consumers cannot diferentiate between high and low-quality products [81]. Consequently, these consumers are less likely to make a purchase decision and be satisfied with their product choice. By contrast, if buyers can discern the true characteristics of the product and evaluate a product correctly, they are more inclined to buy the products and are less likely to return those products [18].

The ProU model identifies three dimensions of uncertainty: DeU, PerU, and FitU. These dimensions of uncertainty are related yet distinct. DeU is observed because online sellers may be unable to perfectly describe their products via the lean Internet interface, such as the “touch and feel” of using a tablet. DeU may also result from a seller’s unawareness of, his/her unwillingness to describe, or his/her misinterpretation of the true characteristics of a product [18]. Customers in an online environment are faced with higher DeU compared with those in an ofline market because they are unable to physically inspect the product. Complete information about the products being sold on a website including the absence of a direct “touch and feel” diagnostic experience in online environments [25, 36] is unavailable. Hence, customers rely on diferent types of recommendation signals to reduce their ProU. Specifically, credible information signals can efectively reduce ProU [18] by helping buyers infer the value of products with unobservable quality and uncertain value [15] and facilitate their decision-making [74].

Performance uncertainty refers to the dificulty that buyers face when predicting how a product will perform in the future [18]. Customers may believe a product is described completely and accurately (DeU). However, they may still question whether its performance can be sustained over a certain period (PerU). Customers may know a laptop’s processor description is 1.5 GHz by reading the product descriptions. However, they may not know when a 1.5-GHz processor will fail in the future. The other diference between description and performance is similar to the diference between materiality and afordance [45]. Materiality describes the physical properties of a system, whereas afordance is what one can accomplish with that system. Knowledge of material property diferences (16- vs. 8-GB RAM) is disconnected from what can be accomplished with those diferences. Online shoppers may not necessarily know by how much an increase in RAM will influence performance by what amount. Thus, PerU is a separate dimension from DeU.

In the context of product recommendations, customers want to reduce the DeU and PerU of a product. In addition, they desire to reduce their FitU. FitU reflects “consumers dificulty in assessing the fit between a product’s attributes and their own preferences” [34, p. 329]. Individual preference mostly determines a person’s selection of a given product alternative [65]. Extant studies have demonstrated that once the content fits consumers preferences, they will seriously consider and eventually choose it [33, 42, 86]. Although products such as wired headphones may be clearly described, customers may walk away as they prefer wireless earphones. Similarly, even though customers may have confidence in the performance of a product, they worry about whether such a product can fit their individual needs and preferences. For example, a luxury car can be well described with excellent performance, but it does not fit an ordinary worker’s preferred budget and is beyond the daily commute needs. Simultaneously reducing all three dimensions of uncertainty can provide a worry-free product selection experience for customers. However, the literature does not theorize on how this simultaneous reduction can be achieved.

The conceptualization of the nature and dimensions of ProU provides a theoretical framework for identifying, designing and using IT-enabled solutions to reduce DeU, PerU, and FitU. The ProU model has been expanded and empirically tested to understand the efects of ProU and how it can be mitigated. For example, it has been used to examine the efects of product reviews [83], online product description [18], visualization system [34], a customer’s selection of new online goods (vs. used goods) [8], and a user’s adoption of new products [54].

As reviewed in Table 1 of Supplemental Online Appendix A, the three dimensions of ProU have emerged as key dimensions in diferent contexts, such as ofline shopping [32], online car auctions [8], and website evaluation [80]. We depart from prior studies on ProU in three aspects. First, previous studies have focused on only one or two dimensions (e.g., [43], [88]), whereas, we investigate three dimensions. Second, we examine the combined efects of ProU dimensions in contrast to previous studies that only examined their individual efects (e.g., [18]). Third, we examine how diferent dimensions of ProU can be reduced by diferent recommendation sources.

Contributing to the ProU research, we propose the recommendations of experts and consumers as two alternative information signals that help reduce DeU and PerU. These recommendations as signals of product quality have been well documented in the IS [18], marketing [7], and economics literature [39]. The IS and marketing literature suggests that information sources, such as word-of-mouth and recommendations from consumers or experts, influence consumers’ decision-making [66]. Information sources mitigate the uncertainty they face when they have minimal information about product quality [68, 76]. In addition, we identify an RA as a recommendation that can reduce FitU. RAs have become increasingly important to the success of online commerce [2, 38]. In response to the growing pressure from competitors [2], retailers with an online presence have developed RAs that can help customers select a product that best matches their personal needs. The efect of RAs in reducing product FitU was theorized and empirically supported by [34]. They found that if online shoppers visited websites with RAs before their product purchase, then they will perceive a low product FitU.

In the present study, we focus on experts and consumers’ numerical ratings (e.g., #1 ranked product) and not on their written and descriptive reviews because of several considerations. First, these ratings allow us to answer the research question on the convergence of recommendations. As convergent recommendations may facilitate product choice selection, users may not necessarily read written reviews to a great extent. Second, numeric ratings serve as a cue for written reviews by sending a visible, clear signal of aggregate opinions [40, 62], and meanwhile, prevent potential ambiguity arising from the mismatch between numerical ratings and reviews that can be frustrating and annoying to consumers [56]. Third, consumer product ratings have been used frequently in the IS literature to represent consumer reviews (e.g., [29]). Fourth, these ratings provide a similar context in which the products are rated by RAs (without reviews). Hence, bias in the comparison among the recommendations of experts, consumers, and RAs is prevented.

## Recommendation source, convergence, and acceptance

Senecal and Nantel [68] proposed a typology of four recommendation sources on the basis of whether the source is personal (human) and whether the source provides personalized information. Accordingly, these four sources are (1) Personal source providing personalized information (e.g., “My Mum says that this product is best for me.”); (2) Personal source providing non-personalized information (e.g., “other consumers rate that this product is the best.”); (3) Impersonal source providing personalized information (e.g., “Based on your input preference, the RA recommends this product to you.”); and (4) Impersonal source providing non-personalized information (e.g., “According to Consumer Reports, this is the best product on the market”). Following this classification, Senecal and Nantel [68] examined the diferences among consumers, experts, and RAs using a scenario-based approach. In the present study, the first category of recommendation is less common than an online recommendation source and thus excluded. Accordingly, we also select consumers, RAs, and experts to represent the other three categories of online recommendation sources, respectively, given that Consumer Reports or PC Magazine in the fourth category are based on experts’ opinions.

We also followed the focus of Senecal and Nantel [68] to examine the efects of recommendation sources while keeping the recommendation content largely constant. Wells et al. [81] applied signaling theory and empirically found that extrinsic cues can be used by sellers to convey product quality information to shoppers by reducing uncertainty. For example, sellers use websites’ visual appeal and navigability (similar to store owners who use furnishings and decorations) to signal product quality. These extrinsic cues (e.g., recommendation sources) can serve as good heuristics and even surrogate for intrinsic cues to assess product quality (e.g., recommendation content) because they are readily available and easily evaluated [91] with any expertise or knowledge of the product [64].

Integrating all three sources’ recommendations without convergent recommendations between any of the two sources (e.g., expert’s and RA’s recommendations are completely diferent) may complicate a consumer’s decision-making process and even reduce a recommendation’s acceptance. By contrast, for improved recommendation acceptance of a certain product, the product may have to be recommended by more than one source (i.e., recommendation convergence). A product recommended by a sole recommendation source cannot result in recommendation convergence. To achieve recommendation convergence, a product must be recommended by more than one source. In other words, the product recommendations of one source (e.g., RA) and another source (e.g., experts) should converge.

However, recommendation convergence varies across diferent combinations of multiple signal sources. The efect of recommendation convergence between RAs and experts may not be the same as that between consumers and experts. As we hypothesize in the following sections, what we advance in this paper is that recommendation convergence must be able to efectively reduce all three dimensions of ProU to achieve a better outcome. In other words, recommendation acceptance is a function of the combination of three diferent uncertainty dimensions that are addressed, and the extent to which each dimension is addressed. A product recommended by a sole recommendation source cannot efectively reduce all three dimensions of ProU. Recommendation convergence between any two sources cannot necessarily be able to do the same, such as convergence between experts and consumers, as either source cannot reduce FitU. However, convergence between RA ∩ experts (∩ consumers) can reduce all three dimensions of ProU efectively. Moreover, these convergent recommendations likely receive a high acceptance rate.

The rationale for focusing on recommendation acceptance is fourfold. First, the RA literature has considered recommendation acceptance to be a key outcome in the evaluation of the efects of diferent RA designs (e.g., [42]). Thus, extending this stream of literature that uses recommendation acceptance to investigate the combined efects of RA with other recommendation sources seems appropriate. Second, we investigate the efects of reducing ProU on a customer’s recommendation acceptance of products that are recommended by multiple sources. In doing so, we distinguish our work from previous studies (e.g., [42]) that focus on a consumer’s acceptance of a single source’s recommendation. Hence, the antecedents of recommendation acceptance are also diferent. For example, Komiak and Benbasat [42] identified a user’s perceived fit of the product to his/her needs as the sole factor that could influence his/her intention to accept the recommendations of an RA. However, we contend that recommendation acceptance is a product not only of reducing FitU but also of reducing DeU and PerU. Third, consumer review literature has focused mainly on a user’s perceptions of online reviews, such as the perceived helpfulness of the consumer review [55] and its perceived value [67]. On the contrary, the literature on information sources has focused on perceived quality [59]. We extend this stream of research by studying recommendation acceptance, which is a behavioral variable. Prior research has studied how a certain recommendation source increases sales. However, recommendation acceptance can better capture the direct efect of recommendation sources. Sales as a dependent variable may be influenced by other factors not related to the recommendation sources, such as a complicated checkout process, payment issues, and high shipping fees. Fourth, as a behavioral measure, studying recommendation acceptance has practical implications for online merchants. McKinsey reports that 35% of what consumers purchase on Amazon.com and 75% of what they watch on Netflix come from product recommendations [49]. A total of 76% of the top 500 Internet retailers feature product recommendations on their websites [27].

## Hypothesis development

## Diferential efects of RAs ∩ experts vs. experts ∩ consumers

We draw upon the ProU model to determine whether the convergence between the recommendations of RAs and experts will lead to a diferent level of recommendation acceptance. The result was compared with that of the convergence between the recommendations of experts and consumers.

An RA elicits a customer’s preferences and recommends based on such information [38]. RA can reduce FitU for two reasons [34]. First, RAs compare diferent products by eliciting the product preferences of customers. Second, RAs help overcome the limitations inherent in human cognition by helping customers process what may be a large number of alternatives. RAs also lead shoppers through the initial search and pre-screening process toward forming a relevant product consideration set. In summary, RA’s recommendations signal that these search products fit a user’s preferences for product attributes. The experts or consumers’ ratings do not consider a particular customer’s individual product preferences. Hence, we do not expect that FitU can be reduced by either of these ratings. Compared with experts and customers, RAs by design are less able to inform customers on the real-life descriptive usage experience and long-term performance of products, and accordingly are less able to reduce DeU and PerU. This is because sellers typically provide the product databases and descriptions that RAs screen and analyze, respectively.

Consumers and experts are information signals that can help reduce DeU and PerU and allow potential shoppers to distinguish high-quality (recommended) from low-quality (non-recommended) products. Consumers have presumably used the products they purchase for a certain period before making recommendations. Thus, these consumer recommendations can signal whether the actual product features are consistent with what has been described online. If actual product features are found to deviate from the description (e.g., the application loading time of a tablet takes longer than what is claimed by the seller), then these products will not be highly recommended (rated) by consumers. In addition, during their product usage, those consumers can evaluate its basic performance, such as a tablet’s response time to screen tapping and any lags when scrolling through pages. If consumers have experienced any performance issues, they will be less inclined to rate the products favorably or recommend them to other consumers. Therefore, these recommendations are expected to reflect the actual customer experiences and satisfaction [55].

Expert sources can also reduce DeU and PerU by providing product recommendations. Experts are perceived to have experience and an in-depth understanding of the product [72]. Although certain product features (e.g., a tablet’s capability to connect to a desktop monitor screen) are not included in a seller’s product description, experts may infer or discover these features and factor in efectively them when recommending a product. If a seller misrepresents a particular product feature (e.g., a tablet’s battery lasting for five hours and yet advertised as lasting for 10 hours), then experts can also identify these features and incorporate them into their product recommendations (rating). Thus, experts’ recommendations are expected to signal the accuracy of the product attribute descriptions even if certain descriptions have been overlooked by the seller in his/her own description. In addition, experts presumably have tested the product to know its performance and help reduce PerU. For example, they can detect issues in a tablet’s basic (e.g., required interface gestures and Wi-Fi connection quality) and advanced features (e.g., compatibility with certain applications). Empirical findings in marketing show that experts have a positive efect on decision confidence [23].

Taken together, RAs ∩ experts are expected to reduce all three dimensions of ProU, whereas experts ∩ consumers are expected to reduce only DeU and PerU. The ProU model implies that if many dimensions of uncertainty can be reduced, then the price premium, buyer satisfaction, and intention to repurchase are high. This finding is consistent with information signaling literature that multiple cues can help to address information asymmetries [81]. Thus, we expect those mechanisms that reduce simultaneously all three dimensions of uncertainties the most are better than those that can reduce only two dimensions. Specifically, as compared with the convergent recommendations between experts and consumers, the convergence of RAs’ recommendations with those of experts can additionally reduce FitU, since the positive efect of reducing FitU is stipulated in the ProU model and supported empirically.

One may argue about the additional efect of the consumer source in reducing DeU and PerU in the case of experts ∩ consumers convergent recommendations. However, the breadth and depth of knowledge of the average consumers are usually far less extensive than those of experts. Although experts are outnumbered by consumers, experts are considered authorities on the domain subject [94]. In addition, the diverse amount of consumers’ usage experiences could result in recommendation noise and biases. Thus, consumers’ assessment of the accuracy of a search product’s description and performance is usually seen as less credible than that of an expert (additional details are provided in the discussion of H2). Experts have professional knowledge on the domain subject, can discover hidden features of products, and provide information on the products’ longterm performance. Hence, experts’ recommendations should reduce DeU and PerU by a relatively considerable degree compared with those of consumers. In the presence of expert sources, the addition of consumer source to reduce further ProU has a marginal efect. Previous studies investigating multiple information cues suggested that when people are presented with multiple information cues, the more salient information cues may dominate the less salient ones [37]. Experts may dominate the consumers’ role in reducing DeU and PerU. Thus,

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The recommendation convergence between RAs and experts (RAs ∩ experts) will lead to greater recommendation acceptance of the jointly recommended products as compared with convergence between experts and consumers (experts ∩ consumers).

## Diferential efects of RAs ∩ experts and RAs ∩ consumers

As noted earlier, RAs are best at reducing FitU, whereas experts or consumers can reduce DeU and PerU. RAs ∩ experts and RAs ∩ consumers can reduce all three dimensions of uncertainties simultaneously. We acknowledge that consumers’ recommendations have benefits compared with their absence in a website. Consumers, who have purchased and used the product in reality, know the product they own intensely and consequently, they can relate their actual use experience. These experiences may add value in reducing customers’ DeU and PerU. However, we predict that RAs ∩ experts will lead to greater recommendation acceptance than RAs ∩ consumers because experts’ recommendations will reduce DeU and PerU to a greater extent than consumers’ recommendations. Moreover, experts’ recommendations are more credible than those of consumers; the reasoning behind this diference is based on the costs of providing a false signal and the literature regarding relatively biased consumer reviews.

First, information signaling literature [71] states that in situations with information asymmetry (e.g., the level of quality is known by the business but not by the customer), a business sends its customers a signal to relay information on an unobservable product (or company) quality credibly [63]. The objective is to reduce ProU [18]. Signal credibility is an important criterion for determining an efective information signal [31, 63, 81]. High signal credibility is observed when the signal receivers believe the signal senders have made a significant investment in sending this signal. Moreover, their investment is at risk if they have sent a false signal [18, 81]. A false signal is considered costly for experts who recommend low-quality products because their reputation is at stake when they send a wrong signal on the quality of the products. By contrast, consumers incur a relatively low cost when recommending low-quality products because the origin of such recommendations cannot be traced easily considering numerous anonymous contributors [12]. Thus, signals sent by experts through their product recommendations are relatively more credible and able to reduce DeU and PerU as compared with signals sent by other consumers.

Second, experts’ opinions are valued consistently by prospective customers because of their perceived credibility [24, 69]. We argue that the efects of experts over consumers in reducing DeU and PerU will be prominent for search products associated with technical information (e.g., health issues) and technology products [9]. The value of consumer’s usage experience fades for search products because complete information on product attributes can be acquired prior to experience and purchase. Findings from the studies of the Forrester Research [24] and Nielsen [58] support this contention. For example, through a survey of 900 respondents, Nielsen compared experts and consumers directly across nine product categories, including smartphones, smart televisions, and automobiles. He found that customers’ purchase consideration was 83% higher with third-party expert content than with consumer reviews from major online retailers. Nielsen concluded that prospective customers place more trust on experts than on consumer reviews because the former can provide unbiased assessments of products.

Experts typically evaluate products with an accepted standard, which results in less variation in the product ratings [61]. In contrast, even when consumers’ usage experiences are aggregated, their efects may not be as strong as experts’ recommendations in reducing DeU and PerU because heterogeneous consumers’ experiences result in noise and biases. This assertion is supported by many studies showing that consumers’ recommendations are less credible than those of experts [21 26, 61]. For example, Duan et al. [21] found that the rating of online user reviews does not afect box ofice revenues and has a little persuasive efect on consumer purchase decisions. Other studies also find that the online product reviews on Amazon.com do not reflect the true quality of the products being evaluated [35] due to acquisition (customers with a favorable disposition usually obtain a product first and then write a review later) and underreporting biases (customers with extreme ratings have a higher tendency to write reviews than those with moderate product ratings). For example, online consumer ratings and physician quality are not necessarily correlated because physicians with low patient-perceived quality are less likely to be rated online while those with high patient-perceived quality ratings tend to receive exaggerated reviews[26]. Even worse, fake reviews were reported as a common problem on the Web [41, 44], and a large proportion of consumers is aware of the presence of bias in online consumer reviews [90]. Certainly, expert recommendations are not always better than consumer recommendations in all circumstances. Consumer reviews stood out in categories in which customers tend to have a higher degree of product expertise. For example, mothers perceived other mothers as “experts” in purchasing child car seats [58]. However, this example shows ordinary consumers’ recommendation is not as credible as experts authoritative advice in reducing ProU.

Based on the reasons presented (cost of sending a false signal and signal credibility), we infer that experts’ recommendations are more efective than those of consumers in reducing DeU and PerU and in improving recommendation acceptance.

Hypothesis 2 (H2): The recommendation convergence between RAs and experts (RAs ∩ experts) results in greater recommendation acceptance of jointly recommended products as compared with the convergence between RAs and consumers (RAs ∩ consumers).

## Efects of RAs ∩ experts and three sources on recommendation acceptance

We further propose that RAs ∩ experts will have the same extent of recommendation acceptance as that of RAs ∩ experts ∩ consumers. The arguments for H2 show that consumers’ recommendations are less credible than those of experts [9, 58], particularly for search products. The efect of expert source dominates the efect of the consumer one. Moreover, adding consumers’ recommendations to RAs ∩ experts is not expected to contribute substantially to the already high level of recommendation acceptance provided by RAs ∩ experts. Adding a powerful argument to an average argument could make the point convincing. However, adding an average argument to an existing powerful argument may not enhance the persuasiveness of an argument all that much. Prior research has discussed the diminishing efect of social influence. In the context of Technology Adoption, Venkatesh and Morris [75] suggest “individuals begin to ‘internalize’ others opinions” (p. 122) and focus on their own judgments.” Likewise, when experts can reduce DeU and PerU substantially, adding a consumer source as an additional source may also have a diminishing efect. More importantly, the number of ProU dimensions that can be best reduced is the same between RAs ∩ experts and RAs ∩ experts ∩ consumers. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3 (H3): The recommendation convergence between RAs and experts (RAs ∩ experts) will lead to the same level of recommendation acceptance of jointly recommended products as that of the recommendation convergence among RAs, experts, and consumers (RAs ∩ experts ∩ consumers).

## First experiment

Before testing these hypotheses, we performed Experiment 1 with a 1×3 recommendation sources (RAs only, experts only, consumers only) between-subject design to confirm our following theorizations: 1) DeU and PerU are best reduced by experts’ recommendations (H2’s arguments); 2) FitU is best reduced by RA’s recommendations (H1’s arguments); and 3) DeU, PerU, and FitU is negatively associated with recommendation acceptance. The results from this experiment will lay the groundwork for testing the hypotheses in Experiments 2 and 3.

The subjects were recruited from an online research panel via Amazon Mechanical Turk™. In recent years, MTurk has become a popular platform for behavioral research in many disciplines because this panel provides access to high-quality online data from working professionals and produces highly generalizable results [5]. We recruited MTurk subjects in North America with task acceptance rates of higher than 90%. 160 respondents completed their questionnaires, among whom 26 failed to correctly answer the attention checking questions (e.g., “For this question, please select the third button counting from the left-hand side.”). These 26 questionnaires are excluded.

## Shopping task and incentives

The subjects were randomly assigned to one of the following groups: i) recommendations based on RAs; ii) based on consumers; or iii) based on experts. They were tasked to choose a tablet for themselves from one of the shopping websites specifically designed for this study. Each experimental website featured the same 30 tablets. Seven key product attributes and features (e.g., price, storage, processor, memory, screen size, weight, and battery) were included for each product. The number of product attributes was delineated because a person’s immediate memory span is about 7 +/– 2 items [52]; in this case, providing a person with 10 or more product attributes drastically decreases his/her ability to cognitively process alternatives [50].

The subjects were provided with a \$2.50 incentive for their participation in the study. They were also informed that only the serious and conscientious subjects would be awarded an additional \$1.50. We verified the seriousness and conscientiousness of the subjects by 1) checking if they answered the “trap questions” correctly (e.g., “Please select the third button counting from the left-hand side”) and 2) checking if they spent a reasonable amount of efort to answer the open-ended questions (e.g., “Please tell us why you selected that tablet”).

## Second experiment

The objective of Experiment 1 was to establish the relationships between recommendation sources and diferent dimensions of ProU. Specifically, it evaluates whether each source can reduce certain dimensions of ProU, and whether the certain source can reduce the certain dimension of ProU more efectively than other source(s). In addition, it determines the relationship between the reduction of ProU and recommendation acceptance. They serve as the foundations for the three hypotheses testing. However, only one source was presented to the participants in Experiment 1. Thus, the efects of convergent recommendations cannot be determined. Experiment 2 addresses this issue by examining the relative efects of convergent recommendations from multiple diferent sources.

## Shopping task, incentives, experimental procedures, and website design

To test the hypotheses, we performed an online experiment (Experiment 2) where we manipulated the recommendation convergence of multiple sources. The subjects were randomly assigned to one of the following five experimental groups: 1) no convergence, 2) recommendation convergence between RAs and experts, 3) between RAs and consumers, 4) between experts and consumers, and 5) convergence among three sources. As noted in the Introduction, many websites provide more than one recommendation source in their single website to facilitate online shoppers’ speedy choice decision process. Although website store owners may control sources’ recommended products if sources are integrated into one website, we assume that vendors are honest or they risk customer backlash.

## Manipulation checks

Similar to Experiment 1, we probed the subjects’ awareness of the three recommendation sources in the study and checked computer logs to determine whether the subjects clicked and accessed each of the three recommendation sources. Around 15.2% of the subjects accessed only one source, 23.7% accessed two sources, and 61.1%, accessed all three sources. Other manipulation checks were performed to measure the perceived convergence among the sources, perceived product fit, and perceived source credibility. Supplemental Online Appendix A Table 3 shows their measurements. The subjects perceived a higher level of source convergence when some products (vs. none) were recommended jointly by multiple sources $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , mean diference = 0.72). To examine whether users perceived $\mathrm { R A } s ^ { 2 }$ recommended products had a better fit, an independent sample t-test was conducted between those users who followed the RAs’ recommendations and those that did not. A significant diference $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 ,$ , mean diference = 0.46) in perceived fit was observed. Given that credible signals are highly efective in reducing DeU and PerU [18], we expect that the experts’ recommendations are perceived to be more credible than those of consumers in reducing DeU and PerU. Paired-sample t-tests between experts’ and consumers’ perceived credibility indicated that experts were perceived to be more credible than consumers (p < 0.01; expert = 5.91, consumers = 5.64, mean diference = 0.27), thereby supporting our expectations. These results were consistent with those from Experiment 1, which show that experts can reduce DeU and PerU more than consumers (see Table 3). In addition, subjects highly trust the website that integrated all three sources (mean = 5.78/7.00).

## Third experiment

Experiment 2 investigated the relative efects of convergent recommendations from multiple diferent sources. However, it does not directly measure the participants’ perceived reduction of three dimensions of ProU. In addition, its sample size is relatively small (about 27 per cell), and thus the evidence of the generalization is limited. Thus, Experiment 3 measured the three dimensions of ProU from the subjects accessing three recommendation sources simultaneously. It also intended to demonstrate the robustness of the key results from the first and second experiments with a large sample size (about 74 per cell), which far exceeds the sample required to assure a suficient statistical power of 0.80 for detecting a medium efect size [13]. Experiment 3 also difers from Experiment 1, wherein subjects viewed one recommendation source without awareness of the other two. As it turns out, the data sample is diferent from that of Experiment 2, wherein subjects are relatively older and possess less product knowledge (Table 2).

The design, manipulation, and procedures were the same as those of Experiment 2. The measurements for three uncertainty dimensions are the same as those of Experiment 1. Like Experiment 1, the subjects were recruited from MTurk and were provided with a \$3.00 incentive and a potential award of \$2.0. A total of 590 respondents completed their questionnaires, but 53 did not follow the instructions appropriately, so these 53 questionnaires were excluded. Table 2 summarizes the demographic data of the subjects.

Among the remaining 537 respondents, 21.8% of them accessed only one source, 9.3% accessed two sources, and 68.9%, accessed all three sources. Among those who accessed all three sources, they perceived a higher level of source convergence when recommendation convergence was available than when recommendation convergence was absent (p < 0.001, mean diference = 1.77). In particular, subjects assigned to the respective groups (e.g., RAs ∩ experts) perceived a higher level of source convergence between those sources (e.g., RAs and experts) than the other groups that do not have such convergence $\left( \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 \right)$ 2 indicating the success of the manipulation. In addition, recommendations from RAs were perceived to reduce more FitU than those from experts $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 $ , mean diference = 0.25) and consumers $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , mean diference = 0.58). Moreover, recommendations from experts were perceived to reduce more PerU than those from RAs $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , mean diference = 0.34) and consumers $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , mean diference = 0.57). Regarding DeU, recommendations from experts reduced more DeU than those from RAs $( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1$ mean diference = 0.31) and consumers (p < 0.1, mean diference = 0.16). Overall, findings in Experiment 3 confirm those found in Experiment 1 wherein subjects accessed one source only.

As we did in Experiment 2, we ran a binary logistic regression with the same set of control variables to test our hypotheses. The results show that recommendations from RAs ∩ experts lead to a greater acceptance than those from experts ∩ consumers (diference = 28%; p < 0.001) and those from RAs ∩ consumers (diference = 20%; p < 0.05), thereby supporting H1 and H2. These findings confirm the results obtained from Experiment 2.

## Contributions

## Theoretical contributions

The present study makes several theoretical contributions. First, we extend the ProU model by elaborating that ProU consists of three dimensions. Dimoka t al. [18] proposed two ProU dimensions (DeU and PerU), and Hong and Pavlou [34] focused on FitU without separating the other two uncertainty dimensions. The absence of a tripartite concept of ProU prevents readers from comprehending the actual meaning of ProU; consequently, how to reduce ProU systematically is still not understood. Drawing on related research, we theorize that ProU consists of three uncertainty dimensions to provide a comprehensive and foundational concept for future research. We contribute to the literature on ProU by first integrating these dimensions in one study. We further propose that if the recommendation sources can best reduce the three dimensions of ProU, then the likelihood for customers to accept these sources’ recommendations is high. We reveal that reducing one or two dimensions of uncertainties cannot lead to the best outcome, thereby implying the absence of substitution efects between the three ProU dimensions. For example, the reduction of DeU and PerU cannot substitute the reduction of FitU (e.g., experts ∩ consumers cannot substitute experts ∩ RAs in H1), and vice versa (see analysis in Supplemental Online Appendix C). Instead, the reductions of the three uncertainty dimensions need to complement one another to achieve a better level of recommendation acceptance. All three dimensions of ProU need to be reduced independently and simultaneously to obtain an improved outcome. The degree of their reduction is also important when the same number of ProU dimensions is reduced (RA ∩ consumers < RA ∩ experts in H2).

We also advance the ProU model by including a new variable, namely, recommendation acceptance. Selecting a product with less ProU and placing the product into a shopping cart does not guarantee that the product will be purchased at the end. Online shopping cart abandonment rate worldwide in 2019 is 69.57%.<sup>9</sup> Thus, compared with price premium and purchase amount, which are related remotely to uncertainty reduction, our focus on recommendation acceptance allows us to observe the direct causal efect of ProU.

Second, the present study also contributes to the recommendation source literature. We respond to the call of Dimoka et al. [18] for attempts to reduce ProU in online markets with IT-enabled solutions by integrating three recommendation sources into a single website. Accordingly, we challenge the common sense that convergence among multiple recommendation sources always results in higher recommendation acceptance than that among fewer sources. H3 is not rejected and once the RAs and experts are in place, the consumer source is no longer needed, thereby indicating that experts can be substitutes for consumers’ recommendations. In fact, the number of ProU dimensions best reduced by sources is important. We also adopt the ProU model as our theoretical basis to explain the efect of sources on choice selection. The present study is the first to apply this model to the context of recommendation sources. This model provides a new lens with which to investigate the efects of recommendation sources on recommendation acceptance, and highlight the importance of the credibility of recommendation sources that can reduce ProU.

Third, our work contributes to broad information signaling literature in two aspects. On the one hand, we show that sources are perceived diferently in terms of their ability to reduce certain types of ProU. Thus, one cannot assume that sources have similar efects. Certain signals may be stronger than the others [81]. We extend this literature by showing that experts convey better signals than consumers in reducing DeU and PerU. On the other hand, information signals literature notes that knowing how receivers combine signals to make their decisions is an important yet under-researched area. Prior literature has focused mostly on a given signal, but scholars should pay attention to highly complex formulations of signals [14]. Obviously, the outcome would be better if the signals are consistent. However, which signal consistency among diferent recommendation sources leads to better results is not clear. In other words, when these three sources (RAs, consumers, and experts) are presented together to customers, the efect of diferent convergent recommendations among the sources is largely unknown. The present study fills the void in the literature by investigating the diferential efects of signal consistency among multiple recommendation signals.

Finally, we contribute to RA literature by demonstrating the importance and benefits of combining experts and RAs’ recommendations in the e-commerce context. Prior RA studies assumed that RAs are useful when they can reduce FitU. Our research indicates that customers require the reduction of FitU but also desire the reduction of DeU and PerU. In this regard, RAs ∩ experts fare better as compared with other combinations of any other two sources. Moreover, previous studies on RAs rarely use adult subjects [78, 79, 86]. By contrast, we obtain our findings from adult subjects (over 34 years old on average) who appear to be highly representative of our target population.

## Practical contributions

The present study ofers significant implications for practitioners. First, we determined that participants in our study tend to access two or three recommendation sources (84% in Experiment 2 and 78% in Experiment 3) when making their choice decisions. Readers should be cautious in generalizing our results to all real-life contexts. Customers may not behave the same way and access more than one source when faced with an actual product decision situation (vs. the experimental setup). This finding is because the participants may know they were part of a study and might have adapted their behavior accordingly. However, for high involvement products (e.g., car or laptops), we expect customers would have the motivation to look at multiple sources from multiple websites before making a purchase. Thus, e-commerce merchants should provide their customers with multiple recommendation sources to reduce information asymmetry and ProU. With the rapid development of technology, customers are becoming increasingly overwhelmed by the amount of product information they receive. For example, an online retailer such as Amazon.com ofers its customers over 18 million items. Given the sheer volume of information available on the Internet, customers may lack the time and efort to access complete information before making a product selection decision. Providing multiple online sources can help customers make highly informed product choices. More than 70% of the subjects in our study accessed more than one recommendation source. Hence, providing only one source may result in losing customers who prefer to access two or three sources. Customers might leave an online merchant that does not provide them with their preferred sources. This finding is an important yet often overlooked implication as few online vendors provide all three sources together.

Second, by providing diferent recommendation sources, online merchants can leverage the recommendations from diferent sources further to create recommendation convergence. People tend to avoid uncertainty and reduce risks when making decisions. Many dimensions of ProU can be reduced when many sources are available. Thus, website merchants should strive to incorporate multiple recommendation sources into a single website. Accordingly, customers reduce uncertainty by following convergent recommendations from multiple sources. Without question, we do not advise merchants to manipulate the recommendation convergence among sources. However, no convergence efect can be present if only a single source is available on a website.

Third, our findings can also inform online merchants on which combinations of two sources are most important. The RAs can reduce product FitU while the experts can reduce DeU and PerU more than consumers can. Hence, those online merchants that can implement only two sources due to resource or budget constraints must incorporate RAs and experts into their websites. The present study’s findings are relevant to practitioners who sell search products, such as electronic products (e.g., laptops, and TVs), ofice stationery (e.g., printers), and home appliances (e.g., dishwashers and refrigerators). These products typically have a set of objective attributes for users to evaluate. They difer from experience products whose attributes are subjective wherein consumers’ recommendations could be more useful and accurate.

An interesting question arises regarding whether the lowest fitted products supported by multiple sources or better-fitted products without any convergence are better. Group 1 in Experiments 2 and 3 has no recommendation convergence. The recommendation acceptance for each source in Experiment 2 is as follows: 40% for experts, 33% for RAs, and 26% for consumers. RA’s recommendations, in this case, are better-fitted products without any convergence. Group 4’s (experts and consumers) recommendation acceptance is 32%. The convergent recommendations in Group 4 are the lowest fitted products supported by multiple sources (experts or consumers). A comparison of Group 1 RA’s recommendation acceptance (33%) with Group 4’s experts ∩ consumer’s recommendation acceptance (32%) in Experiment 2 shows that the diference is not significant. Basing on the data of Experiment 3, the same conclusion was reached. Thus, we conclude that recommending better-fitted products supported by multiple sources is important.

From the customers’ perspective, they should be aware that merchants likely present recommendations that are only convergent in recommendation sources to influence consumer behavior. The convergent recommendations could be genuine. Particularly, they are presented from reputable merchants. However, when visiting a less reputable website, customers should investigate the convergent recommendations carefully. They need to confirm whether these products really fit their preferences and crosscheck other websites to verify the products’ description and performance.

In our study, three diferent recommendation sources are integrated into one website with experts and consumers based on third-party websites (i.e., CNET). If consumers are from the same website and experts are self-appointed, then online shoppers may perceive that the website owner has control over all of these recommendation sources and discount the credibility of the product recommendations from sources, especially those from experts. Senecal and Nantel [68] manipulated experts with a web store’s own experts<sup>10</sup> and found that experts have the same trustworthiness level as consumers. Wang and Doong [76] manipulated experts with third-party experts (similar to the New York Times in the USA) and revealed that experts are perceived to be more trustworthy than consumers. These results, along with our study outcomes, indicates that when the expert source stated from a third party is perceived to be more trustworthy than the source from its own online store.

The main thrust of the paper is about the influence of the efective reduction of the three ProU dimensions on recommendation acceptance. Our developed hypotheses test this main idea and use the sources that can efectively reduce the three types of ProU (RAs ∩ experts) to compare with those that are less able to (i.e., RAs ∩ consumers and experts ∩ consumers). To this end, we employ the strong form of experts (i.e., CNET experts, a third-party website) who are expected to efectively reduce DeU and PerU. If in-house experts are adopted, then their efectiveness in reducing ProU may have been limited and may not have served the goal of the study.

Thus, one may expect that the influences of the convergence of various sources can difer. For example, convergent recommendations from sources (experts ∩ consumers) based on self-appointed experts can have fewer impacts on recommendation acceptance than experts from third-party websites. Accordingly, our advice for practitioners is that experts should be solicited from publicly available and reputable channels such as CNET and Consumer Reports to make the expert recommendations valuable. They are less biased. Self-appointed experts may be perceived to have afiliation with websites and be biased. Hence, a website should avoid appointing private experts or training its own staf to be experts.

## Limitations and future research

The present study has several limitations that may open avenues for future research. First, we included only one product (tablet) in our experiment. Tablets are search products that have attributes that can be determined online. However, consumer recommendations may have more weight in influencing the customers’ perceptions of experience products. Determining whether certain attributes of experience products (e.g., restaurants) is desirable is subjective. Therefore, direct experience from numerous consumers will be valuable. By contrast, RA may not be able to reduce FitU efectively for experience products because the attributes of experience products are dificult to describe using specific attributes [16]. Although experience products can be categorized into diferent attribute values, customers may have dificulty evaluating these products even if the RA’s recommendations meet customers’ expectations prior to purchase (e.g., what may be delicious to some may not be the same as others). On the contrary, product attributes of search products can be evaluated based on the values attached to their attributes without necessitating users to experience the products directly. Together, we believe the reduction of all three ProU dimensions is still important for experience products. However, the extent of the three recommendation sources in reducing uncertainty dimensions might difer. Hence, future studies should consider experience products (e.g., restaurants and movies) and other search products when replicating the findings of this research.

Second, we only consider three specific recommendation sources. From the theoretical perspective and the purpose of testing the ProU model, recommendation sources serve as proxies to diferent levels of ProU. The RA implemented in our study is a content-filtering RA that elicits users’ preferences and incorporates those preferences towards making product recommendations [85]. Another type of RA is a collaborative-filtering RA based on the opinions of like-minded people to generate recommendations for users [47]. Users may have many diferent interests and an item’s name may have diferent meanings [38]. Hence, collaborative-filtering RA may not be able to interpret accurately a user’s true preference and generate recommendations that fit a user’s preference as content-filtering RA does. We chose the content-based RA as the best RA for reducing FitU to test the ProU model. However, other kinds of RA deserve further research to determine whether the current study results still hold. For example, if an RA uses collaborative filtering, its recommendations based on similar consumers can more likely be convergent than those based on consumer sources, and if an RA is based on certain expert inputs, RA results may converge to expert recommendations. Similarly, future research should also investigate the influence of other recommendation sources, such as friends or family members, especially with the growth of online social networking [10]. The work presented in the present study is an important first step before proceeding to the evaluation of other sources.

Our operationalization of consumers and experts’ recommendations is not very sophisticated. Experts can recommend products that may be perfect for a specific context (e.g., computers perfect for gaming). A website can also separate consumers’ rating scores into business travelers, couples, families with kids, etc. In that way, FitU could be reduced by expert and consumer sources. However, we believe RAs will still be the best tool in reducing FitU because of the heterogeneous nature of the potential customers (e.g., a tablet with seven attributes, each possessing five values, can lead to 78,125 diferent potential product fit scenarios). Nevertheless, implementing an alternative design of recommendation sources should be an interesting extension. We adopted experts or consumers’ ratings as a proxy for the recommendations of these sources. The absence of the written reviews (e.g., the product breaks after three years) may not capture fully the long-term performance of the product and thus help users reduce PerU. Providing written reviews may not necessarily change the study results that experts are best in reducing PerU. However, future research can employ an advanced operationalization for recommendation sources (e.g., human-like RAs [17], why and how explanation facilities for RAs [77, 92, 93], and written text reviews with diferent lengths, and personal feedback for experts and consumers). We hope our research has opened avenues for future research on recommendation convergence between experts’ and consumers’ review contents. However, the presence of convergent recommendations from multiple sources (signaling better quality of the product) may reduce the need for customers to read product review comments, given their limited time and resources. Indeed, humans are “cognitive misers” [22] who are not willing to depend on more cues than necessary to form their impressions.

To test our proposed theory, we integrate three diferent sources into one website and manipulate their recommendation convergence. The results likely difer if the subjects are asked to access third-party websites for reviewing sources’ product recommendations, given that subjects may trust these external sources. However, subjects’ perceived credibility of stores and recommendation sources is high, indicating that they have little doubt about stores’ trustworthiness in the study. Expert and consumer sources are based on CNET.com, which is a reputable website. Shoppers may still perceive expert sources situated in original third-party websites to be more trustworthy. Although this setup may further improve the trustworthiness of the combination of RA∩ experts and thus may not change our hypothesis testing results (e.g., RA∩ experts is better than RA ∩ consumers), examining whether the same case occurs in future research may be prudent.

Finally, we investigate the relative efects of convergent recommendations from the perspective of the reduction of ProU. Our study finds out which of the sources’ convergent recommendations are better when facing these three recommendation sources. Future research aiming to study multiple recommendation sources may explore what optimal number of sources should be presented to online shoppers with respect to other shopping goals, such as product awareness enhancement and information overload avoidance. For example, a great number of sources may increase product awareness but increase information overload. How to strike a balance in the number of recommendation sources to be presented in an E-commerce website is an interesting and practical future research question. Future research can also investigate the efect of recommendation convergence from another perspective, such as whether the number of convergent recommendations presented (e.g., one vs. four) can cause an absolute diference in recommendation acceptance.

## Conclusions

E-commerce is full of ProU and online shoppers want to reduce all three dimensions of such uncertainty. Based on the ProU model, we investigate the relative efects of converging the recommendations from various sources that reduce the diferent degrees of ProU. Recommendations from RAs ∩ experts can result in higher recommendation acceptance than those from sources of RAs ∩ consumers or experts ∩ consumers. This finding can be explained theoretically by the fact that RAs ∩ experts can reduce all three dimensions of ProU simultaneously to a greater extent. Online firms are increasingly utilizing RAs, consumers’ opinions, and, to a lesser extent, experts’ opinions to assist online shoppers in making informed decisions. We contribute to theoretical advancements in consumer decision-making in the e-commerce context by investigating customers’ choice behavior when faced with recommendations from multiple sources. In particular, the present study fills the research gap by ofering insights into the joint roles of multiple recommendation sources in shaping customers’ product choices. In doing so, we advance understanding of how and why multiple recommendation sources can influence customers’ decisions. These results may also guide practitioners in designing and implementing multiple sources of recommendations.

## About the Authors

Jingjun (David) Xu (davidxu@cityu.edu.hk; corresponding author) is an associate professor of Management Information Systems at the City University of Hong Kong. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of British Columbia. His research interests include human-computer interaction, social media, e-commerce, technology adoption, and mobile commerce. His papers have been published in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, INFORMS Journal on Computing, among other journals. He is serving as an associate editor for Information Systems Research and Information Systems Journal.

Izak Benbasat (izak.benbasat@sauder.ubc.ca) is a fellow of the Royal Society of Canada and professor emeritus at Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He holds a Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota and a Doctorat Honoris Causa, Université de Montréal. He was editor in chief of Information Systems Research, editor of the Information Systems and Decision Support Systems Department of Management Science, and a senior editor of MIS Quarterly. He received the LEO Award for Lifetime Exceptional Achievements in Information Systems from the Association for Information Systems, and is a Distinguished Fellow of the Institute for Operations Research and Management Sciences (INFORMS) Information Systems Society.

Ronald T. Cenfetelli (Ron.cenfetelli@sauder.ubc.ca) is a Professor of Management Information Systems at Sauder School of Business, University of British Columbia. He conducts research into e-business, online customer service; the strategic uses of information technology; the behavioral and emotional aspects of technology usage; and human-computer interfaces. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and the Journal of AIS. He is serving as the senior editor of MIS Quarterly.
