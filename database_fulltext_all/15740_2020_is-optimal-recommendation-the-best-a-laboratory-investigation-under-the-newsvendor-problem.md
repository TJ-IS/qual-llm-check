# Is optimal recommendation the best? A laboratory investigation under the newsvendor problem

A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Newsvendor Decision support system Algorithm aversion Regret aversion Behavioral operations management

## A B S T R A C T

We investigate the impacts of the decision support system's recommendations on decision makers' psychology and decision behaviors under uncertain contexts where optimal solutions exist. As a representative of such contexts, the newsvendor problem is studied by using the method of laboratory experiments. Through providing an elaborately designed decision support system in Experiment I, we validate that the optimal recommendations help to alleviate human newsvendors' Pull-to-Center bias, i.e., the actual orders fall in the range between mean demand and optimal order that maximizes the expected profit theoretically, and decrease the bias asymmetry under two profit conditions (high or low). We also reveal that optimal recommendations can't eliminate the bias as decision makers exhibit two competing psychological factors simultaneously when using the decision support system: algorithm aversion and regret aversion. Algorithm aversion persistently impedes them from following the superior recommendations, while regret aversion sometimes pulls them to approach to the recommendations driven by the feeling of experienced regret. Further, we redesign the decision support system in Experiment II and find that, although the conservative system recommendations are valueless compared with the optimal one, the well-designed radical system recommendations may eliminate the Pull-to-Center bias under the high-profit condition, through the interaction of the dominant regret aversion, dominated algorithm aversion, and the anchoring efect.

## 1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSS) are designed to help people make forecasts or decisions especially in environments where uncertainty exists and decisions are repetitive [1]. Evidence shows that the algorithm<sup>1</sup>-based DSS can consistently outperform human decision makers when both parties make forecasts alone (e.g., [2,3]). Nevertheless, it seems that human decision makers are resistant to use the recommendations provided by DSS [4,5]. Dietvorst et al. [6,7] refer to this phenomenon as “algorithm aversion”, i.e., people tend to adopt the inferior decisions made by humans rather than the superior algorithmic recommendations.

Previous literature provides plenty of evidence for the existence of algorithm aversion when forecasts are made under uncertain environments across various industries (e.g., [8–10]), however, few studies touches on the uncertain context when there is an optimal solution, which can be easily solved by DSS rather than by humans. Under the uncertain contexts without optimal solutions, DSS success can be measured from many diferent dimensions, such as decision performance, users' satisfaction, decision quality, or eficiency [11,12]. While when there is an optimal solution that maximizes the profit, DSS success should be measured by how close the users' decisions are to the optimal solution. It is unknown whether people exhibit algorithm aversion or other psychology when the optimal DSS recommendations are provided. Our research fills this gap by investigating users' psychology of using DSS under the settings of newsvendor problem, which is a good representative of decision contexts where both uncertainty (i.e., stochastic demand) and optimal solutions (i.e., optimal order that maximizes the expected profit theoretically) exist.

The newsvendor problem is a classical model in operations management. In a short selling season, a newsboy who purchases and sells newspapers everyday has to tradeof between the overage costs caused by unsold papers (ordering too many) because they become worthless on the following day and the underage costs caused by the unsatisfied demand (ordering too little) because they are potential sales. Given the stochastic demand, the newsboy needs to make decisions on the ordering quantity to maximize the expected profit. It has been proved that the newsvendor problem hold a theoretically optimal ordering quantity, i.e., a fixed number depending on the cost, the price, and the demand distribution (see details in Section 2.2).

It is easy for a DSS to compute the optimal order as long as it has access to these parameters. By contrast, the research area of behavioral newsvendor reveals that it is dificult for people to make the optimal order because of the fact that people are not fully rational. The seminal experimental work done by Schweitzer and Cachon [13] discovers that human newsvendors' (HN) average order quantities show a “Pull-to-Center” (PtC) bias, that is, actual order quantities fall between the mean demand and the optimal order quantity that maximizes the expected profit theoretically. Given the strong evidence that dozens of sub sequent studies get similar findings, the PtC efect is believed to be a very stable observable phenomenon in the newsvendor problem (see Zhang and Siemsen [14] for a review).

Although the behavioral newsvendor problem has been well-studied, the newsvendor behaviors of using DSS recommendations are still under studied. Lee and Siemsen [15] and Zhang and Siemsen [14] have tried to provide decision makers with the system-generated optimal order decision (i.e., the optimal recommendation) at the beginning of each decision period, and find that the PtC bias still exists. We suspect that HNs in these two studies sufer from strong algorithm aversion because the fixed recommended order makes the DSS seem to lack flexibility or intelligence. Thus, suppose a seemingly more-intelligent DSS is provided (what we proposed), whether decision makers exhibit algorithm aversion and whether the PtC efect disappears become our concerns. In this research, we intend to answer the following research questions through the method of laboratory experiment under the newsvendor settings in the presence of DSS: (1) Can the optimal DSS recommendation correct the decision bias completely under uncertain contexts? (2) What psychological factors do people exhibit when using DSS and what are the impacts of these factors on decision making under uncertain contexts where optimal solutions exist?

Specifically, two multiple-round experiments are conducted to in vestigate the efects of DSS on newsvendors' psychological factors and PtC bias for both the high-profit and low-profit products. The purpose of Experiment I is to investigate the impacts of the optimal DSS. The purpose of Experiment II is to test whether the redesigned DSS would improve the impacts compared with the optimal DSS. In experiment I, we provide the HNs with a seemingly more-intelligent DSS that ofers optimal recommendations (optimal DSS) varying across decision rounds and feedback on the performance of both parties at the end of each round. From the experimental results, we validate that the optimal DSS helps to alleviate PtC bias only for the low-profit product, decrease the PtC asymmetry under two profit conditions, and increase profits; but it can't fully extinguish the PtC bias. We show that this is caused by algorithm aversion that people tend to deviate from DSS recommendations. However, we also find out people may approach to the DSS recommendation in the following round if they experience the feeling of regret from the performance feedback, which we call as regret aversion behavior. (Note that the findings on regret aversion behavior as a psychology of using DSS is one of our contributions). In order to further decrease PtC bias, we redesign the DSS in Experiment II by al lowing the recommendations to move around the optimal order, i.e., providing conservative recommendations (conservative DSS) closer to the mean demand or radical recommendations (radical DSS) closer to the demand extremes. The results tell that conservative DSS seems to be valueless compared with the optimal DSS, while radical DSS is valuable since it can help to eliminate the PtC bias for the high-profit product. Thus, our research contributes to the behavioral newsvendor problem by providing efective de-biasing techniques.

Overall, providing DSS to the newsvendor not only triggers algo rithm aversion but also stimulates the impacts of the experienced regret on the subsequent regret aversion behaviors. These two competing forces pull the decision behaviors into opposite directions. The experimental results show that the regret aversion behavior is robust across three diferent types of DSS (optimal, conservative, or radical) under the high-profit product condition, rather than under the lowprofit product condition. Simultaneously, we also discover that the degree of regret aversion is stronger under the low-profit condition than under the high-profit condition. Therefore, when regret aversion dominates algorithm aversion, PtC bias may be decreased and even eliminated by the elaborately redesigned DSS, otherwise, DSS may become inefective in correcting PtC bias.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 provides the theory development by introducing the relevant literature and proposing hypotheses. Section 3 describes the experimental design for Experiment I, followed by the results and analysis to test hypotheses. Section 4 introduces Experiment II. Discussion on the interaction of diferent psychological factors and decision behaviors appears in Section 5. We conclude our research in Section 6.

## 2. Theory development

Our research contributes to the research stream of DSS acceptance behaviors (e.g., [16,17]) in two ways. Firstly, we focalize the decision contexts of this stream from where only uncertainty exists to where both uncertainty and optimal solutions exist. This is meaningful because it is important to test whether people still exhibit algorithm aversion when the recommendations are optimal and it is impossible to do better than DSS. Our experimental results indicate the stubbornness of algorithm aversion in such contexts. Secondly, except for algorithm aversion, we also find supporting empirical evidence for one conflicting psychological factor of using DSS, i.e., regret aversion. Consistent with Hung et al. [18] who find that DSS reduces users' regret for stock investment, we reveal that people exhibit regret aversion behaviors spontaneously when DSS is provided under contexts where optimal solutions exist. Given our findings that regret aversion is robust across diferent types of DSS (optimal, conservative, or radical), we suggest that the key issue should be trying to figure out solutions to overcome algorithm aversion as much as possible.

This research also makes contributions to the literature stream of behavioral operations management by adding values on the de-biasing techniques. As a good representative of the aforementioned contexts, the newsvendor problem is selected to be the basic settings of our two experiments. The well-designed radical DSS in Experiment II is eventually found to be an efective de-biasing tool to eliminate the PtC bias. In Experiment I, although our research provides optimal DSS recommendations similar to the ones used in Section 4.1 of Zhang and Siemsen [14], our experimental settings are diferent from theirs on three aspects. One, we introduce a more plausibly intelligent DSS into the newsvendor settings, tactfully avoiding to recommend a fixed order quantity across all decision rounds. Two, our DSS provides feedback on the performances of not only the decision makers' decisions but also the DSS recommendations. Three, we do not inform decision makers of the optimality of recommended order quantities provided by the DSS.

Specifically, we implement the first distinction by varying the demand composition, because of which the optimal order quantity changes with decision rounds. The second one helps to decrease decision makers' algorithm aversion, but simultaneously arouses their social comparison with machines (i.e., DSS), and thus activates the impacts of experienced regret on subsequent regret aversion behaviors, which allow us to investigate the dynamics in the decision process, beyond the aggregate decision behaviors caused by the experienced regret usually studied in previous literature (e.g., [13,19–21]). The last distinction allows us to redesign the DSS to provide recommendations slightly deviating from the optimal order quantities and to test whether the redesigned DSSs would improve the decision makers' decision performance compared with the optimal one.

In the following parts of this section, we introduce the relevant theory development in detail, including the algorithm aversion, newsvendor problem, and hypotheses on PtC bias and profit, and hypothesis on regret aversion.

## 2.1. Algorithm aversion

Surveyed research has concluded that algorithm aversion is ubi quitous in various domains, including manufacturing, mining, financial service, healthcare, real estate, and cosmetics, etc. For example, in the retail industry, more than 90% of retail managers make the pricing decisions by experience and intuition, rather than adopt and use the DSS in the early 21st century [8]. Although the adoption rate increases after several years' development, the recommended prices of the DSS are frequently adjusted by salespeople during usage [9]. Forecasts suggested by the DSS are also often adjusted by decision makers in firms, where the proportion of changed forecasts is 10%–15% in one retail firm, and reaches 60%–90% in three nonretail firms [10].

The reasons why people exhibit algorithm aversion have been discussed by many previous studies. For example, Umanath and Vessey [22] speculate that people do not know the rationale underlying the suggestions proposed by the DSS and thus suspect its performance. Dawes [23] mentions the factors such as humans' desire for perfect forecasts, algorithms' inability to learn, and concerns about the ethicality of relying on algorithms to make important decisions. Grove and Meehl [3] propose the reasons such as the notion that algorithms are dehumanizing or that algorithms cannot properly consider individual targets, and the presumed inability of algorithms to incorporate qualitative data. Highhouse [24] thinks that the drivers are the presumed ability of human forecasters to improve through experience. However, all of the above factors are based on anecdotal experience. The earliest empirical evidence proposed by Dietvorst et al. [6] is that people lose confidence more quickly in algorithmic than human forecasters after seeing both parties make a similar mistake.

## 2.2. Newsvendor problem

In the newsvendor problem, the newsboy tries to make money through purchasing the newspapers at a unit cost c and selling them at a unit price $p \ ( p \geq \ c )$ . The demand d is stochastic with a continuous density function g and an increasing cumulative distribution function G. The newsboy chooses an order quantity q and they arrive at the beginning of each selling period. Without loss of generality, assume that the unsold newspapers at the end of each period have to be thrown away without producing any extra value and that the unsatisfied demand will be lost without bring any extra costs. The expected profit function of the newsboy can be written as π(q) = pE min (d,q) − cq.

It is well known that the optimal order quantity q∗ that maximizes the expected profit is the unique solution to $G ( q * ) = ( p - c ) / p . ( p - c ) /$ p is defined as the critical ratio, which can be used to classify products. A product is considered to be high-profit when its critical ratio is higher than 0.5, otherwise, it is the low-profit.

## 2.3. Pull-to-Center bias and profit

Previous studies in the field of behavioral operations have examined the newsvendor problem in the lab from many diferent angles of view, while almost all of them point to one common phenomenon referred to be PtC. Recently, Zhang and Siemsen [14] conduct a meta-analysis of 24 papers on newsvendor to revisit the actual ordering behaviors observed in experiments and conclude that the phenomenon of PtC efect stably exists across studies.

The causes of PtC efect under the newsvendor experiment have been well discussed in many literatures that follow Schweitzer and Cachon [13]. Several behavioral biases are believed to be influential, including anchor and insuficient adjustment (i.e., anchor on the mean demand and adjust inadequately toward the optimal order) [13], preference for reducing ex-post inventory error (i.e., the absolute diference between the amount ordered and realized demand) [13,19,20], demand chasing (i.e., adjust the order quantity toward previous demand than away from it) [13,25], bounded rationality (i.e., all decision alternatives could be chosen and better decisions are made more often) [26], reference dependence (i.e., realized demand acts as the reference point and the psychological cost on underage or overage matters in the utility) [27], impulse balance equilibrium (i.e., the equilibrium is reached when expected upward impulses are equal to expected downward impulses, where upward impulse occurs when higher[lower] order would have brought more[less] profit) [28].

In order to decrease the obstinate PtC bias, existing literature have proposed feasible solutions, such as manipulating the relative salience of overage costs and underage costs [27], reducing decision makers' overprecision on the estimation of order variation [29], and decom posing the decision task [15], etc. Besides, providing DSS has also been verified to be an efective way to alleviate the PtC bias, which is related to the first research question of this paper.

Several existing studies have answered the first research question with “No” by providing diferent forms of seemingly “not-intelligent enough” DSSs. Specifically, three studies, Schweitzer and Cachon [13], Kremer et al. [19], Käki et al. [30], display graphs or tables of the profit distribution as decision support. Bolton et al. [31] provide a graphic describing the expected profit as a function of order quantities with the optimal order decision highlighted before decision makers start to make any decisions. Lee and Siemsen [15] and Zhang and Siemsen [14] suggest the optimal order quantity to subjects at the beginning of every decision period, with the diference that the former study allows the subjects to revise the suggestion directly as their order quantities, while in the latter study, subjects need to input a new number as their order decisions. In general, these studies have found that DSS, regardless of its forms, could help to correct the PtC bias partially, but not completely.

Our research adopts the DSS analogous to that in Zhang and Siemsen [14] but providing decision makers with the optimal order decisions varying across decision rounds. Recall that for a given de mand distribution, the optimal order quantity in the newsvendor problem is one specific number depending on the critical ratio and the cumulative distribution function of the demand distribution. Thus, the suggestion provided by the DSS in Zhang and Siemsen [14] is fixed across all decision rounds, which may seem to be lack of intelligence to people. To avoid this, we employ a two-part demand with one random part observed and the other random part unobserved before decision making, leading to a changing optimal order quantity depending also on the observed part of the demand that fluctuates with decision rounds.

Following the existing studies' common findings, we speculate the DSS we proposed can also help to mitigate the PtC bias. However, although this DSS seems to be a more intelligent one than the previous literature, we would not expect the disappearance of the PtC bias be cause of the concerns on humans' algorithm aversion. Given that algorithm aversion is a widespread phenomenon across industries [2,6,32], it seems that algorithm aversion is not likely to go away as long as the uncertainty exists in the decision environment where the algorithm inevitably makes errors [6,7]. Therefore, given that the newsvendor problem is one of such decision environments because of the demand randomness, we suspect that people's algorithm aversion<sup>2</sup> would probably exist in the newsvendor settings with DSS, and that it would impede the elimination of PtC bias. Further, the decrease of PtC efect generally comes along with the increase in profits, leading to the following hypotheses.

Hypothesis 1. (PtC Bias) Human newsvendors' Pull-to-Center bias can be alleviated, but not be eliminated by the decision support system that provides optimal recommendations.

Hypothesis 2. (Profit) Human newsvendors' profits can be increased by the decision support system that provides optimal recommendations.

Although the PtC efect is one stable phenomenon, the degree of this efect is not, instead, it may either be higher in the condition of the lowprofit product than in the condition of the high-profit product (e.g., [13,15,33]), or be the contrary case (e.g., [27,29]). That is, the PtC efect is asymmetric under two conditions of products with diferent profits. Through the meta-analysis of newsvendor experiments, Zhang and Siemsen [14] reveal that the PtC asymmetry is afected by several factors of the experimental design. Availability of DSS is one of such factors that can reduce the PtC asymmetry, in the sense that, if the spontaneous deviation is significant (i.e., decisions in the absence of DSS deviate significantly from the optimum), the order behavior will be strongly afected by DSS, as decision makers should realize that their spontaneous order decisions are far away from the optimal order decisions suggested by DSS, otherwise, if the spontaneous deviation is small, the order behavior will be slightly impacted by DSS. Based on this clue, in this paper, we also expect to see the decrease of PtC asymmetry, even if the optimality of DSS is not publicly known to decision makers, as we believe that DSS recommendations play a role of anchor during the decision process and the superiority of DSS will be indicated from the observed feedback on the recommendation perfor mances. This leads us to hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3. (PtC Asymmetry) Human newsvendors' Pull-to-Center asymmetry can be decreased by the decision support system that provides optimal recommendations.

## 2.4. Regret aversion

According to the technology acceptance model initiated by Davis [34], the perceived usefulness positively afects user acceptance of information technology through the moderation efect of the behavioral intention to use [35]. On the one hand, many methods have been proposed to present the perceived usefulness of DSS to decision makers, such as providing more explanations in regard to the recommendations of the system [36], showing the benefits of using this system [37], or providing specific feedback on the likely outcomes of alternative decision options [1]. On the other hand, although the systems have been shown to perform better than humans in most cases under uncertain decision environments, people still exhibit the algorithm aversion behavior [6], leading to a decreased behavioral intention to use the algorithm. Recently, it is found surprisingly that allowing people to (even slightly) modify the algorithm could help to overcome algorithm aversion partially, as people get a sense of control over the algorithm resulting from the modification [7]. Following the above literature's guidance, in this paper, we introduce a well-designed DSS into the newsvendor settings, that is, the DSS not only allows the decision maker to modify its recommendations freely, but also provides the feedback on the DSS recommendations in addition to on the decision maker's decision. Through the laboratory experiment, we examine how people ad just their decision making strategy dynamically in the presence of this DSS, and explore the underlying psychological factors that afect their strategy adjustment.

Considering the fact that social comparison is a natural tendency of human and that decision maker tends to make social comparison with others to evaluate his own options and abilities when he is in a group [38,39], we suspect that providing explicit feedback on two parties' decisions may unintendedly induce the decision maker's ex-post social comparison behavior, even though the other party is an algorithmbased machine (i.e., DSS). Thus, in the newsvendor settings with the well-designed DSS, the decision maker is likely to make a comparison between his decision outcomes and the DSS recommendation outcomes based on the ex-post feedback, then two situations may happen, i.e., if the decision maker performs worse than DSS, he would have a feeling of regret that he did not follow the system's suggestion, otherwise, a feeling of rejoicing would come over the decision maker. Both emotions are produced based on the feedback on the foregone decision making, so they are regarded as experienced regret [40].

Anticipated regret is one concept that similar to experienced regret, but with the diference that both emotions of the anticipated regret are related to the expected feedback on decision making in an uncertain environment. Anticipated regret will be activated once two conditions are satisfied: (1) people compare the actual outcome with what the outcome would have been had a diferent choice been made, and that they experience emotions of regret or rejoicing as a consequence; (2) the emotional consequences of decisions are anticipated and taken into account when making future decisions [41,42]. It is believed that both anticipated regret and experienced regret lead to humans' regret aversion, i.e., making regret-minimizing choices [40,43], and the phe nomena have been demonstrated in diferent contexts, such as the experiments on consumers' purchase decisions [44], the longitudinal study on the adoption and continued use of technology [45], and the second-hand data analysis on stock trading [46]. Particularly, Cooke et al. [44] show that experienced regret has a greater influence on reducing future regret than anticipated regret. This is consistent with the intuition that occurred emotions make more significant impacts than anticipated emotions.

In the literature stream of newsvendor experiments, the newsvendor's regret has been studied from two perspectives: (1) the anticipated regret of not hitting the ex-post realized demand results in the preference for minimizing the ex-post inventory error, and it is shown to be able to explain the PtC bias [13,19,20]; (2) the anticipated social regret (i.e., a feeling of inferiority) of being outperformed by the other newsvendor leads to the herding behavior when anticipated social regret dominates anticipated social rejoice (i.e., a feeling of superiority), and the polarization behavior comes along with the contrary scenario [21]. Both kinds of anticipated regret afect the decision makers' aggregate decision patterns through introducing an extra negative part into the decision makers' utility function, with the distinction that the former is built on the distance between the anticipated decision and the anticipated realized optimum, whereas the latter is related to the anticipated performance distance from the competitor. We believe that the anticipated regret also plays its role in our newsvendor settings with DSS, but beyond that, we expect to test the impacts of newsvendors experienced regret, caused by a social comparison based on the observed feedback on two parties' previous decisions, on the subsequent decision making in the presence of DSS. In addition, our research difers from the latter anticipated regret on three aspects: (1) we test whether the newsvendor engages in social comparison with an algorithm-based machine (i.e., DSS), but not with a human; (2) we focus on the newsvendor's experienced regret that has actually happened, but not the anticipated regret that is hypothesized to happen; (3) we investigate the impacts of the newsvendor's regret on his subsequent decisions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ decision dynamics), but not the aggregate decision patterns. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 4. (Regret Aversion) Human newsvendors' experienced regret leads to subsequent regret aversion behaviors in the presence of the decision support system.

To summarize the last hypothesis, under the newsvendor settings in the presence of DSS that provides feedback on the decision outcomes of both the decision maker's decisions and DSS recommendations, the decision maker is likely to make social comparison with the DSS, re sulting in the experienced regret. The experienced regret is hypothe sized to induce decision maker's subsequent regret aversion behaviors, which is specifically defined in our settings as 1) if HN experiences the feeling of regret, he will make the next order to reduce the future regret, i.e., shortening the distance diference between his order quantity and the suggested order quantity of DSS in two successive decision rounds; 2) if HN does not experience the feeling of rejoicing, he will insist on his own decision strategy and move his next decision away from the re commendation, i.e., increasing the above distance diference. Further, considering that negative results or emotions tend to afect the sub sequent decisions more significantly than positive ones, which borrows ideas from the prospect theory [47], we speculate the impact of ex perienced regret is greater than the experienced rejoicing.

## 3. Experiment I: optimal recommendation

## 3.1. Experimental design and implementation

To study the decision-making process of HNs in the presence of a seemingly more-intelligent DSS that provides optimal recommendations, we implemented $\textsf { a 2 } \times \textsf { 2 }$ between-subjects experimental design that varies the product profit condition (“high” or “low”) and the introduction of DSS (“no DSS”, “optimal DSS”). In the condition of “no DSS”, we replicated the standard newsvendor problem in the absence of DSS, and got the HNs' spontaneous PtC bias. Under the treatment of “optimal DSS”, we studied the newsvendor's adjusted PtC bias and the decision dynamics related to regret aversion in the presence of DSS. The condition of product profit allowed us to confirm the asymmetry of PtC bias, and together with the condition of “optimal DSS”, we could test the change in this asymmetry.

We recruited 87 subjects from a public research university. The majority of the subjects were undergraduate students and the remaining were graduate students. Before attending the experiment, all of the subjects had taken some training on probability theory and statistics at the undergraduate level, which helped them to understand the demand distribution very well. Subjects were randomly assigned into one of the four treatments and were asked to repeatedly make order decisions for 50 rounds, with an incentive that better experimental performance would be transformed to higher cash payment. Each treatment session lasted for about 50 min and the average payment was 45 in the local currency, including the show-up fee of 5.

After subjects sat down in the laboratory, the experimenter handed out a hard copy of experimental instruction to each subject, and then started to read the experimental instruction to them loudly (see Appendix A for the detailed instructions of all treatments). Subjects were told that each participant played the role of a store manager, making profits through purchasing one kind of product and selling them to the customers who entered his store. The selling price per unit was 12 pesos, and the cost per unit was either 3 pesos under the high profit condition or 9 pesos under the low-profit condition. The salvage value of unsold products was zero. The decision was to choose an order quantity in each round, with the objective to maximize his own cumulative profits across 50 rounds. The true demand for each round would be realized randomly based on some specific rules after the decision was made. They were told that the sales quantity would be the minimum of order quantity and the realized demand. Finally, two numerical examples were provided to help subjects better understand the newsvendor problem and learn how to calculate his profit.

Under the condition of “no DSS”, the order decision was made di rectly, whereas under the condition of “optimal DSS”, the subject made the order decision after observing the DSS recommendation in each round, with the additional information in the experimental instruction that his store had installed a DSS, which could provide a recommendation on the order quantity in each round for their reference. (Note that subjects are not informed of the optimality of DSS recommendations.) To avoid the case that the recommendation orders were fixed across all 50 rounds, we adopted a demand structure that included two parts: $\Chi + \ Y$ . Before the order decision, X was known to be a specific random number in the range of 50 to 75, but Y was un known and would be randomly drawn from a uniform distribution between −49 and 50 (Özer, Zheng and Chen [48] also employed a twopart demand, but under the forecast information sharing context.). In each round, all of the subjects in four treatments were faced with the same X value and Y realization. Thus, the underlying recommendations of the “optimal DSS” were $\mathrm { ~ X ~ } \pm \ 2 5$ , where $\Chi + 2 5$ under the high-profit condition, and $\Chi \mathrm { ~ - ~ } 2 5$ under the low-profit condition.

After finishing reading the instruction, the experimenter proceeded to implement the experiment on the computer based on the software Ztree [49]. Firstly, three practice rounds were run to help subjects get familiar with the computer interface and decision process. Then, the subjects started to do the formal experiment for 50 rounds. In each round, subjects made the order decisions by inputting an integer between 1 and ${ 1 2 5 } ^ { 3 }$ into the blank decision box, with the diferences across treatments that subjects made this decision directly in the condition of “no DSS”, while they did this after observing a recommended order quantity of DSS in the condition of “optimal DSS”. Once subjects completed their order decisions and pressed the button of “Done” on the screen, the computer would turn to the next screen and display the feedback on the demand realization, the order quantities, the sales quantities, the pesos profits earned in the current round, and the cumulative pesos profits. Additional feedback on DSS's recommended order quantities, DSS's pesos profits in the current round, and DSS's cumulative pesos profits were provided only under the condition of “optimal DSS”. Furthermore, the historical feedback of all the previous rounds was available to subjects. Once subjects finished all the 50 rounds, the experimenter announced the ending of the experiment. Finally, subjects received their cash payment privately and left the laboratory.

## 3.3. Test Hypotheses 1–3 (Pull-to-Center efects and profit)

In this subsection, we report the analysis results based on the experimental data of both the actual order quantities and the actual profits (pesos earnings) to test our Hypotheses 1–3. Before that, we first check whether algorithm aversion exists, which we speculate to be one possible reason to hinder the elimination of PtC bias, we compare the HN profits with the DSS profits, and then examine how often HNs adopt the DSS recommendations. The experimental results firstly show that HNs' average profits are significantly lower than DSS profits by −17.12 [−13.45], in the high [low] profit condition, which means it is obseryable that DSS performs better than HNs. Then we find that the percentage of not exactly following DSS recommendations is 80% [85.22%], in the high [low] profit condition, implying that HNs do not adopt DSS recommendations in the most cases. Additionally, at the individual level, 6 out of 43 subjects did not follow the recommendations at all across 50 rounds (i.e., the adoption rate is 0%), inferring that they might be intended to deviate from the DSS. Overall, our experimental results provide evidence for the existence of algorithm aversion under the newsvendor problem when an optimal DSS is provided, and thus algorithm aversion is one reason that prevents HNs from escaping from the PtC bias.

To standardize the measurement of the degree of PtC efect, we adopt the definition commonly used in the literature on newsvendor experiments as below

$$
\beta_ {i, j} = \frac {q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - q _ {i , j}}{q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - u _ {i , j}} = \frac {q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - \frac {1}{n _ {i , j}} \frac {1}{T} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {i , j}} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} q _ {i , j , k , t}}{q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - u _ {i , j}},\tag{1}
$$

where $q _ { i , j } { } ^ { * }$ is the optimal newsvendor order quantity, $u _ { i , j }$ is the mean demand, $q _ { i , j }$ is the HNs' actual average order quantities and $q _ { i , j , k , \ t } 1$ is each HN's actual order quantities, with the DSS condition $i = \left\{ N , O \right\} ( N$ and O refer to the condition of NDSS and ODSS respectively), profit condition $j = \{ H , L \}$ (H and L represent the high- and low-profit condition respectively), subject $k = \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , n _ { i , j } \} ( n _ { i , j }$ is the total number of subjects in each treatment), and decision round $t = \{ 1 , 2 , . . . , T \}$ (T is the total number of decision rounds in the experiment, which equals to 50 in this research). The value of the PtC efect $\beta _ { i , \ j }$ may have three cases: 1) a positive value between 0 and 1 refers to the normal PtC bias, and a larger value implies a stronger PtC bias; 2) a negative value represents that the actual order is more extreme than the corresponding optimal order; 3) a value larger than 1 means that the actual order goes beyond the mean demand in the opposite direction of the corresponding optimal order.

In the last column of Table 2, the value of $\beta _ { i , \cdot }$ under each group is shown to be significantly larger than zero (0.28 [0.22] and 0.76 [0.47] under the high- and low-profit condition in the absence [presence] of DSS). Further, through checking the PtC efect value at the individual level [50], we find that, only four [three] subjects under the high [low] profit condition in the absence of DSS do not exhibit the PtC bias, and the corresponding number of subjects are four [one] for the high [low] profit condition in the presence of DSS. The above evidence implies that the PtC pattern is robust across both the profit and DSS condition.

Next, we run regression analyses to test Hypothesis 1 and 2. Spe cifically, we create a dummy variable, IsODSS, which equals to 1 if the data are from the ODSS condition and 0 if the data are from NDSS condition, and adopt the general linear regression model with standard errors clustered at the individual level, to check whether the PtC bias is decreased by the DSS, leading to a higher profit. The regression results are summarized in Table 3. Model 1H and Model 1L regress HNs' PtC efect values on this dummy variable in the high- and low-profit condition, respectively, showing that the HNs' PtC efect is decreased by the DSS significantly in the low-profit condition, but not significantly in the high-profit condition, partially supporting the Hypothesis 1. Similar results are also supported by Model 2H and Model 2L which regress HNs' order quantities on this dummy variable. Finally, Model 3H and Model 3L regress the HNs' profits on this dummy variable in each profit condition, and get the result that HNs' profit is increased by the DSS, which supports Hypothesis 2.

Given the DSS condition $i ,$ if the PtC efect value in the high-profit condition is the same with that in the low-profit condition, we say that the PtC is symmetric, otherwise, the magnitude of PtC asymmetry could be measured by the efect size statistics [11], which is defined as:

$$
d _ {i} = \frac {\beta_ {i , H} - \beta_ {i , L}}{S _ {i}} = \frac {\beta_ {i , H} - \beta_ {i , L}}{\sqrt {\frac {(n _ {i , H} - 1) \operatorname{var} (\beta_ {i , H}) + (n _ {i , L} - 1) \operatorname{var} (\beta_ {i , L})}{n _ {i , H} + n _ {i , L} - 2}}},\tag{2}
$$

where $S _ { i }$ is the pooled standard deviation, $n _ { i , j }$ is the sample size $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ the number of subjects), and var(β $_ j )$ is the between-subject order variance for the profit condition j and DSS condition i, with the following definition:

$$
\operatorname{var} \left(\beta_ {i, j}\right) = \operatorname{var} \left(\frac {q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - q _ {i , j}}{q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - u _ {i , j}}\right) = \frac {\operatorname{var} \left(q _ {i , j}\right)}{\left(q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - u _ {i , j}\right) ^ {2}} = \frac {\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {n _ {i , j}} \left(q _ {i , j , k} - q _ {i , j}\right) ^ {2} / n _ {i , j}}{\left(q _ {i , j} ^ {*} - u _ {i , j}\right) ^ {2}},\tag{3}
$$

where $q _ { i , ~ j , ~ k }$ is the average order quantity of each subject across all rounds. Note that a positive [negative] value of $d _ { i }$ represents that the PtC efect is higher in the high [low] profit condition compared with low [high] profit condition, and that a larger absolute value of $d _ { i }$ refers to a higher extent of PtC asymmetry.

The magnitude of PtC asymmetry is reported in the last column of Table 2. Specifically, the value of d in the NDSS condition is −0.17, which is negative with the meaning that the PtC efect in the low-profit condition is greater than the high-profit condition. The value of d in the ODSS condition becomes −0.12, still negative but with a smaller absolute value. These experimental results show that the PtC asymmetry is indeed decreased by the DSS that provides optimal suggestions, supporting Hypothesis 3.

## 3.4. Test Hypothesis 4 (regret aversion)

In order to study the impact of HNs' experienced regret, we turn to the decision process of HNs to investigate how they make adjustments on orders to minimize the future regret when facing with DSS recommendations across rounds. The hypothesis on regret aversion makes the following predictions: 1) when the HN observes that the DSS's current profit is higher than his own, he will feel regret, and tries to minimize his future expected regret through approaching to the DSS recommendation in the next decision round; 2) on the reverse, he will feel delighted, and insists on his own decision strategy, i.e., moves away from the next recommended order; 3) the impact of experienced regret is bigger than the experienced delight.

The current experimental design, i.e., providing detailed feedback on decision performances, allows subjects to compare the decisions and profits of both parties in each round, such that we are able to construct an intertemporal measurement to depict the regret aversion. The measurement quantifies the change on the absolute distance diference between HN's order and DSS's recommended order across two successive rounds, and can be written as

$$
R A _ {i, j, k, t} = | q _ {i, j, k, t - 1} - s _ {i, j, t - 1} | - | q _ {i, j, k, t} - s _ {i, j, t} |,\tag{4}
$$

where $s _ { i , ~ j , ~ t }$ is the DSS recommendation for all subjects at the round $t = \{ 2 , . . . , T \}$ in the profit condition $j = \{ H , L \}$ and the DSS condition $i ~ = ~ \{ O \}$ . The left side of the measurement $R A _ { i , ~ j , ~ k , }$ is the absolute distance between HN order and DSS recommendation in the first round of two successive rounds, and the right side represents the absolute distance in the second one. There will be three cases on the value of the measurement of regret aversion $R A _ { i , j , k , t } \colon 1 )$ a positive value indicates that, compared with the first round, HN gets closer to the DSS recommendation in the second round; 2) a negative value implies that, compared with the first round, HN gets away from the DSS recommendation in the second round; 3) the value of zero means HN keeps the same distance from DSS recommendations in two successive rounds, i.e., the HN's order is not afected by the DSS recommendations at all.

There are totally 50 rounds in our experiment, so each subject's regret aversion measurement has 49 values. Based on the comparison of two parties' profits in the first round of every two successive rounds, we divide the experimental data of each treatment into two cases: 1) DSS profit is higher than HN profit; 2) DSS profit is lower than or the same as HN profit. A dummy variable, IsDSSBetter, is created to diferentiate these two cases, which is equal to 1 for case 1) and 0 for case 2). And then we calculate the average value of this regret aversion measure ment in each case for further analysis.

Firstly, to test whether the HNs' experienced regret leads to the future regret aversion behaviors, we regress regret aversion measurement on this dummy variable in each profit condition when the DSS is present, by using the general linear regression model with standard errors clustered at the individual level, i.e., regress $R A _ { O , \ j , \ k , \ }$ on IsDSSBetter, where j = {H,L}. From the regression results in the second and fourth columns of Table 4, we can see that the impact of experi enced regret on the regret aversion behaviors appears only in the high profit condition, but not in the low-profit condition. Specifically, in the high-profit condition, when the DSS's profit is not higher than HN's profit (IsDSSBetter = 0), the HN's order decision significantly goes away from the DSS recommendation in the next round (the coeficient of the constant is (=−1.543) significantly lower than zero), otherwise, the HN's order decision approaches to the DSS recommendation in the next round (the coeficient of the constant (=−1.543) plus the variable IsDSSBetter (=4.021) is 2.478, which is significantly<sup>4</sup> higher than zero). However, in the low-profit condition, the HNs neither come closer to nor get further away from DSS recommendations in two successive rounds, no matter of the comparison results of two parties' profits (the coeficient of the constant (=−0.253) does not significantly difer from zero. and the sum of the coefficient of the constant $( = - 0 . 2 5 3 )$ and the coeficient of IsDSSBetter (=0.0369) is also not significantly<sup>5</sup> from zero), suggesting that they stick to their own order strategy without referring to the DSS recommendations. Moreover, in the high-profit condition. the adiustment step size that follows the experienced regret (=2.478) is bigger than that of the experienced rejoicing (=1.543), so we can say that the influence of the experienced regret is greater than the experienced rejoicing in the high-profit condition.

Secondly, except for the existence of regret aversion, we also in vestigate the magnitude of regret aversion. We create ProfitDmHlag as the measurement that DSS profit minus HN profit in the first round of two successive rounds. Then, we regress $R A _ { O , j , \ k , }$ on ProfitDmHlag by using the general linear regression model with standard errors clustered at the individual level. Regression results in the third and fifth columns of Table 4 show that, the magnitude of regret aversion increases with the previous profit diference between DSS and HN only in the highprofit condition (=0.0461) but does not significantly change in the low-profit condition (0.000011). Therefore, we get empirical evidence that supports Hypothesis 4 in the high-profit condition, rather than in the low-profit condition.

In conclusion, Experiment I provides evidence that introducing the DSS that ofers optimal recommendations into the newsvendor settings: 1) cannot extinguish the decision makers' PtC bias in either high- or low-profit condition; 2) alleviates the decision makers' PtC bias significantly only in the low-profit condition rather than in the high-profit condition; 3) raises the decision makers' profits under both profit con ditions; 4) indeed helps to decrease the PtC asymmetry; 5) arouses the emotion of experienced regret through providing feedback on the performances of both parties, resulting in that regret aversion behaviors exist in the high-profit condition rather than in the low-profit condition and that the magnitude of regret aversion increases with the previous profit diference between DSS and HN.

## 4. Experiment II: conservative or radical recommendation

## 4.1. Experimental design for two redesigned DSSs

Given the impacts of optimal DSS on HNs' psychological factors and PtC bias, we propose two redesigned DSSs (i.e., conservative or radical DSS) in Experiment II and check whether they could increase these impacts compared to optimal DSS and achieve the goal of eliminating PtC bias. Similar to the optimal DSS, these two redesigned DSSs allow decision makers to freely modify the recommendations and provide feedback on the decision performances of two parties. However, the diference is that, these two redesigned DSSs provide recommendations slightly deviating from the optimum, specifically, the conservative DSS ofers recommendations located between the mean demand and the optimal order, while the radical DSS provides recommendations located between the optimal order and the same-side demand extreme.

The conservative DSS is created because of the similarity of its recommendations to the decision makers' spontaneous order quantities in the absence of DSS, both of which fall into the range of mean demand to the optimum, but the former is closer to the optimum than the latter. Given that similarity is one driver of conformity and mimicry [51], we conjecture that decision makers could follow the conservative DSS recommendations more often, exhibiting weaker algorithm aversion, and thus get the improved decision performance. However, the radical DSS is proposed because of the efect of anchoring and insuficient adjustment [13], that is, the radical DSS recommendations may play the role of anchors for decision makers, and help to pull their order decisions close to the optimum after decision makers' insuficient adjustment.

In order to investigate the impact of these two redesigned DSSs on HNs' order decisions, we recruited 80 more subjects and conducted a follow-up 2 × 2 between-subjects experiment, which varies the product profit condition (“high” or “low”) and the type of DSS (“conservative DSS” or “radical DSS”). The experimental instruction of the ODSS can be directly used in Experiment II, as the features of the DSS recommendations are not specified in the instruction. Besides, the settings of Experiment II are the same as the ODSS's except for the values of the recommendations. That is, the conservative DSS provides recommendations based on $\mathrm { ~  ~ { ~ X ~ } ~ } \pm \mathrm { ~  ~ { ~ 2 0 ~ } ~ }$ , while the radical DSS recommendations are $\mathrm { ~ X ~ } \pm \mathrm { ~ 3 0 ~ }$ , where the recommendations are higher than X in the high-profit condition, and lower in the low-profit condition.

## 4.2. Results and analysis

We firstly run regression analysis to test whether the two redesigned DSSs improve the HNs' decision performance in terms of PtC and profit, compared with the optimal DSS, through the general linear regression model with standard errors clustered at the individual level. Two dummy variables are constructed to diferentiate three types of DSSs in the regression analysis, i.e., IsCDSS [IsRDSS] gets the value of 1 when the conservative [radical] DSSS is provided, and 0 when the other two types of DSSs are. Table 5 lists the regression results, in which Model 4H [4L], Model 5H [5L] and Model 6H [6L] respectively regress the HNs PtC efect value, HNs' actual order quantities, and HNs' actual profits on two dummy variables in the high [low] profit condition. From the regression results of Model 4H. 5H. and 6H, we can see that in the high. profit condition, compared with the optimal DSS, conservative DSS does not significantly afect the PtC efect $( p ~ > ~ 0 . 1 )$ , and the radical DSS decreases the PtC efect slightly significantly (p < 0.05), but finally neither of these two redesigned DSSs helps to increase the HNs profits. Besides, the regression results of Model 4L, 5L and 6L show that, in the low-profit condition, although both redesigned DSSs increase the PtC bias and decrease the profit, they are not significant statistically, compared with the optimal DSS.

The basic results of Experiment II in Table 6, including the number of subjects randomly assigned, HNs' actual average orders, actual average profits, PtC efect, and PtC asymmetry. We find that the HNs' PtC bias is eliminated by the radical DSS in the high-profit condition (HNs' actual average orders are 23.11, and the PtC efect value is 0.075). Besides, PtC asymmetry is decreased by the conservative DSS (−0.14), but not by radical DSS (−0.22), compared with the NDSS condition (−0.17). We also revisit the regret aversion behaviors in the presence of two redesigned DSSs by using the regression method similar to that used in ODSS. Regression results show that human newsvendors' regret aversion behaviors (both the existence and the magnitude) in the presence of two redesigned DSSs are consistent with that in the presence of ODSS (see Appendix B for details).

To summarize, the redesigned radical DSS performs better than the optimal DSS, as it helps to alleviate the decision makers' PtC bias, and even eliminate it, while the redesigned conservative DSS does not perform significantly diferent from the optimal DSS on PtC bias or profit. Besides, the PtC asymmetry is decreased by the conservative DSS, but not by the radical one. ## 5. Discussion

The above analysis shows regret aversion is asymmetric under two profit conditions. One may wonder that whether the algorithm aversion is also asymmetric, given both psychological factors come along with DSS. In this section, we firstly test the asymmetry of algorithm aversion, and then discuss how the asymmetry between two profit conditions in terms of algorithm aversion and regret aversion is connected to the asymmetry in terms of the efect of DSS on PtC.

The aforementioned evidence for the existence of algorithm aver sion in Section 3.3 is based on the decision results on the aggregate level, now we switch to the decision process to revisit the algorithm aversion. The bottom half of Table 4 lists the frequency of three cases under two profit conditions in the presence of optimal DSS, i.e., DSS profit is higher than, lower than, or equal to the HN's profit. From these data, one can observe that the frequency of the case of HN [DSS] better in the low-profit condition is higher [lower] than that in the high-profit condition, i.e., 51.02% vs. 37.24% [34.07% vs. 42.86%], indicating that DSS is outperformed by HN more often in the lowprofit condition than in the high-profit condition. (Similar patterns are also observed in the presence of CDSS or RDSS, as listed in the bottom half of Appendix Table B.1.) Recall that one reason for exhibiting algorithm aversion is that people lose confidence faster in algorithms after seeing them make similar mistakes to humans [6]. Following our experimental results, we infer that the degree of algorithm aversion can be strengthened after people see DSS makes inferior performance more frequently than humans. Therefore, HNs exhibit a greater degree of algorithm aversion in the low-profit condition than in the highprofit condition, which verifies the asymmetry of algorithm aversion under two profit conditions.

Algorithm aversion and regret aversion are two competing forces during the decision process of the newsvendor in the presence of DSS. On the one hand, algorithm aversion impedes the decision makers from following the DSS's superior recommendations. On the other hand, regret aversion pulls the decision makers back to the DSS recommendations under some conditions (i.e., when DSS's most recent recommendation is superior). Compared to the low-profit condition, subjects' algorithm aversion under the high-profit condition is weaker, so they pay more attention to DSS recommendations and more likely to experience a strong emotion of regret or delight, and thus exhibiting more significant regret aversion behaviors. In the case that regret aversion dominates algorithm aversion, decision makers' PtC bias may be decreased and even eliminated by the elaborately designed DSS with the further drive of anchoring efect (e.g., radical DSS in high-profit condition), otherwise, DSS may become inefective on correcting humans' decision bias (e.g., radical DSS in low-profit condition).

## 6. Conclusion

Through the approach of the laboratory experiment, we conclude that the optimal DSS recommendations may not always be the best for managerial decision-making under the uncertain decision environment, because of the existence of conflicting psychological factors of using DSS, i.e., algorithm aversion and regret aversion. However, our redesigned radical DSS recommendations could help to clear of the decision bias through the interaction of dominant regret aversion, dominated algorithm aversion, and the anchoring efect.

Algorithm aversion has been revealed to be quite pervasive in various organizations and industries and be generally harmful to humans' decision making, because of the fact that algorithms perform better than humans in most cases [6]. Previous literature shows that although the algorithm aversion is dificult to be got rid of, it can be decreased through allowing people to modify it even though to a small extent [7], or providing more information on the recommendations for decision makers [1,36]. In our research, both approaches are implemented when we design the DSS that provides optimal recommendations (Experiment I) and redesign the DSS by providing conservative or radical recommendations that deviate from the optimum (Experiment II). During the decision process under the laboratory settings of the newsvendor problem, regret aversion is activated by the second approach.

Regret aversion is not always harmful to decision making, instead, it leads people to approach to the DSS recommendations under certain conditions. Our experimental evidence shows that when the regret aversion dominates the algorithm aversion, the DSS may help to eliminate the PtC bias, otherwise, the DSS may become valueless. Overall, algorithm aversion and regret aversion play a role in removing HNs' PtC bias under the high-profit condition, which is the weaker side of the asymmetric spontaneous PtC efect. Therefore, in reality, the DSS providers should consider users' psychological biases into the design of DSS [52], to help decision makers adjust or even overcome their behavioral biases (especially the regret aversion) and reach the true best decision making.

Our research contributes to the research stream of DSS acceptance behaviors by expanding the applied decision contexts to where both uncertainty and optimal solutions exist and by providing empirical evidence for learning more about the psychology (algorithm aversion, regret aversion) of using DSS under such contexts. Experiments are designed based on the settings of newsvendor problem, as we believe that it is a good representative of such contexts (i.e., there are demand randomness and optimal order that maximizes the expected profit). Our research also contributes to the area of behavioral operations management by providing efective de-biasing techniques (i.e., the radical DSS that helps to eliminate the PtC bias). Considering that Newsvendor problem is a classical model in operations management and its rationale for profit-maximization has been applied into many research areas and industries, we believe that our findings on the impacts of optimal/redesigned DSS on humans' psychological factors and decision behaviors based on the newsvendor problem could also be generalized, especially to the uncertain contexts where there are optimal solutions.

Algorithm aversion is a challenge for not only algorithm users but also algorithm designers. DSS would become valueless if users do not adopt the superior DSS recommendations sophisticatedly designed by designers. Our research provides three possible implications for de signers to avoid this paradoxical phenomenon. One, the optimal recommendations may not be the best for users, instead, it would be beneficial to study their psychology and behaviors before designing the algorithms. Two, it is recommendable to find out alternative ways to directly help people overcome the algorithm aversion [7] given that it may be caused by many diferent reasons [24]. Three, designers could counteract the negative efect of algorithm aversion through strengthening the positive efect of regret aversion. For example, the degree of regret aversion could be reinforced by providing users with detailed feedback on the performance of both parties when DSS performs better, and with feedback only on users' own performance when DSS performs worse.

One of our research limitations is that subjects are assumed to not hold any private information, which may aggravate algorithm aversion.

Incorporating private information into human-machine interaction could be one potential extension. Future research opportunities may include investigating the impact of experienced regret on decision be haviors when the newsvendor competes with another in the competi tive environment [53–55], and discussing the impacts of DSS on decision behaviors under the supply chain contract context [56], the bullwhip efect context [57,58], or the principal-agent context [59].

## Appendix A. Instructions

Please read the experimental instructions and make your decisions carefully. Your cash payment in the experiment will be decided by your every decision and some random factors. Your decisions are anonymous to all other participants, and your privacy will also be protected strictly.

Your profits in this experiment will be firstly calculated in the unit of peso, and finally be exchanged to the local currency at the rate of “600 pesos = 1 local currency” at the end of the experiment. In addition, each participant will get a show-up fee of 5 local currency. Please note that the highe cumulative profits you get in the experiment, the more final cash payment you will earn. [The rate is “90 pesos = 1 local currency” in the low-profit condition]

The whole experiment includes 50 rounds. Meanwhile, you play the role of store managers, making profits through buying and selling one kind of product. The operation process is as below.

(1) At the beginning of each round, you buy some products from the supplier, at the unit purchase price of 3 pesos. [The number is 9 in the low-profit condition]

(2) You sell the products to customers who enter your store, at the unit selling price of 12 pesos.

(3) If the order quantity is higher than the demand, then the excess products do not have any value, and you have to throw them away. If the order quantity is lower than the demand, then you lost these potential sales opportunities. Therefore, your sales quantity is the minimum of orde quantity and customer demand, which can be written as “min (orders, demand)”.

(4) At the end of each round, the computer will calculate your ordering cost, sales revenue, and net profit automatically and give you detailed feedback. Then, you enter into the next round and repeat the whole process.

Customer demand ${ \bf \Xi } = { \bf X } + { \bf Y } ,$ where X is known before you make the decision on order quantity, but Y is unknown in advance. X is an integer between 50 and 75. Y is a random integer in the range of −49 to 50. In each round, the realization of Y will be randomly drawn from this range, meaning that each within integer will be selected as the realization at the probability of 1/100

You need to decide how many units of products to purchase from the supplier. Notice that, the realization of Y is independent across rounds, and the same to customer demand. That is, the realized values of demand in the previous rounds do not afect the demand realization in the following round.

Besides, your store is equipped with a decision support system, which provides recommendations on the order quantity at the beginning of each decision round, for your reference. [ODSS, CDSS, and RDSS only]

Your objective is to maximize your cumulative net profits across 50 rounds. In each round, net profit = revenue − costs = unit price × min (demand, your order) − unit cost × your order.

The following two numerical examples show how to calculate the profits:

(1) Suppose in some round. X is known to be 50. and you have decided to purchase 80 products from the supplier. Then. vou observe the realized value of Y is −20. Thus. Your demand in this round will be $\Chi + \textsf { Y } = 3 0$ , and your sales quantity will be min (orders, demand) = min (80, 30) = 30. Therefore, you will earn a profit of 12 × 30–3 × 80 = 120. [12 × 30 − 9 × 80 = −360 in the low-profit condition]

(2) Suppose in some round, X is known to be 60, and you have decided to purchase 50 products from the supplier. Then, you observe the realized value of Y is 10. Thus. Your demand in this round will be X + Y = 70. and vour sales quantity will be min (orders, demand) = min (50. 70) = 50. Therefore, you will earn a profit of 12 × 50–3 × 50 = 450. [12 × 50 − 9 × 50 = 150 in the low-profit condition]

In each round, you should input an integer between 1 and 125 to represent your order quantity. Your payment will depend on the sum of your cumulative profits across all the 50 rounds and the show-up fee. Once the experiment is completed, you will get your cash payment privately.
