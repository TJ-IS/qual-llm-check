# Winner’s curse and parallel sales channels—Online auctions linked within e-tail websites

Douglas A. Amyx <sup>\*</sup>, Michael S. Luehlfing

College of Administration and Business, Louisiana Tech University, P.O. Box 10318, Ruston, LA 71272, USA Received 27 March 2005; received in revised form 24 July 2006; accepted 19 August 2006 Available online 21 November 2006

## Abstract

A sample of 416 online auctions was examined to determine the extent of overpayment (winner’s curse) where online auctions and e-tail websites were linked together to form a parallel sales channel. The results indicated that 8.7% of the highest winning online auction bidders exceeded e-tail posted reference prices of identical retail merchandise found at the same website. Significantly, such bids exceeded the reference prices by a mean percentage dollar amount of 14.1% thus suggesting the existence of a winner’s curse. The results also indicated that (1) there was a significant negative association between reference price and mean percentage dollar amount overbid; (2) there was a significant negative association between auction lot size and mean percentage dollar amount overbid; and (3) there was no significant association between overtime auctions and mean percentage dollar amount overbid. While manipulation of reference price and auction lot size might minimize winner’s curse, erratic or irrational behavior (by online auction and/or e-tail websites) may lead to disinformation. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: E-tail; Disinformation; Online auction; Parallel sales channel; Reference price; Winner’s curse

## 1. Introduction

Winner’s curse, the phenomenon of buyers paying too much to win a bidding competition and experiencing buyer’s regret, has plagued bidders in conventional auctions for years. Similarly, it persists among bidders who participate in online auctions. Given the relative proximity of posted reference price information, the existence of winner’s curse among bidders participating in online auctions becomes particularly interesting when the online auction is associated with an electronic retailer (e-tailer), that is, when the online auction is a component of a parallel sales channel.

Initially thought to be competitors, online auctions are increasingly becoming website partners with e-tailers; indeed, such a link appears to be a win–win situation for both seller and buyer (bidder). Online auctions attract attention and bring additional customers to the e-tail site while consumers are provided with an opportunity to obtain a bargain if they choose to participate in the auction. However, such is not always the case. Therefore, we decided to investigate the winner’s curse when an online auction is a component of a parallel sales channel.

## 2. Winner’s curse and online auctions

Winner’s curse is well documented among participants in conventional auctions [2,4,7,29]. In essence, it occurs when an individual in a competitive scenario overestimates the worth of an item, then makes a bid and wins, yet in a sense actually loses by having paid more than the market value.

Value creation for customers is a core concept that successful organizations must embrace when marketing their products and services [22]. Yet when an individual experiences winner’s curse, he or she does not have a value added experience but receives a value deficient experience. Such experiences promote customer dissatisfaction and the customers will not repeat the experience to become loyal customers; they may even damage the organization’s reputation through negative ‘‘word of mouth’’ electronic communication. For example, Lightfoot [25] reported that ‘‘One disgruntled customer . . . set up a Web site called Eggheadsucks.- com to voice his opinion about what he saw as bad business practices. And other customers used online bulletin boards to complain loudly about Egghead.- com’s poor service.’

When there is uncertainty about an item’s value, the chance of experiencing the winner’s curse increases [21]. However, because increased availability of information promotes decreased uncertainty, easy access to relevant information is critical in avoiding winner’s curse. Because the Internet provides opportunity for buyers to collect pre-purchase comparative information, such as posted prices, it becomes an interesting place to test for the occurrence of the winner’s curse phenomenon.

Mehta and Lee [28] found evidence of winner’s curse in their study of Ubid, an online auction. Significantly, at the time of the study, it was not a component of a parallel sales channel, that is, it was not associated with an e-tail site. Thus Ubid auction participants had to search the web for reference prices. While their study did not address the winner’s curse phenomenon specifically, Vakrat and Seidman [32] reported that ‘‘that there are few cases where probably uninformed shoppers bid for more than the catalog price’’—that is, they noted that a few SurplusAuction participants had bid more than the posted reference price provided by Egghead, an e-tailer, via an external hyperlink. Thus SurplusAuction participants were specifically directed to the posted reference prices provided by Egghead.

Egghead subsequently merged with SurplusAuction and, thereafter, it became the primary operating entity. Thus, at the time of our study, Egghead was both an etailer and an online auction where both were internally hyperlinked together within a common website (Table 1). Stated otherwise, Egghead had posted reference prices for e-tail items internally hyperlinked with their online auction thus forming a parallel sales channel. Such convenient access of posted reference prices has benefits to both buyers and sellers. The e-tail price information gives bidders additional reference price information, which reduces uncertainty about the value of the auction merchandise.

Thus as bidder uncertainty decreased, we expected that confidence in the bidders’ ability to avoid winner’s curse increased. Providing access to posted reference price information was also an advantage to sellers. Bajari and Hortaqsu [1] found empirical evidence that reducing uncertainty about the value of auction merchandise increased overall seller profits. Thus convenient access to posted reference price information was critical to efficient and effective website design [9,23,31,33,34].

## 3. Theoretical development

If posted reference prices reduce uncertainty about the value of merchandise, then the bidder is better able to avoid the winner’s curse; in turn, the bidder will have no reason to be disloyal to the seller. Admittedly, erratic and irrational pricing behavior may create disinformation. The consequences of this are discussed in Appendix A.

To explain how bidders’ may be influenced by posted reference price information, we consider Adaptation Level Theory [17]. This suggests that a stimulus is evaluated against the norm (what the customer finds usual). Thus, when bidders use the e-tail posted reference price information, their pre-existing memory-based reference price may be adapted by the e-tail posted reference price. Lichtenstein and Bearden [24] showed that this occurred. Specifically, merchant supplied reference prices may be assimilated into the buyer’s perceived normal price range if the merchant’s price is close to the highest perceived normal price. Thus, Egghead could easily adjust bidders’ pre-existing internal reference prices and, in turn, reduce the uncertainty of the value for their merchandise (reducing the possibility of winner’s curse). Because of the internal hyperlink between Egghead’s e-tail and auction sites, Egghead’s prices serve as the benchmark from which to identify overbidding.

We recognized that bidders had access to other online reference prices and that it was conceivable, if unlikely, that some online bidders did not examine Egghead’s e-tail posted reference prices. Yet, we used the Egghead e-tail posted reference prices as a logical frame of reference from which to establish overbidding.

## 3.1. Overbidding versus winner’s curse

It is vital to stress here that overbidding is not the same as the winner’s curse. Overbidding occurs when an auction bidder has the winning bid but pays more than the posted reference price. In contrast, the winner’s curse occurs where the auction bidder has the winning bid but pays so much more than the posted reference price that he/she feels annoyed because of the overbidding; indeed, some bidders experience winner’s curse if they pay even 1 cent above the posted reference price.

While we are unable to observe or make any direct psychological measures of the bidder’s emotions, it seemed intuitive that overbidding may lead to winner’s curse. Taking this step further, overbidding by a statistically significant amount would almost certainly suggest the customer felt the winner’s curse. Thus, it was identified in our study by testing the statistical significance between the mean percentage dollar amount overbid and the e-tail posted reference price. By deferring to statistical analysis, we were taking a conservative approach (minimizing the risk of overstating the existence of winner’s curse).

Beyond individual price sensitivity differences, there are inherent time and effort differences between participating in an online auction and buying from an e-tailer: there are extra costs in online auctions, such as the delay in having to wait until the auction is over to know whether the item was won and the cost expended in monitoring and completing the online auction. Thus many online buyers would prefer to achieve a much lower auction price than the e-tail posted price. In turn, it is possible that the extent of winner’s curse that we identified was understated, given these extra costs.

It was a major focus of our study to see how winning bids compared to e-tail posted reference prices when both the online auction and e-tail sites were internally hyperlinked. In this case, the effects of winner’s curse may be further understated, because online bidders were able to compare e-tail posted reference prices through the hyperlinks, thereby reducing the search ‘‘costs’’ of establishing an item’s market value. Coy [11] indicated that the ‘‘soundest way to dodge winner’s curse is to gather more information about the true value of what’s being sold.’’ Thus, bidders should be aware of the existence of reference prices, understand how to access them, and be motivated to access them.

## 3.2. Identifying winner’s curse

For each Egghead auction, there could be multiple winners, because multiple items of the product were available. Only the highest winning bidders were included in our analysis, because they would represent the most extreme instances of experiencing the winner’s curse. Thus the winner’s curse exists when, among the highest winning bidders in the online auction, some have significantly exceeded the e-tail posted reference price. Therefore, first we asked the following research question.

Research Question 1: Will any of the highest winning online auction bids exceed the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference prices and if so, how often?

If overbidding occurred, then the following research question was relevant.

Research Question 2: Among the highest winning online auction bids that exceed the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference prices, will the mean percentage dollar amount overbid be statistically significant—that is, will winner’s curse exist?

Because we observed auctions with a wide assortment of merchandise and price ranges, differences in value between winning bids and corresponding e-tail posted reference prices were based on the percentage dollar amount.

## 3.3. Parallel sales channel—key elements

Pinker et al. [30] stated: ‘‘We have yet to see any theoretical work on the impact of auctions on parallel sales channels like posted price.’’ In our study, we examined how winner’s curse was influenced by certain key elements within an online parallel sales channel: posted price, auction duration, and lot size.

## 3.4. Magnitude of reference price

We assumed that the winner’s curse may be influenced by the magnitude of the corresponding etail posted reference price. For example, an individual may be more likely to pay 10% above the stated reference price for a \$1.00 item than to pay an additional 10% on a \$100.00 item. List and Lucking-Reiley [26] found that the price did indeed matter in determining auction bidding behavior. They conducted a field experiment using a sealed-bid auction for sports cards and sold cards ranging between \$70.00 and \$3.00. Their results suggested that bidding strategies significantly changed for the \$70.00 cards: auction participants were more careful with their bidding strategies and spent more time contemplating their decisions when prices increased. Ultimately, bidders were less likely to overbid with the \$70.00 cards. Similarly, we assumed that bidders would spend less time contemplating their bids for lower priced items because of the reduced level of monetary risk.

Further, Carr [8] examined the influence of costly bid evaluations among online auctions and found that a higher valuation lead to a lower bidding threshold. His conclusion suggested that online auction participants would be more sensitive to overbidding as the valuation of the item increases. Thus, we posed the third research question below.

Research Question 3: Among the highest winning online auction bids that exceed the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference prices, will the mean percentage dollar amount overbid be negatively associated with the Egghead e-tail posted reference price?

## 3.5. Auction duration

Typically, there is a scheduled closing time for online auctions. However, the Egghead online auctions would be extended by 5 min if a bid was placed within 5 min of the scheduled closing time. We thought that as the time of the auction extended past the scheduled closing time, the tendency to overbid would also increase. Bidders may be willing to escalate their bids to justify their prior investment of time and effort, even though this was a sunk cost.

Heyman et al. [18] offered two possible causes of increased overvaluation or overbidding as online auctions progress: ‘‘quasi-endowment’’ and ‘‘opponent effect’’ (or auction fever). Quasi-endowment is a sense of ownership that bidders develop during the progress of the auction; the bidder feels entitled to the item. The opponent effect involves an increase in the subjective value of winning the auction when the behavior of other bidders is considered competitive. We asserted that such forces are likely to affect bidding behavior and increase the likelihood of overbidding as the length of the auctions extend past the scheduled closing time.

Research Question 4: Among the highest winning online auction bids that exceed the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference prices, will the mean percentage dollar amount overbid be positively associated with overtime auctions?

## 3.6. Auction lot size

A seller in a multi-item auction must determine how many identical items to place for auction. Increasing the lot size would increase the likelihood of a bidder winning the desired item(s) but would tend to drive down the price of the winning bid. Conversely, decreasing the lot size would probably intensify competition, drive up the highest winning bid, and increase the probability of significant overbidding. Thus, we asked:

Research Question 5: Among the highest winning online auction bids that exceed the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference prices, will the mean percentage dollar amount overbid be negatively associated with lot size?

## 4. Data collection

The first step was to identify Egghead auctions that contained items that were also being sold through the Egghead e-tail site. Egghead’s merchandise primarily consisted of computer hardware and software and various consumer electronics. The second step was to monitor the closing time for the auction being observed. Once the auction closed, the winning bids were recorded. Immediately thereafter, the Egghead e-tail posted reference price was recorded.

## 4.1. Winning bid criteria

Recorded auction information included: the bidder, time the bid was placed, the quantity bid, and the dollar amount of the bid. As an example, an auction for PRODUCT1 started at 09:00 h and ends at 10:00 h and there are three identical items of PRODUCT1 available. BIDDER1 places the first bid for all three items at \$25 at 09:01 h. BIDDER2 then enters a bid of \$30 at 09:05 h for two of the three items. At this point, two items are reserved for BIDDER2 at \$30 per item and one for BIDDER1 at \$25. BIDDER3 then enters the auction at 09:10 h with a bid of \$30 for one item of PRODUCT1.

The auction now has BIDDER2 holding the high bid on two items and BIDDER3 holding the high bid on the remaining item. At this point BIDDER1 bids \$30 for all three items of PRODUCT1. Because BIDDER1’s original bid was made before either BIDDER2 or BIDDER3, it takes precedence. If the auction ended at this point, all items in the auction belong to BIDDER1, with a bid of \$30 per item for three items of PRODUCT1.

## 4.2. Hidden winning bids

A unique aspect of the Egghead auction was that the winning bid amounts were not displayed once the auction had closed. Therefore, the researchers had to begin recording the auction results before its scheduled end. That is, at least 5 min prior to the auction’s scheduled end, a ‘‘show bidders’’ option was selected by the auction observer to display the data on the auction. Once the bidders and bids were displayed, the last bid was found and recorded. Immediately before the anticipated end of the auction, the observer again refreshed the ‘‘show bids’’ screen. If no new bids had been placed, the observer recorded the bids by printing the page and subsequently confirming that the auction had officially closed. However, if the observer refreshed the screen, and found that another bid has been placed, the process was restarted; i.e., a new anticipated auction closing time was calculated and, immediately before this, the observer again refreshed the ‘‘show bids’’ screen. Once the auction officially closed, the last printout of the winning bids were obtained and recorded. This procedure was performed for 416 auctions.

## 5. Analysis and results

To answer the first research question, we first performed a frequency analysis to assess observed instances of overbidding among highest winning bidders. The results of the analysis indicated that, among the 416 highest winning online auction bids, 36 exceeded the corresponding Egghead e-tail posted reference price. This means that 8.7% of the highest winning online auction bids exceeded the e-tail posted reference price found at the internally hyperlinked e-tail website where identical items were sold.

The second research question required two procedures to answer it. First, the mean percentage dollar differences between the Egghead e-tail posted reference prices and the highest winning online auction bids were computed. The results indicated that, of the highest winning online auction bids that exceeded the e-tail posted reference prices, the bids exceeded e-tail posted reference prices by an average of 14.1%. Second, to determine if this was significantly greater than the e-tail posted reference prices, this mean percentage dollar difference was statistically evaluated using a t-test. The results of the t-test procedure (shown in Table 2) indicated that the mean percentage dollar amount overbid was significantly higher $( p < 0 . 0 0 3 )$ than the mean corresponding e-tail posted reference prices. Thus, our study results suggested that when the highest winning online auction bids exceeded the corresponding e-tail posted reference prices, they were significantly higher than the Egghead e-tail posted reference prices.

## 6. Discussion

Overbidding occurred in 8.7% of the online auctions observed in our study. Obviously, the occurrence of overbidding is very low when compared to the results of a non-online experiment where Foreman and Murnighan [15] found that ‘‘[a]ll of the 128 auctions produced cursed winners.’’ Admittedly, 8.7% is unacceptable if a ‘‘zero tolerance’’ threshold is deemed appropriate.

We found that the average percentage dollar amount overbid was 14.1% greater than the average corresponding e-tail posted reference price. Bidders who significantly overbid the e-tail posted reference prices may not have been aware of the existence of the corresponding e-tail posted reference price. One way to educate such bidders would be to locate the corresponding e-tail posted reference price information at the auction site itself.

Our finding of a negative association between the mean percentage dollar amount overbid and the Egghead e-tail posted reference price suggested an inverse relationship between reference price and overbidding. Thus in order to reduce winner’s curse, lower priced items in auctions should be avoided. Historically, many items were not offered in traditional auctions because they were too common or insignificant. However, due to low transaction costs, many lower priced items are now available in online auctions.

Our study did not suggest an association between significant overbidding and the continuation of bidding past the scheduled closing time. One possible explanation is that the overtime periods in our study were simply not of sufficient length to facilitate auction fever-type bidding behaviors.

A negative association exists between winner’s curse and lot size; this implies that as the lot size decreases, the mean percentage dollar amount overbid increases, and vice versa. Since we found that a negative association exists between winner’s curse and lot size, this supports the notion that lot size is an important design parameter for online parallel sales channels.

Designing an online auction with an excess of items would probably drive down bid prices. Conversely, restricting the number of auction items may alienate bidders and create customer dissatisfaction from the winner’s curse or frustration from an inability to obtain the desired items. While shifting of customers (or bidders) from one site to the other is part of the strategy in a parallel sales channel, customer/bidder shifts should occur by design, not by accident.

## 7. Implications

When all highest winning bidders were examined in aggregate, it was evident highest winning bidders were saving money on online auction items compared to identical Egghead e-tail items. Specifically, Egghead highest winning bidders paid on average 23.5% less than Egghead’s corresponding e-tail items.

We examined a relatively new and exciting online format, the parallel sales channel, and assessed the existence of winner’s curse when an online auction was a component of a parallel sales channel. Much of the attraction for the parallel sales channel was that e-tailers could use auctions as a marketing tool.

When an online auction and e-tail site are combined to create a parallel sale channel, irrational bidding behavior may occur if the e-tailer employs a common strategy such as ‘‘hit and run’’ pricing. Baye et al. [3] indicated that such pricing may be an effective ‘‘weapon’’ for e-tail managers by utilizing short-term price promotions that are offered at non-regular or unpredictable intervals. Similar to the use of loss leaders, hit and run pricing could help attract attention and draw consumers to e-tail websites by randomly promoting merchandise at a reduced price.

By using the parallel sales channel approach, online firms are trying to broaden their appeal to different types of customers. Without directly profiling online e-tail and auction customers, we do not know whether those customers who use online auctions to buy merchandise differ significantly from those who purchase from e-tail sites with a posted reference price. As discussed in Appendix B, we believe that the benefits of parallel sales channels will probably offset any costs of channel cannibalization.

Generally speaking, online organizations employing parallel sales channels must simultaneously manipulate the posted reference price of e-tail items with an understanding of how that will affect the online auction.

Raising the e-tail posted reference price may also adjust the online bidder’s valuation of the merchandise and reduce the possibility of the winner’s curse. Online organizations should take great care when manipulating lot sizes. The results of our study suggested that smaller online auction lot sizes tend to increase the occurrence of winner’s curse. Similar to the manipulation of posted reference prices, erratic or irrational manipulation of auction lot sizes may create disinformation.

Once potential customers are drawn to the website, they may participate in one of the auctions, decide to purchase a product at the e-tail store, or do both. In all cases, the parallel sales channel is an approach to increase web traffic for an online organization. Given the perceived benefits to both sellers as well as buyers, the trend of linking online auctions with e-tail sites seems likely to increase as e-business continues to evolve.

## 8. Limitations

We have examined the winner’s curse when an etailer internally hyperlinked an online auction with a corresponding e-tail website to form a parallel sales channel. While a total of 416 online auctions were observed, only 36 had winning bids that exceeded e-tail posted reference prices. This is a fairly small sample size, and this should only be viewed as a first step.

We used an observational approach by monitoring the results of bids as they occurred. While observational data of actual auctions offers the advantage of realism, no demographic information was observable.

## Appendix A. Consequences of disinformation

While parallel sales channels potentially provide customers with useful information, parallel sales channels may also create disinformation. For example, disinformation may be created if Egghead decided to manipulate e-tail posted reference prices without adequate disclosures. In turn, such disinformation may change customers’ expectations about the value of the e-tail products as well as change their expectations about the value of the corresponding online auction products.

Disinformation has negative consequences at both the operational as well as the strategic level. At the operational level, disinformation resulting from the lowering of e-tail posted reference prices may increase the occurrence of winner’s curse (and dissatisfaction) among auction customers. That is, online auction participants who use Egghead e-tail posted reference prices may have expectations of winning auctions below such reference prices. Thus, with such expectations, the likelihood of experiencing winner’s curse would be increased with lower prices. In addition, lower prices may lead to dissatisfaction among e-tail customers who previously purchased the same product at a higher price.

At the strategic level, disinformation resulting from e-tail posted reference price manipulation may lead to serious corporate reputation issues. For example, if current customers realize that prices have been adjusted upward, such customers may become confused about whether Egghead was a good place to find value. In turn, this confusion may motivate such customers to compare Egghead’s prices with competing e-tailers in order to find value. Once current customers become disloyal, sales promotions may be employed to bring such customers back into the fold. Unfortunately, given that switching costs are low, using sales promotions to lure back customers (or lure in new customers) does not ensure long term loyalty; thus the safest approach may be to employ a consistent pricing strategy in order to maintain a positive corporate reputation [16]. Significantly, erratic or irrational pricing behavior on the part of e-tailers may have been a major factor contributing to consolidations among key industry players in the past [20].

## Appendix B. Channel cannibalization

Channel cannibalization occurs when the sales of products through one channel (e.g., an online auction) take away or cannibalize the sales of identical products being sold through another channel (e.g., an e-tail site). In essence, channel cannibalization represents the loss of sales by the current channel as a result of the introduction of new or different sales channels [12]. In contrast, product cannibalization represents the loss of product sales for existing products because of new product introductions [27].

Intuitively, channel cannibalization is an issue inherent to parallel sales channel strategies such as the use of an e-tail site by a traditional brick and mortar retailer. While a number of researchers and practitioners have expressed concern over the Internet’s potential for channel cannibalization with respect to retailer sales [6,10], others have suggested that channel cannibalization does not pose a significant problem [5,14].

Arguments can be made that the benefits of parallel sales channels outweigh any costs associated with channel cannibalization. For example, some suggest that the use of e-tail sites by retailers provides enhanced product offerings which ultimately increases customer satisfaction and retailer (or e-tailer) loyalty [35]. Others suggest that purchase mode flexibility may explain the fact that parallel sales channel customers tend to not only have more frequent purchase experiences but also buy more (in terms of dollars) per purchase experience [19]. Admittedly, it may be that parallel sales channel customers do not view the different channels as independent of each other. Rather they may view both channels as elements of a total customer experience [13].

With respect to channel cannibalization, we believe that the prior thoughts (regarding the use of e-tail sites by retailers) also apply to parallel sales channels where an online auction is associated with an e-tailer. For example, Egghead provided additional customer-based values (e.g., convenience and choice) by offering two very unique ways to purchase products (i.e., through either an e-tail site or online auction site that were hyperlinked together). While both channels were relatively independent of each other (except for the hyperlink and the Egghead brand name), both channels represented two different aspects of a total customer experience. In essence, both channels represented complementary elements working toward the common goal of building a positive reputation for the Egghead organization, rather than competing against one another as separate channels (i.e., e-tailer versus online auction).

Arguably, some channel cannibalization will occur when identical products are sold through each online channel. However, the benefits of an online parallel sales channel should exceed the costs of channel cannibalization if the parallel sales channel entices new online customers to visit and buy at one or perhaps both channels, satisfies the new customers with a favorable purchase experience, and ensures that the new customers return again as loyal customers. Admittedly, the extent of channel cannibalization may require tracking and analyzing online customer behavior.
