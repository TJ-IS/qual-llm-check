# MULTIHOMING USERS’ PREFERENCES FOR TWO-SIDED EXCHANGE NETWORKS

Tat Koon Koh

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, HONG KONG {koh@ust.hk}

Mark Fichman

Tepper School of Business, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA 15213 U.S.A. {mf4f@cmu.edu}

Online business-to-business (B2B) exchanges are proliferating, giving firms numerous platforms from which to choose. Many firms are also multihoming, using competing platforms concurrently. In this study, we examine how selling and buying activities on B2B exchanges affect multihoming buyers’ preferences for exchanges. We posit that these activities influence buyers’ perceived returns and risks of using the exchanges, and impact buyers’ preferences. Using a unique dataset of 118 buyers’ participation in two B2B exchange over seven months, we find that buyers prefer exchanges with more selling activities. However, buyers’ prefer ences and buying levels on the exchanges are non-monotonically related. At low buying levels, an increase in buying by others positively affects buyers’ preferences. This effect may result from observational learning, where individual buyers learn from other buyers’ behaviors. On the other hand, as buying level increases further on the exchange, competition among buyers also increases. Consequently, buyers lower their preferences for the exchange. In addition, we find that the effects of selling and buying activities on buyers’ preferences change over time. Our results highlight the need to correctly model buyers’ homing behavior; failing to do so could bias the picture of competitive dynamics between platforms and lead to suboptimal strategies by exchanges.

Keywords: Online platforms, B2B exchanges, multihoming, network effects, observational learning

## Introduction

Two-sided networks are platforms that facilitate interactions between distinct but interdependent groups of users, such as buyers and suppliers (Rochet and Tirole 2003).<sup>2</sup> These platforms play an important role in today’s business environment (Bughin et al. 2010). Various new ventures are based on the two-sided network model (e.g., online exchanges, social lending clubs, “deal-of-the-day” websites), and some merchants are introducing platform-type services that connect their buyers with other sellers. As online exchanges proliferate, buyers and sellers can and do use several platforms at the same time to fulfill a particular task. For example, buyers post purchase requests for a particular product on competing business-to-business (B2B) exchanges (see Appendix A). Sellers also use multiple auction platforms to get greater market exposure and reach a larger variety of buyers (Walczak et al. 2006). We say a user is multihoming when she participates on multiple competing platforms concurrently (Armstrong 2006; Choi 2010; Rochet and Tirole 2003). Multihoming behaviors can have important impacts on twosided platform competition and strategy. Consider the case where all buyers multihome on two platforms. Suppliers can participate in just one of the platforms to access all of the buyers. In this case, multihoming behavior by buyers intensifies competition among platforms to attract suppliers and affects their pricing strategy (Rochet and Tirole 2003).

In this study, we examine drivers of multihoming buyers’ participation on and preferences for B2B exchanges. We relate selling and buying activities on the exchanges to multihoming buyers’ perceptions of the returns and risks of using those exchanges. We then ask how these activities influence buyers’ participation on competing platforms. We also look at how neglecting buyers’ multihoming behaviors may lead platform owners to err in their strategic and competitive behaviors. Practitioners (and researchers) may misspecify users’ behaviors in their analyses when they assume users are single-homing; this could happen in part because platforms usually do not have data about their users’ behavior on competing sites. Our research design overcomes this limitation and allows us to compare results between correctly (i.e., users are multihoming) and incorrectly (i.e., assuming users are single homing) specified models. We find that incorrect assumptions about users’ behaviors bias the picture of competitive dynamics between platforms.

This study extends existing research in online platforms in three ways. First, we focus on two-sided exchange networks, and explicitly consider the interactions among different types of users (i.e., buyers and suppliers) on the platforms. In contrast, past work in IS has examined one-sided networks such as peer-to-peer (P2P) sharing networks (Asvanund et al. 2004; Xia et al. 2012) and virtual communities (e.g., Butler 2001; Gu et al. 2007; Ren et al. 2012; Zhao et al. 2013; Zheng et al. 2012). Such one-sided networks typically facilitate interactions among specific types of users, such as file sharers on P2P networks, and investors on virtual investing-related communities. Certain insights from one-sided networks may not be applicable in two-sided network contexts given the social and market dynamics that take place across and within the two distinct sides of the platforms. For instance, the relationship between price and cost on two-sided platforms is complex, such that optimal prices depend on demand elasticity on both sides of the platform and the profit-maximizing price may be below marginal cost (Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009; Evans and Schmalensee 2007).

Second, we consider multihoming behaviors in this study, whereas most studies on two-sided platforms look at singlehoming scenarios where users participate on only one platform (e.g., Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009; Tucker and Zhang 2010). Although multihoming behaviors have been discussed in two-sided networks research (e.g., Armstrong 2006; Rochet and Tirole 2006), little empirical work has looked at multihoming. One exception is Jin and Rysman’s (2012) study of sportscards conventions, which can be thought of as platforms connecting sportcards dealers and consumers. Jin and Rysman could not directly observe whether dealers single- or multihome; instead, they inferred dealers’ homing decisions from the distances between conventions and dealers’ costs to participate in multiple conventions. While inventive and plausible, we prefer to directly observe homing behavior because inferring multihoming behavior introduces another source of uncertainty. In this study, therefore, we directly observed multihoming buyers participation on two exchanges over seven months. This approach allows us to make robust inferences of buyers behaviors on the platforms.

Third, by investigating how non-price factors affect buyers’ platform usage over time, this study complements extant research that (1) examines pricing structures on platforms and their impacts on users’ participation (e.g., Armstrong 2006; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Rochet and Tirole 2003, 2006), and/or (2) focuses on sellers’ behaviors on the platforms (e.g., Jin and Rysman 2012; Tucker and Zhang 2010). In addition, existing studies typically focus on the implications of multihoming behaviors (e.g., Armstrong 2006; Choi 2010), without explicitly considering why and how multihoming users may have different levels of participation on competing platforms. As we discuss later, even though a buyer is participating on multiple exchanges, she may use one exchange more than the other(s). By not focusing just on multihoming behaviors per se, but explaining why multihoming buyers use competing platforms to different extents, we gain insights into post-adoption behaviors of such users.

## Business-to-Business Exchanges

Firms participate on B2B exchanges (e.g., Alibaba.com, ECEurope.com, and ECPlaza.com) to connect to potential buyers and suppliers. These platforms serve as marketaggregators, -makers, and -facilitators (Bakos 1998; Dai and Kauffman 2002), increasing the pool of trading partners by creating centralized marketplaces (Spulber 1999). Prominent operational features of B2B exchanges include increased reach, deep customization capabilities, and reduced transaction costs (Mahadevan 2003). On exchanges, firms can access large databases of companies and business opportunities. They can create customized corporate profiles and online catalogues to promote their business. Firms also benefit from transactional efficiency by using features such as online directory and third-party verification services on the exchanges. Thus, B2B exchanges are useful channels for firms to extend their reach globally (Koh et al. 2012), and reduce search and transaction costs (Lucking-Reiley and Spulber 2001).

Most of the buyers and suppliers on B2B exchanges are small and medium size enterprises (SMEs) (Guo et al. 2006; Yang et al. 2012). To disseminate information about their business needs and offerings to others on B2B exchanges, buyers post buying requests and suppliers post product listings. Buying requests (or requests for price quotations) provide details of buyers’ purchase requirements, while product listings specify items that suppliers are selling. These buying and selling activities constitute the market liquidity on the exchanges, and are valuable market opportunities to users. Many B2B exchanges thus highlight the numbers of buying requests and product listings to attract new users (Tucker and Zhang 2010).

The main revenue source for most B2B exchanges is supplier membership fees. For example, almost 90 percent of Alibaba.com’s US\$995.7 million in revenue is from membership packages.<sup>3</sup> B2B exchanges usually provide free memberships to buyers, while allowing suppliers to choose between free or paid memberships. Buyers can create a company profile, post buying requests, and view suppliers’ product listings on the platforms. Suppliers having a free membership can create a company profile, post product listings, and view buyers’ information. Suppliers who upgrade to paid memberships enjoy additional and/or enhanced services such as priority listings in online directories, and higher limits on the number of product listings that they can post. Most B2B exchanges do not charge transaction-based fees because they cannot reliably observe transactions between firms (Rochet and Tirole 2006; Roson 2005). Fixed membership fees (in contrast to per-transaction charges) allow buyers and suppliers to retain the benefits of their transactions on the platforms, and make getting and keeping both sides on board even more critical to the platforms’ success (Armstrong 2006; Evans and Schmalensee 2007).

As the presence of buyers helps B2B exchanges attract and generate revenues from suppliers, platforms also compete to acquire and retain buyers. Competition for buyers can be intense as many buyers multihome on competing platforms. Buyers’ participation may vary across platforms, where some buyers post more buying requests on certain platforms than on others. Therefore, B2B exchanges not only have to compete to get buyers to join their platform, but they must also get these buyers to prefer their platforms to others. Achieving higher preferences from multihoming buyers helps exchanges have an edge over their competitors in attracting and retaining suppliers in the long run.

## Drivers of Multihoming Buyers’ Preferences

Buyers’ participation on B2B exchanges is a function of their expected returns and risks of using the exchanges. Their returns are likely to be higher on exchanges that offer them greater supplier choices and bargaining power, and thus help them realize higher savings in purchasing costs (Ordanini 2005). Buyers are also concerned with the risks and uncertainties that they face on exchanges (e.g., Koh et al. 2012; Pavlou 2002). Because B2B transaction values tend to be relatively high, it is critical to buyers that they lower their exposure to opportunistic suppliers and/or fraud on the exchanges.

Among other activities, buyers’ participation on exchanges involves posting and/or updating details of buying requests, and managing quotations from suppliers. These activities (as well as other job tasks that are unrelated to using exchanges) are constrained by the availability of buyers’ resources (e.g., time and attention). Hence buyers’ resource constraints, together with the expected gains and risks, would affect their participation levels on various exchanges.

The different extent to which buyers participate on competing exchanges reflects their preferences for the exchanges. Ceteris paribus, buyers will participate more actively on their preferred platform relative to other platform(s) on which they are also participating. In this study, we infer multihoming buyers’ preferences for B2B exchanges from their relative usage of competing platforms. We assume that a buyer’s preference between two exchanges, A and B, is revealed when one of them is chosen more often than the other. That is, the preference for A over B is indexed by the proportion of times A is chosen over B. This operationalization of buyers preferences is conceptually similar to classical methods for measuring preferences in psychology and marketing (Coombs et al. 1970; Thurstone 1927). In this section, we look at how actions of other buyers and suppliers affect multihoming buyers’ participation on and preferences for exchanges through the effects of network externalities and observational learning.

## Network Externalities

Network effects occur when the value of a product to a user depends on the number of users of that product (Katz and Shapiro 1985). Positive network externalities arise when the product becomes more attractive as the number of users increases. Many technology-related products, such as software and communication systems, demonstrate positive network externalities (Brynjolfsson and Kemerer 1996; Gallaugher and Wang 2002; Kraut et al. 1998; Tucker 2008). However, network effects can also be negative. For instance, negative network externalities occur in P2P networks due to consumption of scarce network resources or free riding by users in larger networks (Asvanund et al. 2004).

In two-sided networks, network externalities can be categorized as either inter-network externalities or intra-network externalities.<sup>4</sup> Inter-network externalities refer to how characteristics of one side of the platform affect users on the other side. In most cases, inter-network externalities on two-sided networks are positive, where the expected gain for users on one side of the platform increases with higher activity levels on the opposite side (Eisenmann et al. 2006). In our context, the levels of selling activities on B2B exchanges are important to buyers, as higher selling levels could increase buyers’ expected returns through more supplier choices and/or greater bargaining power.

Positive inter-network externalities lead to a “chicken-andegg problem” on two-sided platforms: to attract users of one type, a platform needs to have a sufficient number of users of the other type (Caillaud and Jullien 2003). B2B exchanges thus need to get “both sides on board,” attracting buyers and suppliers to adopt their platforms simultaneously. One way to achieve this objective is to use appropriate pricing strategies (Armstrong 2006; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Eisenmann et al. 2006; Rochet and Tirole 2003, 2006). Many platforms adopt pricing structures that are heavily skewed toward one side of the market; generally, the side that enjoys greater positive inter-network externalities is more likely to face higher prices than the side that experiences lower internetwork externalities (Eisenmann et al. 2006; Evans 2003). This pricing strategy is observed in many B2B exchanges, where suppliers face higher membership fees and buyers are heavily subsidized with free memberships.

How do inter-network activity (selling) levels affect multihoming buyers’ preferences? On the one hand, buyers typically join and use platforms for free. Also, as the required information in buying requests is similar across different B2B exchanges (e.g., buyer’s contact information, product category, and request details), buyers frequently copy-and-paste their buying requests across multiple platforms. Hence the cost for buyers to multihome may appear relatively low, and selling activity levels may not affect buyers’ preferences after they join various competing exchanges. Since these buyers have already joined multiple exchanges, their strategy could be to post buying requests on as many exchanges as possible to increase their choices of suppliers in fulfilling their purchase requirements.

On the other hand, multihoming buyers do have limited resources (as noted above) and it is not costless for them to participate on multiple exchanges. Given their resource constraints and that they have various platforms from which to choose, these buyers are likely to prioritize and actively use beneficial platforms more. We expect multihoming buyers to prefer those platforms that have higher levels of selling activity, where they tend to enjoy stronger bargaining power and receive more competitive quotations. Moreover, since these buyers are using multiple exchanges, they are by definition not locked into a specific platform and would reduce the use of “under-performing” exchanges in the long run. In other words, the positive impacts of selling activities on multihoming buyers’ preferences are likely to strengthen over time. Hence, we hypothesize

H1: A positive relationship exists between buyers’ preferences for B2B exchanges and selling activity levels on the exchanges.

H2: The impacts of selling activity levels on buyers’ preferences for B2B exchange strengthen over time as their platform experience increases.

The other type of externality in two-sided networks is intranetwork externalities, which refers to how characteristics of one side of the platform affect users located on that same side. In our context, intra-network externalities relate to how variations in buying activity levels on the exchanges affect individual buyers’ benefits. While most studies of two-sided networks focus on inter-network externalities, intra-network externalities are “either abstracted away or not central to the analysis” (Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009, p. 247). Among the few studies that look at intra-network externalities on exchange networks, the general conclusion is that these externalities are negative due to competition effects: as the number of users on one side of a platform increases, these users face greater competition, less attractive prices, and lower benefits on the platform (Anderson et al. 2010; Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009; Eisenmann et al. 2006; Tucker and Zhang 2010). For example, the presence of many sellers has negative effects on whether a potential seller participates on the platform (Tucker and Zhang 2010). Covisint, a B2B exchange launched by major auto manufacturers in 2000, failed to attract sufficient auto parts suppliers as these suppliers were concerned with rivalry and downward pricing pressure on the exchange (Eisenmann et al. 2006). Negative intra-network externalities suggest that multihoming buyers’ preferences for a particular platform would be inversely related to intra-network activity (buying) level on that platform (Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009). When the level of buying activity increases on a platform (causing prices to increase) buyers are likely to seek out other platforms where they might find lower competition and prices.

However, the relationship between multihoming buyers’ preferences and buying activities might not be monotonic. Competitive interactions among buyers should occur at high levels of buying activity. Ceteris paribus, suppliers have greater power to raise prices when there is much buying going on in the platform. At low levels of buying activity, competitive effects are relatively weaker, and a different type of process among buyers may be occurring. Specifically, a sufficient level of buying must occur to lower the perceived risk of using a given exchange and motivate individual buyers to participate on it. In the following section, we explain how buyers can infer potential risks of exchanges from buying activities, and hypothesize how multihoming buyers’ preferences are shaped by these activities.

## Observational Learning

Until now, we have discussed how expected returns from platform participation affect buyers’ decisions through interand intra-network activities. Nevertheless, there is always some residual uncertainty in market transactions. To reduce this uncertainty, buyers use various sources of information they can acquire, such as market and seller reputation, contractual safeguards, and verification. Social information, such as other buyers' behaviors, is another informative factor that influences buyers’ choice and behavior. Using such information in decision-making is part of observational learning, which is the process by which individuals learn by observing the actions of others (Bikhchandani et al. 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1998). This process has also been referred to as social learning (Bikhchandani et al. 1998; Çelen and Kariv 2004) and social proof (Cialdini 2008; Rao et al. 2001). Information about the behaviors of others can help decision-makers learn what is the appropriate or best response in a given situation. As Cialdini (2008, p. 99) points out, individuals in part “determine what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct.”

A possible outcome in observation learning is that everyone behaves identically; such an extreme outcome has been called an information cascade or herd behavior (Bikhchandani et al. 1992; Bikhchandani et al. 1998; Çelen and Kariv 2004). The key difference between an information cascade and herd behavior lies in how decision makers incorporate their private information with inferences from others’ actions. Whereas individuals in an information cascade ignore their private information when they imitate others, those demonstrating herd behavior may still consider their private information even though they make the same decisions as those whom they observe (Çelen and Kariv 2004). IS researchers have examined information cascades and herding in areas such as online music sampling (Dewan and Ramaprasad 2012), online software adoption (Duan et al. 2009), online word-of-mouth (Gu et al. 2013), and new technologies adoption (Li 2004; Sun 2013).

It is critical to distinguish the process of observational learning (what and how an individual learns by observing others) from the learning outcomes (the individual’s decisions or actions after observing others), especially extreme ones such as information cascades and herd behavior. Various studies associate information cascades or herding with observational learning (see above). However, learning from others’ decisions does not always cause everyone to behave in a similar manner, and observational learning could occur even when we do not see all individuals decide identically. While this learning process reflects inferences from observing others’ actions, it only provides one piece of information that affects the outcomes we discuss in this paper. This is because multihoming buyers also consider other factors such as their bargaining power and resource constraints in deciding which exchanges to use more. Thus, while we examine the process and outcomes of observational learning, we do not have an ex ante expectation that information cascades or herding will necessarily occur among these buyers.

Observational learning is particularly salient under two conditions (Cialdini 2008; Rao et al. 2001), both of which often occur in two-sided networks. The first is the similarity of other people to oneself. We are responsive to the observed actions of those who are similar to us. The nature of twosided networks is such that a clear boundary separates types of users (e.g., buyers, suppliers) from each other on the platform. Moreover, B2B exchanges often structure their platforms by industries or product categories. This structure, together with the clear distinction between user types, facilitates prospective buyers observing activities of other buyers like themselves in their category of interest.

The second condition required for observational learning is uncertainty, which causes us to seek information about similar others’ behaviors. With substantial user turnover on B2B exchanges and potential fraud in online transactions, one uncertainty that buyers face concerns the platforms’ quality in terms of supplier trustworthiness. For example, Alibaba.com needs to acquire 35,000 suppliers a year to maintain its revenue, as 35 percent of its suppliers do not renew their annual paid memberships (BusinessWeek 2010). In 2011, more than 2,300 Alibaba.com suppliers who joined between 2009 and 2010 were found to have engaged in fraud (Time 2011), causing its market capitalization to drop by almost US\$1 billion and its key executives to resign (Bloomberg 2011). This event demonstrated the uncertainty in platform quality that buyers are exposed to on B2B exchanges, and its impacts on the market’s valuation of these platforms.

Observing other similar buyers on a B2B exchange is informative to a buyer when there is uncertainty about platform quality. What matters is not just the presence or number of other people that an individual observes; rather it is the actions of other people that the individual observes that are informative (Cialdini 2008). In our two-sided platform context, it is important to consider not the number of other users on the same side per se, but what these users do: the intranetwork activity level. A buyer who observes that other buyers are actively posting buying requests on a platform is more likely to do the same (assuming he has purchase requirements to fulfill). Yet this positive effect is likely to have an upper bound and increase at a diminishing rate with respect to the number of observed actions of others (e.g., Rao et al. 2001). Initial observations of other buyers’ behaviors should provide significant informational value about the platform’s quality to the particular buyer. Beyond a certain level, additional observations would add little to what the buyer may have already inferred.

Here is how we expect observational learning and intranetwork externalities to jointly affect multihoming buyers’ preferences. Consider a buyer who participates in a particular product category on two competing exchanges, E1 and E2. When the buyer observes few buying requests in that category on E1, he could perceive E1 to be unattractive to other similar buyers. Even though it is beneficial to this buyer when there are few competitors to bid up prices, the lack of other buyers participation raises his uncertainty about E1’s quality. This concern could reduce his preference for E1 and, all else being equal, drive him to participate more on E2. Hence, when the level of buying activities is low on an exchange, the buyer’s preference for it is likely to be low (due to observational learning), rather than high (due to little competition).

As buying in that category on E1 increases, the buyer sees evidence of platform quality in the buying of others (observational learning) and has a stronger willingness to participate. Although competition is higher with more buying (negative intra-network externalities), the positive signal from other buyers’ participation can have greater weight as it reduces the buyer’s uncertainty about the exchange. Thus, at low buying levels, we expect the buyer’s preference for an exchange to increase with the number of buying requests.

However, when buying increases beyond a certain level on E1, the buyer could be deterred from participating as the exchange turns overly crowded. This is because intense competition among buyers on E1 could lead to higher prices and reduce the buyer’s expected returns. In this case, the buyer might once again be driven to participate more on E2—only this time it is because of the greater buyer competition in his product category on E1.

We see here that buyers’ preferences are conditional on price and platform quality considerations, which are affected by other buyers’ participation on the exchanges. Based on the relative strength of observational learning and intra-network externalities at different levels of activities, we expect a nonmonotonic relationship between multihoming buyers preferences for exchanges and buying activity levels.

H3: As buying activities increase, buyers’ preferences for B2B exchanges (a) increase when activity level is low and (b) decrease when activity level is beyond a certain point.

Over time, direct participation and experience on a particular B2B exchange reduces buyers’ uncertainty about that platform and their reliance on observations of other buyers’ behaviors (e.g., Dewan and Ramaprasad 2012). Buyers also build up their network of suppliers on the exchanges with time, and this may lessen the negative competition effects from increased buying activities on the platforms. Furthermore, buyer–supplier relationships, once established, often have some continuity (Anderson and Weitz 1989; Baker et al. 1998; Heide and John 1990; Levinthal and Fichman 1988; Williamson 1979). Interactions between trading partners over time promote mutual commitment and cooperation (Doney et al. 2007; Poppo et al. 2008), and suppliers on the platforms may give preferential treatment to buyers with whom they have prior and/or longer relationships. Such treatment helps ease some pressure from increased competition for these buyers. Thus, although competitive buying activities on the platforms should affect multihoming buyers’ preferences for

B2B exchanges over time, we hypothesize that the influences of these activities weaken as buyers’ experience on the respective platforms increases.

H4: The impacts of buying activity levels on buyers’ preferences for B2B exchange attenuate over time as their platform experience increases.

## Data

We observed buyers’ usage on two B2B exchanges between July 2009 and February 2010. These platforms were highly popular with similar Alexa site popularity rankings.<sup>5</sup> (We could not collect data from the most popular B2B exchange based on Alexa rankings, as that exchange blocked certain information from non-registered users. This constraint would have affected our identification of multihoming buyers, as we discuss below.) Both platforms cover multiple industries such as chemicals, computers, and electronics. Each industry is further segmented into various product categories. For instance, the chemical industry segments include inorganic and pharmaceutical chemicals. As B2B exchange users typically are SMEs with specific industry specialization, they usually focus on one primary segment on the exchanges.

Both B2B exchanges offered free memberships and similar services for buyers (e.g., create profile pages, view suppliers’ information). Buyers can also post an unlimited number of buying requests for free on both platforms. In contrast, the platforms provided differentiated services for suppliers. Suppliers can post up to a certain number of product listings depending on their membership type (free or paid) on the platforms. In addition, the platforms offer multiple categories of paid memberships with different limits on the number of product listings.<sup>6</sup> By focusing on buyers’ usage in this study, our results are less likely to be confounded by system constraints due to differentiated supplier services across the two platforms.

We collected data over four time periods. In the first period, we retrieved buying requests posted in all product categories by buyers on both B2B exchanges. We did this on a daily basis for a month. By the end of the month, we identified 690 buyers in Exchange A and 902 buyers in Exchange B who posted at least one buying request. For each of these buyers, we gathered information about the number of buying requests they individually posted and the corresponding platform characteristics at the product category level (the numbers of product listings and buying requests posted by other users, as well as the number of suppliers with a paid membership).

Appendix A shows sample buying requests posted in the exchanges. To identify multihoming buyers, we matched information about the buyers (e.g., company name, address, country, and contact information) and their buying requests (e.g., product requested, product category, and product description) across the exchanges. We considered a buyer to be multihoming if she posted the same buying request in both exchanges in the first period. On average, buyers in our dataset posted the same buying request in both exchanges less than four days apart, with 50 percent of buyers posting the same buying request in both exchanges on the same day. By the end of the first period, we identified 118 multihoming buyers, who constitute 17.1 percent and 13.1 percent of buyers who posted a buying request in Exchanges A and B, respectively. These are conservative estimates of multihoming buyers in our sample, since some out-of-sample buyers could be using other B2B exchanges beside Exchanges A or B. The percentages of multihoming buyers might also be affected by the exchanges’ popularity (proxied by Alexa site popularity ranking), as being in popular platforms negate some buyers’ need to use competing platforms. This is consistent with Walczak et al.’s (2006) observations of online auction sellers in eBay, Amazon, and Yahoo. In that study, 10 percent of respondents in eBay, which is the most popular among the three auction platforms, indicated that they use multiple auction sites. In contrast, 53 percent and 74 percent of respondents in Amazon and Yahoo, respectively, use multiple auction sites.

In the subsequent three time periods, we collected data on buyers’ usage and platform characteristics for the 118 buyers at two-month intervals.<sup>7</sup> At the end of the data collection stage, we had 472 observations of 118 buyers in 79 product categories. Around 85 percent of these buyers had US\$10 million or less in revenue, and 90 percent had 500 or fewer employees (Table 1). In addition, our sample includes users with diverse experiences of the platforms (Table 2). Almost 55 percent of buyers in each platform had more than one year of experience with the platform when we first included them in our dataset. The average membership tenure of buyers in Exchange A and Exchange B was 837 and 827 days, respectively; the median membership tenure of buyers was 498 and 407 days, respectively. Also, individual buyers in our sample had relatively similar experiences with both platforms. The difference in the median buyer’s registration dates on the two platforms is approximately 15 days (i.e., the median buyer joined both platforms about two weeks apart).

## Model Specification

To model multihoming buyers’ behavior, we adapt the model specification that Chevalier and Mayzlin (2006) used in their study of the effects of consumer reviews on relative sales of books on two online booksellers. We specify the number of buying requests posted in each platform by a multihoming buyer as a function of (1) the number of product listings posted by suppliers, and (2) the number of buying requests posted by other buyers in the relevant product category. Since buyers are multihoming, we allow the characteristics of each platform to affect individual buyers’ usage of the other platform. Equations 1 and 2 show the function of buying requests posted by a buyer in Exchanges A and B, respectively.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log \left(\text { Request } _ {i, t} ^ {A}\right) = \\ \alpha_ {1} \log \left(\text { Selling } _ {t} ^ {A}\right) + \alpha_ {2} \log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {A}\right) + \alpha_ {3} \left(\log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {A}\right)\right) ^ {2} + \\ \alpha_ {4} \log \left(\text { Selling } _ {t} ^ {B}\right) + \alpha_ {5} \log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {B}\right) + \alpha_ {6} \left(\log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {B}\right)\right) ^ {2} + \\ \alpha_ {7} \text { Time } _ {t} + \alpha_ {8} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot \log \left(\text { Selling } _ {t} ^ {A}\right) + \alpha_ {9} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot \log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {A}\right) + \\ \alpha_ {1 0} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot (\log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {A}\right)) ^ {2} + \alpha_ {1 1} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot \log \left(\text { Selling } _ {t} ^ {B}\right) + \\ \alpha_ {1 2} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot \log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {B}\right) + \alpha_ {1 3} \text { Time } _ {t} \cdot (\log \left(\text { Buying } _ {- i, t} ^ {B}\right)) ^ {2} + \\ X _ {t} \Gamma^ {A} + C _ {i} + v _ {i} ^ {A} + \mu_ {i, t} ^ {A} \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l} \beta_ {1} \log (S e l l i n g _ {t} ^ {A}) + \beta_ {2} \log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {A}) + \beta_ {3} (\log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {A})) ^ {2} + \\ \beta_ {4} \log (S e l l i n g _ {t} ^ {B}) + \beta_ {5} \log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {B}) + \beta_ {6} (\log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {B})) ^ {2} + \\ \beta_ {7} T i m e _ {t} + \beta_ {8} T i m e _ {t} \cdot \log (S e l l i n g _ {t} ^ {A}) + \beta_ {9} T i m e _ {t} \cdot \log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {A}) + \\ \beta_ {1 0} T i m e _ {t} \cdot (\log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {A})) ^ {2} + \beta_ {1 1} T i m e _ {t} \cdot \log (S e l l i n g _ {t} ^ {B}) + \\ \beta_ {1 2} T i m e _ {t} \cdot \log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {B}) + \beta_ {1 3} T i m e _ {t} \cdot (\log (B u y i n g _ {- i, t} ^ {B})) ^ {2} + \\ X _ {t} \Gamma^ {B} + C _ {i} + v _ {i} ^ {B} + \mu_ {i, t} ^ {B} \end{array}
$$

where superscripts A and B refer to Exchange A and Exchange B, respectively; $R e q u e s t _ { i , t }$ represents the number of buying requests posted by buyer i in period $t ; S e l l i n g _ { t }$ is the number of product listings posted in the relevant product category in period $t ; \ B u y i n g _ { - i , t }$ is the number of buying requests posted by all buyers excluding buyer i in the relevant product category in period $t ; X _ { t }$ is a vector of variables to control for differences between the platforms in period $t ; C _ { i }$ is the unobserved platform-invariant individual effect; and $\upsilon _ { i }$ is the unobserved time-invariant individual-platform effect. We add a time trend variable (Time ) to account for systematic differences in the buyer’s participation on the respective exchanges across time periods. We use a log specification for activity levels in our models as the data in their original form have high skewness and kurtosis. For instance, the numbers of buying requests posted in Exchanges A and B by individual buyers have skewness greater than 3.0 and kurtosis greater than 20.0. After log-transformation, the skewness and kurtosis of activity levels are closer to normally distributed: skewness is now between .4 and .9 and kurtosis is between 3.0 and 4.5.

The platform-invariant individual effect, $C _ { i } ,$ in Equations 1 and 2 accounts for heterogeneity of buyers’ characteristics that could affect their perceptions and use of the platforms— for example, buyers’ computer self-efficacy (Venkatesh 2000) or preferences for high profile users on the exchanges. To eliminate $C _ { i } ,$ we difference Equations 1 and 2. By doing so, we also eliminate industry-level effects that influence platform characteristics and a user’s preference. For instance, an industry shock may affect the numbers of product listings and buying requests in the platforms, and a buyer’s purchase needs. Equation 3 shows the differenced equation.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text {Pref} _ {i, t} ^ {B} = \\ \gamma_ {1} \log (\text {Selling} _ {t} ^ {A}) + \gamma_ {2} \log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {A}) + \gamma_ {3} (\log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {A})) ^ {2} + \\ \gamma_ {4} \log (\text {Selling} _ {t} ^ {B}) + \gamma_ {5} \log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {B}) + \gamma_ {6} (\log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {B})) ^ {2} + \\ \gamma_ {7} \text {Time} _ {t} + \gamma_ {8} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot \log (\text {Selling} _ {t} ^ {A}) + \gamma_ {9} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot \log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {A}) + \\ \gamma_ {1 0} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot (\log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {A})) ^ {2} + \gamma_ {1 1} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot \log (\text {Selling} _ {t} ^ {B}) + \\ \gamma_ {1 2} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot \log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {B}) + \gamma_ {1 3} \text {Time} _ {t} \cdot (\log (\text {Buying} _ {- i, t} ^ {B})) ^ {2} + \\ X _ {t} \Pi + (v _ {i} ^ {B} - v _ {i} ^ {A}) + \mu_ {i, t} \end{array}
$$

where $\begin{array} { r } { \gamma _ { j } = \beta _ { j } - \alpha _ { j } \mathrm { a n d } P r e f _ { i , t } ^ { \beta } = l o g ( R e q u e s t _ { i , t } ^ { \beta } ) - l o g ( R e q u e s t _ { i , t } ^ { d } ) } \end{array}$ $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { \varepsilon } = l o g \Biggl ( \frac { R e q u e s t _ { i , t } ^ { B } } { R e q u e s t _ { i , t } ^ { A } } \Biggr ) } \end{array}$ . The term in parentheses denotes the ratio of buying requests posted in Exchange B to those posted in Exchange A by buyer i in period t. We treat this ratio as buyer $i ^ { \circ } \mathrm { s }$ revealed preference for Exchange B. The log specification in our model ensures the dependent variable is symmetric. Our measure of buyer’s preference is conceptually similar to the constant-sum scale measure used in marketing research (e.g., Amir and Levav 2008; Carpenter and Nakamoto 1989; Griffin and Hauser 1993), where an individual’s preference is expressed as ratios of points allocated to different options. However, instead of asking users to allocate points, we observe their relative usages of various options (in this case, the respective platforms). This approach is appropriate as we assume users reveal their preferences in their market behaviors (Samuelson 1948).

The time-invariant individual-platform effects, $\boldsymbol { \upsilon } _ { i } ^ { \boldsymbol { A } }$ and $\boldsymbol { v } _ { i } ^ { B } ,$ capture the unobserved heterogeneity in a buyer’s preference for the platforms.<sup>8</sup> The difference in these effects in Equation 3 will bias the parameter estimates if it is non-zero. For example, if one of the exchanges offers a unique and/or superior service, this unobserved factor could bias our results. To address such a concern, we eliminate unobserved individual-platform effects by using a panel structure for our data and estimating our main models using fixed effects (Wooldridge 2002). As the product category is timeinvariant, we cannot introduce a fixed effects model for each product category. Instead, we cluster our observations by the product category to which buyers belonged to appropriately adjust the standard errors (Wooldridge 2002).

To control for differences between platforms, we account for the number of suppliers with paid memberships in the respective product categories on each platform $( P a i d _ { t } )$ . B2B exchanges often promote suppliers who have paid memberships; different membership types may affect buyers relationships with suppliers on the platforms (Koh et al. 2012) and their preferences for exchanges.

## Preliminary Analyses

We conducted a series of preliminary analyses to guide us in specifying the models that we would estimate (see Appendix B). First, we estimated our models using time period dummies, where we observed the possibility of a nonlinear time trend. Next, we specified a dynamic model that included a lagged dependent variable to account for possible effects of buyers’ past preferences on their current preferences. However, the lagged dependent variable is not statistically significant in this model, and the Arellano-Bond test failed to reject the null hypothesis that there was no autocorrelation.

## Main Results

Based on the preliminary analyses, we included a quadratic time trend in our model specification. We also decided to use a static model without a lagged dependent variable so that we could use the full sample in our estimations. (Including a lagged dependent variable would require us to drop observations from the first time period.) We estimated Equation 3 with fixed effects transformation at the buyer level. Regression 1 in Table 4 shows the results of a baseline model with only the main effects. The Sargan-Hansen test rejected the null hypothesis that the orthogonality assumption is valid (χ<sup>2</sup> = 22.80, df = 8, p < .05), supporting our use of fixed effects.<sup>10</sup> In addition, to compare a model with fixed effects for buyers against a model using pooled OLS, we estimated the fixed effects using dummy variable regression without clustering the observations by product category. The F-test rejected the null hypothesis that fixed effects were zero (F-statistics = 10.50, p < .001), indicating that using fixed effects estimators is appropriate.

## Additional Analyses

A valid concern in our setting is that buyers’ preferences might have affected (instead of being affected by) the levels of selling activities on the exchanges. We provide three counter-arguments against reverse causality in our context. First, suppliers were likely to pay attention to the activities of most buyers on the exchanges, and not just those of the 118 buyers in our sample—particularly since these in-sample buyers only constituted 17.1 percent and 13.1 percent of all buyers on Exchanges A and B, respectively, who posted buying requests. To verify this assumption, we examined the correlations between requests posted by in-sample buyers and selling activities in the respective exchanges. If these correlations were strong and positive, it would indicate that buying requests posted by in-sample buyers might have significantly affected suppliers’ behaviors (a necessary condition for reverse causality). However, the correlations between insample buyers’ request postings and selling activities were not significantly positive $( r = . . 0 6 , p > . 1 0$ on both exchanges). Moreover, these correlations were lower than those between selling and (all) buying activities on the exchanges (r = .60 and .30 for Exchange A and B, respectively; see Table 3). It thus appears that suppliers’ behaviors were more affected by overall buying activities on the exchanges than by buying requests of in-sample buyers.

Second, for reverse causality to occur, suppliers must have (1) information about in-sample buyers’ usage on Exchanges A and B (condition 1), and (2) compared these buyers’ usage on both exchanges before they posted product offerings (condition 2). (Recall that the dependent variable, buyers preference, is operationalized by multihoming buyers’ relative usage of the two exchanges.) The relatively low correlation between selling activities on Exchanges A and B (r = .15) suggests that the proportion of suppliers who multihomed on these platforms was unlikely to have been very large. Suppliers who only used Exchange A or B could observe, at best, buyers’ usage in that particular exchange (i.e., condition 1 not satisfied). Although suppliers who used both exchanges could compare in-sample buyers’ usage of the exchanges, it is unlikely that all of these suppliers had done so each time prior to posting their product offerings, as it would have been too time consuming and costly. Furthermore, as pointed out above, suppliers seemed to be more interested in the overall buying requests on the exchanges (i.e., condition 2 weakly satisfied, at best).

Third, using differencing regression, we regressed the change in buyers’ preferences between July 2009 and February 2010 on changes in platform characteristics between July and December 2009. (This specification is similar to that in Chevalier’s and Mayzlin’s (2006) study of the effects of book reviews on online sales, where the dependent variable is the change in sales rank of books between May and August 2003, and the independent variables are the changes in book reviews between May and July 2003.) Differencing allows us to control for unobserved time-constant effects that could bias the estimations (Wooldridge 2002, 2006). More importantly in our context, the longer period for the dependent variable (7 months) than that for the independent variables (5 months) in the regression minimizes concerns about reverse causality.

Table 6 presents the results from this analysis. The change in buyers’ preferences for Exchange B was positively related to the change in selling activities in Exchange B $( \gamma _ { 4 } = 1 . 0 2 , p <$ .05), and non-monotonically related to the change in buying activities in Exchange B $( \gamma _ { 5 } = - 2 . 9 5 , p < . 0 1$ and $\gamma _ { 6 } = - 1 . 2 5 , p$ < .01). These results are qualitatively similar to those in our hypothesized models, supporting H1 and H3. However, the change in buyers’ preferences was not significantly related to changes in selling and buying activities on Exchange A $( p >$ .10 for $\gamma _ { 1 } , \gamma _ { 2 } ,$ and γ ). One possible explanation for these nonsignificant estimates is that using differencing regression may reduce variations in the explanatory variables (Wooldridge 2002). Between July and December 2009, the median changes in selling and buying activities on Exchange A were 120 product listings and 9 buying requests, respectively, whereas the corresponding changes in Exchange B were 929 product listings and 19 buying requests. As in the case of our earlier results, the small variations in activity levels in Exchange A could result in non-significant estimates. However, the results for Exchange B are still robust and consistent with our theory.

## Model Misspecification Analyses

Our models assume that multihoming behavior influences buyers’ participation on B2B exchanges. Our results suggest it is a valid assumption. A related question is whether this is a useful assumption for practitioners and researchers. In practice, B2B exchanges may analyze activities on their platforms without considering their users’ participation on other exchanges. In doing so, these exchanges essentially assume users are single-homing on their respective platforms.

In prior published work, researchers sometimes assume single-homing behaviors even though multihoming behaviors are possible (e.g., Armstrong 2006; Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009; Chandra and Collard-Wexler 2009). We conducted an analysis by assuming (contrary to fact) buyers in our sample are single-homing in each platform. The results in Table 7 show the estimates of Equation 1 (buyers’ participation in Exchange A) and Equation 2 (buyers’ participation in Exchange B), respectively, with a fixed effects model. Each of these regressions assumes that participation in a focal platform is a function of activity levels of the competing platform but not of its users’ specific level of participation there.

We find a positive relationship between selling activities and buyers’ participation in Exchange A $( \alpha _ { 1 } = . 2 3 , p < . 0 1$ in Regression 1a), and a non-monotonic relationship between buying activities and buyers’ participation in Exchange B (β = -3.06, p < .001 and $\beta _ { 6 } = - 1 . 2 9 , p < . 0 0 1$ in Regression 2a). These findings are consistent with what we observed in the correctly specified model (Equation 3; Table 4). However, there are three important differences between the results in the Equation 3 models and those in the misspecified models. First, while the results in our hypothesized models indicate that selling activities in both exchanges affected the buyers relative usage of the exchanges, the results in the misspecified models suggest that selling activities in the competitive exchange did not affect buyers’ participation in the focal exchange $( p > . 1 0$ for ${ \mathfrak { a } } _ { 4 }$ and $\beta _ { 1 }$ in Regression 1a and Regression 2a, respectively). (Such results would be consistent with what we expect had the buyers been single-homing. Selling activities on competing platforms should affect single-homing buyers’ decision of which platform to join. After they joined a particular platform, selling activity levels on other platforms should not affect their participation on the platforms that they were on, especially in the short run.) Second, the estimate for selling activities in Exchange B is significantly positive in our hypothesized model, but it is not so in the misspecified model $( \beta _ { 4 } = . 1 7 , p > . 1 0$ in Table 7 Regression 2a). Finally, buying activities in Exchange A had negative impacts on buyers participation on the exchange in the misspecified model $( { \mathfrak { a } } _ { 2 } =$ $- . 5 3 , p < . 0 1 )$ , which is not consistent with what we found in the correctly specified model.

Comparing the results between the hypothesized and misspecified models, we observe two problems. First, the misspecified models paint a biased picture of competition between the platforms. The estimates in these models suggest that the buyers’ usage on one platform is not affected by selling activities on the competing platform. Such conclusions may cause a B2B exchange to focus insufficiently on what competing platforms are doing to improve their services for suppliers.

Second, the discrepancies in results due to model misspecification differ across platforms. For Exchange A, results about the impacts of selling activities are qualitatively similar in both the hypothesized and misspecified models, but this is not so for results about the impacts of buying activities. For Exchange B, however, the converse is true. The differences in discrepancy of results between the hypothesized and misspecified models across platforms (1) make it difficult for practitioners and researchers to know a priori how wrong assumptions of users’ homing behaviors bias results, and (2) could lead to suboptimal strategies. For example, based on the misspecified models, Exchange B may underinvest in increasing selling activities on the platform since these activities do not appear to increase in-sample buyers’ participation. However, based on the correctly specified model, for every 1 percent increase in selling activities on Exchange B, the amount of buying requests posted by in-sample buyers on Exchange B would be higher than that on Exchange A by .54 percent. Thus, ignoring multihoming behaviors could cause Exchange B to lose out on potential market liquidity (in terms of additional selling and buying activities), which is a key value proposition for B2B platforms (Mahadevan 2003), relative to its competitors.

## Discussion

In this study, we examine a prevailing trend of users’ concurrent participation in multiple competing platforms. We find that multihoming buyers’ preferences for a B2B exchange are positively associated with the inter-network activity (selling) levels. We also find that there are two opposing effects of increasing intra-network activity (buying) levels: (1) a positive observational learning effect, and (2) a negative competition effect. As a result, the relationship between multihoming buyers’ preferences and buying activity levels is non-monotonic. As buying activities on an exchange increase, buyers’ preferences for this exchange would initially increase and subsequently decrease. This result is both new and surprising as economic theory suggests that higher buying activity levels increase competition among buyers and reduce the benefits to their participation on the platforms (Anderson et al. 2010; Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009; Tucker and Zhang 2010).

Furthermore, there is evidence that the impacts of selling and buying activities on multihoming buyers’ preferences change as buyers’ experience on the exchanges increase. One might say that these moderated effects of (inter- and intra-) network externalities and observational learning are unsurprising.<sup>1</sup> However, notice that the effects of selling activities on multihoming buyers become stronger with time, but those of buying activities turn weaker (see Table 4). This difference in moderated impacts of selling and buying activities on multihoming buyers’ preferences provides interesting insights into industrial markets and relationships.

Establishing buyer–supplier relationships is a major challenge, and once such relationships are established, there is often continuity. For buyers, working with existing suppliers can reduce their search and information costs (Williamson 1979). To some extent, the attenuated impacts of buying activities on multihoming buyers’ preferences over time are consistent with transaction costs economics. Besides acquiring direct experiences on the exchanges, buyers are likely to establish their supplier bases with time. As such, they could leverage existing relationships with suppliers when competition increases on the exchanges. Consequently, multihoming buyers’ preferences are less sensitive to buying activities on the exchanges over time.

Similarly, since buyers can work with existing suppliers to fulfill new buying requirements, one might also expect the impacts of selling activities on multihoming buyers’ participation to decrease with time. However, our results show that selling activity levels become more important to multihoming buyers over time. An explanation is that sourcing for suppliers on B2B exchanges involves relatively low and few barriers. Since it is useful for buyers to complement (or compare) existing suppliers with outside options, buyers may continue to participate on exchanges despite having built their supplier bases over time. Yet, given their resource constraints and because participation on exchanges in not costless, multihoming buyers’ preferences for exchanges with higher selling activities grow stronger with time.

A key contribution of this study is the theorizing of the joint effects of market characteristics and observations of others behaviors on buyers’ participation on online exchanges. Our theory introduces a new yet complementary perspective about the roles and impacts of intra-network activities on two-sided networks. Existing studies primarily focus on the competitive implications of the presence or absence of buyers or sellers on exchanges (e.g., Anderson et al. 2010; Belleflamme and Toulemonde 2009; Tucker and Zhang 2010). However, uncertainty about platform quality is also a critical issue for these users. To address this concern, buyers could learn about the exchanges’ quality by observing other buyers’ participation. By integrating the effects of competition and learning among buyers, we gain insights into how buyers might choose amongst multiple B2B exchanges in this study.

An important implication from our findings is that we cannot ignore multihoming behaviors when studying or managing platforms; such behaviors affect various areas of platforms’ growth and success (e.g., user participation, marketplace liquidity, and competitiveness). When thought about more broadly, multihoming is ubiquitous. Firms and consumers are likely to use multiple platforms with similar functionality in various domains. Some examples outside the B2B domain include online investment communities (e.g., Seeking Alpha and The Motley Fool), recommendation sources (e.g., Facebook, Foursquare, and Yelp), and social group buying websites (e.g., Groupon and LivingSocial). We should, therefore, account for users’ homing behaviors when examining the usage of and competition among respective platforms.

## Theoretical and Managerial Implications

Our results show the importance of correctly modeling users behaviors across online exchanges. The assumption of singlehoming behaviors facilitates theoretical and analytical discussions, and makes it easier to conduct empirical studies since researchers do not need to involve competing platforms in the research design. However, as we show in our analyses using misspecified models, abstracting away multihoming behaviors (when they exist) could affect results and give an incomplete and potentially biased picture of competitive dynamics. For platform operators, this means that they should recognize the extent to which their users are participating on other platforms. Such behaviors affect the exchanges’ positions in users’ choice sets and competition. As we pointed out earlier, ignoring multihoming users’ participation on other platforms could lead to suboptimal strategies. Although monitoring their users’ participation on competitors’ platforms (as we have done in this study) is an ideal way to get information on these users’ behaviors and preferences, it may not be possible for individual online exchanges to do so. A feasible alternative is to survey users to find out their usage on and preferences for competing platforms. Online exchanges can also learn from what other industries do to observe users’ multihoming behaviors. For instance, balance transfer services allow credit card companies to observe cardholders’ usage of other credit cards. Similarly, price-matching guarantees help retailers learn about other places where their customers shop. Platforms can adopt similar initiatives to encourage their users to provide information about their homing behaviors.

This study also extends research that examines conditions in which information about platforms’ intra- and inter-network sizes affects user participation. Tucker and Zhang (2010) suggest that when users only have information about intranetwork size, they would impute a commensurate internetwork size by inferring from the number of users on their side of the platform. Consequently, a large intra-network size alone does not deter user participation as users assume the platform is in equilibrium. However, when users know the sizes of both sides of the platform, they no longer need to make the above inferences but instead would respond to the observed ratio of buyers and sellers on the platform. In this situation, the intra-network size signals the extent of competition that the users face. In other words, negative intranetwork externalities only kick in when information about both sides of a platform is shown to users.

While the conditions in which intra-network information is shown on the platform are important (i.e., whether it is shown in conjunction with inter-network information), we find that the levels of intra-network activity also matter. Intra-network activities have different signaling effects at different activity levels. When the level is low, an increase in intra-network activities sends a positive signal about similar users’ evaluations about the platform. However, an increase in intranetwork activities when the level is high signals stiff competition among users on the same side on the platform, consistent with the observations in Tucker and Zhang (2010). Thus, online exchanges need to strategically manage users’ perceptions of activity levels on both sides of their platforms. This can be challenging as users’ preferences are asymmetrically related to inter- and intra-network activity levels. On a platform, high buying activity levels benefit the suppliers, but not necessarily the buyers. There is also a natural interplay between inter- and intra-network externalities on two-sided exchange networks, and strategies aimed at one side of the platform would affect the opposite side indirectly. Therefore, contrary to what many B2B exchanges are doing, being perceived as the largest exchange may not be the best strategy to influence users’ preferences!<sup>14</sup> The results in this and related studies (e.g., Tucker and Zhang 2010) show that it is necessary to consider what information to provide (e.g., whether to present information about buying and/or selling activities), and when to provide the information (based on the activity levels).

## Limitations

There are some limitations in this study. First, since the B2B exchanges from which we gathered data in this study are relatively popular, one can argue that buyers on these exchanges are not concerned with what other buyers are doing. Instead, the popularity of these exchanges could provide sufficient assurance to buyers to overcome any uncertainty about participating in the exchanges. That is, observation of others’ behaviors may not be necessary or salient when users are in popular platforms. However, a popular platform may also invite more fraud, just as the incident on Alibaba.com demonstrated (Bloomberg 2011; Time 2011), and increase the uncertainty faced by users. Furthermore, one must recognize that just because a B2B exchange is popular does not mean that it is useful for buyers and suppliers in every market segment. While the general popularity of a platform is important to users, what also matters to them is how well suited the platform is for specific product categories. For example, in B2C contexts, book sellers have a stronger inclination to use Amazon Auction than eBay; even though eBay has better name recognition and higher web traffic among online auction markets, Amazon is perceived to be more efficient in the book segment (Walczak et al. 2006). In a similar manner, buyers on popular B2B platforms still consider what other buyers in the same product categories are doing. Thus, by analyzing buyers’ behavior at the product category level in this study, we managed to observe phenomena in specific market segments where other buyers’ behaviors did in fact matter to the focal buyers.

Second, by using secondary data, we can only infer users preferences indirectly from the data. Our research design also restricted us from directly measuring observational learning and competition to which buyers are exposed. It would strengthen this study if we could directly establish buyers’ preferences, and the social and market dynamics that they experience on the platforms. Finally, we only examine buyers’ usage of the B2B exchanges. Although suppliers incentives to participate on the platforms may be affected by the pricing strategies of the exchanges (in the form of fees that exchanges charge suppliers), our hypotheses in general should also hold among multihoming suppliers. Future research can investigate how pricing and non-pricing factors affect multihoming suppliers’ behaviors and preferences.

## Conclusion

Today, not only can users select from multiple competing platforms, they can also choose to participate on multiple platforms at the same time. In this paper, we tackle a challenging but important research question: What factors affect users’ preferences when they are multihoming on two-sided exchange networks? We explicitly study a situation where individuals use multiple competing platforms concurrently, and address challenges presented by the dynamics of interand intra-network externalities on the platforms.

By observing multihoming users’ behaviors in two B2B exchanges over time, we find that users’ preferences for exchanges are associated with the levels of activity that occur within and across different sides of the platforms. Our results suggest that there should be a greater emphasis on investigating multihoming contexts and users’ preferences in future platforms research. Doing so helps us to better understand social and market dynamics that drive platform users behaviors on exchanges, and competition between platforms.

## Preliminary Analyses

We estimated Equation 3 using fixed effects regression with time period dummies $( d 1 , d 2 ,$ , and d3) (Table B1). Regression 1 is the baseline model that only includes the main effects. Results show that buyers’ preferences for Exchange B were negatively related to selling activities on Exchange $\mathrm { \ A } \left( \gamma _ { 1 } = . . 1 1 , p < . 0 5 \right)$ , but positively related to selling activities on Exchange B $( \gamma _ { 4 } = . 5 4 , p < . 0 5 )$ . In addition, buyers’ preferences were nonlinearly related to buying activities on Exchange B $( \gamma _ { 5 } = - 1 . 6 0 , p < . 0 1 ; \gamma _ { 6 } = . 6 6 , p < . 0 1 )$ . These results support H1 and H3.

Next, we included interactions between time dummies and selling activities in Regression 2. Although all interactions involving selling activities on Exchange A were not significant $( p > . 1 0$ for $\gamma _ { 1 2 } , \gamma _ { 1 8 } ,$ and $\gamma _ { 2 4 } ) _ { ; }$ , there was a significant interaction between period 1 and selling activities on Exchange $3 ( \gamma _ { 1 5 } = . 1 2 , p < . 0 5 )$ . Hence the positive relationship between selling activities in Exchange B and buyers’ preferences for the exchange was stronger in the last period than it was in the first, supporting H2.

In Regression 3, we added the interactions between time dummies and buying activities to the baseline model. There were significant interactions between buying activities on Exchange B and period 2 $( \gamma _ { 2 2 } = . 1 5 , p < . 0 5$ and $\gamma _ { 2 3 } = . 2 0 , p < . 0 5 )$ and period 3 $( \gamma _ { 2 8 } = . 0 7 , p < . 1 0$ and $\gamma _ { 2 9 } = - . 2 4 , p < . 0 5 )$ . These estimates indicate that the absolute value of $( \log ( B u y i n g _ { - i , t } ^ { B } ) ) ^ { 2 }$ , which represents the rate of change in buyers preferences for Exchange B with respect to buying activities in that exchange, was the least in the last time period, supporting H4. The nonsignificant interactions between period 1 and buying activity levels in Exchange B suggest a possibility of nonlinear time trend, which we accounted for in the main analysis by using a quadratic time trend specification.

We then estimated a model that includes all the interactions between time dummies and activity levels in Regression 4. The results are qualitatively similar to those we found above, with one exception. The interaction between period 1 and selling activities on Exchange B was only marginally positive $( \gamma _ { 1 5 } = . 1 2 , p < . 1 0 )$ .

Next, we examined a dynamic model with a lagged dependent variable as regressor. This specification addresses the concern that multihoming buyers’ usage of the exchanges over time might be path-dependent, where their preferences in the previous time period could affect their current preference. We estimated this specification using the one-step system generalized method-of-moments (GMM) estimator with robust standard errors. GMM estimation is appropriate in our context due to our “large N, small T” panel, where we have a large number of buyers but each buyer was observed only a few times (Roodman 2009). However, the use of a lagged dependent variable as regressor limited our sample to observations in the last three time periods.

GMM used the lagged values of all variables in our model as instruments. Table B2 presents the dynamic model results, which show that buyers’ preferences in the previous time period did not significantly affect their current preferences $( \gamma _ { 2 3 } = - . 1 2 , p > . 1 0 )$ . Both Sargan and Hansen tests of over-identification were not rejected, indicating that the instruments were suitable. Furthermore, the Arellano-Bond test failed to reject the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation.

Based on these preliminary results, we decided to use a static model with quadratic time trend function in our main analysis. This specification allows us to use all the observations in our sample in our estimations, and to account for a possible nonlinear time trend.
