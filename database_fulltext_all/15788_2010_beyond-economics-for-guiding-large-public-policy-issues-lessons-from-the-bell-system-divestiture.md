## a b s t r a c t

During the summer (2000), wholesale electricity prices in California were approximately 500% higher than those during the same months in 1998–1999. This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that the price hike has occurred due to an increase in fuel prices and real demand. The change of the two market fundamentals explains 45.73% of the price increase and <sup>fl</sup>uctuation during the crisis. The responsibility of energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms is 21.41%. The policy implication regarding the California electricity crisis is different from well-known economic studies which have attributed the price hike to the exercise of market power. The difference points up a need for drawing on researchers from multiple disciplines who are capable of checking each other's methodologies in guiding large policy decisions. This need was suggested by Professor Cooper regarding the AT&T breakup two decades ago. © 2009 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

## 1. Introduction

This research is prepared to celebrate the 95th birthday of Professor William W. Cooper. He has dedicated his whole life to the development of various methodologies and concepts in business research. He made a major contribution to the initial development of linear programming, goal programming, stochastic programming, non-linear programming and multi-objective optimization. Later, he has extended the technique of linear programming and non-linear programming into the development of Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) which has been widely applied to performance analysis in both public and private sectors. See the reference [6] that lists more than 200 selected contributions of Professor Cooper.

Among his contributions, Professor Cooper and his associates [4] have published an article entitled “A Goal Programming/Constrained Regression Review of the Bell System Breakup” in 1988. The article [4, p.1] has mentioned “the court decision to break up Bell (= American Telephone & Telegraph Co.) to accord with US anti-trust laws represents a highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant policy decision which is proving to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in other counties as well as the USA. Studies commissioned by the US Justice Department on this topic, utilizing econometric methods, concluded that the evidence failed to support the contention that the Bell system was a natural monopoly”. The research [4] applied a goal programming/ constrained regression developed in Management Science to the same functional form and the same data, but it nevertheless reversed the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the economic studies in every one of the 20 years covered. The research [4] has added that “the difference in results obtained by the two methods of analysis points up a need for drawing on persons from multiple disciplines who are capable of checking each other's methodologies when important policy decisions may be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by results…..”.

The main message conveyed to the audience in [4] is that there is a methodological bias in research. It can be easily imagined that different methods produce different empirical results. Hence, we should not make an immediate conclusion based upon an empirical result obtained from a single discipline (e.g., economics). In particular, when addressing a large policy issue, an approach originated from economics is often used as a conceptual and methodological basis. Policy makers do not pay attention to alternative approaches that originate from the other disciplines. Therefore, it is important for us to combat a methodological bias by applying an alternative approach to a large policy issue. The research [4] prepared their message 20 years ago. However, their message is still valid and very important in the current research on public policy. To document the current validity of Professor Cooper's message, this study reviews the California electricity crisis by shifting our interest from the Bell System divestiture in the 20th century. In this study, we compare empirical results obtained from well-known economic studies with those obtained from an application of an advanced Arti<sup>fi</sup>cial Intelligence (AI) technique in computer science. That is the purpose of this study.

The remaining structure of this article is organized as follows: The next section brie<sup>fl</sup>y describes the California electricity crisis. Section 3 summarizes previous studies on this large policy issue and economic hypotheses examined in this study. Section 4 proposes the use of a Multi-Agent Intelligent Simulator (MAIS) as the advanced AI technique to numerically examine rationales regarding why the crisis has occurred in California. Section 5 applies the MAIS to a data set related to California electricity markets before and during the crisis period. The performance of the MAIS is compared with that of the other well known computer science approaches (i.e., support vector machines, neural networks and genetic algorithms) in terms of estimating a dynamic <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of wholesale electricity price. In the section, MAIS-based sensitivity analysis investigates rationales regarding why the crisis has occurred in California. The empirical results obtained from the MAIS-based sensitivity analysis are compared with previous well-known economic studies [2,9] in which the electricity crisis was attributed to the exercise of market power by large energy <sup>fi</sup>rms. Section 6 summarizes this research along with future research agendas.

## 2. California electricity crisis and market structure (2000–2001)

During the summer (2000), wholesale electricity prices in California were approximately 500% higher than those during the same months in 1998–1999. The price explosion was unexpected. Policy makers questioned whether the electricity restructuring enacted in 1996 brought a bene<sup>fi</sup>t of competition promised to consumers. Federal and State governments investigated and issued reports about California's wholesale electricity markets.

Unlike previous price hikes observed in other US wholesale markets, the experience of California was not a transient phenomenon of a few days' duration. Rather, it was a series of events from May 2000 to May 2001. Table 1 (source: http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California\_ electricity\_crisis) lists the times and events related to the California electricity crisis.

## 2.1. Business of electric power industry

The electric power business is separated into the following four functions: (a) generation, (b) transmission, (c) distribution and (d) retailing. The main functions are generation and transmission, both of which are traded in US wholesale markets of electricity. Generally speaking, two types of transactions can be found in the US wholesale markets. One of the two is a bilateral (usually long-term) exchange contract between a generator(s) and a wholesaler(s). The other is a short-term auction-based transaction. In the short-term transaction, a market operator accepts biddings from both generators and wholesalers. The operator then determines the market price and quantity of electricity. Thus, this type of wholesale market is controlled and coordinated by ISO (Independent System Operator) and RTO (Regional Transmission Organization) that operates not only a wholesale market but also a transmission market [26].

The wholesale market is functionally separated into a power exchange market and a transmission market. The power exchange market is functionally separated into a Real Time (RT) market, an Hour-Ahead (HA) market, a Day-Ahead (DA) market and a long-term contract market. These markets need to integrate the supply capabilities to satisfy a constantly changing demand on electricity. Coordinated auctions are used for the <sup>fi</sup>rst three types of exchange markets. Bilateral contracts are found in the fourth market [17].

## 2.2. California ISO and PX (power exchange)

Before the electricity crisis (2000–2001), energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms in California used power trading in short-term auction markets: DA market run by PX and HA market run by ISO. Both PX and ISO are market institutions for power trading. The DA market is a “<sup>fi</sup>nancial and forward” market because all the transactions in the DA market stop 1day before real delivery of electricity. The bidding decisions in the DA market are determined by the speculation of traders. Meanwhile, the HA market can be considered as a “physical and spot” market, because the delivery of electricity in the HA market is not optional as in the DA market. All traders enter the HA market to correspond to actual <sup>fl</sup>ows of electricity. Hence, the aspect of <sup>fi</sup>nancial speculation is very limited in the HA market. Thus, HA is a physical market. In the HA market, traders need to make their decisions within a limited time (1 h). Hence, it can be considered as a physical spot market in this study.

The wholesale power exchange market of California is divided into three zones for the purpose of pricing as depicted in Fig. 1: NP-15 is in the north, SP-15 is in the south, and ZP-26 is in the center of the state. The central zone (ZP-26) has only two transmission links: one to a northern path (NP-15) and one to a southern path (SP-15). The northern path and southern path are not directly connected to each other. If they need excess electricity, they have to obtain it from other states as shown in Fig. 1. The zoning is based on the geographical location of nodes and a transmission grid structure. Each zone consists of multiple generators and loads.

## 3. Previous studies and economic hypotheses

After examining most of the governmental reports and research efforts on the California electricity crisis, this study <sup>fi</sup>nds that they have descriptively explored the large policy issue without any scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence. Many reports blamed wholesalers such as Enron as a source of the crisis. See the website (http://en.wikipedia.org/ wiki/California\_electricity\_crisis), reporting that “wholesalers such as Enron manipulated the market to force utility companies into daily spot markets for short term gain”. After extensive investigation, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) substantially agreed in 2003: “...supply-demand imbalance, <sup>fl</sup>awed market design and inconsistent rules made possible signi<sup>fi</sup>cant market manipulation as delineated in <sup>fi</sup>nal investigation report. Without underlying market dysfunction, attempts to manipulate the market would not be successful”. “...many trading strategies employed by Enron and other companies violated the anti-gaming provisions...” “Electricity prices in California's spot markets were affected by economic withholding and in<sup>fl</sup>ated price bidding in violation of tariff anti-gaming provisions.”

This study poses a simple question concerning whether the judicial claim was really true. Was it possible for wholesalers such as Enron to control the California wholesale power exchange market? Was there any scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence that supported the judicial claim? After carefully examining the previous research works, this study <sup>fi</sup>nds that only the two previous studies [2,9] have investigated the California electricity crisis with scienti<sup>fi</sup>c evidence. All other research works do not provide this study with any persuasive evidence. They are disquali<sup>fi</sup>ed as scienti<sup>fi</sup>c works due to their descriptive investigations only.

To investigate rationales regarding why the price hike has occurred during the California electricity crisis, this study reviews the research frameworks of [2,9] and summarizes their core concepts by seven economic hypotheses. The <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis is summarized as follows:

Hypothesis 1. (Increase in marginal cost). An increase in the marginal cost of oil and natural gas in<sup>fl</sup>uenced an increase in the wholesale price during the California electricity crisis.

This hypothesis is based upon the studies [2,9]. The research [2] reported that production costs contributed to 21% of the power price increase. A drought in Northwest Paci<sup>fi</sup>c region caused a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant shortage of electricity from hydro-electric dams. The price of natural gas started increasing gradually from the beginning of 2000. See [9, pp.5–7 and p.17]. The research [10] reported a similar description on the fuel increase. Thus, it is expected that the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant increase in the price of natural gas has contributed to an increase in the marginal cost of oil/gas power plants.

Hypothesis 2. (Increase in real demand). An increase in electricity consumption in<sup>fl</sup>uenced an increase in the wholesale price during the California electricity crisis.

The second hypothesis is based upon a data set on real demand in the three zones of the California market. This increase in demand attributes to the temperature and economic growth in California. The summer of 2000 was “unusually hot” [10]. While the temperature increase explained the increase in power price for summer, it failed to explain the reasons for the continued increase in power price for the rest of the year. The economic growth due to the opening of many startup companies increased the power demand. See also [9, p. 28] in which the increased demand was considered as one factor of the price hike in the crisis. In addition, the data set indicates a load increase during the crisis.

Hypothesis 3. (Greed of traders). Traders exhibited overwhelming desire for more pro<sup>fi</sup>t during the California electricity crisis. Generators increased their bidding prices, while wholesalers reduced their bidding prices.

The hypothesis is due to the previous studies, including the report of FERC, which has attributed the crisis to the exercise of market power by large energy <sup>fi</sup>rms. An important capability of the proposed simulator is that it can numerically examine the level of trader's greed by observing the utility function of each trader. It is now widely known that the wholesale market price of electricity has maintained an increasing trend, but the retail price of electricity has not increased signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly during the crisis. Rather, the retail price has almost remained the same under the control of regulatory agencies. The increase in market price implies that the generators have tried to obtain a large pro<sup>fi</sup>t and the wholesalers have suffered from an economic loss from the power market. Thus, it is assumed that generators were looking for more risk-taking behavior to produce more pro<sup>fi</sup>t by bidding high prices. Meanwhile, the wholesalers were anticipating an opposite direction in the manner that they reduced their bidding prices in the power market.

Hypothesis 4. (Electricity withholding by generators). Large generators withheld electricity during the California electricity crisis. The exercise of market power contributed to the price hike during the crisis.

The fourth hypothesis is based upon [9, pp.19–28]. See also [2]. The empirical analyses of [2,9] suggested that some generation <sup>fi</sup>rms deliberately did not make their maximum supply capacities available for the California electricity market. This study reexamines their assertion.

Hypothesis 5. (Capacity limit in transmission lines). A capacity limit in transmission lines was a source of supplier withholding. The transmission congestion occurred during the California electricity crisis and in<sup>fl</sup>uenced the price hike of electricity.

The research [9, p.8] reported that there was relatively little signi<sup>fi</sup>cant transmission congestion during the crisis period. However, it is important to investigate whether the supplier withholding during the crisis was intentional by generators or was due to an occurrence of congestion in transmission lines (so, the withholding in the supply side was accidental).

Hypothesis 6. (Competitive rent). An increase in competitive rent in<sup>fl</sup>uenced an increase in the wholesale price during the California electricity crisis.

The research [2, p.1397] has de<sup>fi</sup>ned competitive rent as pro<sup>fi</sup>t that fossil-fuel and reservoir generators make in a competitive market. Since the out-of-state generators cannot participate in the competitive power exchange market, those generators supply power at a <sup>fi</sup>xed price. Thus, competitive rents apply only for instate generators. The in-state generators increase the market price by increasing the competitive rent.

Finally, the occurrence of the price hike may be due to a combination of the above multiple hypotheses.

Hypothesis 7. (Combination). The price hike in the California electricity crisis was not produced by a single source (i.e., a market fundamental that is speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by each hypothesis). Rather, the price hike was produced by some combination of multiple market fundamentals.

## 4. Agent-based approach for power trading

## 4.1. Literature review

An important feature of the agent-based approach is its role in modeling and simulation. The structure of a complex system is modeled with many different types of agents equipped with various learning capabilities. Their learning processes are characterized by adaptive learning through which agents determine their decisions by interacting with an environment and other agents. The proposed model is numerically expressed and examined by a simulation-based investigation. The incorporation of a problem structure, along with a simulation study, provides us with a numerical capability to handle a large complex system where many components have interactions among them [3,11].

The agent-based approach has been recently applied to investigate a dynamic change of wholesale power trading. For example, [8,12–14] developed multi-agent adaptive systems that incorporated a dynamic bidding process for power trading. Furthermore, [18–22] have recently discussed the agent-based approach to investigate a dynamic change of wholesale electricity price. The study [19] considered two groups of adaptive agents. One of the two groups incorporated multiple learning capabilities in agents. The other group incorporated limited learning capability in agents. The study [20] also developed an agent-based simulator, referred to “Multi Agent Intelligent Simulator (MAIS),” that incorporated their adaptive behaviors and algorithms discussed in the previous studies. To extend the applicability of MAIS further, the research [21] considered various in<sup>fl</sup>uences of capacity limits in transmission on the wholesale price of electricity. The study [22] applied the MAIS to investigate an occurrence of the California electricity crisis as a primary study. This research can be considered as an extension of [22].

## 4.2. Agents in Type I and Type II

This study considers the following two groups of adaptive agents in order to investigate the dynamics of wholesale electricity trading:

Type I: The <sup>fi</sup>rst group consists of agents equipped with multiple learning capabilities. Their learning capabilities include a risk-averse utility function and a speculation capability to predict the dynamic change of electricity price over time. This group is referred to as “Type I”.

Type II: The other group of agents looks for a short-term gain via their limited learning capabilities. They do not incorporate any utility functions and any speculation capability, as found in Type I. Thus, the second group is less informed than Type I. This group of agents is referred to as “Type II”.

This study avoids a descriptive duplication on the two groups because [19] provides a detailed description on adaptive agents in Type I and Type II and these related computer algorithms.

## 4.3. Market clearing process

The research [21] discussed the market clearing process for US wholesale power trading. An important feature of [21] is that the bidding strategies of agents in multiple zones are connected by links with a capacity limit. This study fully utilizes the computational algorithm for market clearing process.

## 4.4. Comparison with the previous work [22]

The previous work [22] investigated the California electricity crisis. However, it has four methodological drawbacks that this study addresses and remedies. First, the learning process of all agents in [22] is modeled by conventional “reinforcement learning” [23]. In contrast, this research restructures the learning process from the perspectives of “partial reinforcement learning” in economics [1]. The reinforcement learning assumes that the learning of an adaptive agent always occurs in all trading experiences. Meanwhile, the partial reinforcement learning extends the concept by incorporating a limited number of their learning chances to obtain rewards. For example, an agent cannot always win in a power exchange market. In some trades, he may win and obtain a reward. However, he often loses in the other trades. That is the reality of power trading. Thus, the adaptive behavior of an agent in power trading should be characterized by partial reinforcement learning. As a result of such an extension to partial reinforcement learning, the agent-based approach proposed in this study may express the reality of power trading more closely than the previous study [22].

Second, this study compares the performance of MAIS with the three well-known approaches in computer science (i.e., support vector machines, neural networks and genetic algorithms), using a real data set on power trading during the California electricity crisis (2000–2001). The previous research [22] did not include the computation result of support vector machines, to which many computer scientists have recently paid attention. The methodological comparison examines whether the MAIS performs as well as the other well-known approaches in predicting a dynamic price <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of wholesale electricity during the crisis. Thus, the comparison with support vector machines enhances the methodological validity of the MAIS.

Third, the previous study [22] assumed that all traders during the electricity crisis consisted of a single group (e.g., Type I) of agents and did not consider the coexistence among different groups of agents (Type II). The assumption is dropped in this study. This study investigates the coexistence of two types (Type I and Type II) of agents and a dynamic change of the market composition during the crisis period.

Finally, this study applies the sensitivity analysis of MAIS to identify rationales regarding why the electricity crisis has occurred in California. We compare economic implications obtained from the MAIS' sensitivity analysis with those of the well-known economic studies [2,9]. The previous study [22] compared its empirical results with only those of [9]. This study incorporates additionally the economic framework of [2] into the sensitivity analysis of MAIS. Consequently, the sensitivity analysis becomes much larger and more reliable than the previous study [22].

## 4.5. Multi-Agent Intelligent Simulator (MAIS)

The MAIS consists of many software agents which interact with each other. They also interact with a power market (as an environment) by observing a price <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of wholesale electricity. The research [20] describes the architecture of MAIS. There are <sup>fi</sup>ve types of agents in MAIS: market administration, supply-side, demand-side, network operation, and utility policy making. The MAIS can arti<sup>fi</sup>cially create many generators in the supply-side and many wholesalers in the demand-side. The market environment typically consists of a pool market for DA and RT. In the computer simulator, HA is considered as RA because both are the same physical markets.

## 4.6. Adaptive behavior

In the proposed MAIS, traders are represented by software agents. Fig. 2 illustrates an adaptive learning process and its related knowledge base development, both of which are incorporated into the agents. As depicted in the <sup>fi</sup>gure, each agent recognizes that there is an opportunity to obtain a reward by participating in wholesale power markets. He understands that the market participation is always associated with risk. Therefore, he tries to obtain a risk-hedge ability through trading experience.

In the simulator, each market consists of many agents who can accumulate knowledge from their bidding results to adjust their proceeding bidding strategies. The agents operate in two modes: practice and real experience, as depicted in Fig. 2. During practice (at the left hand side of Fig. 2), the agents use non-reinforcement learning (or self-learning) where a power trading market determines the win or lose of each practice bid. The non-reinforcement learning is separated into three processes: (a) knowledge generation, (b) knowledge accumulation and (c) knowledge creation. In the knowledge generation, each agent has to generate knowledge by itself. The purpose of “knowledge generation” process is to discover or become familiar with the market as an environment. Thus, the agent bids in the markets by changing decision parameters, using random data generation. After their biddings, the values of decision parameters and their win-lose results are stored in a “knowledge accumulation” process. The accumulated knowledge is further processed by a sigmoid decision rule (for speculation on a winning probability) and an exponential utility function (for risk preference of each agent) if the agent belongs to Type I. This process is referred to as “knowledge creation.” This nonreinforcement learning is repeated until the practice period (H) is over. The period can be considered as a training process for each agent. The <sup>fi</sup>nal knowledge developed at the knowledge creation process becomes a starting basis for the own-bidding process of the partial reinforcement learning (at the right hand side of Fig. 2).

After the practice is completed, each agent starts the real trading experience. The bidding decisions during the real experience period are based upon the knowledge obtained from the previous trading practice period. The real experience period follows “partial reinforcement learning” because the agent reacts according to the feedback obtained from the external environment (i.e., a power trading market). The partial reinforcement learning is functionally separated into three subprocesses: (a) “knowledge utilization,” (b) “knowledge accumulation” and (c) “knowledge creation.” In the knowledge utilization process, each agent fully utilizes both the processed information from the knowledge creation process of both non-reinforcement learning (practice period) and the information from the partial reinforcement learning (real experience period) in order to create a new bidding strategy. Based on the positive and negative experience, the agent may change the direction of decision parameters according to the partial reinforcement learning algorithm. The positive feedback is generated from successful bidding experience which the agent wins and obtains a reward in the market. In contrast, the negative feedback is generated from a bidding result when he loses and does not obtain any reward in the market. The learning from mistakes is very important in examining adaptive learning [5]. The previous studies [18–22] did not include this important aspect in the agent development processes. The win-lose results and the corresponding values regarding decision parameters are stored in the knowledge accumulation process of each agent. The knowledge accumulation and knowledge creation process is similar to that of the practice period. The partial reinforcement learning is repeated until all iterations (T) are completed.

## 5. An application to California electricity crisis

## 5.1. Data statistics

As depicted in Fig. 1, the California market is divided into three zones for the purpose of pricing. A data set on the California electricity market used in this study is available from the University of California Energy Institute website. The data set consists of market information such as time of transaction, date of transaction, price at each zone in DA and HA markets, unconstrained price and quantity of the system, import/export quantities in each zone and prices of various auxiliary services. The DA trading was stopped from the California electricity market after 31st January 2001.

Table 2 provides a summary of the data set regarding the three California zones (DA and HA) over two different periods: before crisis and during crisis. Each sample represents hourly prices representing 24h per day. The before crisis period for SP-15 DA and HA and NP-15 DA and HA is from 1st April, 1998 to 30th April, 2000. The before crisis period for ZP-26 DA and HA is from 1st February, 2000 to 30th April, 2000. The during crisis period for SP-15 DA and HA, NP-15 DA and HA, and ZP-26 DA and HA is from 1st May 2000 to 31st January 2001. For all the DA markets, a maximum price of \$2499.58/MWH was observed at 7 PM on 21st January 2001. All the HA markets had a maximum price of \$750/MWH starting from 26th June 2000.

A data set regarding the capacity limits of California transmission links is not available publicly. To estimate a capacity limit on the lines between zones, we calculate the difference between import and export quantities in each wholesale market. A rationale behind choosing such a method is the assumption that a quantity more than the transmission limit has not been transferred on the transmission lines. After observing the data set, this study set the transmission limit to a value of 11752 GWH (maximum difference between imports and exports). The limit was applied on both the transmission links: the transmission link between central zone and northern zone and the transmission link between central and southern zones.

## 5.2. Estimation criterion

## 5.2.1. Evaluation criterion

An evaluation criterion is estimation accuracy (%) which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as

$$
1 - \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {N} \left| \frac {\text { Real   Market   Price } (t) - \text { Estimated   Market   Price } (t)}{\text { Average   Real   Market   Price } (t)} \right|\tag{1}
$$

Here, N stands for the number of evaluation periods. This criterion is suggested by the research [16].

## 5.3. Alternate approaches and comparison of estimation accuracies

Each agent in the MAIS can bid using either a Type I learning algorithm or a Type II learning algorithm. Table 3 summarizes the estimation accuracy of MAIS under 11 different combinations of trader composition.

## 5.4. Shift of agent composition over time

In Section 5.3, we assumed that the composition of the MIAS is initialized as one of 11 different possible combinations between Type I and Type II. To examine the change of agent composition over time, this study incorporates a time horizon in the adaptive behavior of agents visually summarized in Fig. 2. In the incorporation process, each agent has an opportunity to change his learning strategy over time. Fig. 5 summarizes a computational <sup>fl</sup>ow regarding the adaptive behavior in a time horizon. The <sup>fi</sup>gure describes the <sup>fl</sup>owchart for an adaptive strategy for a generator. A similar algorithm can be extended to a wholesaler. There are n generators $( i = 1 , . . , n )$ that can participate in a market. The generator is represented by an agent in the proposed MAIS. Each agent has to choose either Type I learning or Type II learning. The premise of the algorithm is that an agent prefers a learning strategy that fetches more reward in the previous learning experience. The agent evaluates his previous learning strategy at the end of every month based on the reward obtained in previous month.

In Fig. 5, let x represent the percentage of agents who are of Type I. Then, (100-x) represents the agents in Type II. Each agent is assigned two variables: Ownstrategy (the learning strategy of agent) and AltStrategy (the alternate strategy of agent). For example, if Ownstrategy(i)=Type I, then AltStrategy(i)=Type II, and vice versa. As a starting step, the learning strategies of each agent are initialized by setting the iteration period t to 1 April 1998. The variable, monthly reward, calculates the total reward obtained by an agent during each month. The time variable is incremented by one day. During the simulation, the i-th agent calls both Type I and Type II learning. Then, the agent bids based on the value in OwnStrategy(i). If the bidding price is less than the market price, the reward obtained is added to the monthly reward of OwnStrategy. Similarly, if the bidding price obtained by using the alternate strategy is less than the market price, the reward obtained is added to the monthly reward of alternate strategy. Note that the market price is obtained as a result of bids using own strategy, not alternate strategy. The alternate strategy is used to analyze a “what-if” case. Such a simulation is repeated for each month. At the end of each month, the monthly reward obtained by own strategy is compared with the hypothetical monthly reward that could have been obtained if the agent followed an alternate strategy. If the monthly reward of the alternate strategy is larger than the monthly reward of own strategy, then the agent switches strategy for the next month. That is, the agent follows an alternate strategy that could fetch more pro<sup>fi</sup>t than the own strategy. The value of x is calculated at the end of each month and is plotted in the graph.

Hypothesis 1. (Increase in marginal cost). The sensitivity analysis examines the <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis by increasing the marginal cost of 373 gas-<sup>fi</sup>red generators according to the rates depicted in Fig. 1 of [9, p.7]. This study used marginal costs for hydroelectric, geothermal, oil/gas, coal, wind, and nuclear is \$25, \$30, \$40, \$35, \$10, and \$50, respectively as the initial starting values of the simulation analysis. These marginal costs are all observed before the crisis. The marginal costs, except that of oil/gas, are used to examine the price <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of electricity during the electricity crisis, because a major price change has been not found for those fuels during the observed period.

Hypothesis 2. (Increase in real demand). The data set used in this study indicated a load increase during the crisis. Hence, the sensitivity analysis increases the real demand by 20% from the original values. This increase in demand was applied only during the peak load time (from 7:00 to 21:00), because the price hike occurred during the peak load period. Any major price change was not found in the other period. The 20% increase is due to the information that is obtained from the website (www.ucei. berkeley.edu/datamine/uceidata/uceidata.zip). According to the information source, the real demand has increased during the period from 4th May 2000 to 31st January 2001. For example, the load was 20,880.40 GWH (at 9:00 on 1st June 1999) and 23,694.10 GWH (at 9:00 on 1st June 2000), respectively. This implies an increase of 13.48% during that time. An average of all such increases in loads is 19.69%. Hence, the proposed sensitivity analysis used 20% for increase.

Hypothesis 3. (Greed of traders). The simulator assumes that generators were looking for a more risk-taking behavior to produce more pro<sup>fi</sup>t by bidding high prices. Meanwhile, the wholesalers were anticipating an opposite direction in the manner that they reduced their bidding prices in the power market. The experiment controls a parameter that represents the level of risk aversion in an exponential utility function. For evaluating the economic assertion, the parameter range of for generators was changed from (0.004, 0.065) to (0.0003, 0.0015). Similarly, the parameter range for wholesalers was changed from (0.004, 0.065) to (0.01, 0.07). See [19] for a detailed description on the parameters.

Hypothesis 4. (Electricity withholding by generators). The proposed sensitivity analysis used the mean values of output gap summarized in Table 7 of [9, p.23]. In the experiment, the maximum supply was reduced to its adjusted vale (= the maximum supply−a mean value of output gap).

Hypothesis 5. (Capacity limit in transmission lines). There is no data available on the transmission line limits in the California transmission grid. This study utilized the information about system-wide imports and exports for both DA and HA market, available at (www.ucei. berkeley.edu/datamine/uceidata/uceidata.zip). To estimate a transmission limit, we calculated the average of the absolute difference between the imports and exports for both DA and HA markets. The average was 11,752 GWH, which was chosen as the line limit for all the transmission lines. For the sensitivity analysis, the line limit was reduced by 25%. So, the line limit was 8814 GWH.

Hypothesis 6. (Competitive rent). The research [2, p.1397] has de<sup>fi</sup>ned competitive rent as pro<sup>fi</sup>t that fossil-fuel and reservoir generators make in a competitive market. Since the out-of-state generators cannot participate in the competitive market, those generators supply power at a <sup>fi</sup>xed price. Thus, competitive rents apply only for in-state generators. The in-state generators increase the market price by increasing the competitive rent (pro<sup>fi</sup>t). In the proposed simulation, this means an increase in bidding price, or an increase in the two mark-up ratios: β in the DA market and η in the HA market. Each mark-up ratio indicates the level regarding how much a generator increases his biding price from a marginal cost in the two markets. Therefore, β=0 implies that the competitive rent for DA is 0. Similarly, η=0 implies that the competitive rent for HA is 0. Originally, we assume that the two mark-up ratios (β and η) exist in the range (0, 1). See [2, p.1396, Table 3] in which the competitive rent increased 3 times approximately from 708 (in 1999) to 2101 (in 2000). To verify the hypothesis, this study changed the values of $\textrm { \textsf { B t o } } { \frac { 3 \beta } { ( 1 + 2 \beta ) } } { \ a n d \ } \eta \ \mathrm { t o } \ { \frac { 3 \eta } { ( 1 + 2 \eta ) } }$ in this simulation. See [19] for a detailed description on the mark-up ratios.

Hypothesis 7. (Combination). It can be easily imagined that the price hike in the California electricity crisis was not produced by the change of a single market fundamental. Rather, the price hike was produced by some combination of multiple market fundamentals. Hence, the proposed use of MAIS needs to consider such a simultaneous change of multiple market fundamentals.

The simulator starts from the initial values on decision parameters and markup ratios that are obtained from a data set before the crisis and then it computes the estimation accuracy, using a data set during the crisis. The average estimation accuracy of MAIS (at the <sup>fi</sup>rst row of the left hand side) is 73.06% that serves as a benchmark score for proceeding comparison. The estimation accuracy indicates how much a price <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of electricity during the crisis can be explained by the market fundamentals before the crisis and a change of a market fundamental as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by each hypothesis. ## 5.6. Comparison with previous economic studies [2,9]

This study compares the numerical results obtained from the MAIS-based sensitivity analysis with the empirical results reported in the two economic studies [2,9]. The economic studies are very wellknown among researchers and individuals who are related to the energy utility industry. Differences and similarities between this research and the previous studies are summarized in Table 6.

First, the four research works used different approaches to investigate the California electricity crisis, as summarized in Table 6.

Second, the research [9] investigated only the DA market in California, using a descriptive approach. The study [2] investigated the HA market, using an analytical (economic) approach. In contrast, this study examines the bidding strategies of traders in both DA and HA markets before and during the electricity crisis, using an agent-based approach developed in computer science. The bidding strategies of traders in the crisis were not considered in the previous economic studies [2,9]. Moreover, the two (DA and HA) types of power exchange market existed in California before 31st January 2001. Hence, it may be expected that this study provides more reliable evidence on the California electricity crisis than the previous studies [2,9].

Third, [9, p.8] reported that a capacity limit on transmission lines did not in<sup>fl</sup>uence the price hike during the California electricity crisis. The study [2] considered transmission links but the study did not examine an occurrence of congestion. The proposed MAIS-based sensitivity analysis numerically con<sup>fi</sup>rms the validity of their previous <sup>fi</sup>nding on the line capacity and the congestion issue during the crisis period.

Finally, [9, p.29] concluded that “the high prices experienced in the summer of 2000 re<sup>fl</sup>ected the withholding of suppliers from the market by suppliers (generators or marketers).” They added that “observed prices in California in summer 2000 were greater than the benchmark competitive price level. The differences were not fully explained by higher loads, reduced levels of imports, high gas prices or by high prices for NOx (Nitrous Oxide) emissions.” The study [2, p.1376] supported the opinion by concluding “in summer 2000, wholesale electricity expenditure were \$8.98billion up from \$2.04billion in summer 1999.

The study [2] <sup>fi</sup>nds that 21% of this increase was due to production costs, 20% to competitive rents and 59% to market power.” In contrast, the sensitivity analysis of MAIS indicates an opposite priority among these market fundamentals. That is, the California electricity crisis was initiated by an increase in the marginal cost of oil/natural gas. Such a price increase of fuel occurred along with increased real demand of electricity. Under such a business scenario, all traders became greedy for more pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Large generators withheld electricity during the crisis period. This study explains the difference between observed prices and MAIS estimates at the level of 91.15% estimation accuracy. Thus, the change in the two market fundamentals (fuel price and real demand) was the main reason of the price hike in California. It is true that energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms became greedy and withheld generation capacities during the crisis. In this sense, this study found an in<sup>fl</sup>uence of market power by large energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms, as discussed by [2,9]. However, the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the greedy strategy (electricity withholding) by energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms was less than that of the natural changes in terms of estimating the price hike and large <sup>fl</sup>uctuations of electricity during the crisis. Thus, the numerical <sup>fi</sup>ndings identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by the MAIS-sensitivity analysis were different from the analytical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the economic studies [2,9] in which the withholding of generators (as a market power) was mainly attributed to the price hike during the California electricity crisis.

## 6. Conclusion and future extensions

Professor Cooper asserts that there is a methodological bias in research. Therefore, we need to examine different methods for addressing large policy issues such as the breakup of AT&T and the California electricity crisis. Although the assertion of Professor Cooper [4] was prepared twenty years ago, his assertion is still valid and very important in the current research and discussion on public policy. To document the validity of Professor Cooper's assertion, this study reviewed the California electricity crisis. This study used MAIS to numerically examine rationales regarding why the crisis occurred during May 2000–Janurary 2001. MAIS generated numerous trading agents equipped with different learning capabilities and arti<sup>fi</sup>cially duplicated their bidding strategies in the California electricity markets during the crisis period.

In this study, we con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the methodological validity of MAIS by comparing its estimation accuracy with those of the three well-known computer science techniques (SVM, NN, and GA). The estimation accuracy of MAIS outperformed those of the three techniques. The result indicates that MAIS performs as well as the three other techniques in terms of price estimation.

After con<sup>fi</sup>rming the methodological validation of MAIS, this study investigated the dynamic change of agent composition over time. We found that all agents in Types I and II coexisted in the electricity market before the crisis. However, they gradually shifted to Type I (multiple learning capabilities) so as to adjust themselves to the price <sup>fl</sup>uctuation of electricity during the crisis. This result indicates that the agent composition depends upon the change of market fundamentals in an electricity trading market. Different types of agents can coexist in a stable market (like the California market before the crisis) where they can easily predict the change of price and market fundamentals. However, all agents need to speculate more carefully the change of electricity price and market fundamentals in an unstable market (like the California market during the crisis) where they cannot predict the dynamics between price and market fundamentals. Consequently, all agents shift to Type I in such an unstable market.

The sensitivity analysis of MAIS found that 40.46% of the price increase was due to an increase in marginal production cost, 17.85% to traders' greediness, 5.27% to a real demand change and 3.56% to market power. The remaining 32.86% was attributed to other unknown market fundamentals and an estimation error. This numerical result indicates that the price hike in the crisis has occurred due to an increase in fuel price and real demand. The change of two market fundamentals explained 45.73% $( = 4 0 . 4 6 \% + 5 . 2 7 \% )$ of the price increase during the crisis period. The responsibility of energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms was 21.41% $( = 1 7 . 8 5 \% + 3 . 5 6 \% )$ . Consequently, the price hike during the California electricity crisis was mainly due to the increase in production cost and real demand. This study found the exercise of market power during the crisis, but the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the market power was less (half) than that of the increase in fuel price and real demand. Thus, the policy implication obtained from this study is different from very well-known economic studies [2,9] which have attributed the exercise of market power by large energy utility <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Finally, the <sup>fi</sup>nding in this study demonstrates that Professor Cooper's assertion about the need for applying alternative methodologies to guard against unwarranted conclusions is still valid and very important for not only research on public policy but also new science development (e.g., [24]) in the 21st century.
