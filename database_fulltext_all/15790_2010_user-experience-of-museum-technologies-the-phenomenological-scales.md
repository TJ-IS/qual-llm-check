## Abstract

Museums increasingly rely on technology as a guarantee of enhanced visitor experience. However, both in Information Systems and Museology research, little attention has been paid to the evaluation of user experience (UX) with technologies in cultural heritage environments. This paper reports research in this area that uses a framework of interpretive archaeology and phenomenology. Users of museum technologies are studied to determine whether the framework’s criteria correspond to visitor expectations and can be met by Information Technologies (IT). Our epistemology is methodologically independent and by using a multi-methodological approach, mixing both qualitative and quantitative approaches, it is consistent with the original spirit of the theory of understanding. The findings confirm the importance of phenomenology and post-phenomenology as a reference to assess IT UX in museums. In addition, our field study indicates that technologies available in museums – namely audio guides, interactive kiosks and computers – contribute positively to an experience of the past. ## Introduction

Museum studies in a wide range of journals have noticed that individuals have an increased interest in the past in general (Liew, 2005), and in heritage sites in particular (Poria et al., 2006). The principal motivations for heritage site visits are the desire to connect with one’s heritage and the desire to learn more about history (Poria et al., 2006). According to Kimmelman (2001), ‘we go to museums to remind ourselves who we are’ (p. 1). Visitors gain many benefits from heritage sites, from making connections with the past and finding an identity (Chronis, 2005) to the ‘nostalgic bonding’ linked to the recollection of things belonging to the past (Holbrook & Schindler, 1994). Learning about the past is a valuable experience both for its educational effects and its potential to become a highly personal event for the participant.

Consequently, providing visitors with a valuable experience has become a crucial mission for cultural heritage institutions. Information Technologies (IT) have been presented as a guarantee of enhanced visitor experience. Indeed, numerous studies promote technologies as a reasonable way for museums to re-energize their relationships with their visitors (e.g. Fopp, 1997; Digicult Report, 2002; Messham-Muir, 2005; Vom Lehn & Heath, 2005). Common museum technologies such as audio guides and interactive multimedia kiosks generally provide historical background and tutorial aids to visitors (Deshayes, 2004). Tussyadiah & Fesenmaier (2009) also shed new light on the role of online videos in tourism. They analyse the effects of online shared videos on viewers’ perceptions and their results indicate that this medium offers an opportunity both for information sharing and for reflection. More precisely, online viewers were able ‘to re-experience New York City as daydreams and reminiscences’ (Tussyadiah & Fesenmaier, 2009, p. 36).

Museum experts seek to take advantage of all these technologies by combining them to offer highly sensorial experiences to visitors. Accordingly, more and more cultural exhibitions rely on IT-based materials. For example, the British Music Experience, that takes place in London, is a completely immersive and interactive exhibition that traces the history of music in Great Britain.

However, while some prior research has focused on visitor experience, there have been few studies evaluating the technologies that propose to enhance experience. In effect, professionals from cultural heritage sites still have few guidelines for assessing visitor experience, particularly in the context of IT use (Institute of Museum and Library Services, 2006; Pujol Tost & Economou, 2007; Tussyadiah & Fesenmaier, 2009).

The IS literature offers a framework that aims at evaluating technologies used with cultural heritage sites. Specifically, the phenomenological framework developed by Monod & Klein (2005) provides six criteria that capture the essence of a museum experience and the role of IT in this experience. Nonetheless, this framework presents several limitations. First, it claims to rely on Heidegger’s phenomenology, whereas some concepts, such as embodiment, clearly refer to post-phenomenology. Second, this framework is only normative and its criteria have not been operationalized with subjects. Therefore, this research aims to examine these phenomenological concepts.

The subsequent research questions that guide this study are the following:

1. Are the six phenomenological criteria suggested by Monod and Klein relevant to the assessment of visitor experience with IT in museums? Do these criteria correspond to individuals’ needs when visiting a museum?

2. Further, to what extent does IT meet the criteria proposed by Monod & Klein (2005) and successfully convey a phenomenological experience to visitors?

To answer these research questions, we have adopted a multi-methodology, mixing focus groups and a field study based on questionnaires. Therefore, this research also challenges common epistemological assumptions. Qualitative methods tend to appear more adequate than quantitative methods when applying a framework relying on phenomenology and post-phenomenology. Nevertheless, referring to the origins of the theory of understanding (Schleiermacher, 1810), we suggest that an epistemology may be methodologically independent.

This is also consistent with Max Weber’s ‘explicative understanding’. This paper breaks new ground by using a multi-methodological approach, mixing both qualitative and quantitative approaches; containing no reference to the functionalist or rationalist aspects of positivism, it claims to be consistent with the original spirit of the theory of understanding.

Our findings confirm the value of a phenomenological approach in assessing visitor experience in museums. This research is important because ‘guidelines for future museological design and self-evaluation are increasingly requested by professionals and institutions within the field’ (Pujol Tost & Economou, 2007, p. 82). Furthermore, IS researchers have paid little attention to the evaluation of technologies (1) with an interpretive framework that focuses on user experience (UX) and (2) within the context of cultural heritage.

This paper is structured as follows. First, we highlight the gap that exists in IS literature with respect to evaluating visitor experience with IT. Second, in the next section, we introduce the framework developed by Monod & Klein (2005). Third, we discuss the epistemo logical position and methodologies held in the paper. Fourth, we present our two studies, the focus groups and the field study, and report their results. Fifth, we discuss the empirical and theoretical contributions – as well as the limitations – of this research. The fifth section concludes this work.

## The evaluation of visitor experience

Researchers from different disciplines have developed a wide variety of frameworks to study visitor needs and to provide guidance to museums (Kotler & Kotler, 2000; Anderson, 2004). It is noteworthy that these frameworks have remained conceptual: they have not been empirically tested within museums or with visitors. Furthermore, they do not explicitly address the role of technologies in museums. For instance, marketing researchers Kotler & Kotler (2000) propose three categories on which museum professionals should focus in order to improve visitor experience. They are (1) the variety of visitor experiences, (2) the level and depth of visitor experiences, and (3) the design and orchestration of visitor experiences, such as the availability of certain facilities and services. While Kotler & Kotler (2000) principally identify the operational means to enhancing visitor experience, Anderson’s framework (2004) includes the organizational functions of museums that serve to develop a visitor perspective. More precisely, Anderson (2004) suggests four fields on which museums should focus in order to be visitor-oriented: (1) governance, (2) institutional goals, (3) management strategies and (4) communication style.

Certainly, a number of empirical frameworks do address visitor experience and a few examine visitor experience with technologies (e.g. Falk & Dierking, 1992; Peacock & Brownbill, 2007). But while these studies do focus on visitor experience, they rarely identify specific criteria that could be used to evaluate technologies. Additionally, museum professionals still have few guidelines for assessing visitor experience, particularly in the context of IT use (Institute of Museum and Library Services, 2006; Pujol Tost & Economou, 2007). An American report states,

[Museums] would like information, training, and guidance on how to better assess user needs, including methods of collecting information about the characteristics of users, how they use an institution’s technology and digitization services and products, and for what purpose. (Institute of Museum and Library Services, 2006, p. 124)

It seems that IS research could fill this gap and answer this call for more studies on the evaluation of museum technology. As a matter of fact, the evaluation of UX is an important field of research in the IS discipline and it has given rise to several frameworks in Human–Computer Interaction (HCI). UX refers to research that ‘goes beyond the purely cognitive and task-oriented perspective’ that is generally taken when studying information systems (Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 2006, p. 92). More precisely, Hassenzahl & Tractinsky (2006) highlight three attributes of UX research: first, that it goes beyond the instrumental perspective; second, that it encompasses emotions and affect; and third, that it deals with experiential issues. For instance, the UX field has paid attention to the very diverse needs addressed by technology such as play (Mandryk et al., 2006), enchantment (McCarthy et al., 2006; Ni Chonchuir & McCarthy, 2008) and enjoyment (Blythe et al., 2003). Generally, emphasis is placed on the emotional, hedonic or aesthetic dimensions of experience (Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 2006).

Several HCI researchers assert that UX is varied and multidimensional, thus requiring a multi-layered approach to capture it (McCarthy & Wright, 2004; McCarthy et al., 2006; Isomaki, 2009). For instance, McCarthy et al. (2006) have studied two types of interactive experiences involving enchantment that users can have with films and mobile phones. Their research objective has been to identify key ‘sensibilities’ that HCI designers can take into account when designing systems. The presence of two different contexts of enchantment stresses the importance of having multiple criteria to assess UX. While McCarthy et al. (2006) have paid attention to a specific type of experience, namely, enchantment, McCarthy & Wright (2004) as well as Isomaki (2009) have striven to develop a holistic framework to facilitate the understanding of UX. Isomaki’s non-instrumental framework takes root in phenomenology and depicts human beings’ experience as a holistic entity.

The field of UX has also inspired the creation and development of new IT-based applications called experiential computing systems ( Jain, 2001; 2003). As an illustration, Jain and his research group at the Georgia Institute of Technology are focusing on developing applications that acknowledge UX, and store and process these user insights as traditional data. Experiential computing systems can integrate components such as sensors, video or virtual reality in order to address user needs from multiple perspectives (Jain, 2001; 2003).

The aforementioned literature provides several interesting frameworks for assessing UX, but none of these focuses on the context of cultural heritage. Furthermore, Isomaki (2009) acknowledges that she has created a meta-theoretical framework that needs to be adapted to specific research contexts. Therefore, the next section is devoted to the presentation of Monod & Klein’s (2005) framework through the phenomenology of Heidegger and its criticisms and developments through postphenomenology (Don Ihde, 1999).

## Introduction of the conceptual framework

## Presentation of the framework through phenomenology and post-phenomenology

Monod and Klein’s framework includes six criteria: historicity, possibilities of being, self-projection, embodiment, re-enactment and context. As illustrated hereafter, the three former criteria are directly extracted from Heidegger’s Being and Time, while the situation of the three later criteria is more mixed: embodiment refers to post-phenomenology, re-enactment to history and context to hermeneutics. However, these three criteria have in common their linkage to historicity, possibilities of being and self-projection. Phenomenology represents a promising way to move beyond the simple idea of ‘enchantment’ developed by HCI literature (McCarthy et al., 2006).

Why are historicity, possibilities of being and selfprojection so important for us? Because, according to Heidegger in Being and Time (1996), we constantly struggle against everydayness and averageness. In averageness everyone is other, and no one is himself. In this situation one is in the manner of dependency and inauthenticity (p. 128). We feel that we are not ourselves, especially in public transporations (p. 129). Our quest for authenticity is the quest for our Dasein, often translated as our ‘presence’, or our ‘being-present’. What is Dasein? Dasein is the being which I myself always am (p. 53). In everydayness, we still have a being, a Dasein, but in an average mode: We call this everyday indifference of Dasein averageness (p. 43). In our everyday life, our greatest fear is to be dissolved in the other. This general ‘other’ is referred to as the ‘they’. Initially, ‘I’ ‘am’ not in the sense of my own self, but I am the others in the mode of the they (p. 129). Initially, I am not myself, I am dissolved in the ‘they’. We feel that ‘They’ tell us how to feel. ‘They’ tell us how to think. We feel that what is primordial is flattened out, that there are no mysteries in the world, that our possibilities are levelled down. We feel that we live in a world of things rather than a world of beings. Therefore, we seek for meaning in the things and people around us. How can a museum contribute to our quest for meaning and authenticity? Heidegger responds to this question through the example of The ‘antiquities’ preserved in museums, which introduces the concept of historicity.

## Historicity

In the ‘antiquities’ preserved in museums something is still objectively present in the ‘present’ (Heidegger, 1996, p. 380). What we seek in a museum is not the objects, but the world within which these antiquities were encountered: This world is no longer. But what was previously innerwordly in that world is still objectively present (p. 380). We seek for what was previously innerwordly in that world. We seek the ‘disclosure’ of beings that have-been here (p. 393).

Why is this disclosure of past beings linked with our quest for authenticity? Because we are our past (p. 20). The meaning of who we are is in temporality. This is what Heidegger calls historicity: The quest of our being must receive its directive through the quest of our own history. Historicity is when the inquiring of the being becomes the inquiring of a historical being. That means that we ‘become historical’ and we seek positively [to make] the past our own (p. 21). By doing so, we come into full possession of our most proper possibilities of inquiry (p. 21). We understand that we are a temporal being or a historical being. Temporality is at once the condition of possibility of historicity and a temporal mode of being of ourselves. Therefore, our being is linked to historicity, but also to possibilities.

## Possibilities of being

We always understand ourselves in terms of our existence, in terms of our possibility to be ourselves or not to be ourselves (Heidegger, 1996, p. 12). But we don’t ‘have’ a possibility only as a mere attribute or something objectively present. Indeed, according to Heidegger, we are essentially our possibility, we can ‘choose’ ourselves in our being. We can only have lost ourselves and we can only have not gained ourselves because we are essentially possible as authentic, that is, we belong to ourselves. (pp. 42–43). Authenticity does not mean egotism, however. Rather, it means genuine selfhood as opposed to miserable egotism (p. 116). Our quest for meaning and for authenticity, should always start with our existence. Who we are, our ‘essence’, lies in our ‘existence’ (p. 42). This permits self-projection.

## Self-projection

In order to move beyond everydayness and averageness, our existence is decided by each of us only in our manner of seizing upon or neglecting our possibilities (p. 12). Authenticity relies on projecting authentic possibilities (p. 178). Once again, there is a link between this projection and historicity. We don’t possess our past as a property that is still objectively present. Further, our past is not simply something that has an effect on us. We are our past in the manner of our being, which on each occasion ‘occurs’ out of our future. (p. 20). Our own past does not follow after us but rather goes always ahead of us (p. 20). We always define ourselves in terms of a possibility that we are and that we somehow understand in our being (p. 43).

## Embodiment and post-phenomenology

As we have announced previously, embodiment is not a central concept in the phenomenology of Heidegger. But both Heidegger and Husserl laid the foundations of this concept when they tried to overcome the ‘classical modernist backdrop’ represented by Descartes. As Don Ihde (2003) remarks, Husserl, through the idea of intentionality, has gone beyond the separation between ‘subject’ and ‘object’. Relation becomes central in Husserlian phenomenology through intentionality: subjectivity becomes intersubjectivity. However, consciousness and subjectivity remain present.

Heidegger moves towards these ideas by claiming that one of the main characteristics of Dasein (being) is ‘always-being-mine’ (Heidegger, 1996, p. 43) and by the priority of existence over essence. Indeed, being in a world belongs essentially to us. Thus the understanding of being that belongs to us implies just as primordially the understanding of something like the ‘world’ and the understanding of the being of others accessible within the world (p. 13). However, only with Merleau-Ponty can we begin to speak of post-phenomenology.

Post-phenomenology is the final move beyond subjectivity, which is replaced by embodiment. Bodies cannot be transcendental, they are existential (Ihde, 2003, p. 11). There is no more separation between internal and external world, Truth does not ‘inhabit’ only ‘the inner man,’ or more accurately, there is no inner man and the world, and only in the world does he know himself (p. xi). More radically y even the phantoms of ‘internal experience’ are possible only as things borrowed from the external experience (p. 27). Experience therefore happens through the corps ve´cu, or lived body as experiencing body (Merleau-Ponty 1962). This is the body-in-action, already outside itself in a world. To live my body is simultaneously and yet experientially to be both inside and outside.

There is a second aspect of post-phenomenology that get rids of the ‘consciousness’ of Merlean-Ponty, especially through the ‘social body’, the ‘situated body’ and the ‘de-centered’ body described by Foucault (1979). Nevertheless, our framework will not go that far and will therefore stay within the boundaries of the Merleau-Pontian phenomenology. Further, as we do not use the whole spectrum of post-phenomenology, we have decided to retain the phrase ‘phenomenological scales’ for the title of this paper.

## Re-enactment

Re-enactment is suggested by Heidegger concerning the antiquities in museums when he claims that what we seek is the ‘disclosure’ of beings that have-been here (p. 393) and that we want to understand the beings that have-been-there upon their ownmost potentiality-ofexistence (p. 395). This idea is brilliantly developed by Collingwood (1946), when he argues that the mission of the historian is to think himself into this action, to discern the thought of the agent (Collingwood, p. 213). Through ‘a priori imagination’ (p. 241), the historian has a double task: to construct a coherent image and to construct a picture of things (p. 246) through an imaginary picture of the past (p. 248). Therefore, an historical event known historically, survives in the present (p. 225). This idea is also clearly linked to the concepts of empathy and intropathy suggested for the philosophy of history (Droysen, 1858), then for human sciences from an ontological standpoint (Dilthey, 1883) becoming finally two of the principal methods in human sciences (Weber, 1910; Nielsen, 1981).

## Context

As important as it can be, intropathy is just one of the two techniques in the theory of understanding as originally presented by Shleiermarcher in General Hermeneutics (1810). Indeed, the theory of understanding that is also the theory of interpretation of a text (Schleiermarcher, 1810) not only implies the ‘psychological interpretation’, in which we try to grasp the author’s intention, but also a ‘grammatical interpretation’. This second kind of interpretation requires being sensitive to the linguistic, cultural and historical context of the text in order to be able to have a full understanding of it. Surprisingly, through the influence of Dilthey, Husserl and Heidegger, this second kind of understanding has been forgotten and only psychological interpretation has remained. The discipline that has kept the tradition of this original theory of understanding is hermeneutics.

However, context is still present in Heidegger’s phenomenology through the importance of the world: we tend to understand our own being in terms of that being to which it is essentially, continually and most closely related – the ‘world’. In ourselves, and therefore in our own understanding of being, the way the world is understood is in its essence reflected back upon the interpretation of our presence (p. 16).

## The link between the six criteria: a holistic approach

What is the link between the six criteria? As we have stated, the first three are extracted directly from Heidegger: when, in a museum, we seek the disclosure of beings that have been here, we want to understand them in their ownmost potentiality-of-existence (p. 395) and we want to disclose the silent power of the possible (p. 395). Through the antiquities in a museum, we understand that we are made of history, of the great History and of our own history. We seek to understand the possibilities of past characters partly because they may inspire our own possibilities of being, partly because we may project ourselves into them in order to seek our own possibilities of existence and find meaning for our being. The three remaining criteria, embodiment, re-enactment and context, belong more to other sources, namely postphenomenology, history and hermeneutics. However, they are all linked to phenomenology. Indeed, the phenomenological experience is not only an inner experience, but also an outer one. Re-enactment may be considered as a kind of self-projection into possibilities inspired by history that is linked to empathy. This contextual understanding is complementary to the psychological understanding as established in the General Hermeneutics of Schleiermarcher, which inspired Dilthey and then Heidegger. For all these reasons, even though for convenience of presentation, we have had to separate the phenomenological experience into six criteria, these criteria do have a strong link and may be taken as complementary aspects of a holistic experience.

## Epistemology and methodological issues

We have conducted two studies to operationalize Monod and Klein’s phenomenological framework. The first study uses a focus-group methodology – a qualitative approach – in order to ascertain the relevance of the phenomenological criteria and to determine how visitors value them in their museum experience. The second study builds on the results of the first, to further analyse these criteria. In this second stage, employing a field-study methodology, we have distributed questionnaires to visitors of a French museum. The purpose of this second study has been to propose an operationalization of the phenomenological criteria, and at the same time to evaluate the role of IT in visitor experience. Hence, the first study addresses the first research question, while the second study focuses on the second research question.

The multi-methodological approach has been promoted by several IS researchers as a means of creating a deeper comprehension of one’s research objectives (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988; Mingers, 2003; El Amrani et al., 2006). Furthermore, Morgan (1996), reviewing the use of focus groups in sociological research, has shown that focus groups are often mixed with another approach in order to yield more satisfactory results. For instance, conducting a survey after a focus group greatly expands study population.

However, one could object that the use of a phenomenological approach with a positivist instrument is inconsistent because a methodological dependence is assumed for each epistemology. Moreover, turning to the difference between explanation (erklaeren) and understanding (verstehen), phenomenology seems to belong to the latter because of the ontological distinction between these two terms. For this reason, before introducing the two field studies, we will first justify the feasibility of mixing quantitative methods with an interpretive epistemology. In Box 1, we put forward the two following principles:

1. A distinction of research goals rather than an ontological distinction (for explanation and understanding).

2. A methodological independence of epistemologies rather than a methodological dependence.

Since the framework proposed by Monod & Klein (2005) has not been applied before, this research is clearly exploratory and focus groups thus appear to be an appropriate methodology.

Recruitment of the participants We have conducted focus groups with the students of a French University located in Paris. Graduate students in management had a current course dealing with European civilization, a course that included a few museum visits. Although the course content required students to engage in museological experiences, nevertheless the students were generally not experts in either arts or museology. Therefore, this sample appeared to offer good representativity of the average museum visitor. Furthermore, we chose this target in accordance with the goals of museums, which increasingly target young people in their communications (Kotler, 2001). In France especially, several government programmes have taken place during this last decade to understand better the needs of young people. Recently, in April 2009, the French government even decided to offer free admission to all individuals under 26 years of age. Consequently, surveying this population appears to be relevant.

Specialists in focus-group methods also recommend paying attention to a certain number of features when creating groups. These are group size, compensation, number of groups, degree of acquaintance of the group members and degree of social homogeneity (Duchesne & Haegel, 2006). Our group sizes varied from nine to 13 subjects (Table 1). No compensation was given to the participants, but this study enabled them to voice their opinion on the culture course in which they were enrolled and express their interest in future museum visits. Three focus groups were conducted over a period of 1 month with a total of 33 subjects interviewed. Each focus group lasted an average of 1 h. Finally, our groups were socially homogeneous, which avoids domination effects (Duchesne & Haegel, 2006). Nevertheless, sociodemographic diversity was ensured by samples including both men and women.

Procedures We have followed a semi-structured approach, using an interview template with prepared open questions. The same interview template has been deployed across the groups to ensure the comparability of the results. The interview template is composed of two parts, as shown in Appendix A. The first part aims to explain the purpose of the focus group and inform the participants. The second part focuses on participant experience in museums. Specific questions connected to the phenomenological criteria were also asked in order to gain insight into visitor expectations of historicity. Phenomenology informs the theoretical backgrounds of this research, but not its methodology. The focus groups have been conducted rather with a hermeneutical approach according to the seven principles of Klein & Myers (1999). In Appendix B, we explain in more detail how we have applied these qualitative principles to our research.

Main findings Regarding the phenomenological framework, most of the participants talked (indirectly, of course) about phenomenological concepts in their responses. For instance, recurring words such as ‘context’ or ‘understanding’ were spontaneously used by the subjects. These words are associated with the phenomenological category context (see Table 2). Indeed, this criterion was the most important for our participants: historical context was repeatedly presented as the basis for understanding artefacts and for having a valuable experience in museums. In effect, many students explained that without context, it was difficult for them to enjoy their visit and to realize the importance of the historical artefacts. Some even said that they hated their visits of some museums because contextual presentations were absent. Context is therefore a relevant concept. Other researchers have also paid attention to this visitor need, noting that museums often neglect to provide visitors with adequate information (Hooper-Greenhill, 2000). Indeed, without cultural and historical context it is difficult for individuals to have a comprehensive understanding of their personal history or of history in general. It is often the case at cultural heritage sites that visitors do not understand the purpose of an object or even realize its historical importance. Scha¨rer (1996) contends that information provided in museums is generally more structural (some general indications) than cultural (information on the earlier context of use).

Re-enactment is another concept reported by the subjects as being meaningful for a satisfactory experience in museums. The participants indicated that they enjoyed exhibitions that excite the imagination and project them into the past. Reliving historical events in their minds is important in order to feel immersed in the past during a visit. Furthermore, McCarthy et al. (2006) believe that the experience of enchantment is important in the context of historical artefacts and museums. However, a few subjects from Focus Group #1 also complained about the noise and the crowds at some museums, such as the Louvre, a condition that prevents them from having a re-enactment experience. These perturbing elements may not be present in less renowned museums.

Embodiment is also perceived as a necessary criterion to have a positive experience in museums. The participants made reference to the important role played by their senses during a visit. However, some of the explanations given by the subjects were very specific to the types of museums they most frequently visit. Indeed, they had made several visits to the Louvre, a museum that attracts a great number of visitors and which has a very prestigious image. Therefore, two subjects from Focus Group #2 said that embodiment would not be appropriate in this setting, especially not touching the artefacts. It is reasonable to think that interacting with objects in other places such as science museums/ centres would be more acceptable to the students. Nevertheless, one could infer that the participants’ reluctance to interact with museum objects paradoxically reflects the concept of embodiment. Mingers (2001) explains that ‘Dasein is characterized by a general attitude towards the world of objects – that of concern – as in ‘‘to be concerned’’ that something is going well’ (p. 109). In demurring to touch the artefacts, our subjects at the same time demonstrated a great concern for these same artefacts. Therefore, we conclude that embodiment remains another relevant criterion for visitor experience.

Understanding the lives of other civilizations better and trying to put oneself into the shoes of historical characters is a common process for some of our participants, thus supporting the importance of self-projection. Indeed, the subjects tend to feel empathy for historical characters when visiting a museum. The self-projection concept proposed by Monod & Klein (2005) stems from philosophical developments concerning empathy. The German school, led by Friedrich Vischer (1807–1887), his son Robert Vischer (1847–1933) and Theodore Lipps (1851–1914), particularly contributed to developments in the understanding of empathy (Verducci, 2000). They define empathy (Einfu¨hlung in German) as an affective state that makes people project themselves into the self of another person.

Subjects showed interest in possibilities of being and in the capacity to understand the connection between past events and a current situation. Similarly, the multiplication of points of view on a single subject appears to be an important perspective that curators should adopt in exhibits.

Overall, our three focus groups expect an historical experience in museums. Some subjects even perceive the link between past representations and their own life. However, some collections were more successful than others in conveying this perception. For instance, the Greek civilization, which is closer to French culture than Egyptian culture, was often quoted as a referent by the subjects who had been to the Louvre. Some subjects believe that what they see in museums cannot be related to their own life. For this reason, we think that reactions for historicity were mixed.

In conclusion, context, re-enactment, embodiment, self-projection and possibilities of being are the five phenomenological criteria that are manifest in visitor expectations, while historicity appears to be more remote to some of our subjects.

## Field study two: field study at National Center of the History of Immigration (NCHI)

The first study indicated that individuals seek a phenomenological experience in museums so that they can fully benefit from their visit. This second study aims to examine empirically the extent to which museum visitors believe that museum technologies serve phenomenological concepts. This second study also has the objective of testing a scale for representing the phenomenological concepts, and of testing the phenomenological framework itself with a larger sample than the previous focus groups. Focus groups results served as a means for developing the content of our questionnaires – a common research practice (Morgan, 1996; Straub et al., 2004).

Research site The research site was the NCHI, a public museum located in Paris, France. This museum, inaugurated in October 2007, deals with the history of immigration, tracing its evolution in France from the ninetieth century to the present. NCHI exhibitions also show how immigration has contributed to the shaping of French society. In research conducted before the opening of NCHI, potential visitors expressed strong expectations about historicity and an experience of the past. In fact, Poli et al. (2007) showed that potential visitors expected that NCHI would (1) deepen their knowledge of the history of immigration, (2) offer new perspectives on their personal history, (3) contribute to identity building, and (4) explain the consequences of immigration on today’s world.

In addition to dealing with history, this museum uses modern media to communicate with its public. NCHI is equipped with common museum tools such as televisions, RFID audio guides, and also with more recent technologies such as computers and interactive kiosks offering both audio and video content (see Appendix A). The RFID audioguide is the museum’s central technology, since its use is necessary to hear the content of the videos and audio kiosks. In fact, the permanent exhibition has been designed in such a way as to give limited space to text panels explaining the exhibits.

Moreover, the IT-based permanent exhibition constitutes the real innovation of this museum. In fact, there are very few objects at NCHI besides some archives, pictures and a number of very symbolic articles such as luggage and passports that serve as metonyms for immigration. Consequently, the museum mediation is not achieved through artefacts or works of art but principally through media and dynamic content. For these reasons, NCHI appeared to be an excellent setting for examining the above-discussed historical and phenomenological concepts, and for evaluating the contribution of IT to an experience of the past.

Furthermore, the following considerations justify the choice of a history museum. First, we did not want to study science museums that generally include a great quantity of technological equipment and hands-on activities. In such a case, we believe that perceptions of historicity would be more difficult to measure; they could also be biased in such an environment. Finally, science museums appear less appropriate for an application of the phenomenological criteria, which spring from the humanities and the history of dasein. We also deliberately eliminated art museums because they generally offer less technology to their audiences than other museums. In fact, after visiting several art museums in the Paris area, we have found that this type of museum is comparatively less equipped with new technologies. In the end, our choice of historical setting was instrumental in conducting good research, for we were able to obtain a broad licence to conduct our field study at the NCHI.

Set of scales to measure the phenomenological criteria On the basis of our review of the literature, the phenomenological framework itself, and the focus groups, we developed scales for each phenomenological concept. We followed established IS guidelines for scale development (Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Straub et al., 2004; Lewis et al., 2005). Generally, three steps are suggested for construct development: (1) review the literature to determine the content domain of the constructs. (2) formulate the items for each construct, and (3) test the psychometric properties of the scales.

The literature was employed as a rigorous benchmark for evaluating the content of our scales. The findings of the focus groups also gave us further details on visitors’ expectations and the meaning attached to each phenomenological concept. Therefore these narratives helped us to capture well the essence of the phenomenological experience. As a result of these analyses, we developed three items per construct. After defining the domain of our constructs and developing the scales, we pre-tested the scales with a group of college students, and then refined several items. A few months later, we conducted a pilot study with 20 visitors at NCHI. Visitor comments were taken into account and we again modified several items to fit the scales to the research context. The final operationalization of the phenomenological constructs is presented in Appendix C.

Sampling and selection We used the following procedure to survey NCHI visitors. The researcher was positioned at the museum entrance in which visitors borrow the audio guides in order to ensure the participation of the maximum number of persons. This positioning was strategic because visitors had to return to this desk at the end of their visit to return the audio guides. We took advantage of this time to gather visitor feedback regarding their interaction with the museum technologies. By this process, we collected 111 questionnaires over a period of one and a half months.

Data analysis The descriptive statistics of the sample were computed using SPSS 12. To conduct the analyses, we deleted questionnaires that were unusable because of too many unanswered questions. Hence, we collected 111 questionnaires but used 106 questionnaires for our final sample.

The descriptive statistics indicate a higher proportion of females than males, with 66% of the participants being women and 34% men. This result is not surprising, however: French statistics on museum attendance similarly report a higher percentage of women in museums (Cardona & Lacroix, 2007). The mean age was 39 with an age range from 18 to 74. Appendix D provides details on the technologies used by our sample.

Subsequently, we analysed the visitor responses in order to establish the psychometric properties of the constructs. Five out of the six scales we have developed demonstrate good psychometrics properties, as indicated in Table 3. Except for self-projection with a Cronbach’s alpha slightly lower than 0.70, the other scales range from 0.735 to 0.889, denoting high reliability. Nunnally (1967) considers 0.6 to be acceptable for exploratory research.

We also conducted a Principal Component Analysis (PCA), with a VARIMAX rotation. Our first run of PCA showed that two items were not loading correctly on their proper construct: these were EMB1 (0.347) and SP3 (0.242). Consequently, we decided to delete them and to run another PCA. The second round led to better discriminant and convergent validity, meaning that the items were loading on their proper construct more than on the other constructs. The results are displayed in Appendix E. The VARIMAX rotation was performed with six factors, which together account for 81.2% of explained variance.

Data analysis also has helped to determine the role of IT in visitor experience of the past. We asked NCHI participants to rate their experience with the museum equipment using Likert scales. Since NCHI offers several types of technologies, we decided to survey the museum equipment globally. Although we cannot assert which technology most contributes to the phenomenological criteria, this approach is consistent with prior research (Sparacino, 2004; Bannon et al., 2005). For instance, Sparacino (2004) has shown that by combining multiple technologies in the same exhibit, individuals feel that their visit becomes more active and enjoyable.

Findings Unexpectedly, self-projection is the criterion that best characterizes the NCHI technologies. This means that visitors who used the provided technologies found that these devices helped them to project themselves into the lives of the characters. More precisely, 23.6% of visitors strongly agree with the fact that IT improves their self-projection, 32.1% of visitors agree and 25.5% slightly agree with this assertion. Visitors likewise felt empathy for the immigrants depicted in the various portraits at NCHI through the videos and interactive kiosks. IS research has already shown that IS can provoke emotional reactions such as flow (Koufaris, 2002; Skadberg & Kimmel, 2004) and enjoyment (Van der Heijden, 2004; Lin & Gregor, 2006). However, affective behaviour such as self-projection (or empathy) has only been lightly studied in the context of IT use. This assertion is supported by Costello et al. Indeed, empathy plays a role not only in hedonic contexts but also in business environments, in which it influences team relationships (Costello et al., 2008). Costello et al. Embodiment comes second: 17% of visitors strongly agree with the fact that IT improves their embodiment, 37% of visitors agree and 31% slightly agree with this assertion. Hence, the sensory experience offered at NCHI is highly rated. Most of the devices convey visual materials (videos, pictures and texts); the audioguides provide audio content, while the computers and the interactive kiosks give an opportunity for physical interaction. Context appears as the fourth construct fulfilled by the NCHI technologies. This is surprising, for one might have thought that context would be the easiest criterion for the NCHI technologies to meet, given that one of the chief roles of Information Systems is to transmit information to users. Our empirical results contradict this pre-conception. More precisely, the results indicate that visitors were not entirely satisfied with the cultural and historical background presented by the technologies. These findings support prior research that has shown the lack of comprehension and context presentation in museums (Scha¨rer, 1996; Hooper-Greenhill, 2000).

In scrutinizing the ratings in Table 4, we clearly see that the most advanced (and conceptual) criteria, namely possibilities of being and historicity, are also the ones that are the most difficult to reconstitute with IT for museum visitors. It is conspicuous that historicity is last in the minds of the visitors, though it is represented as the ultimate step towards a phenomenological experience, in the philosophical literature. Pointedly, 34.4% of visitors are neutral and 30.1% disagree with the statements that NCHI technologies contribute to historicity. Therefore, roughly 65% of visitors think that NCHI technologies do not convey a sense of historicity.

NCHI provides several technologies to its visitors, but the types of IT implemented do not create an entirely satisfactory experience of the past. More advanced technologies such as 3D or virtual reality systems may be a means of addressing this deficiency. Jain (2001) has explained that creating immersive and interactive environments requires multiple devices or sensors because one application can hardly cover all the primary senses. It may also be that technologies cannot replace the physical objects displayed in museums, and that this explains why visitors have trouble feeling historicity. The role of IT should be to contribute to the visit and enhance the appreciation of the artefacts, but not to enclose the visitors in a virtual world (Ciolfi & Bannon, 2002). What is clear for the potential use of IT in museums is that technologies that support all desirable criteria, such as those in Monod & Klein’s (2005) framework, are rare. Furthermore, since few technologies have all the characteristics described in the phenomenological framework, museums need to combine multiple devices in order to meet the overall goal of fulfilling the criteria (Monod et al., 2006).

## Discussion

Several researchers have pointed out the potential of phenomenology for user-driven research and for information systems in general (Boland, 1985; Mingers, 2001; Introna & Ilharco, 2004). Phenomenology presents a viable perspective from which to assess visitor experience with museum technologies. The intrinsic potential of phenomenology for user-driven research may lead this methodology to become the preferred methodology for evaluating technologies. Indeed, phenomenological prin ciples invite us to consider technologies as interpretive supports centred on authentic user needs. This mediation can be achieved through the use of technologies, because, according to Ihde (1999), ‘there are ways, through instruments – technologies – by which things can show themselves’. Since artefacts and textual descriptions may not suffice to explain history and reveal the meanings of objects, Ihde (1999) suggests using material hermeneutics to complement and, in some circumstances, contradict textual hermeneutics: ‘A material hermeneutics is a hermeneutics which gives things voices where there had been silence, and brings to sight that which was invisible’.

Monod & Klein’s (2005) framework also relates to the five ‘Human Modes of Being’ conceptualized by Isomaki (2009). According to Isomaki, the physical and organic modes of being correspond to sensorial experiences and are reflected in haptic user interfaces, which makes these two modes closely related to embodiment. The mental mode of being involves the consciousness and refers to the cognitive and affective reactions that humans have during an experience (Isomaki, 2009). We can find this mode in Monod and Klein’s framework in the criteria of context, self-projection and re-enactment. The cultural mode of being encompasses ‘civilization as signifying the higher spiritual features of humans’ (p. 198). These aspects of civilization can be connected to possibilities of being and historicity. The social mode of being seems absent from Monod and Klein’s phenomenological conceptualization; their holistic framework nevertheless spans four levels of Isomaki’s model.

The environment that we selected for conducting our research has encouraged us to consider topics other than commercial (buying) or work (efficiency) issues. Like Mandryk et al. (2006), who have examined UX with entertainment technologies, we argue that museum technologies require distinctive criteria for their evaluation. It is for this reason that we have considered a set of phenomenological constructs. This philosophical underpinning acknowledges the importance of experience to human beings. Furthermore, Heidegger’s work on historicity offers the added precision of being in harmony with recent developments in archaeology (Tilley, 1993, 1994).

Petter et al. (2008) acknowledge that the criteria selected for IS evaluation should be dependent upon the organizational context. That is, the criteria should fit not only the type of IS under evaluation but also the type of organization: ‘The selection of success dimensions and specific metrics depend on the nature and purpose of the system(s) being evaluated’ (p. 239). Prior research studying evaluation has mainly focused on a utilitarian perspective of IS evaluation that tries to determine how IS can increase organizational or individual performance. As is appropriate for this utilitarian context, users are often represented as managers or employees.

What is different about the cultural heritage context is that IT users are best compared to visitors. However, visitor performance in museums does not appear to be as important as employee performance in company settings. Rather, enhancing UX by providing entertainment and education is the crucial target for museums (ICOM, 2002). Consequently, it appears that museums should try to meet both functional (education) and hedonic (entertainment) needs. By extension, museum technologies should also support these two dimensions of visitor experience, and for this reason they fall into the category of hybrid systems (Valacich et al., 2007). Valacich et al. (2007) have proposed that the nature of information systems can be seen as a continuum, with hedonic needs and utilitarian needs forming the two poles. Similarly, in marketing research, consumer experience has been presented as a continuum between utilitarian and hedonic concerns (Voss et al., 2003). This figure of a continuum is particularly interesting in the case of museums, because these institutions welcome very diverse audiences (e.g. children, groups, students, connoisseurs, tourists) who each come with different expectations. For instance, depending on visitors’ needs, the same technology can be used in a hedonic way, for example having fun or having a rich sensory experience, or in a functional way, for example learning something new or seeking. Consequently, we decided to rely on Monod & Klein’s (2005) phenomenological conceptualization, a framework that includes both functional (e.g. context) and hedonic (e.g. re-enactment, embodiment, self-projection) criteria for assessing systems used in cultural heritage sites.

Although not grounded in a phenomenological framework, other studies validate the importance of these six phenomenological criteria for IT users’ experience. For instance, Pujol Tost & Economou (2007) surveyed visitors of the Ename Museum (Belgium) asking them about their favourite rooms and devices at the end of their museum visit. The devices the visitors preferred corresponded to the ones that were able to convey context, empathy, interactivity and sensation. More precisely, Pujol Tost & Economou (2007) found that context information constitutes one of the most important expectations of visitors. Moreover, empathy contributes to visitor engagement and satisfaction. The presence of interactivity and sensation (described by the participants as the possibility to touch) also lead to better learning.

The criterion ‘context’, which is very important to museum visitors, appears also to be significant in other contexts. Tussyadiah & Fesenmaier (2009) have analysed the role of online shared videos in tourist experience and have found that in the pre-tour phase, tourists use online videos heavily to look for information about the site they intend to visit. Consequently, providing a comprehensive and detailed description of the context of an experience seems to be one of the key missions of IS. The tourists surveyed by Tussyadiah & Fesenmaier (2009) were also interested in opportunities for the imagination and for mental evasion. The tourists were thus expecting the technologies to offer re-enactment experiences. These findings seem consistent with ours. In future research, our phenomenological categories could be merged with, or inserted into, existing scales that serve for the evaluation of information systems.

Monod & Klein (2005) have applied their phenomenological framework to the evaluation of the Archeoguide system, a technology used in a Greek archaeological site.

We have investigated visitor perceptions in order to determine the relevance of this phenomenological framework to user requirements. This work extends the framework’s domain by applying it to museum technologies.

Our research methodology has given us a more comprehensive view of user perceptions and experience. We have adopted a multi-method approach, namely focus groups and surveys, in order to examine UX. Multimethodology and methodological pluralism have been promoted by a number of IS researchers (Mingers, 2003; Becker & Niehaves, 2007).

The findings suggest some practical guidelines for museum professionals. Relying on Monod & Klein’s (2005) phenomenological framework, we recommend that museum professionals assess visitor experience of the past through the six criteria contained therein. Additionally, our operationalization of these criteria in the final instrument can serve as a practical tool for assessing visitor perceptions. We have proposed a set of 18 items that can be used by museum professionals to conduct further visitor studies. These criteria provide a more comprehensive evaluation of visitor experience with IT than traditional constructs such as attitude or satisfaction.

Our measures are also well adapted to the goals of cultural heritage institutions. Indeed, the phenomenological constructs capture the experience of historicity at a time when museums are increasingly expected to participate in history building and in promoting a sense of belonging (Krebs & Maresca, 2005). Poulot (2005) also points out the necessity that history museums offer visitors a perspective on history and identity by ‘collecting elements of the past and making people aware of them’ (p. 28). Further, this research has resonance for science or art museums as well.

Our results support and legitimize the presence of IT in museums. More precisely, the technologies studied at NCHI do contribute to a positive experience. Therefore, we have been correct in positing that individuals need a dynamic representation of history to understand better the influence of past events on personal existence. Our results are related to a set of specific technologies: televisions, RFID audio guides, computers and interactive kiosks. Therefore, this research could be followed by other studies that will examine more closely the role of each type of technology in the process of historicity. Museums are a place in which people look for experience (Carr, 2001) and meaning. Thus, if museum professionals successfully meet visitor expectations and place an emphasis on validating visitor experiences, museums could increase and broaden their audience.

This research does not measure the effects of a specific type of technology but rather the contribution of the set of technologies that was available at NCHI. Accordingly, we do not know which of the IT features most contributed to the phenomenological experience. Is it the video, the audio, the interactivity or something else? Therefore, we cannot draw conclusions concerning the role played by each type of technology. However, we have found that, overall, their presence helps visitors to have a phenomenological experience. ## Conclusion

This research has investigated a set of phenomenological concepts that serve as a basis for IS evaluation, and, more specifically, cultural heritage systems evaluation. These criteria were originally developed by Monod & Klein (2005) but had not been empirically tested. We have conducted three focus groups that reveal the importance of a phenomenological experience for museum visitors. The second stage, a field study in a museum setting, has shown that technologies can lead to enhanced visitor experience. These studies contribute to IS research concerning phenomenology and UX. The phenomenological scales that have been developed represent potential guidelines for museum professionals.

## Appendix B

Application of Klein and Myers’s principles to the focus groups

Before conducting the interviews we took into account the context of our participants, as explained in the recruitment process. The subjects were university students attending museums as a result of both a personal agenda and as part of their university degree program.

At the end of each focus group discussion, participants were invited to synthesize their thoughts and list their most important ideas. This way, we mixed two types of exercises and gave uncommunicated ideas the opportunity to surface. Furthermore, we did not want to be blinded by our research instrument, as cautioned by Miles & Huberman (1994). Therefore, the participants were also free to discuss other topics related to the museum and its technologies. This freedom of expression was an attempt to deal with the social construction of our research object (principle of interaction). Even though several researchers served as managers of the focus groups, subjects were considered to be the experts and to lead the interview in order to let their full experiences emerge (Thompson et al., 1989). This also had the desirable effect of creating a dialogical reasoning.

At the end of each session, researchers involved in the focus group management carried out a debriefing in order to elicit further participant responses. Another advantage of a multi-researcher approach is that we obtained a range their degree/major and experience with museums (how often do you go to museums?).
