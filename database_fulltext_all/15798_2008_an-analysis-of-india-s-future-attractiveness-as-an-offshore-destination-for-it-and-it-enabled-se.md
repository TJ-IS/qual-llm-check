## Abstract

Offshoring of services will shape the economic landscape for the coming decades, and present opportunities and challenges for individuals, organizations, and countries. Many countries are jostling in the global services marketplace to take advantage of the emerging opportunities. The success of an organization’s offshoring initiative depends critically on the selection of a right destination; a wrong choice may result in a failure and impose significant costs. This paper presents a framework to assess the attractiveness of a target country, which identifies various motivating, inhibiting, and facilitating factors that may influence its attractiveness. The framework is used to analyze the future attractiveness of India, a topic that continues to be debated. The relative attractiveness of other potential competitors for offshoring of services is also discussed. Contrary to other analysts’ opinions, we conclude that India is likely to sustain its leading position for a long time in the services sector, and that it is likely to emerge as a global hub for the delivery of services, analogous to China’s role in the manufacturing sector.

## Introduction

he information age has been identified as the third industrial revolution, following the agricultural and manufacturing revolutions of the previous centuries (Blinder, 2006). A unique characteristic of the information age is the movement of service-sector jobs from developed economies to offshore locations that are cheaper. In this new economy, the offshoring of services presents opportunities and challenges for individuals, organizations, and countries that are trying to gain or retain competitiveness. Offshoring and outsourcing have moved to the top of CEO agenda in most large enterprises. In a survey of 500 senior executives at multinational firms, 57% of respondents reported that they view offshoring and outsourcing as critical forces shaping the global economy (The Economist, 2005). The volume of services offshored is expected to increase manifold in the future. For example, in the case of information technology (IT) services, offshoring is expected to grow six-fold between 2004 and 2010 (Marriott, 2006).

Many countries are jostling in the global services marketplace to take advantage of emerging opportunities. Among them, India has emerged as a leader in providing offshore services to the industrialized, developed countries on the basis of its relatively low-cost, well-trained, Englishspeaking workforce (MGI, 2005a). India leads in the total volume of services offshored, whether through in-house offshore development centers (ODCs), or through outsourcing to India-based vendors (Kearney, 2004a, b). India is also a leader in the range of services provided. It is among the top locations for different sectors ranging from IT services (e.g., application development, infrastructure management) to IT-enabled services (ITES). Offshored ITES can be classified into business process outsourcing (BPO) and knowledge process outsourcing (KPO). BPO activities focus on lower-skill clerical work in areas such as accounting, HR, and customer relationship management. KPO activities involve higher-skill work such as analysis, design, and research for legal, engineering, architectural, financial, healthcare, and other fields (NASSCOM, 2006).

However, there have been doubts expressed about India’s sustainability as the top destination for offshored services (Herber, 2005). Some consultants (e.g., Marriott, 2006) have even predicted that India’s market share could erode as much as 45% by 2007 (Siddiqui, 2005; Silicon India, 2005). The reasons mentioned include the rise of competitors such as China, the Philippines, Malaysia, Vietnam, Hungary, and Poland; the rising wages of Indian employees in the sectors involved in offshored work; a high turnover of employees in the IT/ITES sector; a shortage of qualified labor pool in India; and poor infrastructure. Indian vendors have also identified the likely shortage of IT personnel by 2010 and infrastructural issues as possible bottlenecks and have urged the government to address these problems (KPMG, 2004). In contrast to the pessimistic outlook of the abovementioned reports, some other analysts have opined that India is likely to remain the most attractive location over the next 20 years (Schaffhauser, 2006). Thus, there is a need for a better understanding of India’s future role in the offshoring of services.

The issue of selection of a target country for offshoring services is critical for the success of an organization’s offshoring initiative. It involves a considerable investment of resources and effort in entering a new country. However, if the country selected turns out to be a wrong choice for the delivery of the needed services, or, if it loses its competitive advantage in a short period, the organization may incur substantial additional costs in sourcing its services from a different location. Therefore, the selection of a country for offshoring is very important, whether the firm decides to invest in the establishment of an offshore development center (ODC) or, to rely on vendors with offshore facilities in that country. Setting up an ODC is likely to involve substantial investment in facilities and development of human resources. When the offshore country is no longer competitive, shifting the ODC can prove to be very expensive, and often not infeasible. For example, former attractive locations such as Ireland and Israel continue to host most ODCs, despite their rising costs.

Even if the firm employs vendors with offshore facilities, significant upfront costs may be involved in establishing communication patterns, cultural compatibility, process coordination, and an ongoing relationship with the target vendor(s). This implies that changing an offshore vendor could also be expensive. Furthermore, if changing a vendor also involves going to a different offshore country, it can substantially add to the costs of the transition. Therefore, an important factor to consider in the selection of a target country would be the availability of alternative vendors within the same country.

The analysis presented above highlights the critical importance of choosing the right country for offshoring services and identifies substantial costs and opportunity losses that may be incurred in case of a wrong decision. The preferred location for a company should depend on its own context and geographic location. The objective of this paper is to develop a framework for evaluating the attractiveness of a country for offshoring. We use this framework to assess India’s future attractiveness as a destination for the offshoring of services.

In the next section, we present the above-mentioned framework. Following this, we present an evaluation of

India and its future role in the offshoring of services on the basis of the factors identified in the framework. Next, we briefly discuss the context of other competing countries before concluding with an analysis of India’s competitive position in the next decade.

## A framework for the selection of a target country for offshoring

The selection of a country for offshoring involves a consideration of several factors. To help analyze the myriad issues that are involved in a systematic way, we propose a framework for evaluating a country’s attractiveness for offshore work. It includes some of the factors identified in the prior literature. Palvia (2004) identified political ideology, information and communication technology (ICT) infrastructure, government regulations, workforce characteristics, judicial/legal system, and language and culture as determinants of a country’s attractiveness as an offshoring destination. Other factors considered in prior research and consultant reports include (a) geopolitical, social, economic risks, global delivery model risks, and vendor-specific business case risks (Marriott, 2006); (b) country-specific factors such as labor market, government policies, supplier base, and barriers within companies (MGI, 2005a); (c) drivers of FDI such as liberalization and privatization; and (d) transportation and communication costs (Farrell, 2004). Most of these factors have been remapped into our framework.

The framework is based on three key factors: primary motivators, inhibitors, and facilitating conditions (Figure 1). We have adopted an analogy of the forces that affect the motion of an automobile in describing the framework. This framework is similar to Lewin’s Force-Field Analysis (Lewin, 1951) that explains the direction of change in a system based on an analysis of the various driving and restraining forces affecting the system. In our framework, motivating factors act as an accelerator, pushing towards offshoring, while inhibiting factors act as brakes that prevent the forward motion. If the motivating factors can overcome the resistance imposed by the inhibiting factors, the role of facilitating conditions becomes important. The facilitating conditions help smoothen the offshoring transactions, once a decision to offshore has been made, whether through an in-house ODC or through vendors with offshore facilities.

Essentially, primary factors are the fundamental drivers of offshoring work to other countries. In response to competitive pressures and to build a globally integrated enterprise (Palmisano, 2006), organizations will be on a lookout for locations that can help them gain a competitive advantage in the delivery of specific services. The advantages that an offshore location may provide include cost savings, access to a talented pool of workers, deployment of a greater amount of service resources, and faster development of new products and services. Some organizations may proactively seek these advantages as early adopters of the offshoring trend, while others may react to competitors’ actions and a changing marketplace.

However, the adoption of offshoring is discouraged by inhibiting factors that act as deterrents. These factors are responsible for a slow take-off of offshoring, despite some of the advantages that it may offer. Some of these inhibiting factors include a potential risk of disruption, an instability in the target country, emerging geopolitical issues, a volatility in foreign currency exchange rates and the resulting risk of a loss of cost advantage, a possible decline in service levels, a loss of control, a loss of intellectual property, and political, employee, and customer backlash.

The facilitating conditions support convenient initial entry, smooth transition, and efficient, trouble-free delivery of services from the offshore location. Among competing countries with similar cost advantages and risk profiles, it is the facilitating conditions that act as the most important discriminator in the choice of a target country. While the motivating factors help define opportunities available in lower-cost countries and inhibiting factors narrow the choices that are feasible, it is the facilitating factors that play a crucial role in facilitating a smooth, trouble-free entry and the subsequent delivery of services. We have identified vendor landscape, supporting ecosystem, and business environment as primary facilitating conditions. Customers should proactively anticipate and assess these factors in selecting a target country. In the next section, we discuss India’s current and likely future attractiveness on the basis of factors identified in the framework presented in Figure 1.

## Analysis of India’s attractiveness

We have identified specific criteria for each factor introduced in the framework presented in Figure 1 to help assess the attractiveness of a country. These criteria are listed in Table 1, which also summarizes our assessment of India’s current and likely future attractiveness in 2015. A detailed analysis based on these criteria is presented below.

## Primary motivating factors

Essentially, these factors are the drivers of an organization’s decision to choose an offshore destination for services. They relate primarily to the value proposition offered by an offshore site in terms of the cost of resources when considered along with the quality and intellectual content of the services provided. The depth and sustainability of this value proposition are also important issues that are reflected in the amount of the human resource base, its quality, and adaptability to provide the needed services.

Some key issues reflecting the value proposition of India are discussed next.

## Cost advantage

Establishing the delivery of offshore services through an ODC or a vendor with offshore facilities involves many costs, ranging from an initial one-time setup to ongoing operational costs of salaries, travel, communication, tax and regulatory compliance, and infrastructure. Currently, India is highly competitive with respect to this factor. Salaries for staff with matching skills and experience are among the lowest in the world, along with the highest supply of suitable graduates (see Figure 2). The rising salaries of Indian employees may be a cause of concern with respect to cost savings. We believe that rising costs are a result of (a) the progression of Indian vendors to higher value-added services; (b) a sudden spurt in the growth of existing, and entry of a large number of new, multinational vendors; and (c) existing constraints in the educational infrastructure. We expect, however, that the influence of some of these factors would be mitigated in the near future. This issue is addressed in the next subsection: Human resource advantage.

India’s per capita income is the lowest among its likely competitors. This factor, taken together with the salary levels in the IT sector in India, results in a high degree of attractiveness of the jobs in the IT sector (see Figure 3). This attractiveness is likely to grow further in the coming years, because the salaries in the IT/ITES sector are growing faster than the per capita income. With India’s inflexible labor laws and a weak physical infrastructure, it is unlikely to succeed in manufacturing as dramatically as China, leaving most of its skilled workforce available for employment in the service sector.

India’s costs of initial setup and regulatory compliance are very competitive. These costs are likely to remain in check as service providers move increasingly to tier-two, -three, and -four cities in India. Several IT industryoriented initiatives of the central and state governments in India aim to develop infrastructural facilities, lower taxes, and create special economic zones to encourage the industry. This is facilitating the launch of new ventures and rapid growth of existing companies in the IT/ITES sector.

A separate ministry for IT has also been created with a mandate to create and implement policies to develop ICT infrastructure in India. Communication and travel costs to India and within India are also falling significantly due to deregulation and privatization in the telecommunication and airline sectors. For example, data communication costs have declined 80% in the last 4 years. Lower communication costs and better connectivity are likely to contribute to the growth of offshoring business.

## Human resource advantage

One of the main reasons for India’s attractiveness as a destination for offshore services is the availability of a large workforce that is well-educated, skilled, readily adaptable, experienced in Western business practices and technologies, and having a high of level of fluency in English that few of its low-cost competitors can match. Employment in the IT/ITES sector (including software and services) reached about one million in 2005 (NASSCOM, 2006). From 2000 to 2004, the number of 4-year engineering degree seats in India increased from 215,000 to 439,000. Additionally, in 2004, the number of seats in 3-year diploma programs in engineering was 265,000, and in Master of Computer Applications it was 54,000 (AICTE, 2006). Thus, over 750,000 technical degrees are expected to be granted in 2008–2009, based on a lead-time of 4 years. India’s supply of non-technical graduates is also rising gradually from a base of approximately 2.5 million in 2004. India is also slowly gearing up its facilities for school and college education. At present, it is estimated that less than 15% of the relevant population age group (19–22) attends college (based on Educational Statistics 2003–2004, Ministry of Higher Education, Government of India).

India also has a well-established network of elite colleges and universities that is capable of producing top-quality graduates in technology and management for leadership roles in the industry. The educational infrastructure of India is improving along with an increasing participation of the private sector and planned changes in the government policies with respect to the establishment of private universities and participation of foreign universities in India. Notably, the Georgia Institute of Technology has signed an MoU and acquired land to establish a large campus in India (Silicon India, 2007). In addition to a growing influx of university-trained graduates, India has a large base of experienced professionals, many of whom are educated in the West and/or have worked in Western countries. This has permitted many new companies to enter India and hire thousands of trained, experienced professionals in a very short duration. For example, IBM increased its staff strength from 9000 in 2003 to 43,000 in the middle of 2005 (Alterio, 2006) and Accenture went from almost nil to about 20,000 employees in India in just 3 years. Other companies have also been entering India with plans to hire thousands of employees each, over the next 2 years. The sudden arrival of new companies into the Indian IT/ITES sector has resulted in high attrition rates of employees of existing organizations in the IT/ITES sector, and in a rapid rise in the salaries of mid-level professionals (15–20% per year). However, the salaries for entry-level graduates have risen between 10% and 12% (Thibodeau, 2006). The salary rise and attrition rates of mid-level professionals are likely to stabilize after 2009, when nearly all technology companies would have already entered India, and could rely on fresh graduates for local expansion. It should also be noted that most experienced IT professionals who command high salaries in India, between \$20,000 and \$25,000, have years of experience working with Western clients on state-of-theart technologies and managing large projects. Their salaries are not comparable with those of the professionals in competing countries who may not have the same background. Yet, Indian salaries for mid-level professionals are lower than those of their counterparts in Eastern Europe, Mexico, Brazil, and China (NeoIT, 2005). While the salaries of some top-level technical staff may reach close to those of their Western counterparts, salaries of a vast number of entry-level and mid-level employees are likely to remain competitive.

Indian vendors are moving up the value chain and providing more sophisticated services to their Western clients. This permits them to remain competitive and gain offshoring market share, despite their rising costs. This progression to the higher-level services is likely to continue and also keep India competitive in the future. In addition to providing high-end services, Indian vendors are also diversifying into non-English-speaking markets, such as German, French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch, Korean, Mandarin, and Japanese. They are hiring foreign professionals for positions in India with the requisite language and professional skills to launch services in new languages (Srivastava, 2006). The availability of Indian graduates who are trained in foreign languages is also increasing (Singh, 2005).

India will emerge as the country with the largest population in the world. However, much before that, it will emerge as the country with the largest labor pool in the world due to its low median age, which was only 23 in 2000, compared to 30 in China, 36 in the US, 38 in Europe, and 41 in Japan. In a report, the Boston Consulting Group has predicted that by 2020, India would have a surplus workforce of 47 million, relative to shortages of 10 million in China, 17 million in the USA, and 9 million in Japan (Sharma, 2006). Therefore, as educational opportunities increase, India is unlikely to face an acute shortage of human resources that other countries may face.

## Overall primary advantage

Given its large population base and an increasing supply of technically trained graduates, India is likely to provide the best value proposition for offshoring of IT and other services for a long time. Although there may be small periods of mismatch between the demand and supply of trained graduates, the mismatch is unlikely to persist due to high supply elasticity of trained graduates, which would result from an increased participation of the private sector in education. As the industry expands to tier-two, -three, and -four cities, a much greater proportion of population would be able to participate in the offshore services industry. Thus, India is likely to remain a competitive destination, with a low risk of saturation or an extraordinary wage rise. The fast rise in salaries currently observed is mainly limited to professionals who are the in middle- and top-level positions and are able to provide a value that is comparable to their counterparts from developed nations. Overall, our analysis suggests that India’s cost-competitiveness is likely to be sustained for a long time.

## Inhibiting factors

Inhibiting factors represent risks and difficulties that act to restrain an organization from choosing a country as its offshore destination. India’s growing participation in the delivery of offshore services indicates that clients consider risks and difficulties associated with India to be manageable. Our analysis suggests that India’s rating on this issue is likely to improve further in the coming years.

India is a stable, secular democracy with independent judiciary, free press, and an established history of peaceful transition of governments at the central and state levels. India’s relations with the major developed countries that offshore services are stable and improving (Europa, 2004; MOFA, 2006; Pan, 2006). India is also improving its relations with China in an ongoing dialog to resolve mutual differences amicably. India is also engaged in a dialog with Pakistan to resolve pending disputes; however, Pakistan’s instability remains a cause for concern (Kapur, 2005).

India has strengthened its intellectual property rights regime and enforcement in line with WTO agreements; several amendments have been made to the Indian Patent Act under Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights, which has increased the confidence of multinational companies having their knowledge-intensive operations in India (World Bank, 2005). This represents an advantage for India over China. Besides, intellectual property rights, India also presents a generally safe and secure environment for western personnel and data. According to a recent Pew Global Attitudes Survey, India ranks highest among the 16 nations surveyed in their attitudes towards the US and US citizens (Pew, 2005). This reflects a positive sentiment that the Indians have towards the West. On the other hand, US consumers also rated India, along with Canada and Ireland, among the top three most trusted countries for offshoring their personal data, out of the 47 countries surveyed by the Ponemon Institute (White and Case, 2006). These mutually positive sentiments and attitudes of trust help foster a favorable climate for offshoring of services to India. Although there may be random cases of data theft due to individual greed, there is no systemic motivation to harm Western interests in India. To reduce the risk to customers data and to increase the security of their operations, Indian vendors have also taken initiatives to establish industrywide security standards and a centralized employee database to better screen and track employees.

As a developing country with a weak infrastructure, India is susceptible to short disruptions due to natural causes or law and order disturbances. Most Indian vendors have established multiple sites within India to provide uninterrupted services. Some have also established overseas facilities to provide additional diversification of risk to their clients.

Another likely inhibiting factor is the distance and time zone difference. India is distant from its major clients. However, its geographic separation from its clients has not adversely affected the growth of India’s offshoring business. India has also successfully turned the time zone difference into an advantage for certain IT and R&D projects that can now be developed around the clock by a globally distributed team working in the two time zones.

## Facilitating factors

Once a firm decides to offshore IT or other services, it needs to select locations and vendors, and establish streamlined, uninterrupted delivery of services. To assist in a smooth transition to the offshore location, the presence of suitable vendors, a supporting ecosystem, and a conducive business environment are essential.

## Vendor landscape

This factor identifies the critical role of vendor competence, numbers, and diversity in establishing the delivery of offshore services. Vendors help define the reliability, trust, technical sophistication, quality of delivery, and security of data and intellectual property. In this respect, one of India’s greatest strengths is its enterprising and technically competent vendor base. Of the nearly 2000 software exporters, in 2004 as many as 250 vendors were CMMcertified, which represents the highest number of vendors with quality certifications from any country (see Table 2). Some Indian vendors are even helping their Western customers in achieving quality software development processes and CMM certification.

Large Indian vendors have accumulated substantial project implementation experience, expertise in vertical industry segments, development process maturity, worldwide marketing presence, strategically located global development centers (e.g., in China, Eastern Europe, and South America), and an extensive recruitment and training infrastructure for the development of their human resources. This has permitted them to achieve, and successfully manage, growth rates of 30–50% per annum over the last decade. For some large firms this has meant the hiring of 25,000–30,000 IT personnel per annum. Indian vendors have acted on data protection and security risks and have established controls on par with international levels (Vijayan, 2004; Basu, 2005; Germain, 2006). Most large vendors are now proceeding to set up operations in different cities in order to provide uninterrupted service to customers despite the occurrence of any city-specific disruptions. Some vendors are also setting up back-up facilities and operations in other countries to provide continuity in services in the event of any breakdown (Vijayan, 2004). The presence of a large, globally competitive, and technically competent vendor community in India also permits flexibility in the use of offshore resources and facilitates the setting up ODCs. As evident from Table 3, out of top 50 best-managed global outsourcing vendors, India has the largest number of global outsourcing vendors, after the US. Furthermore, 11 of the US and European vendors have a significant base of their operations in India.

## Supporting ecosystem

This factor focuses on issues that go beyond the formal educational infrastructure. Most young IT graduates have only academic knowledge of programming and other IT fundamentals. However, to be useful to the IT/ITES sector, they need to be proficient in proprietary vendor tools and technologies. India has a highly developed infrastructure for providing training in such technologies through a large network of private-sector training institutes that operate throughout the country from plush institutional settings to private apartments (Dalmia, 2006). Training in new and established proprietary technologies from leading IT vendors (e.g., SAP, Oracle, Siebel, Microsoft) is readily available in India, and the market responds rapidly to emerging shortages in hot skills. Such training is available at low rates and individuals are willing to invest their own funds to obtain it (based on personal interviews by one of the authors during his trips to India in 2004 and 2005). Indian training vendors are also playing a leading role in the training sectors of China and South-East Asia. Additionally, large Indian software firms have substantial in-house training facilities, as noted earlier. This large training infrastructure will ensure that individuals possessing the latest in-demand skills would continue to be available in to the IT/ITES sector in India in large numbers.

Besides training in proprietary technologies, India has a well-established network of consultants and facilities that provide training, assessment, consulting, and certification in software process quality management (e.g., for CMM, ISO, BS certifications). In India, it is easier to find the necessary help to improve processes and obtain certification when compared to other locations. This has helped Indian vendors achieve the highest number of CMM certifications globally. With a large number of additional vendors seeking software process certifications, India would maintain its lead in the adoption of software process quality standards.

To alleviate the predicted future shortages in human resources (Menon, 2006), large Indian corporations have begun establishing institutes for short-term, specialized training, and for college-level education (Economic Times, 2006; Thomas, 2006). Finishing schools are also being established to provide focused training in deficient skills to existing graduates in areas such as English and communication to make them employable in the IT/ITES sector (The Hindu, 2007).

India has a well-organized association of IT and ITES vendors, called NASSCOM, which is able to influence and guide government policies and actions. It represents Indian vendors in various international forums and interacts with overseas industry associations and government agencies. It assists Indian vendors in organizing their efforts for meeting emerging challenges such as data protection, security, a centralized repository for supporting employee background verification, skill testing for IT and ITES candidates, forecasting the demand for services, and identifying future trends. It also works actively with Indian educational institutions to help update their curricula and programs to meet emerging industry needs in IT and ITES sectors. It is expected to play a strong leadership role in the future growth of the IT/ITES sector in India.

## Government/country environment

India has a well-established, enterprising private sector with links to Western markets, which has been thriving since India’s economic liberalization in 1991. Indian companies conduct their business in English and closely follow Western business practices. Thus, beyond any initial delays in setting up a new operation, it is easy to operate in India. While the overall physical infrastructure is weak, the segments that are important for the delivery of remote services, for example, telecommunications and air travel, are no longer bottlenecks, and, are rapidly improving (Nollen, 2004: 33). Penetration of ICTs is also rapidly advancing in India, particularly in the urban areas where the IT/ITES centers are located. This often provides a growing market for the clients’ own products and services, and acts as a magnet for business interactions. India follows Western legal practices that are derived from British common law. Independent Indian courts help enforce contracts. Yet, low-value commercial disputes may get bogged down in extraordinary delays in the lower Indian courts. Most overseas clients bypass this potential bottleneck by specifying binding arbitration in Singapore or

London in their vendor contracts. Thus, it appears that many of the bottlenecks traditionally identified with India are being overcome or circumvented, which would encourage future expansion of the IT/ITES sector.

India’s physical infrastructure remains weak and needs massive investments for achieving improvements. Given the shortage of funds and bureaucratic delays, its outward physical infrastructure is being upgraded only slowly. It may not improve enough to match Western or Chinese standards for several decades. However, companies in India have learned to cope with these circumstances and, when needed, make their own arrangements for power, local transport, and other infrastructural services. In the critical areas of voice and data communication, and travel needs of the services sector, the infrastructure is not a significant bottleneck and is steadily improving with the participation of the private sector.

Traditionally, the ruling Congress and other socialist parties in India have focused on gaining votes through subsidies and social welfare schemes, at the cost of infrastructural investments. After the opposition party – Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) – came into power in 1999, it emphasized infrastructural development. However, the BJP was viewed as favoring urban development at the expense of rural voters and it lost national elections in 2004. Upon its return to power, the Congress party has reemphasized subsidies and social welfare schemes for the rural population.

In India, even planned and budgeted infrastructural projects are inordinately held back due to bureaucratic delays in approvals, political interference in land acquisition and allotment, and a lack of political commitment, besides a paucity of funds. However, now there are some factors that are beginning to favor the development of infrastructure in India. First, most political parties have recognized the beneficial impact of previous infrastructural initiatives, and, even the Congress party is willing to increase the funding for infrastructure. Second, the high levels of growth in the Indian economy are rapidly increasing the revenues of the Indian government and its ability to invest in infrastructural development. Third, India is slowly allowing partnerships between the private and the public sectors in developing infrastructure such as roads, power, and airports and in the creation of special economic zones. These partnerships have resulted in a reduced role of the government and are expected to accelerate the development of infrastructure in India. However, despite these improvements, it is unlikely that India’s infrastructure would match that of China or other South-East Asian countries in the near future for the reasons noted above.

In the preceding section, we have discussed the context of India and its competitive position; however, its relative attractiveness will also depend on the advantages and actions of other competing nations. In this section, we present a brief analysis of likely competitors that are frequently mentioned in the literature. We have identified the following clusters to analyze their relative positions: China, Eastern Europe and Russia, Southeast Asia, Mexico and South America, Africa, and South Asia.

China is clearly the most potent competitor and alternative to India, aiming to gain greater revenues in the offshoring market (Qu and Brocklehurst, 2003). It has a better-developed ICT infrastructure compared to other developing countries (see Table 4). China is also focusing on building a large educational infrastructure and attempting to train its graduates in English.

Despite its strong position vis-a\`-vis infrastructure, several factors limit the rate of China’s rise as a significant player in relation to India. First, China has no significant comparative advantage over India in the IT/ITES sector. China’s wages and per capita income are higher and growing at a higher rate than those of India. China is also facing a shortage of talent and significantly higher wages, particularly at the middle levels (Kundu, 2006). China’s under-valued currency is likely to adjust in the upward direction, given its large foreign trade surplus and its enormous foreign exchange reserves, thereby resulting in a net rise of the cost of the services it offers.

Second, China’s foreign trade and economic success are sustainable for a long time with its strengths in manufacturing, a sector that attracts the bulk of China’s talents and limits the availability of quality manpower for the offshore services sector. China’s educated workforce also has lower geographic mobility (MGI, 2005c), thereby accentuating the shortage of skilled manpower.

Third, both countries can produce a sufficient number of technical and non-technical graduates in the future: mainly through government investment in education in the case of China, and primarily through private investment in the case of India. However, the overall availability of graduates is not a constraint; rather, the key issue is their suitability for offshore services in terms of language skill, cultural fit, adaptability to Western business practices, and opportunities for learning and using the latest proprietary vendor technologies. In this respect, India has a very significant advantage that China cannot match for a long time. China is placing a great emphasis on teaching English; however, gaining fluency in oral and written communication in a foreign language is a slow process that depends upon having opportunities for practice. Given the size of the current number of English speakers, China and other non-English-speaking countries are at a disadvantage in this respect. In the longer term (8–10 years), China will have higher income levels, stronger currency, and fewer people with the requisite skills available for the services sector, which would further limit China’s relative advantage and growth as an IT/ITES offshoring destination. However, given its geographic and cultural proximity, China is expected to continue being in a position of advantage, relative to India, in the Japanese and Korean markets in the future.

Fourth, China’s vendor community is small, fragmented, and lacks quality and experience, which implies that generally a foreign client would need to invest in its own ODC. This imposes significant costs and additional risks for a potential client, something that only very large organizations or those aiming to serve the Chinese markets may be able to bear. Some of the weaknesses noted above have been confirmed by a Forrester study (Financial Express, 2007a), which reported that contrary to the predictions made 2 years ago, China’s momentum in offshoring is slowing, and, the market has not taken off as predicted, due to concerns about limited English-language skills, high attrition rates, a lack of experienced professionals, a weak protection for intellectual property, and a rise in costs due to an appreciation of its currency.

While the above-mentioned factors would limit its potential in comparison to that of India, China is still expected to make a significant progress in the IT/ITES sector. China would obtain a significant market share in its primary future role as an offshore site for R&D activities related to manufacturing initiatives, as a secondary site for major international vendors in their risk diversification strategy, and as a center for serving markets in Japan, Korea, and Taiwan (McCarthy and Hudson, 2005).

In addition to their technical competence, the East-European nations and Russia have advantages of proximity, closer language, and cultural compatibility with their West-European clients, which would gain them a European market share. However, they are likely to be constrained by significantly higher wages and smaller manpower bases, thereby experiencing only limited future growth. Besides higher local wages, East-European currencies are expected to grow stronger in the future, due to a better integration with Western Europe. Russia presents some additional challenges on account of its political and security risks and language incompatibility, besides higher wages, lower availability of manpower resources, and a smaller vendor base. Russia may also experience currency gains due to rising prices and greater exploitation of its natural resources. Therefore, despite their gains, East-European nations are likely to be less competitive than India.

India’s competitors from South America and North America (Mexico) also face some challenges, despite their proximity to the US and Canadian markets. These include higher wages, language incompatibility, limited technical manpower base and availability. However, some countries such as Chile, Brazil, and Mexico are likely to make inroads into the US market for IT and ITES in the medium term (3–5 years). In the long term, their pace of economic development and technical capabilities will determine the extent of their success in this sector.

The countries of South-East Asia are expected to gain more market share. However, their gains will be limited by factors such as language, cultural fit, and availability of suitable technical and non-technical graduates. Although the Philippines has significant advantages in language compatibility, its small manpower and skill base is expected to permit only limited expansion. Malaysia is already a higher-wage destination with limited opportunities for significant growth in the future. Indonesia and Vietnam are two of the larger countries with a significant population base. However, they lack language and cultural compatibility, a suitable base of technical and non-technical graduates, and a vendor base to effectively compete in this sector, at least for the medium term. They are likely to increase their participation over the long term. Some countries in the African continent such as South Africa, Egypt, and Nigeria have a foundation in English and are expected to make inroads into the ITES market. However, their growth is likely to be limited by suitable manpower availability, political instability, and risk in the medium term. South Africa seems to be capable of playing a noticeable role in ITES in the medium term. However, it faces higher labor costs, high telecommunications costs, a shortage of trained manpower, and a high crime rate. In South Asia, countries other than India are facing a significantly higher political risk, which limits them from being of any immediate interest.

We believe that the primary discriminating factor in determining the development of a developing country’s offshore services sector, and, its competitive position, is the availability of suitably educated talent in the required numbers and quality, rather than just the cost of providing offshore services. Based on the development of IT/ITES offshore industry in different cities in India, it appears that the most critical factor in supporting the growth of the industry in a city has been the availability of talent in that city. For example, Bangalore has continued to expand, despite severe infrastructural bottlenecks and higher salaries compared to many other locations in India. Locations that have attracted business demonstrate a high availability of talent, while several other locations, with adequate infrastructural facilities and lower costs, have failed to attract the industry due to a paucity of talent as indicated by the quality and number of educational and training facilities in each region.

India’s offshore services sector has managed to grow at 30–40% per year for the last 10 years. India has a large base of trained manpower available to it for its future growth as noted earlier. However, it faces some challenges in realizing its full potential in the near future. First, not all states are participating fully in the offshoring sector. India needs to expand the participation of its states to realize its full potential. Second, to sustain the high rates of growth achieved in the past, governments at all levels, and private sector businesses, have to deliver the needed facilities and services. Office space, power, and telecommunication services are now being delivered in greater quantities by the private sector (Ninan, 2006). Therefore, these infrastructural facilities are unlikely to be a severe bottleneck in the future growth. However, the government has a significant role in the acquisition and allotment of land for the service sector. Government action is also needed for providing school-level education and for establishing policies that encourage the participation of private sector and foreign universities in higher education. The government has a role in improving the quality of education and making curricular enhancements to meet the IT/ITES industry’s skill requirements. This is an area of significant concern for the industry, which has been lobbying the government to improve the quality and the number of graduates (KPMG, 2004: 82). India’s future growth is likely to depend on the ability of its government to respond to these issues in a timely manner.

## Discussion and conclusion

The framework identified in this paper provides a useful tool for analysts and policy makers in assessing and improving the attractiveness of a country for offshoring of services. The analysis of India’s attractiveness provides useful insights for other competitors as well as potential clients. It appears that the development of a competent talent base plays a more important role in fostering the offshore services industry, than that played by lower costs alone. Facilitating conditions play a critical role in the establishment and development of an offshoring industry. A competent local vendor base is important for the growth of the offshoring sector. Government policies can play a role in improving a country’s attractiveness by identifying its weaknesses based on the important dimensions identified in the analysis and addressing them. Some of the primary issues identified include educational infrastructure, telecommunication and travel connectivity, and a supportive environment for local vendors. However, there are some issues that may be difficult for many countries to address, at least in the short term: language skills, the quality of education, establishment of competent local vendors, links to potential customers, and the risk profile of a country.

Our analysis of India’s competitive position suggests that for the next 8–10 years, and possibly even beyond that, India is likely to retain its number one position in offshoring of services. A relatively slow growth in India’s manufacturing sector is likely to leave most of India’s growing educated talent pool to seek employment in the service sector. As employment opportunities and wages rise, a much larger base of the Indian population would be attracted by, and be able to participate in, the growth of offshore work. This growth is expected to be facilitated by the rising competence of its vendors, improvements in its educational infrastructure, a significantly higher participation of women in its workforce, higher levels of mobility and flexibility of its workforce, the English-language skills of its educated population, a greater trust for India among overseas clients, and improvements in telecommunication and access to second-, third-, and fourth-tier cities.

During the next 8–10 years, India is also estimated to have the largest surplus workforce due to its much younger population, which would be in contrast to the shortages in other major competing countries. Even at present, despite a global shortage of experienced professionals, Indian firms have reported the least difficulty in hiring suitable talent. In a recent survey of 37,000 employers that was released by the global recruitment firm, Manpower, only 9% of Indian firms reported a difficulty in the hiring of suitable talent in 2007, compared to 19% in China, and 41% globally. Bucking the global trend, India has improved its position in manpower availability, with 9% employers reporting difficulties in hiring during 2007, compared to 13% in 2006 (Financial Express, 2007b). Indian vendors have significant opportunities for expansion beyond their primary locations to tier-two, -three, and -four cities in a hub and spoke architecture. As the educational and ICT infrastructures extend to more cities in India, Indian vendors would be able to access a wider pool of talent and avoid the bottlenecks associated with weak physical infrastructure in large cities.

In our analysis, we suggest that lower cost of operations may not be the main differentiator that determines the growth of the IT/ITES offshore industry among developing countries. Rather, it is the availability of a competent human resource base that primarily determines the ability of a developing country to attract offshored services. We believe that India’s position in this respect would be strengthened in the coming years in contrast to a shortage of talent that most other competing developing countries are expected to experience. As per a recent Forrester report, a corporate executive noted that, ‘China had to be 20 per cent cheaper than India to be [considered] viable’ (Financial Express, 2007a). This highlights the importance of factors other than marginal cost savings, in the range of 10–20%, for the growth of the offshore services in other developing countries.

India has the lowest per capita GDP currently among its main competitors at \$650 and, even by 2020, it is estimated to reach only \$3000, which would still leave India among the low-wage countries. During mid-2005, the salaries for entry-level IT staff stabilized and even declined by 7% due to an increased supply in response to higher wages (Economic Times, 2006). Such episodes of mismatch between immediate demand and supply response may occur from time to time, but generally the supply is likely to rise in line with the rising demand for the next 10–15 years, and the overall wages are likely to remain competitive.

India is gradually moving up the value chain to retain its competitive advantage and market share. Indian vendors are slowly expanding into higher-end KPO work, such as consulting, engineering design, R&D, clinical trials, financial analysis, and legal work. A recent study by Deloitte and Touche reports that in the rapidly growing offshored financial services sector, which has a scope to save \$150 billion for the developed countries by 2010, India has already gained a market share of 80% (Hindustan Times, 2007). India is likely to gain a similar market share in the delivery of other high-end services such as legal, architectural, engineering design, and accounting.

Despite a rise in salaries and costs in Bangalore, one of the costliest locations in India, Indian vendors still found it to be 15–20% cheaper to operate there, compared to their centers in other countries such as the Philippines and Mauritius (Babu, 2006). Although salaries are rising in India and competition for the best graduates is intensifying, skills and productivity of Indian employees are also rising correspondingly. For example, as The Economist (2006) reports on one of the top Indian vendors, wages constitute only 14% of the costs and even with a 15% wage inflation, the impact on margins is only 2.1%, which can be easily overcome with productivity gains. Therefore, it seems that India’s competitive position, on account of lower wages, is going to decline very slowly. This is supported by the Everest Research Institute that estimates the net labor arbitrage to decline only by 1.9% annually (Fernandes et al., 2006). While salaries for the top-notch employees at the leading vendors are increasing rapidly, those of others are rising only gradually. Thus, India is likely to move up the value chain without necessarily vacating the entire lower end of the offshore services sector. Despite a rise in wages, India is expected to sustain its current value proposition due to its higher skilled, low-cost human resource pool.

Most of other developing countries also aim to benefit from the rising offshoring trends. Several countries are already participating in the delivery of offshore services. Their scale of participation, though small, is growing. However, most of these countries face constraints that are likely to limit their rate of growth and ability to play a significantly larger future role. They have weaknesses such as significantly higher wages (e.g., Eastern Europe, Russia), an inability to scale up (e.g., Malaysia, the Philippines), a lack of trained manpower (Indonesia, Vietnam, Africa), unmanageable risks, a poor command over the English language, and a weak vendor base.

Among the potential competitors, China aims to be a significant player, and is likely to gain market share in the coming years. However, the rapid expansion of its manufacturing sector, which is sustained by high FDI rates, would absorb most of China’s educated workforce (MGI, 2005a). Therefore, China’s net availability of graduates for offshore service work would be less than India’s. China’s other weaknesses in the area of English language, its lack of experience and skills in proprietary technologies, and a weak vendor landscape (MGI, 2005b) would also hinder the growth of its offshore sector. Besides in-house R&D associated with manufacturing FDI, much of the offshore work in China is likely to be handled by international vendors who would focus on serving the large and growing internal market in China and customers in Japan and Korea, and on establishing second-tier back-up facilities to provide continuity of services in the case of disruptions in primary locations (McCarthy and Hudson, 2005). China’s wages and currency are also likely to appreciate significantly faster than India’s due to its highly successful export-based manufacturing sector, higher FDI, and faster economic growth. China could also face a shortage of workforce due to its aging population (UN, 2006a, b).

Considering India’s relative advantages and the current trends in the growth of offshoring to India, along with the relative weaknesses of potential competitors, our analysis suggests that India is likely to remain the largest center for offshored services well beyond 2015. Of the 4.5 million jobs to be offshored from the US (3.3 million) and Western Europe (1.2 million) by 2015 (McCarthy, 2004; Parker, 2004), India is likely to retain about 50% market share. With ongoing improvements in education, services-related infrastructure, and a high elasticity of its labor supply, India seems to be well positioned to retain its market share beyond 2015 to become the dominant IT/ITES global services hub, just as China has become the most competitive, dominant manufacturing hub. Contrary to several reports and commentaries in the press, our analysis finds that India would continue to play a dominant role in the global offshoring of services, and the offshored services sector would play an even bigger role in its economy in the next decade.

In this paper, we analyzed the competitive position of India relative to other countries for offshoring of IT/ITES. In future research, it would be instructive to examine the relative attractiveness of different locations within India, and to study the dynamics of the relative growth of the industry in those locations. This can help identify the key determinants of the growth of the offshore services industry across various locations in India. The framework presented in this paper can provide a basis for such a study. The results of such a study would help identify critical success factors in the development and growth of the offshore services industry, which should be useful to policy makers in India and other developing countries.
