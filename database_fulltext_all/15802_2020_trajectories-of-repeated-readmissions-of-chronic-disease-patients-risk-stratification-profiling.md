# TRAJECTORIES OF REPEATED READMISSIONS OF CHRONIC DISEASE PATIENTS: RISK STRATIFICATION, PROFILING, AND PREDICTION<sup>1</sup>

Ofir Ben-Assuli

The Faculty of Business Administration, Ono Academic College,104 Zahal Street, Kiryat Ono 55000, ISRAEL {ofir.benassuli@gmail.com}

Rema Padman

The H. John Heinz III College of Information Systems and Public Policy, Carnegie Mellon University, 4800 Forbes Avenue, Pittsburgh, PA 15213-3890 U.S.A. {rpadman@cmu.edu}

The problem of recurrent, unplanned readmissions, where some patients return shortly after discharge from the hospital and are readmitted for the same or a related condition, has become a challenge worldwide due to care quality, health outcomes, and financial concerns. Predicting frequent, preventable readmissions and understanding the contributing factors is a critical problem that is being widely studied. However, few studies have examined longitudinal risk stratification, profiling, and prediction of multi-morbid, heterogeneous patient populations. We examine how readmission risk may progress over multiple emergency department visits of chronic disease patients, their early stratification into distinct trajectories with related frequencies, and the relationship of these trajectories to patient characteristics. We further extend this analysis to investigate the impact of time-stable and time-varying covariates in predicting future readmission conditional on latent class membership. Results indicate that longitudinal risk stratification can enable early identification of specific patient groups following distinct trajectories based on their presentation for emergency care. Prediction models that incorporate latent classes perform well and demonstrate the promise of trajectory modeling methods combined with advanced prediction models for longitudinal risk assessment in addressing readmission challenges. The methodology and insights from this study are generalizable to other important Information Systems problems.

Keywords: Multiple chronic diseases, repeated readmissions, risk stratification, group-based trajectory model, covariate latent class model, hidden Markov model, electronic health record, predictive analytics

## Introduction

The problem of recurrent unplanned readmissions, where some patients return shortly after they are discharged from the hospital and are readmitted for the same or a related condition, has become a challenge worldwide due to care quality, health outcomes and financial concerns (Kociol et al. 2012).

Studies indicate that the problem is further exacerbated in patients with chronic diseases, particularly those with multimorbid conditions (Friedman et al. 2008). More than 120 million Americans are reported to suffer from at least one chronic condition, 75 million have two or more chronic conditions, and the number of patients with at least one chronic condition is expected to increase by 10 million new cases per decade (Anderson 2010). Specifically, “persons living with chronic disease” will increasingly mean “persons living with multiple chronic conditions” (HHS 2010).

Healthcare systems are largely set up to deal with one disease or condition at a time; as the number of chronic conditions increase, the risk of complications such as adverse drug reactions, unnecessary hospitalizations, readmissions, and mortality also increase (HHS 2010). Recent studies indicate that U.S. readmission rates are higher in areas where people with multiple chronic conditions are clustered (Basu et al. 2016). Patients who have unplanned readmissions not once or twice, but multiple times, are especially costly and challenging for the health system (Black 2014; Kirby et al. 2010; Szekendi et al. 2015) and are the specific focus of this study. With reports calling for solutions and tools to provide integrated care for such patient populations, we focus on multimorbid patient arrivals to the emergency department (ED) to investigate readmission risk stratification and prediction.

Most previous research has focused on one major comorbid condition at a time to predict readmission risk, such as congestive heart failure (CHF) (Wolff et al. 2002). However, it is increasingly important to investigate readmission risk prediction that utilizes all the comorbidities of patients as they develop/progress over time. In fact, patients with underlying chronic conditions have been shown to be readmitted within 30 days at twice the rate compared to those without chronic conditions (Wijlaars et al. 2016). Reducing readmissions has become the goal of virtually all healthcare organizations and policy makers to the point that recent policies penalize health providers for high readmission rates (Cress 2011).

Predictive analytics that leverage electronic health record (EHR) data present an encouraging direction for the identification of patients at increased risk of chronic diseases and multiple readmissions (Bardhan et al. 2014), especially for determining the type and timing of pro-active interventions for chronic patients that are also cost-effective (Lin et al. 2017). Thus, predicting frequent, preventable hospital readmissions and understanding the factors contributing to them is a critical problem that is being widely studied. However, few studies have examined longitudinal risk stratification and profiling of multi-morbid, heterogeneous patient populations and predicted their frequent readmissions related to chronic disease progression when they present for emergency care. Consistent with calls by researchers to integrate prediction analyses in Information Systems (IS) research (Chen et al. 2012; Shmueli and Koppius 2011), and specifically in healthcare research (Bardhan et al. 2014; Kohli and Tan 2016; Lin et al. 2017), we integrate predictive analytic techniques utilizing EHR data of patients with multiple chronic conditions and frequent readmissions that are initiated with an ED visit, and manifest over extended time periods, in order to predict their risk of future readmissions.

An ED visit refers to a patient presenting at a hospital’s ED unit for emergency care that may or may not result in a hospitalization or readmission. A hospitalization refers to an inpatient stay that may or may not be a readmission. A 30- day readmission refers to an unplanned admission for inpatient stay that occurs within 30 days of discharge from the same or another hospital for the same or a related condition. Most current literature on readmissions predicts the likelihood of readmission when the patient is discharged after an inpatient stay using data from the current hospitalization. This population includes hospitalized patients coming through the ED for emergency care as well as those arriving directly to inpatient stay for elective care, which is quite heterogeneous. Recent Centers for Disease Control (CDC) statistics indicate that out of the approximately 140 million visits to the ED, only about 10% to 12% are admitted to the hospital (Marcozzi et al. 2018). Furthermore, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) reports that more than half of the almost 36 million hospital admissions begin in the ED (Moore et al. 2017). As the population ages and lives longer with multiple chronic conditions, both the ED visits and those resulting in hospital admissions are anticipated to increase. ED visit rates have increased significantly for the 45 to 64 age group and ED visits have increased for patients with renal failure and several common chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension (Skinner et al. 2014). Hence, understanding the heterogeneity in disease progression of this increasingly challenging population and predicting the likelihood of their readmission as they move from ED visit to hospitalization is a critical step in developing policies and interventions to minimize adverse outcomes to patients and society.

Thus, we seek to understand how readmission risk may progress over 5 to 12 separate ED visits of chronic patients who may experience between 1 to 11 readmissions. We examine stratification of this heterogeneous patient population into distinct trajectories of readmission risk progression with related frequencies, and the relationship of these trajectories to key patient characteristics using three distinct statistical approaches for longitudinal data. We further extend this analysis to investigate the impact of key time-stable and timevarying covariates (i.e., multiple risk factors that manifest over time), on the trajectories of risk progression, and the earliest visit when a patient’s stable trajectory assignment can be determined. Finally, we evaluate multiple models to predict the risk of future readmission conditional on trajectory group membership and compare them to those without the additional insights into patient stratification. Contributing to both research and practice on addressing repeated, preventable readmissions for multi-morbid patients, this study responds to calls for greater attention in using patient-level data to generate useful and actionable insights (Angst et al. 2010).

This paper is organized as follows. We review the major literature on chronic disease-related readmission prediction in the healthcare arena in the next section, followed by the specific objectives of this study. We then describe the modeling methodologies, summarize the data, empirical context, and analysis framework. The results are then presented, followed by a discussion and the research contributions of the paper. Finally, research limitations and avenues for future research are summarized.

## Literature Review

Predictive analytics in healthcare is gathering considerable momentum, particularly from the convergence of wide EHR deployment, the resulting availability of vast amounts of detailed patient care data, and the impetus provided by the U.S. Precision Medicine initiative (Collins and Varmus 2015). There is increasing recognition that substantial medical wisdom about patient disease patterns can be gathered from utilizing EHR data, such as discovering patterns of disease progression and identifying accurate symptom–disease– treatment associations in chronic and infectious diseases (Bates et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2015).

While recent research has demonstrated good discriminative ability to make predictions in real time for the general population (Cai et al. 2015), it continues to be highly challenging for the rapidly growing multi-morbid population (Kansagara et al. 2011). This fact may partly be explained by the heterogeneity of patients categorized based on a single index discharge. Lin et al. (2017) showed that multifaceted risk profiling for diabetes patients with three different adverse health events— stroke, acute myocardial infarction (AMI), and acute renal failure—provides clinicians with superior medical insights toward a complete and effective care plan. Yet, few studies have investigated such approaches to examine multiple comorbidities commonly occurring among patients with chronic diagnoses (Lin et al. 2017; Parekh and Barton 2010).

Another strategy to reduce preventable readmission rates is to identify patients at higher risk for repeated readmissions as early as possible and understand their risk factors so that timely and appropriate interventions can be designed, applied, and evaluated. Most 30-day readmissions are experienced by patients who have multiple readmissions with the same diagnosis (Black 2014). Efforts to reduce readmissions must look beyond the current focus on a single hospital discharge and transition period that assume a patient discharged from the hospital is at risk for a single readmission since this does not reflect the trajectories experienced by some patients who have repeated readmissions (Black 2014).

The most common chronic differential diagnosis is heart failure (or other heart-related conditions such as myocardial infarction), which seriously affects readmission rates for hospitals (Amarasingham et al. 2010; Bardhan et al. 2014; Boulding et al. 2011; Chin and Goldman 1997; Dharmarajan et al. 2013; Donzé et al. 2013; Felker et al. 2004; Hernandez et al. 2010; Kirby et al. 2010; Shams et al. 2015; Tsugawa et al. 2017). The second is chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Hasegawa et al. 2014; Kirby et al. 2010; Raven et al. 2009; Tsugawa et al. 2017), followed by acute decompensated heart failure (Boulding et al. 2011; Dharmarajan et al. 2013; Felker et al. 2004), which is also one of the most common indications for hospitalization in the U.S., resulting in approximately one million hospitalizations annually (Hunt et al. 2001). Additional prevalent diagnoses include gastrointestinal disorders, Type I and Type 2 diabetes and pneumonia, each of which has been mentioned in multiple studies (Liu et al. 2015; Tsugawa et al. 2017).

Studies of chronic diagnoses associated with readmissions using all the chronic conditions in the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) (Charlson et al. 1987) have been reported in a few studies (Fabbian et al. 2015; Hao et al. 2015). While most studies have focused on one single disease, three recent papers (Donzé et al. 2013; Khanna et al. 2014; Shams et al. 2015) have tried to establish more general prediction models that would be applicable to a wider range of patients with several chronic diagnoses, and all of them have called for more research incorporating multiple chronic diagnoses into readmission prediction models. Some studies have examined readmission outcomes at only one hospital, thus limiting their generalizability (Amarasingham et al. 2010; Chin and Goldman 1997; Felker et al. 2004). In this study, we track all chronic disease patients over four years and include their readmissions at all the hospitals that belong to the same health maintenance organization (HMO).

Another significant issue in this context is the readmission period. The vast majority of studies have considered a readmission period of 30 days, indicating the return of a patient to the hospital within 30 days after being discharged. A few studies have considered 14 days (Roy et al. 2009), 60 days (Chin and Goldman 1997; Felker et al. 2004) or 6-month or 12-month readmission periods (Raven et al. 2009). Others have also considered planned and unplanned readmissions (Kirby et al. 2010). This study examines unplanned readmissions within 30 days from discharge since they result in high uncertainty and poor outcomes.

Chronic disease progression over time is also associated with changes in many important readmission-related variables at each visit, whether in the ED or inpatient setting, such as the number of procedures done, length of stay (LOS), mortality risk, admission source, and discharge disposition, among others, that have rarely been integrated as time-varying covariates in the prediction model. In response, we have integrated multiple chronic conditions that manifest over time as time varying covariates including all chronic comorbidities of the patients, quantified using the CCI (Charlson et al. 1987), their creatinine laboratory results at discharge after hospitalization, and EHR use during ED visits.

Prediction models rather than risk stratification models have been the focus of most readmission research (Amarasingham et al. 2010; Bardhan et al. 2014; Donzé et al. 2013; Hernandez et al. 2010; Khanna et al. 2014; Shams et al. 2015), primarily applying logistic regression or simple Cox proportional hazard models. Two recent studies have extended these with techniques such as the stochastic beta geometric Erlang-2 (BG/EG) hurdle model (Bardhan et al. 2014) and an advanced SQL-based algorithm (SQLape) (Donzé et al. 2013). This study combines longitudinal statistical models for population stratification, to address heterogeneity, with machine learning models for individual risk prediction at every visit.

A large number and variety of variables have been included in prediction models, including patient demographics, medical history, hospital characteristics, social and payer information, and patient behavior (Fabbian et al. 2015; Felker et al. 2004; Khanna et al. 2014). There has been limited focus on electronic health information exchange (HIE) (Kirby et al. 2010; Roy et al. 2009; Vest et al. 2015) and in the context of one EHR (Bardhan et al. 2014; Hasegawa et al. 2014; Kirby et al. 2010). Researchers have also integrated limited comorbidity status of readmitted patients (Fabbian et al. 2015; Kansagara et al. 2011; Shams et al. 2015), which increases LOS, risk of readmission, and death. In this study, we address these concerns by including all possible chronic diagnoses, according to the ICD9 Chronic Comorbidity Index (Quan et al. 2005), as separate time-varying covariates that are diagnosed or progress over time. We include all chronic disease patients who have between 5 and 12 ED visits that include multiple readmissions as well as pneumonia, which is a common, acute diagnosis in the ED (Dharmarajan et al. 2013; Raven et al. 2009; Shams et al. 2015; Tsugawa et al. 2017).

Finally, research that tracks the same patients over time across multiple ED visits and readmissions has received limited attention (Bardhan et al. 2014; Boulding et al. 2011; Hernandez et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2015; Roy et al. 2009). Frequent readmissions have been defined to include patients with more than two visits to the ED or physician per year (Black 2014). Addressing several significant gaps in the literature, summarized in Table 1, we investigate unplanned readmission risk stratification and prediction using three statistical methodologies that stratify the identified chronic disease population into distinct developmental trajectories based on their frequent readmissions and apply this stratification to predict their readmission risk in the future. We estimate developmental trajectories of readmission risk progression across 5 to 12 separate ED visits. We also compute their frequencies, the relationship of these trajectories to key baseline and timevarying patient characteristics, and how quickly patients are assigned to the final trajectory they most likely belong to with high probability. We further incorporate the latent trajectory group membership into multivariable statistical and machine learning models that include a large number of additional covariates to generate predictions for individual patients using de-identified data across four years from a large HMO.

## Methodology

## Latent Class Models for Readmission Risk Stratification

Time- or age-sequenced trajectories of an outcome of interest are becoming increasingly relevant and feasible to analyze as a sufficient volume of longitudinal data becomes available in the healthcare delivery environment. Observing the evolution of an outcome that is measured repeatedly over time, such as multiple readmissions of multi-morbid patients, facilitates not only the design but also the timing of interventions to mitigate adverse effects. However, this requires the ability to flexibly address the challenge of heterogeneity in the patient population so that interventions can be targeted appropriately to the latent subgroups to improve desirable outcomes. Latent class models and their analysis include methods that allow identification of unobservable subgroups in a population (Lazarsfeld and Henry 1968). Under different assumptions and empirical approaches, finite mixture models such as growth mixture models (GMMs) (Muthén 2004), group-based trajectory models (GBTM) (Nagin 2005), covariate latent class models (CLCM) (Vermunt 2010), and others aim to identify and profile a small set of latent trajectories that group individuals into distinct longitudinal clusters that best match the patients’ evolving characteristics. While GMMs allow within-class heterogeneity, CLCM and GBTM assume homogeneity conditional on trajectory group membership. The hidden Markov model (HMM), on the other hand, models the transitions between latent states over time as a Markov chain and the observed outcome of interest is used to learn the parameters of the model (Rabiner and Juang 1986). In this study, we leverage GBTM and its recent modeling extensions (Nagin and Odgers 2010) to discover latent trajectories of readmission risk in a multi-morbid patient population and uti-

## Risk Stratification and Profiling Using Latent Class Models

## Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM)

According to Nagin (2005), there may be clusters of unique developmental trajectories that show original etiologies, and that may explain or forecast individual level heterogeneity. The GBTM approach is suitable for testing if such distinctive developmental patterns are present in the data. It is also appropriate when there are occurrences of path vulnerabilities as a reaction to turning point events, such as severe hospitalization. Longitudinal data provide the empirical foundation for the analysis of developmental trajectories. Key model outputs are the shape of the trajectory for each group capturing the direction and rate of movement (e.g., one may be rising quickly and another declining slowly), and the size of the group as measured by the proportion of the population under study following the trajectory. This method has been widely applied to study phenomena such as crime (Nagin 2005), and in clinical settings to study conduct disorder, schizophrenia, obesity, and disease progression (Burckhardt et al. 2016; Nagin and Odgers 2010; Padman et al. 2014).

In GBTM, the random vector $Y _ { i }$ defines an individual $i \mathbf { \ ' } _ { \mathbf { S } }$ response variable, for example, readmission or not at each ED visit, and the vector X represents characteristics of i measured at baseline or are otherwise invariant. Thus, $Y _ { i t } = ( Y _ { i 1 } , . . . , Y _ { i T } ) .$ 4 a vector of length T, represents the results for the outcome of interest and T denotes the total number of time periods (or ED visits in the current study). For a single response, the GBTM assumes the following density for a sequence of longitudinal measurements $Y _ { i t } = ( Y _ { i 1 } , . . . , Y _ { i T } )$

$$
f (y _ {i t}) = \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {K} p _ {k} f _ {Y _ {i t}} (y _ {i t} | C = k)\tag{1}
$$

where K denotes the total number of latent groups, $p _ { k }$ denotes the probability of belonging to group k and $f _ { Y _ { i t } } ( y _ { i t } \vert ~ C = k )$ is the conditional density of the observed data vector given class k. The probabilities $p _ { k }$ of this mixture model are not estimated directly, but related via the SoftMax function to a k-dimensional vector è of latent class coefficients, and time-stable covariates x with associated weight vectors $w _ { k }$ . Through the time-stable covariates, measured at baseline, the model permits the group membership to vary by individual:

$$
p _ {i k} = \frac {e ^ {\theta_ {k} + x _ {i} T _ {w _ {k}}}}{\sum_ {l = 1} ^ {K} e ^ {\theta_ {l} + x _ {i} T _ {w _ {l}}}}\tag{2}
$$

where ${ p } _ { i k } ^ { \phantom { } } = { p } _ { k } ^ { \phantom { } } \left( x _ { i } \right)$ , specified as a multinomial logit function of the baseline characteristics. This makes it possible to examine whether and how such baseline characteristics influence the probability of an individual following the various baseline trajectories. The response vector for the outcome is modeled as a multivariate normal random variable as

$$
Y _ {i} | C = k \sim N (\mu_ {k}, \sigma^ {2})\tag{3}
$$

where the elements of the mean vector are related to the period t (occurrence of each ED visit) of the individual patient as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {\mu} _ {k t} = \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {k 0} + \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {k 1} t + \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {k 2} t ^ {2} + \boldsymbol {\beta} _ {k 3} t ^ {3}\tag{4}
$$

GBTM assumes that the trajectories of each distinct group have a simple polynomial form. The unknown parameter vector $\beta _ { k } ,$ among other things, determines the shape of the group-specific trajectory. It is important to note that conditional independence is assumed only at the latent trajectory group level, and not at the population level. The trajectory modeling software in STATA and SAS for the estimation of GBTM allows for the specification of $\mathfrak { p } ( . )$ as the normal or censored normal distribution, the zero-inflated Poisson distribution, or the binary logit distribution, but in general there is no restriction on the form of p(.) (Jones and Nagin 2013). The method handles missing data, including exposure time for count data. The trajectory modeling software, with data management and output analysis tasks, allows the user to specify parameters of the model and evaluate the best fit model for different counts of trajectories, polynomial functions up to cubic order, and an appropriate data distribution (binary logit in this study). This procedure builds a mixture model in which each trajectory has its own regression model (Jones and Nagin 2013). The trajectory membership for each individual is decided by the individual’s highest posterior probability of group membership. For each individual $i ,$ the posterior probability of group membership for group $k ,$ $( P P G M _ { i t } )$ is calculated using Bayes’ Rule as

$$
P P G M _ {i k} = \frac {p _ {k} P \left(Y _ {i} \mid X _ {i ^ {\prime}} k \mid B _ {k}\right)}{\sum_ {k} p _ {k} P \left(Y _ {i} \mid X _ {i ^ {\prime}} k \mid B _ {k}\right)}\tag{5}
$$

However, in order to perform predictions, group membership conditional on $Y _ { i }$ cannot be calculated for all time periods until they are available. Hence, a recent insightful extension of the use of $P P G M _ { i j }$ is to determine when an individual’s trajectory group assignment (latent class) stabilizes, which occurs when the highest probability class computed at each time period/visit matches that of the class at the individual’s final period/visit (Burckhardt et al. 2016). By conditioning only on measurements up to the observed time, we can calculate posterior probabilities that take all currently available information into account and allows determination of the earliest stabilization of latent class convergence. As highlighted in this study, the computation of $P P G M _ { i k }$ is also critical in the next stage of our analysis that includes group membership from the penultimate visit to predict an individual’s readmission risk at the next visit. A key decision point in GBTM is to determine the number of groups that best represents the heterogeneity in the developmental trajectories of the population. Model selection for varying K is based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) that determines the number of trajectory groups as well as the order of the polynomials for each trajectory group (Nagin 2005). Normally, the model with maximum BIC is considered a better model, with BIC = log likelihood – $0 . 5 \cdot \log ( n ) \cdot m .$ , where n is the sample size and m is the number of parameters. Additional heuristic criteria for optimizing the number of trajectories have been developed in the literature, such as the average probability of group membership and the odds of correct classification (Nagin 2005). However, another practical criterion is to add a trajectory group only when it represents a distinct new segment of the population whose risk stratification is important from a clinical or patient management perspective.

## Covariate Latent Class Model (CLCM)

A latent class model with covariates is characterized by having a categorical (rather than continuous) latent variable with covariates, which is appropriate for modeling the dichotomous dependent variable for readmission risk stratification. The one-step model for latent class analysis with covariates has the form

$$
P (Y _ {i} | X _ {i}) = \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} P (K = t | X _ {i}) P (Y _ {i} | K = t)\tag{6}
$$

where, similar to GBTM, we assume that $Y _ { i }$ is independent of $X _ { i }$ conditional on $K ,$ where $Y _ { i }$ is the vector of responses and $X _ { i }$ is the vector of covariates for individual i, and K is the discrete latent class variable with T classes (Vermunt 2010).

A multinomial logistic regression model for the probability $P ( K = t | X _ { i } )$ has the form

$$
P (K = t \mid X _ {i}) = \frac {\exp \left(\theta_ {0 t} + \sum_ {q = 1} ^ {Q} \theta_ {q t} X _ {i q}\right)}{\sum_ {S = 1} ^ {T} \exp \left(\theta_ {0 t ^ {\prime}} + \sum_ {q = 1} ^ {Q} \theta_ {q s} X _ {i q}\right)}\tag{7}
$$

Estimates of all the parameters are obtained by maximizing the log-likelihood function

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \log P (Y _ {i} | X _ {i}) = \\ \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \log \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} P (K = t | X _ {i}) P (Y _ {i} | K = t) \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

To fit a latent class model, we need to specify the number of classes in the latent variable. The best model is chosen using the maximum AIC (Reboussin et al. 2008) and BIC. The parameters of the model are allowed to vary across the classes of the observed variables.

## Hidden Markov Model

To benchmark the performance of GBTM-based readmission risk stratification and prediction, we also model the latent health status of the patient that may drive readmission risk over time as a hidden Markov model (HMM). Ayabakan et al. (2016) suggested that patients’ general health condition between hospitalizations may be an important unobservable factor that can lead to new readmissions, and developed an HMM to estimate this effect. HMM is a widely used statistical model that defines the interactions between an observed and a hidden process that evolve over time via a sequence of hidden states, a sequence of observations, a state transition probability distribution, an observation probability distribution, and an initial state distribution (Netzer et al. 2008; Rabiner and Juang 1986; Zhang and Padman 2015). In our study, the sequence of ED visits and whether each visit was a readmission or not formed the observations in HMM. The latent health status formed the hidden states with a first-order, memoryless Markov property, which states that the conditional probability distribution of future states depends only on the current state and not on any past states (Rabiner and Juang 1986). Parameters of the HMM, such as transition probabilities of hidden states in the Markov chain, are learned from the data using expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm (Rabiner and Juang 1986). Given the HMM parameters, we perform readmission risk stratification and HMM decoding for prediction. HMM decoding calculates the sequence of hidden states with the highest probability given the sequence of observations and the probability distribution of the model. We determine the optimal number of states using the BIC (Schwarz 1978). Finally, the HMM with all the covariates was executed for all visits (similar to the GBTM and CLCM models) to capture the unobserved health status across the hidden states for future readmission prediction.

## Prediction Modeling

While risk stratification models assign patients to their most likely trajectory groups, latent classes, or Markov states, predicting an individual patient’s risk is the eventual outcome of interest to decision makers. Hence, the latent class information from GBTM, CLCM, and HMM are respectively incorporated into two widely used statistical and machine learning models from the literature, specifically, logistic regression and boosted decision trees (BDT), to classify individual patients based on their readmission risk. Microsoft’s AZURE ML platform (Barga et al. 2015) is used to conduct the predictive analysis.

## Logistic Regression (LR)

Logistic regression is one of the most well-known and widely used prediction models in the literature (Hosmer and Lemeshow 2000). It has been used extensively in research to evaluate readmission rates, time-varying and invariant predictors, and to discover reasons for readmission (Bardhan et al. 2014).

## Boosted Decision Trees (BDT)

BDT is a group learning method, with predictions based on a complete ensemble of trees that correct for errors together (Freund and Mason 1999; Witten et al. 2016). It is applicable to a binary dependent variable such as readmission. It determines a process where a second tree corrects the errors of the first tree, the third tree corrects the errors of the first and second trees, and so on. Dietterich (2000) reported exceptional results when BDT was compared to other decision tree methods such as decision forest, bagging decision tree, and randomized decision tree, where it was able to enhance accuracy by avoiding the prediction of training cases that were hard to classify (Freund and Mason 1999). Boosting can be considered as fitting an extended additive model (Friedman et al. 2000): logit $\begin{array} { r } { \mathbf { \Psi } ( x ) = et { } { ' } \sum _ { k = 1 } ^ { \Lambda } c _ { k } I \{ x \in R _ { k } \} } \end{array}$ , where $I \{ \}$ is the indicator function that assumes a value of 1 if the condition constituting its argument is true or 0 if it is false. Building a decision tree means estimating the parameters of the decision tree, which includes both the regions $\operatorname { R } _ { 1 } ; ~ . . . ; ~ \operatorname { R } _ { \operatorname { K } , }$ and the corresponding logit values $\mathrm { C } _ { 1 } ; . . . ; \mathrm { C } _ { \mathrm { K } }$ (details can be found in Neumann et al. 2004).

## Data and Empirical Study Context

## Data Sources and Description

This study is based on a de-identified data extract from an Israeli HMO, listed as one of the world’s largest nongovernmental HMOs that serves over 4 million customers. It deploys EHRs at all its hospitals and has an HIE network to link them. The system gathers historical clinical and administrative data from the HMO’s seven hospitals, its community clinics, and other points of care such as labs. Our data extract includes 471,192 unique patients’ demographics, medications, adverse reactions, sensitivities, lab and imaging results, past diagnoses, and healthcare procedures, among others, covering ED visits from 2005 to 2008. Applying our patient inclusion criteria for the study of frequent ED visitors, and specifically to multi-morbid patients with at least one readmission in 5 to 12 ED visits, resulted in 16,117 patients with 110,286 ED visits and 34,651 readmissions (31.4%). The choice of the number of visits was based on evidence from the limited literature on frequent readmissions. Black (2014) defined frequent patients, specifically for readmission purposes, as those who had 3 to 5 ED visits within a duration of 1.5 to 2 years. Kirby et al. (2010) defined frequent readmissions as those patients who had three or more ED visits per year. Thus, by adjusting and relaxing these estimates for our data over four years, we considered it reasonable to include patients experiencing at least five ED visits with at least one readmission as a frequent patient to the ED. At the upper end, there were fewer than 4% of patients with more than 12 ED visits out of all the chronic patients who had at least 5 ED visits. These choices were further validated by key senior physicians and administrators at the HMO. Details of the results of additional robustness checks conducted by analyzing different population subgroups are presented later in this paper.

For each visit, the dependent variable was coded in the HMO database as 1 if the patient was readmitted within 30 days of discharge from the hospital on the penultimate visit, and 0 otherwise. We extracted 72 independent variables, including (1) HIE use, which refers to accessing at least one of several medical history components in the HIE (five specific application functionalities that were accessed, such as previous laboratory and community clinic visits), (2) HMO, whether the patient was a member of the main HMO (which owns the HIE), (3) creatinine levels (result), (4) chronic diagnoses, using dichotomous variables for the most common chronic conditions (Charlson et al. 1987) in the patient cohort, (6) admission for pneumonia and influenza (yes/no), (7) LOS, actual inpatient days, (8) time between this visit and the penultimate one (number of days), (9) multiple chronic conditions (MCC), the number of chronic conditions, (10) season, and (10) demographics (age in years and gender).

## Framework for Data Management, Analysis and Performance Evaluation

Figure 1 depicts the overall schema employed in synthesizing and analyzing the data using the various methods described above, and evaluating the results.

Synthesizing the data: Data from the EHRs of the HMO’s seven hospitals were collected for all patients presenting to the EDs, cleaned, filtered to extract all chronic disease patients with frequent readmissions, and integrated utilizing the existing HIE network. The data was aggregated into 72 predictors of interest as detailed earlier. Some were associated with the current visit, such as demographics and diagnoses. Others were obtained by aggregating medical data up to the penultimate visit such as average creatinine results, number of prior readmissions, number of prior EHR accesses and types of usage.

Analytic approach: GBTM, CLCM, and HMM were applied, respectively, to generate longitudinal clusters of patients following distinct trajectories. Dichotomous variables, one for each trajectory group, provides the group membership of each individual patient for each method. A limited subset of the covariates, such as age and gender at baseline, and every CCI diagnosis separated into 10 dichotomous variables for each diagnosis, pneumonia and influenza diagnosis (current visit), season (current visit), and HIE use (current visit) were included in the trajectory analysis process. Once a patient is diagnosed with a chronic condition at visit t, it remains in the patient’s record permanently. The trajectory group membership was subsequently incorporated with additional covariates into the readmission risk prediction models, LR and BDT, for individual patients.

Performance evaluation: When a patient is discharged from the hospital, the likelihood of the next visit being a readmission for each chronic patient is predicted for two scenarios as either including (or not) the patient’s latent class at the current visit in the longitudinal clustering algorithms in the two prediction models along with the 72 original predictors. For instance, GBTM, CLCM, and HMM are executed for the third visit using information up to the second visit’s discharge.

The training and testing data sets, displayed in Table 2, were generated as follows. The training set includes all patients whose (n + 1)<sup>st</sup> visits were in the first three years of the sample. The model was trained using all predictors until the n<sup>th</sup> visit to predict the readmission probability for the (n + 1)<sup>st</sup> visit using five-fold cross validation. The testing dataset included all patients whose (n + 1)<sup>st</sup> visit was in the fourth year. We evaluated the prediction results using the widely used area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) using the DeLong et al. (1988) test. For example, for each patient in their fifth visit, the models predicted whether the sixth visit would likely be a readmission or not. The training set included 7,394 patients whose sixth visit took place within the first three years in which the prediction model was trained, and the testing set includes 3,419 patients whose sixth visit was in the fourth year. To clarify further, although the total number of patients in the fifth visit was 16,117, the prediction model only included 10,813 who had a sixth visit. The trained model predicted the readmission probability for these patients in the validation set and reports AUC.

## Exploratory Analysis: Risk Stratification and Profiling

## GBTM Results

The optimized, baseline binary logit model of patients’ readmission trajectories over 5 to 12 visits stratified the patient population into three groups (Figure 3(a)). The full model with 11 time-varying covariates and 2 time-stable covariates is displayed in Figure 3(b). The optimization process included five goodness of fit tests (Nagin 2005), including minimum BIC and AIC across the number of groups ranging from two to six. This analysis was repeated for each visit separately. Due to either lower AIC/BIC or lack of convergence of higher order groups, the three-group model was selected for maximum consistency across all visits. In addition, it offers the practical advantage of allowing healthcare organizations to denote the three groups as high, medium and low risk groups, respectively.

The final models clustered 16,117 patients into three distinct trajectories of readmission progression (X-axis depicts visits and Y-axis displays readmission likelihood). While the general shape of the trajectories were unchanged from baseline to the full model, the addition of time-stable and timevarying covariates had an impact on the proportion of patients in each trajectory group. Group 1, following a linear trajectory, was the smallest of the three groups and was estimated to include almost 8% of the patients in the full model, manifesting increasing readmission likelihood over time. Group 2 displays a cubic polynomial trajectory with an estimated 63% of the cohort in the full model. Their average readmission sion probability rises during the early visits, peaks around six visits, and decreases subsequently. Group 3, with a quadratic polynomial trajectory, includes an estimated 29% of the cohort in the full model. Patients in this group have the highest readmission probability during the early visits, then decreases up to visits 7 or 8, and subsequently increases. Thus, with three distinctly differing trajectories of readmission progression, the GBTM stratification captures the heterogeneity in the patient cohort. Additional profiling, presented later, delineates these differences further.

## CLCM Results

A comparison of AIC, log likelihood and BIC for the model for each visit shows that the optimal number of groups is three for all visits. Hence, we chose three groups in order to enable maximum consistency and interpretability (Table 6). Thus, the optimized covariate latent class model also stratified the patient population into three groups; however, they were comprised of significantly different sizes from the GBTM.

With almost equal number of patients in Groups 1, 2, and 3, respectively, the CLCM results do not provide a high degree of discrimination between the three groups that is useful for targeting appropriate interventions. Analysis of the major comorbidities further validates this conclusion. Furthermore, a crosswalk of Group 1 patients in GBTM and CLCM, respectively, shows that 125 patients out of the 205 (61%) in GBTM Group 1 were assigned to CLCM Group 1, and that both groups were at the highest risk for readmission.

## Predictive Modeling Insights: Individual Level Prediction Models

## Predicting Readmissions of Patients Utilizing Group Membership Information

Table 8 presents a summary of the prediction results of the logistic regression and BDT models with and without adding the group membership variables from GBTM, CLCM, and HMM, respectively, to the 72 domain predictors. The individual predictions incorporated the state information at visit n to predict readmission probability at visit (n + 1). We report the improvement in AUC for both classifiers, though all other prediction metrics followed the same ranking (recall, precision, accuracy, etc.). As the number of visits increased beyond three, the clustering information improved the AUC, increasing to between 63% and 72%, on average (for GBTM), from 52%. Whereas the CLCM and HMM variables showed the best performance during the early visits, GBTM had significantly (DeLong et al. 1988) better results after the fourth visit. When comparing only CLCM and HMM based results from the fifth visit to the eleventh visit, the HMM state information provided better AUC than CLCM for the BDT classifier, and between the sixth and the tenth visits for the logistic regression classifier.

Additionally, models that combined GBTM with BDT had the lowest false negative values (FNs). Not recognizing a correct readmission and falsely classifying a non-readmission have differing but significant penalties associated with them for both patients and providers. Minimizing FN is thus of major value in actual practice.

We also compared the prediction scores using both logistic regression and BDT of the most seriously ill groups stratified by each latent class model to better understand the value of stratification in these individual-level predictions. For the tenth visit, for example, GBTM demonstrated the highest accuracy, precision and recall levels, with the recall levels showing a greater contribution of GBTM to the prediction scores (86.7% versus 22.7% and 18.2% for CLCM and HMM, respectively).

## Analysis of Risk Factors

To improve the practical value of prediction models, healthcare organizations need to be able to identify the critical factors that increase readmission risk in their population. For a complex, multi-morbid patient population, this is even more challenging and critical. We analyzed the significance levels of patients’ attributes and the prevalence of their chronic diseases on prediction outcomes in each group and for all visits. The most significant features for prediction across all visits are age, gender, recent creatinine result, number of prior 30-day readmissions, and MCC (current sum). The main chronic diagnoses that have a significant impact on the prediction scores are CHF, chronic nephritis or nephrotic syndrome, COPD, and diabetes. Additionally, the number of significant parameters are quite stable after the second visit.

## Separate Analysis of Three Major Chronic Conditions

While the literature on predicting readmissions is rich in the modeling and analysis of individual conditions such as CHF and AMI, there is an ongoing debate on the generalizability and value of condition-specific versus all-cause readmission prediction models, especially for patients with multiple chronic conditions (Lavenberg et al. 2014; Tanzer and Heil 2013). We investigated this debate further in the context of the current study by examining three major chronic diseases with high prevalence (Table 5). These included CHF (1,613 patients), AMI (1,478 patients) and chronic nephritis/ nephrotic syndrome (CNNS) (1,220 patients). We repeated the two-stage GBTM-based analysis described earlier for each of the three condition patient groups and included only the time-varying information regarding this chronic condition and all other time-stable variables.

## Discussion

In this study, three statistical methods (GBTM, CLCM, and HMM) were evaluated to risk- stratify chronic disease patients with frequent ED visits and hospital readmissions into distinct groups following separate trajectories of readmission progression over time, and profile them using critical demographic and clinical covariates. We further investigated the discriminative ability of these latent class/state methods to predict future readmissions at the individual level by combining the group membership results with each of the two most widely used statistical and machine learning classification models. By leveraging the availability of a rich and unique, multi-year, healthcare dataset extracted from the EHR of the largest HMO in Israel, we analyzed longitudinal patterns in hospital readmissions of patients with multiple chronic diseases and recurrent ED visits using a two-stage procedure. In the first stage, each of the methods clustered patients into distinct latent groups/states, followed by a second stage in which individual patient-level predictions were generated. We find that these methods (GBTM, CLCM, and HMM) are mostly helpful after about three visits, particularly with cluster refinements as the time-varying covariates are added.

Our findings indicate that in the clinical context, GBTM offers enhanced utility and discriminative ability in comparison to CLCM and HMM methods used for risk stratification. There are four main insights from this research that combine methodological and application features. First, GBTM based risk stratification, with inclusion of time-stable and timevarying covariates, emerged as more discriminating that CLCM and HMM in assigning distinctly differing groups of patients into three latent trajectories based on their presentation at the ED. In particular, GBTM identified one very small group (8%) of seriously ill patients who had the highest risk of future readmissions, potentially facilitating easy identification and personalized care management of this group. Second, the fast computation in GBTM of the posterior probability of group membership (PPGM) as new information becomes available at the end of each ED visit helps to determine the latent trajectory group of each patient at that visit, thus improving the individual level prediction model by incorporating the updated latent group information. More importantly, this knowledge offers a more granular and nuanced opportunity to intervene and potentially prevent the next ED visit and readmission. Third, PPGM also emerged as useful in identifying the earliest visit at which patients converge to their likely final trajectories, which may facilitate timely testing and interventions that are particularly critical for the highest risk patients. Finally, profiling the three trajectory groups revealed 30-day readmission rates that differed significantly by age, gender, creatinine levels, chronic conditions, and LOS, measured at each ED visit, which may enable readmission risk stratification of the patient population for targeted interventions and for developing individualized postdischarge care plans (Hao et al. 2015). Specifically, the explicit inclusion of contemporaneously developing, timevarying comorbidities as they are diagnosed at each visit may change a patient’s trajectory group membership from one visit to the next and may signal a complex patient with unstable health conditions. Such indications can be responded to earlier and more decisively.

The comparison of the prediction results using two wellknown classification models with and without including the GBTM group membership information illustrated the statistically significant improvement that can be obtained by enhancing each model with the GBTM results. Overall, the prediction outcomes using GBTM trajectory groups (with AUC of about 63% to 72%) were not only superior to not using this information, but were better than CLCM and HMM (after the first few visits), and similar to most results reported in the literature for the general readmission problem (Amarasingham et al. 2010; Bardhan et al. 2014; Boulding et al. 2011; Felker et al. 2004; Hao et al. 2015; Shams et al. 2015). Dealing with multi-morbid patients, who are also frequent ED patients with repeated readmissions, increases the readmission challenge due to the diversity and complexity of their health conditions (Wijlaars et al. 2016). We also attempted to increase the current cohort with propensity score-matched patients from low ED visit patient population (one to four visits), resulting in a poorly matched group of only 450 patients (less than 4%) and a low likelihood of an impact on AUC; hence, this was not pursued further.

Comparing the AUC of current and past prediction models for readmission shows that for acute decompensated heart failure, Boulding et al. (2011) reported an AUC of 0.69, for all Charlson comorbidity diagnoses together, Hao et al. (2015) reported an AUC of 0.72 and van Walraven et al. (2010) of 0.68. For CHF risk assessment, Amarasingham et al. (2010) found an AUC of 0.72 and Bardhan et al. (2014) of 0.6. In two recent works, the authors indeed achieved higher AUC values of 0.82 (Cai et al. 2015) and 0.75 (Rajkomar et al. 2018). However, they did not address patients dealing with multiple comorbidities nor chronic disease patients; rather, they developed a non-disease-specific model. On the other hand, our findings were similar to those with multiple chronic conditions; application of these techniques to non-chronic patient data is an important area of future research.

This study extends previous research by following the same patients over multiple visits instead of predicting based on a single visit. Most studies have used data from only the most recent visit to predict readmission likelihood (Felker et al. 2004; Khanna et al. 2014). Studies have also simply explained the statistical connections between different variables and readmissions (Boulding et al. 2011; Donzé et al. 2013). Some have included clinical variables associated with some common primary diagnoses related to hospital readmissions. We include several major chronic diagnoses for each patient that develop over time in addition to aggregated demographic, inpatient, HIE and related variables with two distinct classification models for analyzing the unplanned readmission risk for patients with repeated ED visits.

## Scientific Contribution

Interventions to improve patient uptake of chronic disease management services and improving the availability of alternative nonhospital services should help reduce the readmission rate of multi-morbid patients and the related problems resulting in ED visits (Kirby et al. 2010). This research sets the scene for further examination of the different reasons why patients with multiple chronic conditions repeatedly present in the ED, with a few being subsequently admitted to the hospital, rather than seeking chronic disease management services (Kirby et al. 2010). It is vital to guide the development of policy and design of targeted interventions to develop more appropriate patient management strategies. Two Australian states, New South Wales and Victoria, have reported that the diversion of chronic disease patients to chronic disease management services, including those provided by primary care, can reduce readmission rates (Tachedjian and Zaunders 2007); hence, identifying the distinct risk groups and their specific needs as early as possible is the first critical task in such initiatives.

While investigated already in the literature, we believe that the two-stage approach of combining group-level results from a longitudinal time series analysis for stratification with individual-level outcomes via a single snapshot of prediction with classification models is generalizable to many other contexts. The special characteristic of our approach is that the GBTM model incorporates the progression of several common chronic conditions as they are diagnosed and as they evolve over time (for example, the top ten chronic conditions in the population). Subsequently, the prediction model allows the inclusion of a vast array of other important variables (for example, 72 in this study) that provide a comprehensive view of the multi-morbid population for analyzing ED visits and associated hospital readmissions.

Finally, the findings may enable health policy makers to better understand the potential of HIE networks and interoperability between various points of care by showing the value of medical information and their potential to forecast readmission. This could promote the utilization and implementation of such systems. Future research should further investigate other clinical markers of complex diseases and include additional time-varying risk factors such as critical vitals and other laboratory values.

## Relevance to MIS and Contributions to the IS Field

The relevance and contributions of this research to the broader field of MIS is divided into five components: EHR analytics, methodological contribution, a two-stage solution to a dynamic and complex problem, design science research, and the role of predictive analytics.

EHRs enable integration of patient medical information for planning and executing safe and appropriate treatment. Data mining and decision support capabilities in EHRs empower inspection and development of effective medical care and therapies for chronic disease diagnosis, monitoring and management (Kohli and Tan 2016). Consistent with calls for the integration of such capabilities in MIS studies (Chen et al. 2012; Shmueli and Koppius 2011) and healthcare IS research (Bardhan et al. 2014; Kohli and Tan 2016; Lin et al. 2017), particularly for readmission prediction and risk profiling (Harle et al. 2012; Helm et al. 2016), we utilized the EHR data of patients with multiple chronic conditions and repeated ED visits and readmissions that manifest over extended time periods. An essential and unique contribution of our approach for readmission risk analysis is the consideration of the rapidly growing population of multi-morbid patients using a risk prediction framework that incorporates both population and individual-level perspectives.

GBTM is a powerful statistical approach for risk stratification and profiling that can allow healthcare providers to utilize EHR data and track different groups of patients more efficiently after a few ED visits, which can lead to improved medical insights compared to multiple independent risk models as suggested in a recent MIS paper (Lin et al. 2017). Our results further suggest that GBTM outperforms other longitudinal data modeling techniques, such as CLCM and HMM, that have been tested in risk profiling. From a methodological viewpoint, the addition of a time-series-clustering perspective in our study contributes a different dimension to MIS research, especially in the predictive analytics context. This longitudinal perspective facilitates the analysis of progression of many metrics such as risk, disease, and outcomes as they change over time, additionally providing model estimates with confidence intervals and posterior probabilities.

The two-stage approach combines population-level stratification results from longitudinal time series analysis with individual-level prediction results at a snapshot in time with classification models. Clustering methods have been combined successfully with classification methods in several studies (Yang et al. 2018). However, GBTM clustering follows patients over time rather than using time as another variable in a cross-sectional clustering model. Typically, in such cases, data mining methods are initially used to generate new variables, which are added to subsequent econometric models as independent variables/regressors (Moreno and Terwiesch 2014; Wang et al. 2013; Yang et al. 2018). Furthermore, we examined multiple goodness of fit tests to validate GBTM trajectory assignments before the prediction stage, thus minimizing error cascades, improving prediction results, and also demonstrating the usefulness of the two-stage approach for new research studies in IS.

Design science research focuses on developing improved solutions to problems that are of a particularly complex nature (Baskerville et al. 2015), such as the repeated readmission of multi-morbid patients. Different types of knowledge production may occur through the reuse of past artifacts that can take several forms, including constructs, models, and methods. While this study is primarily a data analytics study, the risk profiling method described here is a probable instantiation of the solution artifact (Gregor and Hevner 2013; Lin et al. 2017). Applying GBTM to the dynamic, complex readmission risk prediction problem demonstrates the design science principle of easing complex tasks (Baskerville et al. 2015). Additional research is needed to further develop such new principles and their evolving theories, which was not the focus of this specific study. Thus, we see our study as prescriptive knowledge, and as “exaptation” (extend known solutions to new problems) suggested by Gregor and Hevner (2013, p. 345).

Shmueli and Koppius (2011) described six roles for predictive analytics: new theory generation, measurement development, comparison of competing theories, improvement of existing models, relevance assessment, and assessment of the predictability of empirical phenomena. Our work demonstrates at least three of these; namely, measurement development (developments of existing models), improvement of existing models using an empirical, two-stage approach (combining GBTM with prediction), and relevance assessment (establishing the relevance of existing methods), and illustrates a useful and flexible existing method for the MIS field.

## Practical Contribution

Recent studies indicate that U.S. readmission rates are higher in areas where people with multiple chronic conditions are clustered (Basu et al. 2016). Chronic patients who have unplanned readmissions not once or twice, but multiple times, are especially challenging for the health system (Black 2014; Kirby et al. 2010; Szekendi et al. 2015) and were the specific focus of this study. The insights from the risk stratification, profiling and prediction methods described here offer actionable opportunities for earlier identification of high risk patients, better triaging at the ED visit, and design and implementation of appropriate and timely interventions for coordinated and personalized care in inpatient and ambulatory settings that may lead to better health outcomes and quality of care.

From an application perspective, with the increasing deployment and use of EHRs in the hospitals and the ED in Israel, the United States, and many parts of the world, data are now available to investigate readmission reduction, a major challenge everywhere. The variables and data we used are representative of this setting, and were drawn from several EDs in Israel, not just one. Hence, the approaches we tested and insights from the analyses should be replicable and generalizable in other similar settings. In particular, the number of prior readmissions, days between ED visits, some key laboratory results (e.g., creatinine levels), and so on, are common risk factors identified in this study that can be tracked in hospitals everywhere.

From a preventive care perspective, the GBTM results may also support clinicians in the application of the chronic care model and keep patients away from the ED, thus reducing ED visits and nosocomial infections associated with hospitalizations. Our focus on MCC patients and the explicit representation of the increasing complexity of their multiple chronic diagnoses with time-varying covariates provide new opportunities for the holistic management of such patients through better coordination with the ambulatory care delivery setting. Furthermore, risk stratification linked to chronic disease progression (Burckhardt et al. 2016; Padman et al. 2014) may enable clinicians to provide targeted care that addresses the specific needs of each group using both online and traditional care delivery models. Each trajectory group demonstrates significantly differing risk profile for which clinical teams need to identify appropriate interventions. As new types of data get integrated in the care delivery setting, such as social determinants of health, the trajectories can be refined to identify further categories of populations to design specific interventions.

From the ED perspective, there is significant evidence in the literature (Moore et al. 2017) that EDs are increasingly having to manage multi-morbid patients with frequent readmissions. Researchers, practitioners and federal agencies (Friedman et al. 2008; Wijlaars et al. 2016). Gerteis et al. (2014) and HHS (2010) have called for solutions and tools to provide integrated care for such patient populations. Since it is ED medicine, not inpatient/outpatient where individual diseases are managed for the patients, a holistic view of such complex patients is sorely needed to deliver better care and improve outcomes. Hence, understanding the heterogeneity in disease progression of this increasingly challenging population and predicting the likelihood of their readmission as they move from ED visit to hospitalization is a critical step in developing policies and interventions to minimize adverse outcomes to patients and society.

From a population health perspective, early identification of patients’ risk profiles should allow better resource allocation, both at the ED and community care environments, similar to research on early diagnosis related group (DRG) identification of inpatients (Gartner et al. 2015). However, despite the availability of advanced, sophisticated prediction models, the results of this study indicate that addressing the heterogeneity of the patient population via risk stratification methods can improve prediction performance better than new models or more covariates. They also result in parsimonious models that are interpretable, useful and actionable at the point of care.

## Guidelines for IS Researchers and Practitioners

This section presents guidelines for IS researchers and practitioners. We observe that the techniques applied in this study appear to be more useful in scenarios such as (one or more of the following): (1) managing complicated patients (chronic ED visitors), (2) extensive, individual-level, longitudinal data is available, and (3) analyzing repeated visits of the same patients (above three, in this study), even if it applies to fewer patients.

There are many similar scenarios where the GBTM method can be applied in the IS and health contexts. They require an understanding of the application contexts, data characteristics, preprocessing requirements and modeling assumptions, that can facilitate appropriate modeling, solution and interpretation.

Application contexts: GBTM has been applied to study problems arising in criminal justice, clinical medicine, technology adoption, disease progression, mental health and medication adherence, among others.

Data characteristics: GBTM can handle data that follows censored normal, zero inflated Poisson and binary distributions, which meet the needs of a wide range of applications. In the healthcare context, however, model estimation for a range of other distributions may be needed since clinical data is increasingly combined with data from claims, social determinants, sensors, devices, images, signals and mobile applications, resulting in complex data types and distributions.

Data preprocessing: It is important to document the type and volume of missing data; imputation from available data can be used if data is missing at random. Data is preprocessed into tabular format for processing by canned software.

GBTM assumptions: (1) the population is made up of a finite mixture of distinct groups who follow distinct developmental trajectories; (2) conditional on group membership, risk factors and outcome variable measurements are independent in the model; (3) maximum likelihood estimation is used to estimate model parameters.

Several additional highlights should be noted, related to the GBTM model fitting, optimization, extension, and GBTM result for prediction:

GBTM model fitting: There are several procedures that researchers should apply in order to determine the best GBTM model. This includes evaluating the best-fit model for different counts of trajectories, polynomial functions up to cubic order, and an appropriate data distribution (Binary logit, Poisson or Censored normal). It is important that the order of the polynomial for each trajectory be high enough to match the shape that theoretically might emerge from the data (Nagin and Odgers 2010).

GBTM optimization: Following model fitting, the optimization process suggests four goodness of fit tests, including (1) the minimum BIC and AIC across the number of groups; (2) p-values of the shapes and their overall fit; (3) the least difference between actual and estimated proportion of assignment to groups; and (4) the highest average posterior probability of group membership for each group. The conventional ÷² goodness of fit test is not appropriate for GBTM (Nagin (2005).

GBTM extensions: Adding time stable and time-varying covariates further refines the longitudinal clustering, group assignment and profiling in longitudinal datasets that follow the same patients over multiple visits to any clinical point of care.

GBTM result for prediction (optional for individual level): Incorporating GBTM group information for analyzing the large number of time-stable, time-varying and aggregate covariates in the individual-level risk prediction models can delineate the key risk factors and their impact to inform guidelines for model implementation and use that are customized for diverse healthcare organizations worldwide.

Following are guidelines for the general process that clarifies how researchers can apply all of the above: After data preprocessing and validating the data characteristics, there is a need to validate the fit of GBTM assumptions to the research problem. The GBTM model fitting will start with a representation of the basic model (without adding covariates) followed by an optimization of this basic model (GBTM Optimization). Once the basic model is optimized, it is recommended to add GBTM extensions (adding the covariates into a full model). We recommend following the same processes of optimization for the full model also (as done for the basic model) to see that there are no major changes from the basic model (in the dominance of this model and also in the actual shapes of the polynomial functions of each trajectory). In case of major differences between the basic and the full models, it is recommended to examine the model fitting of the basic model again, and in extreme cases, it is also recommended to examine the data preprocessing. Finally, the individual level prediction is optional to be executed on the full model.

## Limitations and Avenues for Further Research

Some limitations of the current study are detailed below. The focus of this study was to investigate unplanned readmission risk for patients with multiple chronic conditions; however, a similar approach needs to be investigated for other patient populations, such as non-chronic or all inpatient admissions, to evaluate the generalizability of the proposed analysis framework. The increasing digitization of healthcare in both the provider and consumer self-health management settings is generating large volumes of highly granular data that may provide new and influential covariates for estimating the models, such as social determinants of health, new biomarkers and laboratory results, and new modeling extensions, such as multi-trajectory models for better decision making in actual practice. Furthermore, with large volumes of high dimensional data, deep learning methods, while currently lacking interpretability, may improve the prediction results. Patient mortality data are missing in this analysis due to the significant challenges in obtaining this information from disparate sources, and is a limitation that needs to be addressed in future research.

Future research could also incorporate feature engineering and dimensionality reduction methods to identify a more insightful and minimum set of longitudinal factors drawn from the ED, inpatient and ambulatory care, and related to readmissions. Additionally, a methodological investigation of GBTM to explicate the contribution of time-varying covariates and the timing of their impact may help to further delineate the trajectories of patients with varying combinations of chronic conditions, diagnosed at different time periods and estimate the impact of the evolving conditions over time. Finally, conditional on group assignment, local independence is assumed for outcome variable values in GBTM; hence, for valid comparison, the same was assumed for CLCM and HMM as well. Reboussin et al. (2008) proposed a locally dependent latent class model with covariates that used an interpretable pairwise odds ratio to quantify local dependence between binary variables as an extension of their marginal latent class regression model (Reboussin and Anthony 2001). Extensions of the models for the longitudinal readmission prediction problem that accommodate local dependence is another methodological topic for future research.

## About the Authors

Ofir Ben-Assuli serves as the director of Information Systems Specialization and as Director of Information Systems Research Fields in the Faculty of Business Administration at the Ono Academic College. He completed his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems at Tel-Aviv University; MBA at The Hebrew University; and B.A. in Economics and Computer Science at The Ben-Gurion University. His main research interests are in healthcare information technology, predictive analytics and medical informatics. He has published in European Journal of Information Systems, International Journal of Medical Informatics, Decision Sciences, Decision Support Systems, Health Policy, Journal of Biomedical Informatics, among others. Ofir has been awarded several grants for his research including from the U.S.–Israel BSF and the German– Israeli Foundation GIF.

Rema Padman is the Trustees Professor of Management Science and Healthcare Informatics in the Heinz College of Information Systems and Public Policy at Carnegie Mellon University, and an adjunct professor in the Department of Biomedical Informatics at the University of Pittsburgh School of Medicine. Her research, funded by the National Institutes of Health, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Veterans Health Administration, and the U.S.–Israel BSF, among others, investigates healthcare analytics and operations and data-driven decision support, in the context of clinical and consumer-facing IT interventions in healthcare delivery, such as e-health, m-health, chronic and infectious disease management, and workflow analysis. She has published extensively on healthcare informatics, management science, and information systems research topics.
