# Measuring switching costs in IT outsourcing services

Dwayne Whitten <sup>a</sup>, Robin L. Wakefield <sup>b,\*</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Texas A&M University, Mailstop 4217, College Station, TX 77843, USA <sup>b</sup> Baylor University, One Bear Place #8005, Waco, TX 76798, USA

Received 28 October 2004; accepted 14 November 2005 Available online 26 January 2006

## Abstract

Switching costs play a major role in managerial decisions to (dis)continue an IT outsourcing service. This paper emphasizes the importance of switching cost analysis to the understanding of interorganizational exchange relationships. Based on transaction cost theory and social exchange theory, we specify a second-order switching cost model that provides greater insight into the underlying dimensions influencing switching behavior. The multidimensional nature of the switching cost construct is modeled and tested using data from organizations that outsource the application development function. Findings provide support for a higher-order construct with implications for how switching costs are defined, measured and operationalized in larger nomonological models. - 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Strategy; Switching costs; Transaction cost theory; Social exchange theory; Information technology; Outsourcing; Application development

## 1. Introduction

As information technology (IT) outsourcing continues to grow and impact the business environment, the termination of interorganizational relationships is clearly a major issue for practitioners, managers, and researchers. Subcontracting some or all of a firm’s IT functions to an external provider is motivated by potential benefits such as economies of scale, access to IT expertise and new technologies, faster applications development, and flexibility in managing IT resources (Benko, 1993; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993;

Lacity et al., 1994). However, research shows that many organizations do not realize increased management flexibility or service levels from outsourcing (Hirschheim and Lacity, 2000) and as many as 44% of clients are considering changing service providers (Cox et al., 2005) in favor of back-sourcing (i.e., bringing outsourced functions in-house) and switching vendors (Lacity and Willcocks, 2002). Switching costs are important factors in decisions to terminate contracts; however, the measurement of switching costs and their role in IT outsourcing discontinuance remains unclear.

Prior research discusses switching costs in various ways. Theoretically, the construct is described as the disutility related to change (Weiss and Anderson, 1992), the relational investments among exchange partners (Jackson, 1985), the costs of dissolving a contractual relationship (Porter, 1980) and the combination of psychological and economic cost factors in business relationships (Klemperer, 1987). Typically, the concept of switching costs is operationalized as a uni-dimensional construct (e.g., Heide and John, 1992; Walker and Poppo, 1991) although researchers often define the concept in multi-dimensional terms. Consequently, a global measure of switching costs with undiferentiated components obscures the understanding of how the underlying dimensions interact and hinders the predictive ability of the construct. The objective of this paper is to evaluate the switching cost construct as a higher-order factor comprised of specific first-order dimensions in order to advance theory development and testing related to IT outsourcing. When measured at a higher level, the construct has important implications for how switching costs are defined, operationalized, and interpreted in larger nomonological models of managerial decision making.

The marketing and economics literature contend that switching costs are significant antecedents of repurchase intentions (Weiss and Heide, 1993; Jones et al., 2002), organizational buyer behavior (Heide and Weiss, 1995) and competitive strategies (Farrell and Shapiro, 1988; Klemperer, 1987). Switching costs are mathematically modeled to show how they may segment markets into submarkets, lead to monopoly rents, and induce greater competition (Farrell and Shapiro, 1988; Klemperer, 1987; Von Weizsacker, 1984). Given the importance of switching costs in exchange relationships, an understanding of the individual cost variables would advance our knowledge of the construct and give insight into processes afecting interorganizational relationships. While researchers are studying the outsourcing value proposition in terms of complementarity in organizational design and benefits accruing to client and vendor (e.g., Levina and Ross, 2003), a gap exists in the study of the economic and relational cost factors influencing the continuance/discontinuance of contractual service relationships (e.g., outsourcing). Research conceptualizing switching costs as uni-dimensional is too simplistic for the prediction of managerial behaviors or the management of contractual relationships. This research identifies and assesses salient switching cost components, empirically tests the factors in the context of application development outsourcing and provides a framework for future IT research.

To achieve the stated objectives, we first identify lower-level switching cost variables. We draw from the marketing literature to identify key factors related to the economic and relational aspects of switching costs in the outsourcing domain. The scale items measuring each switching cost factor are assessed and the measures are refined before inclusion in the higher-order model. The higher-order model conceptualizes the switching cost construct as a second-order variable comprised of eight first-order dimensions. The model is tested using data from 160 organizational managers with recent experience in application development outsourcing. Second-order modeling appropriately evaluates the multifaceted switching cost construct, distinguishes individual switching cost components, and shows the interdependence of the eight dimensions.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The subsequent section provides a literature review of the research critical to identifying components of the switching cost construct in the outsourcing domain. This is followed by a discussion of relevant theory that guides the identification and proposal of the second-order construct. The measurement items are then evaluated and refined before testing the higher-order model. The paper concludes with a discussion of the results and implications for future research.

## 2. Switching costs: A review of the literature

Over the past 20 years, the conceptualization of switching costs has moved from math ematical modeling in the economic literature to a uni-dimensional measure in empirical studies. Most recently, the literature emphasizes individual facets that comprise the switch ing cost construct to evaluate customer behavior and managerial decision making. ## 2.1. Switching costs

In the economics literature, switching costs are defined as relationship-specific investments between buyers and suppliers (Farrell and Shapiro, 1988) that may be classified into three categories: learning costs, transaction costs and contractual costs (Klemperer, 1987). Economic research primarily emphasizes the theoretical implications of switching costs on market share and competition (Klemperer, 1987, 1995; Farrell and Shapiro, 1988). However, mathematical modeling is used to show that switching costs in mature markets lead to monopoly rents, raise prices and may induce greater competition in early market stages (Klemperer, 1987, 1995). In a dynamic model, switching costs are shown to encourage new market entrants even though such entry is ineficient (Farrell and Shapiro, 1988).

In buyer–supplier relationships, switching costs are defined as an overall cost or dificulty of switching (Weiss and Anderson, 1992), additional cost and efort in changing suppliers (Ping, 1993), an undefined component of termination (Morgan and Hunt, 1994) and investments that inhibit change (Nielson, 1996). Switching costs in service relationships include perceptions of time, efort, and money in changing service providers (Jones et al., 2000), perceived economic and psychological costs (Jones et al., 2002), perceived disutility (Chen and Hitt, 2002), and onetime costs associated with switching providers (Burnham et al., 2003). Throughout the literature, switching costs are defined and opera tionalized as expenditures of economic (i.e., monetary) and/or relational (i.e., time, efort) resources in switching from one supplier or service provider to another.

In identifying components of switching costs, the literature promotes both economic and relational cost dimensions. Relationship costs are operationalized as backlash by the prior supplier and consequences of supplier revenge (Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Heide and Weiss, 1995), time and efort to locate an alternative (Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Ping, 1993; Morgan and Hunt, 1994; Jones et al., 2000), learning or procedural costs (Klemperer, 1987, 1995; Burnham et al., 2003) and the costs of uncertainty, search and evaluation of an alternative (Jones et al., 2002).

In sum, the literature defines and operationalizes switching costs in terms of economic (i.e., monetary) expenditures and intangible (i.e., psychological or relational) costs associated with changing an exchange relationship. The categorization of switching costs has evolved from broad descriptions of costs (e.g., economic or psychological) to specific costs (e.g., search and evaluation costs or set-up costs) to more definitively understand and study the construct. This paper encompasses these prior definitions to define switching costs as the economic and relational costs of discontinuing a service relationship.

## 2.2. Switching costs and behavior

The impact of switching costs on consumer and managerial behavior has dominated the switching cost studies in the marketing literature. Switching costs are associated with the likelihood of continuing an exchange relationship with a supplier (Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Ping, 1993; Morgan and Hunt, 1994) and customer repurchase intentions (Jones et al., 2002). One recent empirical study examines consumer switching behavior in the online brokerage industry inferring the presence of switching costs from the choice probabilities of new and established clients (Chen and Hitt, 2002). Other researchers find a positive connection between switching costs, satisfaction, trust and/or commitment to an exchange partner (Ping, 1993; Weiss and Anderson, 1992; De Ruyter et al., 1998; Nielson, 1996; Jones et al., 2000; Sharma and Patterson, 2000). Switching behaviors have also been studied in various domains such as consumer purchasing (Klemperer, 1987, 1995; Farrell and Shapiro, 1988; De Ruyter et al., 1998), organizational buying (Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Ping, 1993; Morgan and Hunt, 1994; Nielson, 1996), and non-contractual services (De Ruyter et al., 1998; Jones et al., 2000, 2002; Sharma and Patterson, 2000; Burnham et al., 2003). Weiss and Anderson (1992) examine structural inertia in organizations finding that high switching costs and dissatisfaction are important antecedents in organizational restructuring decisions.

The link between switching costs and behavior is well established; however, most prior studies take a global or one-dimensional approach to measuring switching costs or include specific dimensions in regression models that mask the individual efects of the compo nents (e.g., Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Ping, 1993; Heide and Weiss, 1995; Jones et al., 2000). One disadvantage of a global switching cost approach is the inability to explain the contradiction between economic theory and managerial behavior. For example, researchers identify social costs and psychological costs (see Klemperer, 1987) in addition to economic costs that may explain why structural inertia persists even with unsatisfactory business arrangements (Hannan and Freeman, 1984; Levinthal and Fichman, 1988). Yet, an overall measure of switching costs does not diferentiate the components or evaluate contextual diferences across business environments. Theoretically, the development of a higher-order switching cost factor may help explain such contradictions. From a firm perspective, understanding how vendors may inflate the costs of terminating contractual relationships by efectively managing certain types of switching costs (e.g., loyalty, benefits) will lead to more eficient managerial decisions.

Two more recent studies begin to identify individual switching cost components in non-contractual services relationships. Jones et al. (2002) evaluate specific switching cost variables and develop scale items related to consumer banking and hair stylist choices. Burnham et al. (2003) test scale items related to the procedural, financial, and relational costs associated with changing credit cards and long-distance phone services. The move toward identifying specific cost components contributes to the understanding and predic tion of behavior related to exchange relationships in the service sector.

In summary, prior research defines and evaluates switching costs in the context of customer repurchase intentions, organizational buying decisions and non-contractual service relationships. Furthermore, the operationalization of switching costs is often in uni-dimensional terms or as the average of specific indicators for regression analyses, even though the construct is described in multiple dimensions. Theoretically, the explanatory and predictive ability of the construct is hindered when individual dimensions are uniden tified and their interdependence unexplained. The present study addresses these gaps by presenting a higher-order conceptualization of the switching cost construct, evaluating individual cost factors in the context of contractual interorganizational relationships (i.e., IT outsourcing) and showing the level of interdependence among the individual factors.

## 2.3. Theory

Transaction cost theory (TCT) and social exchange theory are the theoretical bases for identifying specific switching cost components and developing measurement items in the context of outsourcing relationships. TCT supports much IT outsourcing research (e.g., Apte et al., 1990; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993) as it provides the economic rationale for entering and exiting an exchange relationship. TCT holds that transactions represent the exchange of goods or services between autonomous actors with economic motivations, thus serving as the basic unit of analysis in organizations (Williamson, 1975). TCT represents an economic perspective based on self-interest which motivates an organization to either provide a good or service internally or purchase it externally. This theory underpins the classic make-or-buy decision and is consistently used to evaluate the choice of out sourcing or providing in-house IT services (Willcocks and Lacity, 1995; Aubert et al., 2004). Successful interorganizational relationships depend on the eficient management of economic investments since the goal of organizations is economic eficiency (William son, 1975). TCT provides an appropriate framework for evaluating the economic cost components of outsourcing relationships.

Social exchange theory posits that parties to an exchange value the relationship in behavioral contexts related to satisfaction and mutual goal fulfillment (Thibaut and Kelley, 1959). Thus, non-economic and relational investments also comprise important dimensions of interorganizational relationships. Social exchange theory is the basis for the finding that termination decisions are based on the relational value of a present exchange partner compared to expectations about the relational value of an alternative (Ouchi, 1980). Research also suggests that the nature of service relationships is evolving into a kind of partnership given the complex and competitive provider market (Schifman and Loftin, 1991; Ravichandran and Rai, 2000a,b). In addition to monetary investments, the conceptualization of a ‘partnership’ includes other intangible and relational exchanges (e.g., loyalty and commitment) by both parties.

For example, relational investments consist of communication patterns and trust development among exchange parties (Adams, 1976). Relational assets also exist in the form of skills embodied in people as well as organizational routines and processes (Nelson and Winter, 1982). In a behavioral context, the investment of relationship specific assets between two parties is associated with the continuation of the afiliation (Klein et al., 1978) and long-term partnerships with vendors (Ravichandran and Rai, 2000a,b). Furthermore, relation specific assets develop over time (Levinthal and Fichman, 1988) suggesting their value may be greater for exchange partners of longer duration. Social exchange theory is the basis for identifying specific switching cost factors that encompass relational investments by outsourcing parties.

In sum, exchange relationships are sustained by a combination of economic and social contributions (Stern and Reve, 1980; Gassenheimer et al., 1998). Although TCT maintains that economic factors are the primary motive for sustaining or terminating an exchange relationship (Heide and John, 1988; Williamson, 1991), social exchange theory recognizes that emotional costs and benefits are embedded in economically motivated relationships (Kumar et al., 1995; Williamson, 1991). Systems development studies also support the importance of psychological investments in relationships (e.g., Ives et al., 1983; Ravichandran and Rai, 2000a,b). Hence, this research incorporates TCT and social exchange theory to identify the economic and relational investments in IT outsourcing relationships.

## 3. Components of the switching cost construct

A description of switching cost dimensions in the IT sector has yet to be empirically supported, leaving researchers without a consistent framework on which to investigate the concept. Prior marketing research (Weiss and Anderson, 1992; Jones et al., 2002) is the starting point for identifying eight switching cost factors applicable to the outsourcing context. We incorporate two factors with validated measurement items from Weiss and Anderson (1992): dificulty in hiring and dificulty in upgrading management systems. These factors embody costs related to structural changes that occur in the organization subsequent to contract discontinuation. Other cost factors (e.g., rep revenge and backlash) were unrelated to the outsourcing context of our study. The remaining six cost factors are derived from Jones et al. (2002) and represent both economic and relational costs of switching service providers. In reviewing prior studies, switching cost factors were selected based on specificity and relevance to a service relationship. Furthermore, redundancy among items and the general nature of others (e.g., time, soft assets) precluded their incorporation in the present study. The authors acknowledge the limitations of selecting and adapting items, but believe the factors appropriately support our objective to explore underlying facets of a complex construct and provide a footing for future IS research. The following sections discuss each factor as a first-order component of the switching cost construct in the domain of IT outsourcing.

## 3.1. Uncertainty costs

A major factor in the outsourcing value proposition is the provider’s capabilities and level of performance (Willcocks and Lacity, 2000; Levina and Ross, 2003). Case study analysis indicates that the core competencies of an IT application service provide value to the client (Levina and Ross, 2003). Thus, the value of the outsourcing proposition is directly related to the level of service or performance the client receives. However, uncertainty is created when the performance level of a potential or alternative service provider is unknown (Guiltinan, 1989). Replacing a known level of service with an unknown level gives rise to uncertainty, a relational cost described in social exchange theory when the value of an alternative is based on expectations rather than knowledge. Managers unknowl edgeable about the performance of another provider may hold certain expectations, but the gap between expectations and knowledge represents a risk or cost of switching. Thus, one intangible or relation-based cost in terminating an outsourcing contract is represented by uncertainty in exchanging a known level of performance for an unknown level.

Other research suggests that the heterogeneity of services is potentially related to uncertainty costs (Zeithaml et al., 1985). The more homogenous the service outcomes the less influence uncertainty has on overall switching costs or switching behavior. For example, switching service providers for an automobile oil change is likely to depend more on economic cost perceptions (i.e., price or loss of free service) or rela tional costs (i.e., relationship with mechanic) than performance level. The wide variety of IT outsourcing services available is indicative of heterogeneity and thus a broad range of performance outcomes may exist. Additionally, performance issues in an IT outsourcing decision may be related to compatibility with existing systems or workload that magnify uncertainty. The greater the specificity and uniqueness of the outsourced IT function, the greater the uncertainty cost related to backsourcing or switching vendors. Thus, it is expected that uncertainty cost is an important component of the switching cost construct.

## 3.2. Post-switching behavioral and cognitive costs

Post-switching behavioral and cognitive costs are intangible investments of time and efort related to learning and adapting to new service processes and routines (Jones et al., 2002). According to social exchange theory, the investment of time and efort to coordinate the exchange relationship is a relational asset associated with mutual goal ful fillment. Parties to an exchange must learn the processes and procedures of the other as these represent day-to-day business activities for each party (Nielson, 1996). When the continuance of a contract is questioned, these investments are intangible costs that may prohibit contract dissolution. For example, if parties commit daily operations to specific functions that would require substantial modification if the relationship ended, then the cost of terminating the relationship may be high. Klemperer (1987) suggests that learning and adapting costs are provider-specific. Thus, switching providers does not imply these costs are swapped but that additional investments are required. These costs may be particularly significant in service markets due to the key role that vendors play in dictating service procedures and routines (Jones et al., 2002).

Thus, the decision to discontinue an outsourcing relationship may be costly in terms of the investment of time and efort required to integrate new policies, procedures, and routines. This cost occurs regardless of whether the outsourced activity is backsourced or switched to another vendor. We categorize post-switching behavioral and cognitive costs as a component of the switching cost construct that captures relational investments in the exchange partner.

## 3.3. Set-up costs

Set-up costs generally precede the actual contracted services or backsourcing operations. These costs may include direct expenses or relate to human resource investments and/or acquisitions of durable assets (i.e., machines, production facilities) (Spekman and Strauss, 1986). Set-up costs also include relational investments of time and efort to initiate and establish the exchange relationship. The infrequency of contracting may increase transaction costs due to initial ‘relationship building’ activities (Teng et al., 1995).

Either party may incur fixed assets or human capital (i.e., expertise) costs necessary to customize the contracted services. Thus, transaction costs may increase due to asset specificity in which the investment has poor alternative uses (Teng et al., 1995). Although it is not uncommon for the costs incurred by the vendor (e.g., personnel learning or retooling) to be charged back to the client (Guiltinan, 1989; Jackson, 1985), these investments increase the costs of the exchange relationship. Set-up costs may be high if substantial customization of the service is required. In the IT context, we expect that set-up costs are a specific and distinct component of switching costs. Set-up costs represent initial economic and relational investments in the vendor that may influence the decision to switch vendors or backsource the IT function (Jackson, 1985).

## 3.4. Hiring and retraining costs

Outsourcing may represent an investment in human capital of a specialized nature as companies often outsource to access the technical talent not available in-house (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). One problem that can exist in the market is finding skilled IT employees (Violino and Caldwell, 1998; Murray, 2000). Special-purpose knowledge and expertise create human assets that can raise switching costs if alternative providers lack these skills. Generally, smaller firms cannot aford IT specialists and must rely on external expertise available via outsourcing (Greer et al., 1999). According to TCT, hiring and retraining costs relate to the economic self-interest of the firm and are likely significant in the decision to backsource or switch vendors. This aspect of switching costs may represent a barrier to switching if the necessary expertise is not available.

Since many organizations that outsource systematically reduce the human resources needed to carry out those IT activities, hiring and retraining may represent large financial outlays. Companies that backsource cannot escape these potentially high economic expenses. Similarly, alternative vendors may be unable to secure skilled IT personnel in a tight market with short supply and high salaries (Davis, 1998), and those that do may face high retention costs. Thus, the cost of hiring and/or retraining in-house personnel or new vendors represents an important component of switching cost.

## 3.5. Management system upgrade costs

When a firm converts from an independent sales force to an employee sales force (i.e., backsourcing) a more complex management system is required (John and Weitz, 1989; Jackson, 1985), as the number of in-house employees increases (Weiss and Anderson, 1992). Backsourcing creates changes in overall management practices and routine operations when new employees are brought into the organization. The costs incurred to upgrade the management system may be monetary outlays to acquire additional system assets or human resources, or may be intangible disbursements of time and efort to integrate the assets.

We expect that management upgrade costs are greater in the backsourcing decision; however, the management of new outsourcing arrangements may also require upgrades (i.e., systems or personnel) according to the nature of the services. The rapid pace of technological change suggests that management systems may provide limited benefits over time, and switching vendors may require the firm to change management strategies to keep pace with vendor innovations. Furthermore, switching vendors to achieve greater customization of services may require changes in management practices. We propose that management upgrade costs are an important component of the overall costs associated with changing an outsourcing relationship.

## 3.6. Lost benefit costs

Lost benefit costs represent the loss of both tangible and intangible advantages that accrue and are directly related to continued patronage (Gwinner et al., 1998). These benefits and privileges provide an incentive for the client to remain in a service relationship with the provider (Beatty et al., 1996). In the decision to terminate an exchange relationship, the loss of tangible benefits such as volume-based discounts or frequent flier miles (Maute and Forrester, 1993; Reynolds and Beatty, 1999) represents economic costs related to firm self-interest in TCT. These monetary costs are immediately realized and quantifi able upon contract dissolution and may serve as a barrier to switching.

Intangible benefits are more relation-based and may be imparted through strong interpersonal bonds with the provider resulting in feelings of comfort and trust. Relational assets may also consist of well-formed communication patterns and trust behaviors that emerge from the duration of the relationship (Adams, 1976). According to the complexity or customization of the outsourced activity, providers may grant additional intangible privileges or benefits (e.g., tech time, troubleshooting, and consulting). In social exchange theory, relational investments are part of mutual goal fulfillment such as having needs met (the firm) and retaining clients (the service provider). Thus, the economic and relational value of benefits lost upon contract dissolution represents a significant switching cost component.

## 3.7. Search and evaluation costs

Search and evaluation costs occur prior to the decision to terminate an exchange contract. The IT outsourcing search process is initiated with a formal RFP (request for pro posal) to elicit both internal and external bids (Lacity and Willcocks, 2002). These actions represent transaction costs associated with the make-or-buy decision. Both economic and cognitive resources are expended to gather information to evaluate the alternatives. Search costs include the time and efort to locate alternatives (Lacity and Willcocks, 2000) and extensive cognitive efort may be required to assess viable alternatives to arrive at an informed decision (Shugan, 1980).

Search and evaluation costs may be significant. For example, characteristics such as geographic dispersion, limited alternatives in an area, the intangible nature of services, and the inability to separate production and consumption are likely to increase search and evaluation costs (Zeithaml, 1981; Jones et al., 2002). Hence, these costs are an important facet of the switching cost construct.

## 3.8. Sunk costs

Sunk costs are economic and relational outlays of non-recoverable time, money, and efort invested in the exchange relationship. According to economic theory, considering historic and non-recoverable costs is irrational and only future costs and benefits should be included in decisions (Gaumnitz and Emery, 1980; Howe and McCabe, 1983; Soman and Gourville, 2001). However, sunk costs may represent a psychological cost (Guiltinan, 1989) that managers find dificult to ignore and may complicate the decision making process (Jackson, 1985; Keil et al., 2000).

Prospect theory is often used to explain the efect sunk costs have on decision-making (Whyte, 1986). In situations where sunk costs are incurred and project success in uncertain, prospect theory posits that decision makers continue devoting resources to the project, ‘‘throwing good money after bad’’ (Garland, 1990; Keil, 1995). This suggests a cost related to the psychological attachment of a client to a project (Keil, 1995) and IT research shows some degree of escalation in 30–40% of software projects (Keil et al., 2000).

One-time financial outlays such as deposits or initiation fees may be required upon contracting with an exchange partner (Heide and Weiss, 1995). Additionally, physical assets related to the contract may need replacing if providers are changed or functions are backsourced (Heide and Weiss, 1995). Based on TCT, prior economic transactions and investments are motivated by self-interest to obtain provider services. We expect that these sunk costs represent an important part of switching costs that managers account for in contract termination decisions.

## 4. Methodology

## 4.1. Item development

Scales to measure the switching cost components were derived from previous literature. Items relating to dificulty in upgrading management systems and dificulty in hiring and retraining IT personnel were adapted from Weiss and Anderson (1992). These authors developed, pretested and refined the two scales using the input of manufacturing industry executives.

The items measuring uncertainty, post-switching behavioral and cognitive costs, lost benefit costs, set-up costs, pre-switching search and evaluation costs, and sunk costs were derived from Jones et al. (2002). Their study developed scale items based on in-depth interviews and a review of the relevant literature. The authors evaluated the items for clarity and face validity using marketing faculty and doctoral students in a service sector context. Suitable items were then retested in a second study using non-student responses in the service domain of hairstylists/barbers and banks.

In the current study, 33 scale items derived from Jones et al. (2002) and Weiss and Anderson (1992) were adapted to the applications development outsourcing context and assembled into a survey instrument. The items comprising each first-order factor were modified to capture managerial views of the economic and relational costs that influence decisions to backsource or switch vendors. In general, most items were reworded to change the type of service being investigated (e.g., barbershop/hairstylist to outsourcing vendor or in-house team). The instrument was reviewed by a panel of marketing and IS faculty and tested in two industries to refine the scales. The final result was an instrument consisting of 29 items measuring eight facets of the switching cost construct using a 7-point scale. Different versions of the survey instrument were constructed to reflect slight diferences in wording according to the nature of the outsourcing decision (see Appendix A).

## 4.2. Data collection

The sample base consisted of 6731 executives with a job title indicating a responsibility for application development. The contact information was purchased from the Directory of Top Computer Executives, which has previously been used in other IT research (e.g., Byrd and Turner, 2001; Grover et al., 1996; Rajagopalan et al., 1996; Segars and Grover, 1998).

Two groups of 3000 executives were randomly selected from the overall list of 6731. The first group was sent a cover letter describing the study, a postage-paid return envelope, and a returnable form on which to indicate outsourcing experience within the past 3 years, unwillingness to participate in the study, or lack of experience with outsourcing. For those returning the form and indicating a willingness to participate, a survey instrument was mailed specific to their outsourcing decision. In situations where a respondent indicated experience with multiple outsourcing outcomes, the authors determined which instrument to send to achieve parity in the number of each type of instrument distributed.

The second group of 3000 were sent a packet that included: a response form to indicate ‘‘no interest’’, the survey instruments and a postage-paid return envelope. This alternate method was used to increase the response rate by including the survey in the initial mailing. The cover letter instructed recipients to either return the ‘‘no interest’’ form or complete the survey which best fit their experience in the past 3 years.

A total of 160 completed survey instruments were returned in addition to 26 with incorrect addresses. A total of 840 recipients indicated they would not participate. Of the ‘‘non-participating’’ forms that were returned, 77% indicated a lack of experience with outsourcing, while 23% indicated an unwillingness to participate. Therefore, a total of 1000 sample members responded with either the nonparticipating form or the completed instrument for an overall response rate of 16.7%.

## 4.3. Respondent characteristics

Of the 160 respondents to the survey, the average age was 46.5 years (Table 2). One hundred fifteen were male (72%), while 45 were female (28%). The average length of time employed by the company was 13 years and the average time in the current position was 6 years.

A wide variety of industries were represented in the sample. Manufacturing comprised the largest industry (20%), followed by education (19%), health care (10%), and public administration (9%). On average, the total dollar amount of an outsourcing contract was \$5,565,115, with a wide range (\$1,500–\$89,000,000). Fifty-eight percent of the contracts were for single applications, while 42% were written for multiple applications. Contract length ranged from 10 days to 15 years, with the average at 26 months. A majority of the executives (84%) indicated direct involvement in IT outsourcing decisions while the remainder indicated familiarity with the contract and services of the provider.

## 5. Data analysis

## 5.1. Assessment of the measurement properties

Confirmatory factor analysis was used to assess the measurement properties of the constructs modeled in Fig. 1. Confirmatory analysis allows for the a priori arrangement of the relationships among the eight constructs and their indicators. Using the survey data, we tested the fit of the original 33 items specified to load on the eight dimensions of switching cost. The statistics in Table 3 show the fit of the initial model with the data as well as the threshold values suggested in the literature. The initial model with all items included shows mixed results for model fit.

The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic and the Goodness-of-fit Index (GFI) are absolute indices indicating the ability of the model to reproduce the actual covariance matrix. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic (880.43) is significant which rejects the null hypothesis of covariance matrix equality. The GFI is indicative of poor model fit when compared with the recommended value of 0.90 or greater (Hair et al., 1998). However, the significance of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test may be discounted if the model is overly complex, the sample size is large and other fit tests such as the CFI or Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) provide good approximate fit (Hair et al., 1998). Thus, incremental fit measures and parsimonious fit measures were evaluated to complement the above absolute fit measures.

Incremental fit measures include the Adjusted Goodness-of-fit Index (AGFI), the Normed Fit Index (NFI) and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI) with a value of one indicating perfect fit (Hair et al., 1998). Incremental fit measures compare the specified model to the null model containing no measurement error. AGFI adjusts the GFI by the degrees of freedom between the specified model and the null model. The AGFI obtained is below the 0.80 threshold. However, AGFI is also biased downward when the degrees of freedom are large compared to sample size (Bollen, 1990) which is probable in this case. The NFI reflects the proportion by which the specified model improves fit compared to the null model. CFI is similar in meaning to NFI but penalizes for sample size. Both the NFI and CFI fall below the recommended levels, although CFI is only slightly lower than the suggested minimum.

Parsimonious fit indices (i.e., normed $\chi ^ { 2 } ,$ , RMSEA) evaluate the fit of the model relative to the degrees of freedom required to achieve that level of fit. The normed $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic is an attempt to make model fit less dependent on sample size. Acceptable models may exhibit a normed $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of three or less (Kline, 1998). The RMSEA is a measure of the discrepancy per degree of freedom. The original model has an acceptable normed $\chi ^ { 2 } \ ( 1 . 8 9 )$ and the RMSEA (0.074) is also within the acceptable limits (Hair et al., 1998). However, based on the low level of the absolute and incremental measures, we proceeded to respecify the model.

Model respecification uses an iterative process to refine the scales (Joreskog, 1993). The objective is to locate misspecifications in each factor and separately modify each dimension to meet key reliability and validity criteria (Venkatraman, 1989). This procedure is known as ‘‘piecewise model fitting’’ (Bollen, 1989) and was undertaken to improve the model. The properties tested from each first-order factor include unidimensionality, convergent validity, reliability, and discriminant validity.

Tests for unidimensionality and convergent validity assess if the items measure their specified construct. The elimination of items followed an iterative procedure based on factor loadings and modification indices. Suggested minimum factor loadings at the 0.05 significance level (a) for a sample size of 150 are 0.45, and 0.40 for a sample size of 200 (Hair et al., 1998). This ensures indicator reliability meets or exceeds the 0.50 level of significance. Another method to determine factor loading minimum values is based on a calculation using the critical values for a correlation coeficient at $\alpha = . 0 1$ for a two-tailed test (Stevens, 1992). Thus, for a sample size of 160, only absolute value loadings greater than $2 \times 0 . 2 0 4 5$ (i.e., 0.409) are considered statistically significant. Consistent with Stevens (1992), items with factor loadings less that 0.409 were dropped from consideration. Additionally, we evaluated items with high modification indices that may indicate redundant items, methods bias or the omission of an exogenous factor. In sum, scale changes were made based on each item’s factor loadings, modification indices and the assessment of redundancy among items. Appendix B details the above procedures and the modifications made to the scales. A total of four items were dropped from the original instrument including post-switching cost item #2, hiring and retraining cost items #4 and #5, and sunk cost item #4.

Following the scale refinement phase, we evaluated the internal consistency of each first-order factor by examining the estimates of composite reliability and variance (Hair et al., 1998). Composite reliability represents the extent to which each factor is represented by the items. Variance represents the amount of variance in the indicators explained by the construct. Recommended minimum levels are 0.7 for composite reli ability and 0.5 for variance explained (Hair et al., 1998). Results reported in Appendix A show acceptable values supporting the ability of the indicators to embody each of the eight factors.

Discriminant validity evaluates the distinctiveness of each dimension by assessing the distinctness of the measures for each factor. $\chi ^ { 2 }$ diference tests were performed for each pair of constructs to evaluate discriminant validity as prescribed by Anderson and Ger bing (1988). For each pair in Table 4, the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of the model was compared to the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ of the same model with the relationship between the factors constrained to zero. The two models are comprised of the same items and factors, but a significant diference in $\chi ^ { 2 }$ is indicative of discriminant validity, or distinctness of the measures (Venkatraman, 1989). Twenty-eight pairwise tests were performed with all $\chi ^ { 2 }$ diferences but one significant at the 0.5 level. However, all factor correlations in Fig. 1 are below the 0.90 threshold (Bagozzi et al., 1991) which supports the distinctness of the measures for each construct. Since the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ diference between post-switching costs and sunk costs is significant at better than the 0.10 level and the correlation between the constructs is relatively low (.15), the likeli hood that these factors are perfectly correlated is low. Hence, discriminant validity is deemed acceptable for continued testing of the model.

Following scale refinement and reliability and validity testing, we proceeded to test the fit of the revised model containing 29 items. Each of the model parameters shows improvement (Table 3) with CFI surpassing the threshold and AGFI approaching the suggested minimum. Several measurement items exhibit less than satisfactory factor loadings (Fig. 1) based on the 0.60 lower limit suggested by Chin (1998). These include the uncertainty cost items, although two of the items (UC1 and UC3) are only slightly below the mini mum guideline. The authors acknowledge that timing diferences in the wording of these items may contribute to the lower loadings. However, since low factor loadings do not negate the usefulness of the items to explain some portion of the related dimension and acceptable Cronbach’s a and variance extracted measures were obtained, these items were retained.

The following summarizes the assessment of the first-order model: confirmatory factor analysis tested the original items, modification of the items and respecification of the model was based on factor loadings and modification indices, the internal consistency and discriminant validity of the respecified model was assessed and the revised model was re-tested. Overall, the modified first-order factor model shows marked improvement from the original model. However, the various fit measures again show mixed results. CFI, normed $\chi ^ { 2 } .$ , and RMSEA exceed recommended values, although GFI, AGFI and NFI are below suggested thresholds. Since satisfactory results were obtained for the measurement properties of the scale items and several of the model fit parameters, we proceeded to evaluate the switching cost construct as a second-order factor.

## 6. Discussion

Results indicate that switching cost is a multi-dimensional construct. A construct is multi-dimensional when it relates to distinct dimensions that are also interrelated (Law et al., 1998). The strength of the paths between each first-order factor and the second-order construct are strong ranging from 0.72 to 0.93, with the exception of sunk costs at 0.38 (Fig. 2). This is an unexpected finding given theory and prior research supporting the inclusion of sunk costs as a major factor in switching decisions. However, the association between sunk cost and the construct is statistically significant although the factor itself is the least important. Additionally, the items explain only 15% of the variance in sunk costs suggesting that better measures may improve the strength of the path as well as the fit of the overall model. Nevertheless, all eight factors support the multi-dimensionality of the construct.

Moreover, the findings show that switching cost is most strongly related to uncertainty costs in the application development outsourcing market. Uncertainty costs represent intangible cognitive costs that reflect risk about future performance levels. The results indi cate that a change in switching cost is most strongly indicative of changes in uncertainty costs, followed by changes in pre-switching search costs. As switching costs increase, the risks or costs associated with performance level are most likely to increase relative to the other factors. Uncertainty costs are also strongly correlated with lost benefits and postswitching costs prompting changes in these costs as well. As the model demonstrates, the dimensions are intertwined and the related efects are important in understanding behavior.

## 6.1. Switching cost as a second-order factor

A global measure of switching costs leaves gaps in the understanding of the construct and its afect on interorganizational relationships. Although parsimony is maintained in this context, interpretation of the construct and positioning within larger models is problematic since important variance among first-order factors is neglected. For example, measuring switching costs uni-dimensionally forces managers to combine several facets of the construct, such as combining lost benefits with the costs of retraining. This methodology hampers understanding the individual nature of the drivers influencing switching behavior and their interdependence. Rather than contributing independently to the formation of switching cost, the eight first-order factors demonstrate strong relationships with each other. As the results show, the correlations among the eight first-order factors are governed by the higher-order construct. Thus, any change in switching cost compels change in each of the first-order factors.

Theoretically, the conceptualization of a construct has implications for how it is defined, operationalized and interpreted (Stewart and Segars, 2002). As a higher-order factor, the multi-dimensional construct is a holistic representation of complex phenomena. Although each of the eight factors is individually a strong manifestation of the construct, a change in one factor produces changes in the others. The paths to each of the first-order factors represent factor loadings and are not interpretable from a causal perspective. Thus, the diferential efects of each dimension cannot be measured since changes in the construct afect the first-order factors, not vise versa. However, researchers argue that multi-dimensional constructs allow the modeling of broad predictors with broad outcomes and increase the explained variance (Hanisch et al., 1998).

Additional advantages are gained in a higher-order conceptualization. The satisfactory fit of the model indicates that the switching cost construct has potential to explain the covariation among the first-order factors. One benefit of explaining covariations is to assess if switching cost fully mediates the relationship of the first-order factors when applied in a theoretical model. Further research is needed to position switching cost within larger nomonological networks of behavior.

Porter (1980) suggests that the potential impact of switching costs may be so significant to interorganizational relationships that the afect of all strategic activities on switching costs should be evaluated. This study considers the multi-dimensionality of the construct to further understand its impact on managerial behavior in the IT outsourcing context. Williamson (1975) argues that organizational attachment strengthens over time if the parties invest in specialized equipment or unique processes specific to an organization. Since specialized investments may not transfer to other exchange relationships, parties may feel ‘‘locked-in’’ to the existing relationship. Models of managerial behavior that incorporate the dimensions of the switching cost construct may better explain and predict irrational or ‘‘locked-in’’ relationships.

## 6.2. Limitations and future directions

The constraints of the methodology place limitations on the generalizability of this study. Application development outsourcing comprises the sample base preventing inferences of consistency and external validity in other contexts. Thus, it is important for researchers to test the arguments of this study in other service contexts and applications to explicate the contextual importance of each dimension. Furthermore, the wording of the uncertainty cost items does not specify one certain time period. It is unknown how this may afect the factor loadings and more specific measures may improve the secondorder model.

The next step for this line of research is to extend the switching cost construct into more extensive models of practical importance related to client retention and/or managerial behavior. Future studies may also find it useful to ascertain the conditions under which firms are likely to switch vendors or backsource despite high switching costs.

## 7. Conclusion

As organizations embrace outsourcing of all varieties, the study of switching costs is a topic of intense focus. This study illuminates the complex nature of the switching cost construct and second-order modeling gives insight into the interdependence of factors that are economic and relational. We synthesized previous research, refined and validated measurement scales and tested switching costs at a higher level of abstraction within the context of applications development outsourcing. The framework underpinning the study is both economic and relational supporting prior research involving interorganizational relationships (Hirschman, 1970; Ping, 1997). Overall, the results show that switching cost is a complex multi-dimensional construct that represents the commonality among the eight first-order factors.

Practically speaking, at a higher level the switching cost construct is better able to explain and predict compared to uni-dimensional measures. For example, a one dimensional view may place undue importance on a single aspect of switching costs such as economic costs, not realizing how other cost dimensions (i.e., relational costs) intertwine, and may result in unclear or contradictory outcomes. The extensive growth of IT outsourcing and the propensity to terminate contracts provides fertile ground to further examine switching cost factors and their positioning in larger models of decision-making behavior.

The phenomena of structural inertia in organizations has led economists to evaluate switching cost as a factor related to the disutility or dificulty of changing established business arrangements. Even rational managers may resist changing unsatisfactory practices if the actual or perceived costs of switching appear prohibitive (Caves and Porter, 1977). Since switching costs appear to play a major role in managerial outsourcing decisions, the conceptualization is significant for explaining and predicting managerial behavior. The authors hope this study will spur further interest and research of this important topic in IT service relationships.
