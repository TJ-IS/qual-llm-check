# Research Perspectives: The Quest for Innovation in Information Systems Research: Recognizing, Stimulating, and Promoting Novel and Useful Knowledge

Varun Grover<sup>1</sup>, Fred Niederman<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

Research in information systems (IS) is often challenged during the review process with “what’s new” and the “so what” questions. While we believe that there is innovation in IS research, constituents in the field do not have a good or at least consistent understanding of what this entails. This creates a problem for editors, authors, and reviewers in assessing how innovative a study is or what aspects of the work are indeed innovative. This paper is a response to this concern that takes on the challenging task of recognizing innovation in IS research. At the most basic level, we offer a structure that examines a variety of ways that innovation may be manifested in our research output. We describe, illustrate, and discuss the challenges of using our categories of innovative research. We hope that such identification can stimulate and expand our capacity to generate innovative research and to recognize (and promote) it when it is forthcoming.

Keywords: Innovative Research, Innovative Knowledge, Research Categorization, Breakthrough Research, Research Novelty, Innovative Studies

Dirk Hovorka was the accepting senior editor. This research article was submitted on May 23, 2017 and underwent four revisions.

## 1 Introduction

In recent years, at least a part of the information systems (IS) academic community has questioned whether our field is innovative enough (Grover & Lyytinen, 2015); i.e., whether we are producing work that is required to fit a rather rigid script or binding it to the incremental investigation of rather mundane topics. Given the rate of change in the building, deployment, and use of digital information technologies in organizations and by individuals, it can be argued that corresponding academic fields must show clear signs of innovativeness if they wish to assert leadership rather than merely follow fast-moving trends. In this paper, we seek to frame the concept of innovation in IS research. Our examination is not focused on the question of whether or not IS research is currently innovative. We see strong arguments on both sides. Rather, we believe that the IS field is advanced if more work manifests innovation, is recognized as innovative, and combines to bring new understandings, predictions, and/or tools and techniques to expand our knowledge accumulation. To that end, our goal is to present categories of innovativeness that can not only facilitate development and showcase innovative work, but also influence stakeholder and institutional practices in the field.

## 2 Research Innovativeness

A number of IS scholars have criticized the field as being filled with incremental rather than path-breaking work (e.g., Lyytinen & Grover, 2015, 2014). This concern is mirrored in the management field. Alvesson and Sandberg (2013, 2014) have pointed out that expanding numbers of submissions at more highly competitive journals have produced fewer rather than larger numbers of striking new management ideas. In contrast to management, IS, which is viewed as a sociotechnical field, has the potential to examine (and perhaps anticipate) the outcomes of new technology as they are conceived and implemented. Yet, even with this distinct advantage, the perception of the IS academic field as groundbreaking or innovative is modest. We suspect that this may be, in part, because of a lack of clear exemplars illustrating distinct ways in which our research may be innovative.

It is important to clarify what we mean by innovation. As Alvesson and Sandberg (2013, p. 131) pointed out, “if a theory doesn’t challenge some of an audience’s assumptions significantly, it is unlikely to receive attention and become influential—even if it has been extremely rigorously developed.” According to this view, the mechanics of rigor are not sufficient for a paper to be innovative or even interesting. This perspective maintains that interest is generated by challenging rather than affirming a widely held premise. Davis (1971) described dozens of specific ways to challenge widely held views. A field that never challenges its own assumptions and collective findings may seem “dull,” even if that body of knowledge is productive and increased detail makes it more so. On the other hand, a constant stream of challenges may bring attention to contrasting, paradoxical, even incompatible findings and generate a sense of creative excitement. Both Alvesson and Sandberg (2013, 2014) and Davis (1971) have focused heavily on theory, leaving significant room to add innovation through alternatives such as models, designs, taxonomies, methods, problem solutions, and thick descriptions. One goal of this paper is to delineate the various means through which innovation in IS research can be described.

Individual creativity may be viewed as a useful starting point for considering the nature of innovation. Amabile and Pratt (2016) provide a broad discussion, positioning individual creativity within the setting of organizational innovation. Amabile (1997, p. 40) emphasizes a constrained view of novelty as the key component. She uses the term “appropriate” as the constraining idea regarding novelty; however, the term “useful” provides a more effective connection to broader purpose and thus is better suited for the IS domain. In the corporate setting, it may be taken for granted that ideas that are not useful or profitable will not survive, but in academia, such pressures are not as immediate or powerful and the selfdiscipline of a stronger “usefulness” constraint is thus needed.<sup>1</sup> Therefore, innovative scholarly works are ones that generally display high degrees of both novelty and usefulness (Corley & Gioia, 2011). We note that papers that are clearly novel but not recognized as useful may be stimulating without being innovative, and could be accused of pushing novelty for novelty’s sake. Papers viewed as useful but not novel may provide significant contributions without being innovative.<sup>2</sup>

In this discussion, we focus primarily on the individual study, as exhibited in journal articles.<sup>3</sup> We recognize that innovation is a complex concept, and likely not a clear and unchanging attribute of each article. On the other hand, we find it essential to consider innovativeness as it relates to individual studies because studies are units of knowledge that are assessed by authors, readers, and editors within the broader context of knowledge at a point in time. Authors typically work on one study at a time, couched in a larger research stream. However, readers typically consider and attend to particular studies at one moment in time. Editors and reviewers must make decisions about the papers they receive more or less in the sequence they arrive. Thus, individual studies are benchmarked, evaluated, and marketed against extant knowledge at a particular time. Further, individual articles, which are generally restricted in length, represent a summary of content that scholars and editors consider most important (Constantinides et al., 2012).

What, then, makes the work represented in individual articles innovative? We present three metacategories and 12 categories of innovative IS studies. By offering these categories, our hope is that scholars may examine their own work across these categories to accentuate aspects of innovativeness. Scholars can also use some of the suggestions regarding a category or a combination of categories to further develop their work. Readers (including reviewers and editors) can see more quickly and clearly why and how a particular work can be viewed as innovative. Ultimately, our framing can be used to facilitate identification, stimulate creation, and promote the recognition of key aspects of innovative research. Below, we describe our process and framing of the categories of innovation, followed by a detailed discussion and illustration of each category.

## 3 Process of Building Categories

New approaches have been emerging in recent decades regarding how categories and processes of “categorization” are being used in practice and research. Our approach is consistent with recently proposed views of categorization as fluid rather than as a permanent unchanging typology or taxonomy (e.g., Farjoun, et al., 2015). At one end are studies where the typology itself is viewed as a fundamental and complex aspect of theorizing (Fiss, 2011), and at the other end are studies where the manner in which individuals view and modify views on categories (in their own terms and using their own idiosyncratic criteria) influence their behavior and attitudes regarding institutions, products, and organizations (Durand & Paolella, 2013). The latter group of studies shares the view that categories are fluid and purposeful, rather than rigidly bounded, permanent, and characterized by mutual exclusivity. This view does not eliminate or negate the potential value of traditional taxonomies or typologies, but it does expand the utilization of categorization from a normative to a more descriptive viewpoint, highlighting challenges and opportunities to profit from how agents use categories and categorization in practice.<sup>4</sup>

Our goal is to propose a set of categories that describe the central tendency of distinct approaches that describe existing innovative work. Scholars can identify, reflect upon, engineer, or perhaps reengineer more specific procedures that are helpful to them in furthering innovation in their studies. This is consistent with Glynn and Navis’s (2013) conceptualization of the use of categories to stimulate, “creative recombinations for organizational innovation and change” (p. 1132).

## 3.1 Framing of Innovation Metacategories

We propose three key questions to unearth the central tendencies of the varied ways of showing innovation in research. How could different ways of realigning components result in innovative studies? How might researchers create innovation by moving beyond but not abandoning extant components? And what might it look like to step away from existing components to consider problems and phenomena from a fresh perspective? Specifically, we describe the three metacategories as:

1. Leveraging existing knowledge: The novelty emerges from the discovery of new or hidden facets of existing knowledge. Innovativeness is gained by deriving knowledge through filling important gaps, rearranging the knowledge structure, or looking at the knowledge and/or underlying phenomenon from a different level or stakeholder.

2. Adding new knowledge: The novelty comes from knowledge extension, and it goes beyond the use of existing knowledge components by suggesting additional models, theories, methods, or artifacts not represented in extant knowledge.

3. Changing evaluative frame: the novelty comes from re-visioning by inventing or changing the evaluative frame. This can be accomplished by envisioning new futures, concepts, or shifting perspectives with little regard to existing knowledge components.

The first two of these metacategories resemble and add detail to Weick’s (1989, p. 517-518) differentiation in theory building between knowledge growth by intention and knowledge growth by extension. In the former, more light is brought to illuminate the contents of existing knowledge. In the latter, the boundaries in which the knowledge is viewed as operative are expanded. A third type of innovative study recognizes and pushes new content without explicitly defining itself in terms of extant knowledge or theory.<sup>5</sup>

## 3.2 Proposing the Categories

Based on these concepts, the initial proposal of underlying categories emerged based on the authors’ individual reasoning, understanding, logic, and experience. <sup>6</sup> An iterative process was followed to identify candidate papers and classify them within a metacategory. Individual papers were then examined to identify categories of innovativeness, and the categorization was redefined and modified until stable. Specifically, the authors sought to illustrate the central tendency of each category through individual papers that could serve as examples. As part of the process, each author proposed articles based on their experience, which highlighted novelty and/or usefulness and exemplified that particular category. No rigid definition of either novelty or usefulness was preordained in order to embrace papers that were immediately clear, relative to these characteristics or, alternatively, where these values emerged over time. Once proposed, each article was discussed among the authors, with some being discarded as overly ambiguous relative to the category. This iterative process helped us refine our understanding of both the nature of innovation as applied to knowledge work as well as the conceptualization of the categories. Because of our sampling of IS papers, there are some categories that could be viewed as idiosyncratic to IS, but we believe that most of the categories are broadly applicable to other business fields.

We acknowledge subjectivity and judgment in assessing whether a particular study is innovative.<sup>7</sup> We offer the examples in this study as works that we (the authors) find to be relatively innovative, illustrating the central tendency in the categories described. We are not concerned with whether we identified the “most” innovative study in that category, but whether the example illustrates the central tendency of the category in a clear manner. We note that others may identify additional examples for each category or subcategory as well as examples that could be logically included in multiple categories.

In the categories presented below, we address both academic and practical aspects of each innovation type (Corley & Gioia, 2011). We introduce these categories as a way to open rather than constrain innovative thinking. We acknowledge that individual studies can straddle multiple categories and that judgments regarding both novelty and usefulness will vary over time and according to the viewpoint of the evaluator. However, we propose that the more a study is perceived as exhibiting both novelty and usefulness, the more innovative it will be.

## 4 Categories of Innovative Studies

In this section, we describe 12 categories of innovative studies that fall within the three metacategories: deriving, extending, and re-visioning. Deriving, essentially leaves components pertaining to a domain intact but seeks to rearrange them or see them differently. Extending, is additive in that it uses new components to extend knowledge. Re-visioning seeks to reframe or reorient the content of a domain, perhaps looking at it from a substantially different perspective or inventing a new sort of domain not previously mapped.

## 4.1 Deriving Innovation from Extant Work

Below we describe five types of deriving approaches to creating innovative studies (see Table 1a-e). These are filling literature gaps, filling problem gaps, bricolage, differentiating subpopulations, and changing the level of analysis/stakeholder. We see each of these as mostly exploitative,<sup>8</sup> characterized by heavy use of existing literature and/or knowledge about the phenomena but pushing in one way or another toward a more detailed or invigorated view of the knowledge area, primarily within existing boundaries and scopes of interest.<sup>9</sup>

Identifying and filling a literature gap may or may not be innovative. Alvesson and Sandberg (2013) contrast literature gap-filling to work that has more impact and innovativeness. The real question is whether filling the gap offers intrinsic value beyond simply filling the gap for its own sake. When the gap is easily deduced from an existing framing or items surrounding it, there is little novelty in the identification of the gap. Further, even when a novel gap is recognized, it is the researcher’s obligation to demonstrate why there would be value in filling it. The next gap among a series (for example additional cells in a logical matrix created by defining two or more dimensions) can make a contribution—indeed it can certainly serve to bolster our confidence in the overall framework, extend the boundary over which a theory can be applied, and/or integrate consistent concepts across domains—but it still may not be particularly innovative. To be viewed as innovative, the gap should address a real problem related to practice or to the fundamental understanding of the domain. The knowledge<sup>10</sup> gained from filling such a gap, in other words, should be important to at least one identifiable and important stakeholder.

In parallel with the literature gap, filling the problem gap may also focus on an important real-world problem that does not have a documented correspondence in the knowledge world. Historically, practical problems have been relegated to practitioner outlets and not given high credence in the academic community. However, usefulness that is instrumental to innovativeness can stem from practical as well as academic utility (Corley & Gioia, 2011; Van de Ven, 2007). If these studies deal with pressing societal problems and lead to an understanding of the problem and new or improved solutions or understanding, particularly regarding IT artifacts (Markus, 2014), then they are likely to add novelty and useful ideas to the knowledge base. The novelty derives largely from the addition of new approaches and insights to represent the underlying problem. Usefulness, while integral to the problem itself, may vary with the difficulty and pervasiveness of the problem and will also be affected by the degree to which the proposed solution addresses it. The new approach may reinterpret a previously intractable problem, may solve a significant part of it, may illustrate an approach that can be expected to lead to solutions for related problems, and/or may demonstrate a promising but untested problem solution. This last point represents the danger that an author may be addressing an emergent problem before its domain has been thoroughly mapped, where constructs and relationships remain fuzzy and only partly defined. Or the problem may be highly idiosyncratic to a context and result in “consulting-like” solutions rather than knowledge that can transcend the context. The issue for the scholar in such an environment is balancing novelty and potential usefulness with the reasoning and argumentation that render the approach credible in the academic realm (Hirschheim, 2008). It is also worth considering the emergence of studies in data analytics and big data as an avenue for uncovering otherwise obscure relationships regarding practical problems through ad hoc exploration (experiments) that may result in the effective surfacing of new models and theory for subsequent refinement and testing. In many cases, the data sets themselves come from businesses and are novel based on website scraping or electronic touch points—leading to identification of relationships that help resolve practical problems.

Bricolage involves rearranging extant knowledge in search of new insights. For instance, a literature review can organize a substantial body of research by structuring previously unorganized literature. The structure itself can add value by, for instance, presenting an arrangement that facilitates a new way of looking at individual items within the literature. Novelty may also come from grouping study instances such that synergy is created in the combination. For example, an integration of studies from economic and behavioral perspectives regarding IT personnel might lead to nontraditional ways of understanding the interaction of incentives, productivity, and attitudes.

Creating an innovative study based on bricolage faces a number of challenges. One is that the underlying literature itself may be so insubstantial that, even after clever arrangement of its parts, too much remains unresolved. The timing of such rearrangement is critical to ensure enough material but not so much that the area becomes overly complex and intractable. There is also a danger that, in arranging what is known, the result may be little more than restating the obvious. The researcher won’t necessarily know at the outset whether the process of rearranging content will lead to new insights. We anticipate that successful bricolage studies will tend to arise accidentally as scholars look for but cannot find prior knowledge to address important research questions.

Another approach to derivative innovation is to distinguish between different subsets of a population. In this case, aggregation may hide knowledge within the existing phenomenon. For example, consider IT workers. This population varies from the most technical network architects to the most content-oriented web designers. The skill sets may barely overlap, the tools used may be almost entirely different, and the cultural mindset may vary more markedly than, for example, that between network architects and electrical engineers or web designers and marketing managers. A monolithic treatment of this population may obscure attitudinal or behavioral differences. In some cases, subpopulations could have effects that cancel each other out when aggregated. To reveal these differences, one approach would be to add more variables to the model; however, it may be more direct and helpful to simply focus investigation on each subpopulation.

Subpopulation novelty can emerge from splitting a population along any number of dimensions, including ones that may be influential though not necessarily obvious. Usefulness, of course, will depend on the economic, social, and network value of either or both subpopulations. Career path understandings for infrastructure architects (a subpopulation of IT workers) may help organizations encourage more people to specialize in this strategically important employment category. It may also help with their retention.

Dangers for scholars attempting to draw new subpopulations arise from the possibility that even if the differences are very real on one attribute, they may not matter on another attribute of greater concern. Infrastructure architects and web designers may have significant differences in preferred communication style, but both might benefit from being assigned to fewer projects simultaneously. Technical evolution (for example the ability to run packaged software on either Apple or Windows operating systems) may eliminate choices, again making population differentiation irrelevant.<sup>11</sup>

By changing levels of analysis or stakeholders, scholars have another opportunity to create innovative studies. We combine the two ideas of level of analysis and stakeholder because one frequently implies the other. Changing the level of analysis from individual to organization implies also changing stakeholders from, for example, workers or customers to company or community.

Papers that focus on multilevel constructs in management and IS (e.g. Morgeson & Hofmann, 1999; Niederman et al. 2006; Belanger et al., 2014) emphasize that, within a hierarchy of organizational phenomena, the effects at one level may or may not be consistent with those at other levels and that actions at one level influence outcomes at another. Multilevel work opens possibilities for theorizing across multiple dimensions, suggesting new explanations— particularly where effects at one level offset or are emergent at another level. This theorizing could follow multiple genres (e.g., systems theory combined with complex adaptive systems) and enable complementary theories to work concurrently.

Similar to level of analysis, changing stakeholders may show that observations from one perspective do not necessarily translate across viewpoints. For example, much e-commerce research takes a vendor perspective on building trust, getting more participation, or generating more sales. However, from a consumer perspective, these inquires may not address whether vendors deserve trust or whether participants spend their time more wisely online than in face-to-face encounters or enhance their lives by acquiring more things more easily. Like other research genres, adding multiple stakeholder perspectives may create value without necessarily being innovative.

## 4.2 Extending Approaches to Innovative Studies

We have identified four types of extending approaches toward creating innovative studies (see Tables 2a-d): extending models, technology extension, using different methods, and using different theories. Each of these represents a balance of exploitative and exploratory approaches, characterized by the acknowledgment of current knowledge but moving substantially beyond the status quo. Each of these requires moving outside the boundaries of extant knowledge regarding an IS phenomenon or domain.

Extending current models can provide new and innovative insights. However, these studies often tend to seem incremental, given that they are generally adding new components to an existing model. To demonstrate substantial innovativeness, scholars should show the degree to which the extension changes thinking between the old and new model in a novel way and helps to solve problems or provide new insights into the underlying phenomenon.

More specifically, novelty can occur by expanding the nomological network—in particular, by adding precursors, reconfiguring the relationships, and/or extending the model into new contexts. For example, within the technology acceptance model (TAM) there can be novelty in demarcating antecedents to usefulness; alternatively, the directionality of relationship may be explored by suggesting that various types of success can lead to higher quality information or systems reversing DeLone and McLean’s model (1992) or by considering how a model developed in one culture holds up when used elsewhere (Watson et al., 1994; Tan et al., 1995; Im & Straub, 2015).

Innovativeness in model extension is improved if the changes make a significant difference and display demonstrable novelty and usefulness. Does it really make a difference if a new nomological net explains another percent or two of R-squared? Does it really matter if there is a statistical difference between those in one group averaging a 4.3 response on a Likert scale and those in another group averaging a 4.2 response? On the other hand, developing a superior model of training processes to more quickly help enterprise system users become familiar with a reasonably full range of features suggests demonstrable economic benefits.

In building a cumulative tradition of research, model changes are common in IS research. A potential danger is that too fine an incrementalism can relegate these changes to academic exercises that lead to little advancement in knowledge or practice. Therefore, the usefulness of model changes is important. Also, more innovative model changes can affect the trajectory of research in the area through new theories or methods needed to accommodate the revised model.

Technology extension studies in IS involve examination of the evolution of technology and its effects. This may take the form of observing technology as it develops or actively creating or promoting new technology. This includes the broader notion of the impact of evolving technology regarding sponsors, users, data, procedures, and affordances. Such studies may include observing emergent characteristics of stable technology applied in new settings or for new purposes.

Reacting to new or changing technology can trigger a host of questions regarding how that technology creates direct and indirect effects among those using it, new problems that can be solved (or new solutions to existing problems), and the organizational and societal adjustments that result. Challenges and opportunities for such research exist across levels of generality. Hardware and software products tend to move through various versions and upgrades. Ironically, the successful investigation of the strengths and limits of technology may hasten the development of new versions, rendering the initial investigation more historic than of timely value. Such evolution presents the opportunity to investigate whether there is a base of features or affordances that remains constant as the technology evolves, whether there is a general structure guiding the direction and nature of such change over time, and whether there are common responses by individuals, organizations, or society at large across various technology advances.

Demonstrating novelty relative to technology change may be a matter of degree. A shift from version “x” to version “y” of a software package may add little other than modest changes to existing features or it may provide a revolutionary new interface. On the other hand, a sequence of small changes may reach a “tipping point” where the sum of minor changes add up to a de facto redesign. Sometimes the degree of change is in the eye of the beholder (and the ability to rebrand). For example, blockchain (Beck, 2016), may be viewed as a new concept creating a cascade of opportunities for new applications and even the reorganization of industries, or it may be viewed as an incremental set of changes based on an underlying distributed database (Niederman et al., 2017).

Applying another theory base to an IS area or domain, often one thought to be well understood, can provide a fresh, alternate perspective. Additionally, multiple theoretical perspectives can be applied simultaneously in studies. Researchers can contrast multiple theoretical accounts by testing their relative fit with the actual data. By their nature, IS phenomena tend to cross-disciplinary borders (Tarafdar & Davison, 2018). As a result, it is often viewed as standard practice to introduce reference discipline theory into an IS context (e.g., Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). In some cases, this may generate research that is both novel and useful, but it also raises some cautionary signals. These include (1) the use of IS as a background for the study of other disciplines with a modest or no contribution to IS per se, (2) the discovery of little that is new or useful beyond asserting that the theory seems to apply in the IS domain, (3) the misuse or misunderstanding of the reference discipline theory, and (4) the lack of engagement with the origin and debates on the theory in the reference discipline, leading to misapplication.

Naturally, the use of alternative theory does not guarantee innovativeness. The novelty of appropriating reference discipline theory is likely challenging it based on the unique nature of the IS phenomenon or applying it to the phenomenon in an unexpected way. Following Joshi (1991), a widely held view of user resistance to introducing new technology may be challenged with a different explanation derived from an alternative discipline. In this way, appropriating such theory may serve to broaden the assumptions and understandings of common IS phenomena. Usefulness may also be an issue. Does adding theory from another discipline genuinely solve or reflect a deeper understanding of IS problems or does it simply transfer the same theoretical frame into a new context? Contrasting behavioral and economic explanations of adoption behavior may, for instance, produce a shift from ignoring costs to ignoring human choice in models that show similar amounts but different types of explanation concerning human/technology interaction. Integrating theories offers novelty if they are not used additively by cherry picking constructs from each theory rather than actually engaging the theories and discovering reasons for theoretical tensions. Ultimately, it is critical that the new theoretical lens offers a platform for future research that is different from the past trajectory.

Using different methods, particularly for studying well-known phenomena that have been tested mostly in a limited way, can provide innovative outcomes. In the natural sciences, theories frequently require particular instruments for their testing (Popper, 1963). In fact, such theories may be unimaginable before new instruments bring phenomena into the range of visibility. However, it can be difficult to discern whether findings are a reflection of “reality” or only a function of the instrument itself. Thus, findings that withstand examination through multiple “instruments” should generate greater confidence than ones that do not.

The straightforward case where results are replicated using different methods provides evidence that the initial understanding is not the by-product of a particular method. For example, if constructs showing strong association in TAM studies are also found to be influential in a series of case studies or economic simulations, this suggests that the compression of complex issues like “usefulness” into a set of questions in a survey would also be recognizable when technology users are interviewed and conversations are free flowing. This, in turn, would strengthen confidence in theories asserting that usefulness, ease of use, and the other precursors influence the intention to use technology. Thus, the theory itself should be open to testing across the entire range of methodologies based on the imagination and ability of the researcher. However, using a different method per se, would not necessarily make it an innovative study, though we stress that such a study could certainly have substantial incremental and replication value comprising important research components of a healthy and dynamic discipline.

In cases where variation in methods produces contradictory or ambiguous findings, the constructs, appropriateness of each measure, and the strength of causal or correlational influences may be challenged, thus yielding new insights and questions. Improvements in the means of executing particular methods can introduce new life to emerging or previously intractable problems. More powerful tools or advanced procedures offer discovery of contingencies or levels of granularity in stated findings.

Using unexpected or modified methods can offer novelty if the method (1) addresses a problem that has not been effectively addressed in the field and/or (2) alleviates some weakness of other methods in addressing a known problem. Variables or constructs that look stable when formulated from one sort of methodological approach may not behave in the same way when other approaches are applied. For example, interview respondents may well be able to provide consistent answers to written items, such as whether top management support is important to success, but may give varied answers to what top management support means. Also, while the average may be stable it can differ dramatically from project to project.

Contrast in methods may provide stronger results when spanning the qualitative-quantitative divide. For example, by using a grounded theory methodology rather than the typical survey to examine technology adoption, Abraham et al. (2013) discovered that evolutionary psychology provides an alternative view of phenomena with an arguably better fit with extant data. This method is useful if (1) it produces results that are inconsistent with prior results indicating that prior knowledge and/or methods need to be reassessed, or (2) adds support and confidence in critical and important knowledge domains. The researcher can more clearly show such utility if the new results pertain to an issue of significance and lend new or more effective ways to address the underlying issue(s).

Today, big data and analytical techniques (e.g., machine learning) are now accessible and open up possibilities for scholars to address new questions or reexamine existing ones with the potential for new insights. The exposition of such novel methods, however, is part of the equation, along with demonstrating possibilities for generating useful topical insights. Newer qualitative methods, such as deconstruction (Chiasson & Davidson, 2012) demonstrate how consideration of text as a basis through which reality is created and artifacts and processes are interpreted may generate new insights.

## 4.3 Re-visioning Approaches to Innovative Studies

We identify three types of re-visioning approaches that can create innovative studies (see Tables 3a-c). We see each as mostly exploratory, characterized by the light use of existing literature and/or domain knowledge but pushing vigorously beyond the status quo and the boundaries of accumulated knowledge in IS. As we move from exploitation to exploration, the terrain becomes fuzzier, with fewer benchmarks for assessing contribution. In effect, benchmarks that are too clear may ironically indicate that the work does not do enough to open up new research space. It stands to reason that a genuinely new idea will not have the precedents necessary to fully support it. As a result, it is challenging to come up with arguments that convince fellow scholars of the value of these ideas. We expect that showing novelty and potential usefulness will be critical in gaining acceptance for these studies. We propose three approaches to “revisioning”: namely, future orientation, “blue ocean” transformations, and perspective shifts.

Future-oriented studies anticipate new states and the issues or concerns likely to emerge. These approaches are based on the premise that the knowledge of future possibilities may help guide current decisions, research, and actions. We can identify four families<sup>13</sup> of techniques oriented toward the systematic examination of the future—(1) those related to forecasting, including the Delphi technique, (2) those related to scenario building based on extant ideas, (3) those related to speculative design (we would add envisioning the confluence of multiple trends), and (4) radical reimagining including a sort of “future perfect” practice of visualizing desired future states and considering steps that would increase or decrease the probabilities of them being experienced (Hovorka & Peter, 2018).

These techniques are not limited to projecting progress assuming that “things continue as they are going.” Scholars may investigate alternative patterns, such as cycles, exponential rates of change, or step functions. Gartner Group’s famous “hype cycle” presents a predictable pattern of the growth, decline, and leveling off of new technologies. Since this pattern represents a useful but largely untested template, it would be of value to empirically test and improve on futureoriented technology approaches. Niederman et al. (2016) present a cycle following disruptive platform technologies of the growth of technical skills, interface skills, then application skills relative to new technologies.

Alternatively, studies may consider the implications of the interaction of multiple trends. The confluence of business analytics and geographic information systems has already opened up new arrays of affordances but also new ethical questions. Additionally, a valuesoriented approach could be used to consider scenarios of alternative future conditions.

For decades, a small number of IS researchers have used Delphi techniques to generate predictive knowledge (Skinner, et al., 2015). This is being extended with multiple crowdsourcing techniques within organizations, among expert groups, and for the general public.

Thus, observing digital experiments conducted by forward-thinking companies suggests a promising avenue for generating interesting models of the future.

Walsham (2012) promotes the idea of stimulating future social benefits from IS research. What is new in his argument is a shift in attention from the creation of particular technologies to their potential for advancing the future of positive social outcomes, particularly in the developing, world. Hovorka and Peter (2018, p.

Chiasson et al. (2018) lay out a philosophical basis for investigating the effects of current actions regarding the future. They summarize the relevant philosophy of Feenberg as follows:

Feenberg’s philosophy of technology provides a comprehensive, technologycentred, critical foundation for the epistemic assumptions evident in FS: a focus on multiple possible sociotechnical futures (potentialities) realised through the actions and policies of human actors (instrumentalisation of primary and secondary qualities of technology) in relation to the interests and values brought to bear in technology design and implementation (technical codes, technical rationality, democratic rationality). (p. 372)

Such a view can be brought to IS through borrowing from the discipline of future studies. Slaughter (1998) describes how future studies has evolved and is being recognized for its contributions, focusing primarily on tools and approaches within organizations to address their own strategic planning. However, it is not difficult to project this approach across the various levels of analysis of interest to IS, including individual tools, affordances, groups, organizations, and society writ large.

Chiasson et al. (2018) note that, realistically, there are substantial barriers to widespread acceptance of future studies within IS. These include the challenge of mastering critical and other analytic approaches by authors and the ability to evaluate such work by reviewers and editors. For this work to be credible and not summarily dismissed as speculation, the study must provide a basis for verisimilitude. Slaughter (1998) outlines various tactics for creating useful views of the future. He presents an overview of both human and technical issues in environmental scanning, along with criteria for strategic application—for example, that such projections be appliable by decision makers and open up “what Hamil and Prahalad (1994) call ‘future competitive space’” (p. 383). Slaughter is clear that such future studies are not behavioral science (implying that the typical rules such as reliability and validity are therefore not applicable) and are not predictions or forecasts testing whether or not they are indeed consistent with future observations. Rather the purpose of future studies research is to effectively provide guidance and influence for policy makers and action takers. As such, the integration of futureoriented studies may be challenging for many authors, reviewers, and editors in the IS domain, particularly those steeped in and committed to the rules defining quality behavioral science research work.

Future studies can indeed display novelty by extrapolating from previously underconsidered trends, recombining those that might not be intuitively viewed together, and developing realistic guidelines for moving toward positive future scenarios and away from negative ones. The usefulness of such work may derive from the potential for the domain being studied to generate significant benefits or dangers and the degree to which it presents actionable steps for IS leaders in practice or academia.

Blue ocean transformational studies open new territory, typically through challenging assumptions; changing rules, standards, and vocabulary; and examining the domain with a new template.<sup>14</sup> The blue ocean approach represents a transformational reconsideration of previously held interrelated views. The concept is described in detail by Kim and Mauborgne (2005), who present examples of deconstructing the attributes of particular businesses and reconsidering how reimagined relationships among components may offer a very new vision of what consumers may value. Examples emphasize industries in which consumers are willing to accept fewer high priority features at lowered cost. The net effect, however, is a rethinking and restructuring of what was thought possible regarding that industry or product. Straub (2009) takes a broad view of what constitutes enough novelty to constitute a “blue ocean,” discussing the concept as a continuum. While we appreciate new, stimulating ideas regardless of their scope, we reserve the “blue ocean” category for studies that prompt larger shifts of understanding for a particular domain.

For example, adding sensors to a refrigerator to predict new inventory needs may be a good example of an innovative application, whereas considering the interconnection of such appliances into a remote total household management dashboard is more likely to change the way we think of housing and has the potential to initiate a revolution in home building and generate new capabilities based on unanticipated interactions among household components.

This type of research is likely to include a significant amount of deductive and integrative theorizing. Dubin (1969, p. 238) suggests that “every good theorist is an inveterate collector of facts, with an especially welldeveloped sensitivity to their interrelationships.” Whether or not blue ocean conceptualizations follow the procedures laid out by Kim and Mauborgne (2005), the resulting analysis may present the industry, product, or idea in a way that has not been previously been brought into the public domain.

Initiating a full perspective shift may be more than can be expected from any particular study. It represents a looser form of the “paradigm shift” popularized by the work of Kuhn (e.g., Lakatos, 1970; Feyerabend, 2010). We use the term perspective here as something broader than a new theory or a method, more akin to a different discipline or subdiscipline with topics, vocabulary, philosophy, assumptions, and methods that are far better understood within the discipline than outside it (Hassan & Mingers, 2018). Its existence may be rather invisible such that other disciplines may not recognize or appreciate the working within such a worldview. Given our pluralistic leanings, we see the potential value for working within a perspective to extend and elaborate a school of thought, but we also see value in the coexistence and perhaps coevolution of such perspectives that influence each other and grow together to push each other to become more sophisticated and provide more value. Boland and Tenkasi (1995) describe knowledge communities that have their own language, communication, and cognition. They argue that in order for knowledge firms to achieve product success, they have to engage in “perspective taking” which requires “exchange, evaluation and integration” of knowledge across these communities. Similarly, the IS field can benefit from incorporating different perspectives. This is challenging since perspectives are distinct and may require appreciation of conflicting views that appear logically incompatible.

In the IS field, for quite some time, qualitative or interpretive versus quantitative or empirical perspectives were distinct and considered to have incommensurate philosophical assumptions. Lee (1991) illustrated how the use of interpretive methods (e.g., hermeneutics) can inform positivist methods (e.g., experiments) and vice versa. This groundbreaking article provided impetus for numerous subsequent studies crossing perspective boundaries by demonstrating the value of integrating qualitative and quantitative approaches. Alternatively, differing assumptions, for example, of the primacy of efficiency (e.g., efficient markets) versus the primacy of social values (e.g., community growth or well-being) may be used to contrast the planning, execution, and evaluation of particular IS projects from proposal to completion. Here, there are opportunities for groundbreaking insights emerging from viewing problems that are intractable from a different perspective. As Satell (2015) points out, many problems are solved when experts from different disciplines apply what is well-known in their own field.

Whether examination from a multiperspective position or from outside a single perspective suggests conflict or complementarity, we anticipate that it potentially illuminates new questions and exposes new facets of existing problems. Theories could offer new perspective shifts if they are structured along different lines. For instance, variance theory is quite distinct from process theory or economic modeling and will likely generate contrasting assumptions, research questions, tests, findings, and interpretations (Niederman & March, 2019). The problems viewed as paramount in terms of behavioral, design science, and economic research may be different but the engagement of each perspective could certainly foster novelty and usefulness regarding the understanding of IS phenomena. In a sense, each individual perspective offers distinctiveness in terms of proposing theoretical statements and propositions, as well as varied methods for gathering, analyzing, and presenting findings. Usefulness, however, will most likely be manifested if perspectives are engaged with each other. This suggests that the phenomenon of interest ought to be of sufficient complexity and interest to merit investigation from multiple perspectives likely to yield new understanding, identify new paradoxes, and/or open a new domain that is derived from the interaction between perspectives rather than from a dominantly used perspective.

## 5 Implications of Framing Innovativeness

In summary, we have identified three metacategories of innovative studies—namely deriving, extending, and re-visioning—that loosely progress from heavier to lighter use of extant knowledge. We have described and illustrated 12 specific categories, highlighting the central tendency of each. Below, we first discuss some major implications of our conceptualization of innovativeness. We then discuss specific ways in which our framing can guide scholars and editors/reviewers.

## 5.1 Emphasizing Novelty and Usefulness

Since an underlying tenet of our framing is that innovation can exploit existing knowledge or explore new knowledge, it is important for scholars to be explicit regarding the relationship between what they propose and what is generally known, believed, or assumed.

Such a contrast offers readers a link to something familiar while also emphasizing what is novel in the study. By showing how the new knowledge rearranges, extends, or pivots away from prior understandings, authors can demonstrate novelty and create the opportunity to explicitly address how and/or why there is or potentially may be usefulness in such a view. Thus, clearly demonstrating novelty and linking it to a demonstration of usefulness is key for innovativeness. Usefulness sets the boundary conditions for novelty.

How, then, is usefulness demonstrated? It has been common practice in IS research to buttress the back ends of papers with implications for research and practice. However, in many cases, the implications for research regurgitate the front-end setup of the study— at best offering more discussion about novelty and less about the usefulness of the research. Implications for practice often lack specificity and provide high-level social implications (particularly for organizations /behavioral research). We would argue that a more holistic treatment of implications for research based on our conceptualization would identify the categories of novelty that make the work distinct and then highlight the usefulness of these distinctions. This could be done by delineating how the identified novelty offers new insights, concepts, issues, or questions, thereby setting a different path for the research stream.<sup>15</sup> Similarly, implications for practice should problematize the research question by identifying a specific practicebased stakeholder and then delineate how the novelty addresses the solution to the problem in as much specificity as the research permits.

## 5.2 Emphasizing Innovative Theory

Another important implication of our framing pertains to theory. Theory and its role in the IS body of knowledge have been and continue to be the subject of significant and heated debate (e.g. Hirschheim, 2019; Hovorka et al., 2019; Grover & Lyytinen, 2015). Theory also directly or indirectly touches upon all the categories of our framework. Transforming observed patterns of IS use, development, diffusion, and effects into testable statements of theory, particularly when applied to infrastructural, configurational, combinatorial, networked, and data manifestations of digital technology offers a clear path to innovative contributions to the IS body of knowledge.

There are three logical approaches to fostering innovative theory. First, as is common practice, we can borrow theories from other disciplines and apply them to IS phenomena. However, simply borrowing theory without any rethinking of its constructs, configuration, or logic may result in simply providing a new context for extant theory. Innovative theorizing involves greater theoretical fidelity with the phenomenon. Many digital phenomena are unique and could challenge theories and their assumptions. Therefore, engaging in a two-way interaction between the theory and phenomenon would not only enable novel theorizing but could also be useful where there is faithful treatment of the IS phenomenon.

Second, innovative theory can emerge from imaginative thinking. Unfortunately, in the IS field, such theorizing may be difficult to push through the review process. Some reviewers and editors may demand grounding of the ideas in prior research, which would dilute the novelty, or demand testing or “proof,” which could narrow the scope and impact of the theorizing. Our view is that literature should be neither a necessary condition nor an impermeable barrier to innovation. The literature can be framed in a way that identifies novelty but it can also constrain truly new ideas. However, if we could open space in existing journals or even add a new journal that counters these tendencies and allows conceptualization involving more imagination and less grounding, then publishing such work could stimulate new lines of inquiry.

Third, novel theory could be derived from data, inductively, through, for example, qualitative grounded theory approaches or, more recently, through “big data” analytics. While qualitative inductive approaches are well established, exploring massive and diverse (big) data sets in search of useful insights is a more recent phenomenon. Such digital “trace” data coupled with advanced analytics and machine learning algorithms can develop strong predictive models. In some cases, the results of these analytic processes can provide value based on the idiosyncrasies of the data set from which they were obtained. Such findings may be useful for solving immediate and nonrecurring problems, often of a tactical nature but of less use in theory building (Grover et al. 2020). However, in other cases they can lead to generalization of the form: “if this phenomenon is observed in X setting, we can expect it to also occur across a range of Y settings.” Such inductively derived general statements can emerge from data exploration and be structured as theory for further testing and refinement. Another useful path to innovative theory building through big data could stem from anomalies that cannot be explained (i.e., curious relationships) and may lead to abducing theoretical explanations.

In sum, work that significantly shifts extant theory, offers fresh theory, or abduces patterns from interesting data can indeed be both novel and useful. We advocate a respectful and prominent role for theory in the IS research domain but recognize that blindly demanding theory as a condition of publishing can lead to unintended destructive behaviors such as referencing theory for legitimacy over insight.

## 6 Using the Framework

In the section below we shift our attention from the individual paper to the stakeholders of IS research. We begin by noting a few areas where we feel the framework as a whole might be inappropriately used. Then, we turn to three groups of stakeholders— authors/scholars, reviewers/editors, and institutions such as universities and college ranking practitioners.

## 6.1 Some Caveats

Having proposed this framework of metacategories and categories, we intend for it to be used in a way that is helpful to scholars, reviewers/editors, and other stakeholders. Below, we express a few cautions regarding the use of the categories of innovations and the relationships among them.

• The categories themselves do not indicate innovativeness

• A study that touches on more categories is not necessarily more innovative

• Some categories are naturally related to each other

First, the categories themselves are not an indicator of innovativeness. For instance, a category like “blue ocean,” which falls under re-visioning, would not necessarily be more innovative than “filling a literature gap,” which falls under deriving. The latter could be far more innovative in terms of novelty and usefulness than the former, which might possess novelty but may have had insufficient opportunity to demonstrate usefulness. Second, a study that touches on more categories is not necessarily more innovative than one that touches on fewer categories. For instance, greater innovation depth in one category (e.g., different methods) that clearly demonstrates novelty and usefulness, could be more innovative than a study that incrementally improves in terms of stakeholders, theory, or method. Thus, it is most useful to think of the categories as exemplars of innovativeness. It would be foolhardy to claim that they represent a precise gage of the extent of innovativeness, since that is a judgment made by individuals or collectives (i.e., a review team) based on their bounded rationality. However, we envision that studies generally would have a profile of innovativeness that is stronger in terms of some of the dimensions (categories) than others. Third, relationships between some of the categories may be stronger than others. For instance, it is difficult to envision examining a phenomenon from a different level of analysis (e.g., studying knowledge accumulation based on individual absorptive capacity rather than organizational absorptive capacity) without bringing in or creating a new theoretical base. Similarly, changing evaluative frames or perspectives may also involve new theories and methods. While deducing specific relationships between categories is beyond the scope of this study, we encourage future empirical examination of categories and metacategories.

## 6.2 Application of the Framework

How can the framework be used? We view it as a tool for identifying, fostering, and evaluating innovation. This, of course, cannot be done in any mechanistic fashion since each category is nuanced and has strong elements of individual judgment. However, what we do not want it to be is an obligatory passage point for justifying innovativeness.

As individual researchers, reviewers, and editors, we see the value of the framework in explicating the dimensions of innovativeness and fostering deeper thought on the possibilities for pushing the boundaries of innovation. As a grid, it offers diagnostic opportunities prompting researchers to think through the novelty and usefulness of individual studies (see Table 4). It is useful to note that the questions presented in the grid should stimulate answers that are well presented in the submitted paper. We highly recommend Davis’s (1971) approach to “interestingness” in management literature. Interestingness straddles the line between obviousness and absurdity, while attacking the “taken for granted world of the audience” (p. 311).

## 6.3 Stakeholder and Institutional Practices

## 6.3.1 Author Practices

Our essential advice to authors wishing to show innovativeness is, first and foremost, to present ideas and findings in a clear, concise manner as would any author of any paper regardless of the effort to show innovativeness. However, below, we suggest four points of focus for special care in demonstrating innovativeness.

First, we suggest that a stimulating research question is key to demonstrating innovativeness. For instance, a research question like: “What is the distinction between an IS innovation and an organizational innovation and how do these differences affect the way IS innovations originate and diffuse in organizations?” seems to offer something fundamental in demarcating the unique attributes of IS innovation and then pushing their implications. In contrast, a question like: “What is the role of compatibility in adopting ERP innovation?” suggests a less innovative paper. Of course, the question itself is just a starting point, and innovativeness emerges from how the paper develops and presents novelty and usefulness.

Second, authors should consider alternatives to following “the script” (Grover and Lyytinen, 2015). In order to show the innovativeness of the content of the study, it will at times be advisable to also shift the structure of how it is presented. There are advantages to both author and reader of using a standard “script” in terms of assuring that all expectations are met and knowledge is consumed efficiently; however, at other times, this standardized structure may obscure the key messages and what is novel and important about them. A paper focused on why a theory cannot be replicated may be better off, for example, telling the chronological story of the study and clarifying its need to reject hypotheses rather than using the standard structure of literature review followed by hypotheses and results. Alternative formats may be able to better capture ambiguities, diverse interpretations, unexpected discoveries, and false starts. For instance, a “technology extension” study might start off demonstrating the ineffectiveness of well-known findings when applied to an emerging technology, thereby clearly demarcating innovativeness at the outset. A “blue ocean transformational” study on digital innovation might work well using building blocks—i.e., setting up fundamental building blocks of theory and then constructing multiple models based on these blocks that represent different configurations of innovation.

Third, we urge writers to highlight ad hoc findings and unexpected observations (including those pertaining to items expected but not yet observed). Following several years of study of a domain topic, authors could have valuable observations and opinions beyond the results of formal hypothesis tests. Scholars are sometimes wary of sharing such thoughts because of concerns that these would be labeled “speculation” and could be used to discredit their otherwise methodical presentation. However, we find that too many papers have insufficient implications and future research sections where standard methodological issues are enumerated but the understanding of the context and the meaning of the results are underdeveloped. Discussion of statistically nonsignificant relationships could surface issues with methods, subpopulations, or other contingencies pushing future researchers into new and underexplored territory.

Fourth, converting unexpected findings into theoretical statements or propositions can introduce theory building and initiate a process of testing and refinement that leads to new stable knowledge. Such theoretical statements or propositions can be of the form: “if the unexpected finding in this study is robust across a larger domain, we expect future researchers to find support for the existence of a particular relationship (as stated) between entity A and entity B.” Of course, such a theory has little if any support when first proposed but it foreshadows a scientific method process of testing, evolution, and growth (where justified by its potential usefulness) into recognized and applied knowledge.

Additionally, we urge all stakeholders to consider publication outlets outside standard lists like the Basket of 8 (Currie, et al., 2016). While these journals are intended to be broad enough to encompass the interests of all IS scholars, numerous other journals achieve a high level of quality sometimes by focusing on a more particular domain or alternative range of issues.

Targeting journals in addition to those in the Basket offers meaningful but unusual work more avenues of dissemination. With today’s electronic indexing systems, quality work in many journals beyond the Basket can gain high levels of visibility. Naturally, junior scholars should consider their entire research portfolio, the promotion rules at their institution, along with their own values and goals when deciding where to aim their work.

## 6.3.2 Editors, Reviewers, and Journal Practices

Efforts by authors to increase the innovativeness of the IS body of knowledge are likely to fall flat without equivalent and complementary actions on the part of editors and reviewers. While authors may not see as many innovative papers as they’d like entering the body of knowledge, editors often complain that not enough innovative papers are submitted. In the interest of breaking this cycle, we suggest the following.

Paper invitations: Editors serve as central nodes for emerging trends and opportunities to address new issues amid the swirl of their discussions, both formal and informal. To the extent they can conceive of groundbreaking work, talented and open-minded scholars may be enlisted to shape these into innovative products. Invited authors could be incentivized to do this with the promise of a short, single reviewer cycle and conditional acceptance.

Different handling of papers identified as innovative: We expect the recognition of innovative potential in submissions to be more an “art” of imagination than a “science” of following a specified process. Experienced editors can identify innovative potential in newly submitted manuscripts. However, it is more important to identify AEs and reviewers who specialize in developmental review processes, who emphasize underlying issues and challenge preconceptions, and who are committed to seeing new ideas in print. These can be clearly identified as a subset of the journal’s stable of personnel that can nurture innovation. Editors might flag papers that strike them as innovative for “different” treatment intended to enhance their novelty and expose their usefulness.<sup>16</sup> Creation of such alternative routes need to be developed carefully to avoid the moral hazard of simply having an “easier” or less rigorous alternative review system.

Accept a higher amount of type one error: Given the sheer volume of submissions to many journals, some receiving more than 1,000 per year, it may simply be a structural necessity to screen quickly and efficiently based on standard criteria, inadvertently eliminating unusual or “risky” papers. Innovative approaches are often characterized by not having an easily available evaluation infrastructure. Given the intense competition among journals, it is natural for editors to want to accept as few as possible papers that just miss the mark; however, in the process, papers that are unusual but potential “big hits” may be screened out as well. Editors need to explore ways to develop criteria to identify and develop creative but unorthodox papers in terms of content and structure. This might require, for instance, greater tolerance of conceptual innovativeness at the cost of comprehensive testing.

Special issues and special sections: Stimulation of articles specifically aiming to be viewed as innovative can be enhanced by devoting sections of journals to their publication (perhaps following the idea of differentiated evaluation and handling). Calls for special issues may also signal interest in innovative papers and encourage the creation of papers intended to be viewed as innovative as well as the write up and submission of any “on the shelf” that have potential value but are unlikely to be positively evaluated based solely on traditional measures.

Enhancing recognition: In addition to annual “best paper” awards, many journals already recognize the “best reviewer” and “best senior or associate editor” of the year as well. For example, the Journal of Association for Information Systems’s Best Senior Editor award recognizes the senior editor of the paper winning the Best Paper award each year. Extending formal criteria toward innovativeness and/or recognizing two papers—one based largely on innovativeness and the other based on strength of incremental contribution—could help increase submissions of innovative papers. In 2019, ICIS specifically recognized the best theory-oriented paper of the year; perhaps the AIS could also recognize the most innovative paper of the year at one of its forums.

Institutional pressures: Clearly, authors and journals are locked into a system in which their decisions lead to consequences, not only regarding the accumulation of knowledge but also in terms of scholars’ career paths. Our experience is that journal editors rightly consider the impact of their policies on the prestige of their journals in order to maximize the positive effects for the individuals who publish in them. As a result, the assumed and often realized practices of deans and promotion committees have a great influence on editors and authors channeling efforts into predictable rather than “boat-shaking” results. These influences are not only direct in terms of, say, ignoring practicebased work when considering promotion but, more perniciously, in terms of creating a culture where efforts outside of narrow channels are not even considered. If we want more innovative papers to be published in our journals, we need to modify this incentive structure.

AACSB frequently calls for research that has greater community impact rather than merely internal scholarly impact measured by citations. Such policies and actions might include: (1) cultural pressure or even sanctions to broaden the range of journals that are counted as valid outlets for publishing; (2) investment in outlets that specialize in innovative work, particularly ones focused on usefulness; (3) recommendations that lists like those published in Financial Times should be applied as guidelines rather than litmus tests; and (4) consideration of downloads in addition to citations, particularly from the ACM digital library and others like it, where access includes a sizable number and percentage of practitioners.

## 7 Conclusion

Our primary thesis presumes that innovativeness is a desirable characteristic for IS research. We recognize, however, that in building cumulative knowledge, incremental studies are also needed. If early research of an IS phenomenon provides an innovative frame, subsequent work needs to test the frame and examine its contingencies. However, IS should seek to foster an environment where innovative studies are undertaken, nurtured, and promoted. The framework we have presented can be viewed as a set of initial categories subject to further additions, revisions, and alternative definitions.

Prescriptively for editors and reviewers, we cannot emphasize enough that these dimensions and guideposts should be used to suggest enhancements rather than be used merely as screening devices. However, we feel that the essential issue is how to “screen in” more innovative research. To that end, we suggest that editors and reviewers look for ways that individual authors can more explicitly highlight the aspects of their work that are original and show how these ideas move the field forward. We also recommend giving additional weight to interesting outliers, variance, and trends when evaluating papers based on standard statistical analysis. Finally, greater speculation, projection, and consideration of alternative possibilities could be encouraged in research and discussion sections. Concretely, we suggest that journals (1) take steps to create a process for “screening in” innovative papers; (2) establish procedures and cultures to handle innovative papers, emphasizing their potential contribution rather than merely precision; and (3) recognize innovative work when produced.

It is our hope that calling attention to what we see as the many routes to and dimensions of innovative work will stimulate the search for and publication of a greater proportion of innovative research in the IS field. Such growth of innovative work is particularly important as we traverse new digitally laden paths that continuously challenge our understanding.

## About the Authors

Varun Grover is the George and Boyce Billingsley Endowed Chair and Distinguished Professor of IS at the Walton School of Business, University of Arkansas. He has published extensively in the information systems field, with over 300 publications in major refereed journals; ten recent articles ranking him among the top four researchers globally. Dr. Grover has an h-index of 96, which is ranked in the top five in the field, and around 45,000 citations in Google Scholar. Thompson Reuters recognized him as one of 100 Highly Cited Scholars globally in all Business disciplines. He is a senior editor for MISQ Executive, editor of the Journal of the Association for Information Systems Section on Path Breaking Research, and has served as senior editor for MIS Quarterly (2 terms), the Journal of the Association for Information Systems (2 terms) and Data Base. Dr. Grover’s current work focuses on the impacts of digitalization on individuals and organizations. He is the recipient of numerous awards from USC, Clemson, University of Arkansas, AIS, Academy of Management, DSI, the OR Society, Anbar, PriceWaterhouse, among others, for his research and teaching. He is an AIS Fellow and has received the LEO Award for Lifetime Achievement from the AIS.

Fred Niederman serves as Shaughnessy Endowed Professor at Saint Louis University. He serves as editor-in-chief for Communications of the Association for Information Systems. He has published peer-reviewed studies in Basket of Eight journals including MIS Quarterly, Journal of Association for Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Journal of Management Information Systems. His work has been recognized as a “publication of the year” by AIS in 2015 and as the winner of the inaugural Paul Gray Award for the Communications of the Association for Information Systems’s most thought-provoking paper of the year in 2014. He has twice been awarded “senior editor of the year” by Journal of Management Information Systems. His areas of research interest include IS personnel, IS project management, philosophy of science applied to IS, effects on IS of mergers and acquisitions, global IS, and group collaboration and teams. He is proud to be counted as a member of the “circle of compadres” for the KMPG PhD Project.
