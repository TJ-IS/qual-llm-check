# Team Cognition: Development and Evolution in Software Project Teams

ABSTRACT: In software development, team-based work structures are commonly used to accomplish complex projects. Software project teams must be able to utilize the expertise and knowledge of participants without overwhelming individual members. To efficiently leverage individuals’ knowledge and expertise, software project teams develop team cognition structures that facilitate their knowledge activities. This study focuses on the emergence and evolution of team cognition in software project teams, and examines how communication activity and team diversity impact the formation of these structures. A longitudinal study was conducted of 51 database development teams. The results suggest that some forms of communication and team diversity affect the formation of team cognition. Frequency of meetings and phone calls were positively related to the formation of team cognition, while e-mail use had no effect. Gender diversity had a strong and positive effect on the development of team cognition and the effect remained stable over time. Implications for the practical potential and limitations of purposive team construction as a strategy for improving software development team performance are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: expertise location, member familiarity, project teams, shared task understanding, software teams, team communications, team diversity.

MODERN ORGANIZATIONS ARE INCREASINGLY ADOPTING the team approach as a way of accomplishing tasks that surpass the capabilities of single individuals [20]. In addition to issues of time and resource coordination, teams are often created with the expectation that they will enable organizations to “better utilize expertise, minimize the impact of increasing workload on one individual, and maximize the use of increasingly more sophisticated technology” [66, p. 179].

Software project teams are an important example of this trend [17]. Software projects are typically complex, dynamic, and involve unstructured tasks [4, 37]. Execution of these projects requires knowledge and expertise from many domains [14]. Teams are viewed as a primary mechanism for leveraging the specialized knowledge of individual team members [11, 39]. Ideally, a software project team is staffed so that both the levels and the distribution of knowledge within the team match those required for the successful completion of the project [74].

However, the mere presence of individuals with diverse knowledge is an insufficient condition for a software project team to achieve quality performance [17]. The potential value of a team can only be realized if team members utilize their unique expertise in conjunction with the knowledge of other members [52]. Team cognition plays an important role in that it “allow(s) team members to draw on their own well-structured knowledge as a basis for selecting actions that are consistent and coordinated with those of their teammates” [44, p. 274].

In the information systems (IS) literature, team cognition has been suggested as a critical mechanism for facilitating knowledge activities within the software development process [17, 37, 38]. Although the importance of team cognition has been recognized, how a team achieves a high level of team cognition during its software development for quality performance has been relatively understudied. This study examines the development and evolution of team cognition in software project teams, and how communication activity and team diversity impact this process.

## Theoretical Background and Conceptual Model

TEAMS ARE VIEWED AS “GROUP(S) OF TWO OR MORE individuals who must interact cooperatively and adaptively in pursuit of shared valued objectives” [8, p. 223]. The essential task interdependency demands that team members work effectively together, introducing cognitive requirements beyond the individual level [11]. Team cognition refers to the mental models collectively held by a group of individuals that enable them to accomplish tasks by acting as a coordinated unit. This team-level integration of mental models functions as mental templates that are imposed on information environments to give them form and meaning, providing a cognitive foundation for action [73].

The concept of team cognition is first proposed as a powerful explanatory mechanism for understanding interactions in effective teams [8, 12, 36]. Team cognition enables members to formulate accurate teamwork and taskwork predictions [8, 32], adapt their activities and behaviors in a collaborative way, and thereby increase overall team effectiveness [7, 40, 61]. Without well-formed team cognition, team members will not be able to efficiently share knowledge and information, coordinate each other’s activities, resolve conflicts, or negotiate agreed-upon solutions [7, 26, 73]. In software development, team cognition helps software project teams effectively manage their members knowledge, expertise, and skills as integrated assets [74], assign tasks to people with the most capability [17], and coordinate their actions and adapt their behavior to the demands of the project and the expectations of other members [8, 38].

Based on its explanatory power, team cognition has been argued as a potential variable to predict the likely effectiveness of working teams [7, 47, 73]. However, empirical study of team cognition has lagged behind the conceptual work, largely because of much confusion that exists in the conceptualization and measurement of the construct [7, 48]. Team cognition is studied in a variety of disciplines with different approaches and perspectives [47], and has been described in terms of shared cognition [7], metacognition [24], team mental models [36], collective cognition [19], transactive memory [75], shared mental models [8, 69], group-level knowledge structure [73], and team knowledge [11, 12]. The measurement of team cognition is further complicated by interwoven factors, particularly the task and the surrounding environment [11, 12]. For example, the team cognition of a clinic operating team (including a surgeon and a nurse) could be very different from that of a software development team, in that the former requires that portions of members’ knowledge bases be shared or identical, whereas in the latter instance, compatible, but different, knowledge and skills are assumed to be beneficial to team effectiveness [17].

Measures and methods targeting team cognition are sparse [7, 11, 12, 48]. The objective of this study is not to provide a new solution to team cognition issues but rather to better understand the formation and evolution of team cognition in software development contexts. So, we focus on two elements of team cognition that have been suggested in the software development literature—awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding. Awareness of expertise location refers to the common awareness of each team member’s specialized knowledge and unique expertise. Shared task understanding characterizes the degree to which members share an understanding of the focal software development task. Following Cannon-Bowers and Salas’s discussion of various types of team cognition, the former (awareness of expertise location) can be viewed as an element of team-specific team cognition that reflects members knowledge of teammates and “only holds when team membership remains constant” [7, p. 197], and the latter (shared task understanding) can be viewed as an element of task-specific team cognition that reflects members’ knowledge of the focal task and cannot be generalized to other tasks. Mathieu et al. [44] and others have argued that the team types and task types of team cognition are conceptually distinct and have significant effects on team performance. We believe these two elements capture some distinct features of software development. Studying their dynamics will help us understand the formation and evolution of team cognition in software development contexts and how they influence teams’ ability to achieve systems development success.

## Awareness of Expertise Location

Awareness of expertise location is an important element of team cognition in software project teams. When people form a team, they need to develop “metaknowledge” of one another’s knowledge and expertise, so that they can efficiently and effectively assign tasks to those who have the necessary knowledge and skills as well as identify those whose knowledge might be useful in a given situation. This metaknowledge also allows team members to draw on others’ expertise to solve problems that arise during the life of the project. Shared awareness of each other’s knowledge and expertise plays a key integrative and coordinative function in software project teams [17, p. 1557].

Awareness of expertise location is derived from the theory of transactive memory [17]. Wegner [75] defined transactive memory as the knowledge possessed by group members, coupled with an awareness of who knows what. However, social cognition researchers typically focus on the shared awareness of knowledge location, or the transactive memory system, as a crucial component of the social cognitive mechanisms for coordinating learning, storage, and retrieval among individuals [26]. From this perspective, the transactive memory system can be seen as an “index.” Members shared awareness of other members’ knowledge and expertise is an indicator of the degree to which the team has formed transactive memory [2, 50, 68].

While transactive memory systems may include other aspects of team knowledge, such as credibility (members’ beliefs of the reliability of other members’ knowledge) and coordination (effective, orchestrated knowledge processing) [39], an awareness of members’ specialized knowledge and unique expertise is viewed as the central element of this form of team cognition.

## Shared Task Understanding

Shared task understanding is another critical element of team cognition in teams. Mutually shared understanding of a focal task, including the involved procedures, sequences, actions, and strategies, helps team members form common explanations and expectations for the task, and in turn, coordinate activities in a harmonious and efficient fashion [8, 38]. This element of team cognition is particularly important for software project teams. As various people work together on a complex software project, they need to develop a common view of relevant development issues such as “what the software they are constructing should do, how it should be organized, and how it should fit with other software systems already in place or undergoing parallel development” [37, p. 69].

Shared task understanding is derived from the theory of shared mental models. Shared mental models are organized knowledge structures shared by team members about the task, goals, strategies, team members, and so on. With shared knowledge structures, team members are able to interpret cues in a similar manner, make compatible decisions, and take coordinated action [7]. By serving as a cognitive basis for team members to act in conformity with the expectation of others, shared mental models make it less necessary for individuals to explain and discuss their actions [8, 42]. Team performance can also benefit as the result of efficient coordination. It is the ability to quickly adapt individual actions to the needs of team process that enables a team to be successful in dynamic environments [8, 44]. For example, because of their shared understanding of the task, an effective clinic operating team does not need intensive communications among members when performing surgery.

Although multiple mental models may exist in teams (e.g., equipment model, task model, interaction model, and team model) [8], the shared understanding of the focal task is a core component [69] and has been employed here as a main dimension on which to assess the overall shared mental models [38, 44, 70]. It is the highly task-specific knowledge, involving the procedures, sequences, actions, and strategies of taking the task, that leads team members to have compatible expectations for performance [7]. When team members share a common view of task demands and project objectives, they are better able to anticipate the task-specific actions of other team members [36]. Such a common understanding enables individuals to indirectly coordinate their independently performed work efforts while minimizing the need for more costly coordinative mechanisms. This is particularly important for teams working under conditions in which communication is restricted or difficult because of a heavy workload, time pressure, or other environmental features [44]—typical conditions for software project teams [37].

## Conceptual Model

A conceptual model of the formation and evolution of team cognition, as shown in Figure 1, considers team cognition to be created by both preexisting conditions and ongoing team interactions. Preexisting conditions reflect both the prior knowledge of team members and any previous shared experiences that they may have had. Team interactions refer to the interactive activities and behaviors that members perform to carry out project tasks and facilitate team performance. The conceptual model depicts team cognition as being based on these two factors and serving as a mediator with team performance. It also shows a direct link between team interactions and team performance.

Preexisting conditions have significant effects on team cognition, especially early in the life of a team. Moreland and Myaskovsky [50] suggested that knowing of others expertise is a determining factor for the formation and development of team cognition. Preexisting conditions, such as close relationships [75, 76], shared team or working experiences [3], training together [41, 49], and expertise notification and role assignment before or upon team formation [50], provide team members initial knowledge of others as well as temporary expectations about how the project will develop. In addition, certain team diversities, or saliencies among team members, may foster stereotypic thinking of members of other reference groups. This stereotyping may accelerate or hinder the formation and development of cognition in the team.

Software development is a highly interdependent task, requiring team members to interact with each other intensively to produce a successful system [37]. Team interactions are the necessary activities and behaviors within teams to transform members individual knowledge to team knowledge that ultimately guides action for project success [13]. By exchanging and processing information and knowledge, these activities and behaviors help develop the metaknowledge of others and form perception of the focal task, leading to the formation and development of team cognition. Therefore, team interactions affect team performance both directly and indirectly through team cognition, as shown in Figure 1.

The conceptual model also shows that team cognition evolves as the work of the team progresses. Team cognition is equipped from learning and experience. Thus, team cognition is dynamic in nature [24, 36] and changes as team members attend to environmental cues [19]. Given the relative consistency of task conditions and team processes common to many teams, team cognition may undergo certain equilibriums at different points in time as team members balance between factors such as task uncertainty versus routines and role ambiguity versus leadership [19]. The prevailing wisdom is that members develop and share more understanding of one another’s knowledge and skills as well as of the focal task as they work together to achieve project goals [41, 49]. However, there are also studies whose results suggest that, in some contexts, the understanding about the task and each other’s expertise in a team may not become similar over time [44] or may even diverge at the end of the project [38]. Because of this discrepancy, the conceptual model suggests that change occurs, but it does not specify whether that change is expected to be positive, negative, or zero.

## Preexisting Conditions’ Effect on Team Cognition

Preexisting conditions are assessed in terms of team characteristics—member familiarity and team diversity. These factors have been studied in prior work with the goal of developing criteria for the design of teams to enhance the likelihood that they will be effective [6]. Team diversity is examined in terms of gender diversity and background diversity.

## Member Familiarity

The amount of interpersonal knowledge that individuals have regarding other team members is one of the most conspicuous characteristics of teams [21, 53]. Member familiarity reflects the extent of prior interactions among members [22]. The preestablished relationships among members provide a setting for forming an initial perception of one another’s knowledge, expertise, backgrounds, and other factors such as personalities and habits. Therefore, teams whose members have had prior interactions are likely to possess more interpersonal knowledge in their early stages than teams of strangers [79]. Although this initial interpersonal knowledge may not directly relate to the focal project, it provides a basis for formation of the cognitive structures in the team that are needed to facilitate information processing (e.g., pooling unshared information and discussing conflicting ideas [21]) and the performance of software development tasks.

Three hypotheses relate member familiarity to team cognition, both as a single measure and in terms of its constituent elements:

Hypothesis 1: Member familiarity facilitates the formation of team cognition in software project teams.

Hypothesis 1a: Member familiarity facilitates the formation of awareness of expertise location in software project teams.

Hypothesis 1b: Member familiarity facilitates the formation of shared task understanding in software project teams.

## Team Diversity

Another preexisting characteristic that has been found to relate to team cognition and performance is team diversity, or the distribution of team members’ characteristics. Research on team diversity spans multiple disciplines, including social psychology, sociotechnical theory, industrial engineering, and organizational psychology [6]. Often, team diversity has been assumed to reflect the similarity of members’ past experiences [81] and influence the way they perceive other team members as well as the information environment. Team diversity “would predispose team members to invoke and use patterns of thought organization,” affecting the formation of team cognition [36, p. 423].

Although there are many ways in which teams can be diverse, a common consideration is whether the diversity is based on observable or nonobservable attributes [46, 62]. Observable types of diversity include race or ethnicity, age, and gender; nonobservable types of diversity are based on more subtle attributes, such as education, technical abilities, functional background, organizational tenure, personality characteristics, or values. The difference between observable and nonobservable types of diversity is linked to the role of stereotypes in impression formation. Observable types of diversity are expected to evoke responses that are based on biases, prejudices, or stereotypes, whereas nonobservable types of diversity can create differences in orientations toward task and team process issues [46]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that observable types of diversity generally have negative effects on team integration, whereas nonobservable types of diversity are generally beneficial for teams seeking to achieve quality performance [46, 62].

Gender is a salient characteristic that may lead individuals to consciously or unconsciously categorize others into reference groups and from that make inferences about unobservable attributes (e.g., socioeconomic status, education, values). This stereotyping is not necessarily valid, and it may lead to negative affective consequences such as reduced satisfaction, low commitment, and increased turnover [60]. For example, Tsui et al. [71] found that people of a minority gender in an organization were less likely to be attached to their organizations. The negative psychological attachment was more salient for men than for women. As members’ attitudes and motivation are affected, so is their cognitive integration as an effective team. Therefore, the formation of team cognition may be hindered in mixed-gender teams.

Hypothesis 2: Gender diversity hinders the formation of team cognition in software project teams.

Hypothesis 2a: Gender diversity hinders the formation of awareness of expertise location in software project teams.

Hypothesis 2b: Gender diversity hinders the formation of shared task understanding in software project teams.

Empirical studies indicate that nonobservable diversity indicators may be positively related to the creativity and the decision-making effectiveness of teams [27]. Nonobservable diversities such as education background bring differing viewpoints to teams, leading to more creativity and increased performance [80]. In their study of top management teams, Smith et al. [65] found that team diversity in educational level was positively related to a firm’s return on investment (ROI) and to growth in sales. They attributed the instrumental benefits of background diversity to its creation of a heterogeneous knowledge base. This reasoning holds particularly for knowledge worker teams such as software project teams in which members recognize and value specialized knowledge as a critical asset to achieve quality team performance. In the team-forming stage, people tend to assess others’ knowledge by their backgrounds or job domains (for example, user representatives are often assumed to be experts in their specific business application domains [35]). Knowing that others are from different backgrounds helps members develop an appreciation of one another’s knowledge, expertise, and skills; such an appreciation helps members be more accommodative on issues related to the focal task, thereby facilitating the development of team cognition.

Hypothesis 3: Background diversity facilitates the formation of team cognition in software project teams.

Hypothesis 3a: Background diversity facilitates the formation of awareness of expertise location in software project teams.

Hypothesis 3b: Background diversity facilitates the formation of shared task understanding in software project teams.

## Team Interaction Effects on Team Cognition

The second antecedent of team cognition in the conceptual model of Figure 1 is team interactions. Of the various interactive activities that team members may take, such as working together, observing, and monitoring, communication has been described as the “heart of group behavior” [63, p. 252] and is viewed as the primary means for team members to work toward performance ends. It is through communications that team members interact with each other, share views and concerns, clarify team roles, assign subtasks, and coordinate their activities [77].

Although the amount and content of various communicative activities may be difficult to capture, the frequencies of using various communication media (e.g., faceto-face meetings, telephone, and electronic mail) in software project teams can be readily detected and accurately assessed. Communication frequency has been widely used as an indicator for the intensity level of communications in teams [54, 65]. So we adopted communication frequency as the operationalization of team interactions in our research model.

Team cognition requires communications in order for the team to build and maintain mental models to facilitate team performance [42]. Through frequent communications, team members may quickly develop knowledge and understanding of each other, foster an awareness of the situation or context in which the team is functioning, and adjust their behaviors accordingly. Thus, dynamic changes in team cognition occur as a natural result of these information- and knowledge-processing activities [13, 36]. This leads to hypotheses of communication effects on team cognition in general, and the two specific elements of team cognition in particular:

Hypothesis 4: Communication frequency will have positive effects on the development of team cognition in software project teams.

Hypothesis 4a: Communication frequency will have positive effects on the development of awareness of expertise location in software project teams.

Hypothesis 4b: Communication frequency will have positive effects on the development of shared task understanding in software project teams.

## Team Cognition’s Effect on Team Performance

The conceptual model shows that team cognition mediates the impact of preexisting conditions and team interactions on team performance in terms of both elements of cognition—awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding.

## The Effect of Awareness of Expertise Location on Team Performance

The team cognition literature provides strong empirical evidence that teams perform better if they have developed mature team cognition [25, 40, 41, 50]. For instance, in a study of 69 software project teams, Faraj and Sproull [17] found that awareness of expertise location had a strong and positive impact on team performance.

Awareness of expertise location contributes to greater team efficiency because members are better able to anticipate, rather than simply react to, each other’s behavior [51]. Members also have an improved ability to access one another’s specialized expertise, resulting in an expanded pool of knowledge and expertise for decision making and problem solving [25]. In such instances, team tasks are more likely to be assigned to the people who are most able to perform them, thereby improving team effectiveness and avoiding redundancy of effort [25, 50].

This leads to Hypothesis 5a:

Hypothesis 5a: The level of awareness of expertise location is positively related to team performance in software project teams.

## The Effect of Shared Task Understanding on Team Performance

The formation of task understanding regarding a focal project enables team members to interpret cues in a similar manner, make compatible decisions, take appropriate action [36, 47], and conduct discussions that are based more on shared knowledge rather than unshared knowledge [68]. Because shared task understanding encourages more collaborative activities among team members, a general conclusion from the literature is that “team effectiveness will improve if team members have an adequate shared understanding of the task, team, equipment, and situation” [47, p. 89].

In software development, communication gaps often exist among team members. People may be experts in different areas, each of which has its own particular jargon and terminology [1]. Communication gaps hinder collaboration in software project teams. The formation of shared task understanding reduces these communication obstacles by enabling intragroup coordination based on shared mental models rather than on the more problematic interpersonal communication [72]. This, in turn, facilitates effective teamwork and enhances the overall performance of software project teams.

This leads to Hypothesis 5b:

Hypothesis 5b: The level of shared task understanding will be positively related to team performance in software project teams.

## Communications’ Effect on Team Performance

In this study, team interactions are operationalized in terms of communication frequency. In the literature, communication frequency has long been studied as a significant factor in affecting team performance [56, 57]. Generally, high-performing teams communicate significantly more frequently than low-performing teams. This is evidenced particularly in complex task environments such as aviation [18, 31], R&D projects [32], new health-care program development [54], and top management teams working on strategic organizational issues [65]. Because of the essentiality of communication to team performance, some researchers even use communication frequency as a surrogate measure for team performance (e.g., [45]).

Communications in software development contexts are often studied as a main mechanism for team members to exchange information in order to work out the ambiguity and uncertainty arising from complex, interrelated tasks [1, 4, 37]. In addition, the special feature of software development that brings knowledge and expertise from different domains often creates language barriers (e.g., between system developers and user participants [1]) and conflicts of interest [58], to which communication is considered as the primary means to produce satisfactory solutions [4]. Frequent intrateam communications reflect intensive information exchanging and processing activities among team members; as such, frequent communications should have a direct impact on team performance.

Hypothesis 6: Communication frequency will have positive effects on team performance in software project teams.

## Time Effects on Team Cognition

When team members work continuously with each other, the effects of preexisting team characteristics on team cognition may change over time. The initial metaknowledge in a team, which is established either from prior interpersonal knowledge (e.g., member familiarity) or through observations of various surface attributes (e.g., gender diversity, background diversity), eventually should give way to new knowledge emerging from member interactions and teamwork. The new knowledge is probably increasingly more relevant to the project. As team members adjust their knowledge structures regarding other members to the needs of the project, the initial relationship between team characteristics and aspects of team cognition should be obscured and eventually fade away.

This is consistent with the theory of random processes—in particular, Markov chains, in which both the initial state (analogous to preexisting conditions) and the transition probabilities (analogous to team interactions) determine state-to-state transitions during the early stages of a process. But, the effect of the preexisting conditions disappears as the system approaches a “steady state” or equilibrium [59].

This suggests the following hypotheses regarding the relationship between team cognition and various team characteristics, including member familiarity, gender diversity, and background diversity.

Hypothesis 7: The effect of team characteristics on team cognition decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7a: The effect of initial member familiarity on expertise decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7b: The effect of initial member familiarity on shared task understanding decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7c: The effect of gender diversity on awareness of expertise location decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7d: The effect of gender diversity on shared task understanding decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7e: The effect of background diversity on awareness of expertise location decreases over time.

Hypothesis 7f: The effect of background diversity on shared task understanding decreases over time.

The hypotheses are graphically summarized in the research model shown in Figure 2. Signs indicate the associated effects have been hypothesized to be positive or negative. As we hypothesized that the effects of team characteristics (member familiarity, gender diversity, and background diversity) will fade away over time, negative signs are used for time effects.

## Method

## The Synthetic Task Environment

IN THIS STUDY, WE DESIGNED A SYNTHETIC TASK of software development to test the research model. Synthetic tasks are “research tasks constructed by systematic abstraction from a corresponding real-world task” [43, p. 123]. Performance on a synthetic task should exercise some of the same behavioral and cognitive skills associated with the real-world task while avoiding the complexity (for example, the existence of various confounding factors that may lower the opportunity of observing significant effects of the investigated factors) encountered in an uncontrolled field study on real tasks.

The synthetic task employed in this study was to develop a relational database system using Microsoft Access. The subjects were students. Except for the manipulation on team formation and task deadline, students were free to set their own schedules and procedures to carry out their tasks, simulating the software development process in a realistic manner.

## Participants

In this study, 156 undergraduates enrolled in an IS course formed teams to fulfill a course requirement of collaboratively developing a relational database system over a five-week period. The students were juniors (about 24 percent), seniors (about 65 percent), and fifth-year business majors (about 11 percent). When the project was assigned, students were instructed to form three-member teams and were allowed to make their own teammate selections. Some students selected acquaintances as teammates while others chose students who happened to be seated nearby. Fifty-one teams were formed: seven teams with four members, five teams with two members, and 39 teams with three members. This process resulted in a population of teams that varied with respect to preexisting knowledge, shared experience, and team characteristics.

## Data Gathering

The demographics of participants are reported in Table 1. After the formation of the teams, a survey was conducted to assess students’ gender, educational status, major, and familiarity with other team members. During the software development process, students were asked to answer an online survey regarding their participation and team performance every week over a four-week period. The first survey was conducted one week after the start of the project. Although encouraged by the course instructor, taking the survey was voluntary. Students were told that the survey responses would not influence grades in any way. Some students failed to answer the survey on time, and some submitted incomplete answers. This resulted in 559 usable sets of individual data for analysis, or an 89.6 percent effective response rate.

## Measures

## Preexisting Conditions

Preexisting conditions included member familiarity, gender diversity, and educational diversity. Member familiarity was measured at the formation of teams by asking students to rate interpersonal knowledge in their teams on three items: knowledge of other teammates, others’ knowledge of me, and general interpersonal knowledge in the team (Cronbach’s $\alpha = 0 . 9 7 )$ . Individual responses were averaged to form measures of member familiarity for each team. Based on these aggregate team scores, the sample was split around the median to form two categories: unfamiliar teams (coded with 0) versus familiar teams (coded with 1). Gender diversity was also treated as dichotomous: single-gender teams (coded with 0) versus mixed-gender teams (coded with 1). Background diversity was coded as either low or high based on whether a majority of team members shared a common major. Teams in which a majority of students shared a common major were coded as low background diversity with dummy value of 0; otherwise, teams were coded as having high background diversity with dummy value of 1. The characteristics of different teams are reported in Table 2.

## Team Cognition

Team cognition includes both awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding. Awareness of expertise location was measured with a four-item instrument adopted from Faraj and Sproull [17]. Respondents were asked to use a five-point scale to rate the extent of understanding of each other’s knowledge and expertise in terms of (1) the existence of knowledge “map” in the team, (2) the association between task assignments and members’ knowledge, (3) knowing each other’s special knowledge and skills, and (4) knowing who has the relevant knowledge for his or her work.

Shared task understanding was measured with a four-item instrument based on Kraut and Streeter’s [37] comments on having a shared view of the project. Students were asked to use a five-point scale to rate the extent to which their teams shared a common understanding of the development technology, the application domain (or business topic selected for the project), the development procedures, and the overall vision of the software development project.

## Communication Frequency

When working on their software development tasks, all teams relied on three media for their communications—face-to-face meeting, telephone, and electronic mail. Following the work of Smith et al. [65], we calculated the frequency of each medium used in teams during a specific time period. More specifically, communication frequencies were measured by the self-reported single-item instruments asking students for the number of meetings, calls, and e-mails that their teams had conducted during the prior week. Team-level measures of communication frequency were constructed by averaging individual responses after aggregation analysis. These scores reflect the average frequency with which the team used a particular medium in a certain time period.

## Team Performance

Team performance was assessed with five items adapted from Robey et al.’s [58] instrument. Students were asked to use a five-point scale to rate the extent to which their team operated efficiently, met the schedule, produced products with appropriate quantity and quality, and interacted effectively with people inside and outside the team. After aggregation analysis individual responses were averaged to construct the final measure of team performance.

An expert grader who was blind to the goals of this study evaluated the quality of the database applications that were developed by the teams. The correlations between the grade and the self-reported team performance measures collected throughout the project are reported in Table 3. The correlation between the assigned grade and selfreported team performance got stronger over time, and reached 0.567 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ at Time 4, a magnitude typically considered to be large in social science research [10].

## Data Analysis and Results

## Aggregation Analysis

BEFORE AGGREGATING INDIVIDUAL RESPONSES to the team level, it was necessary to confirm response homogeneity or agreement within each team. We selected three classes of statistic tests for the aggregation analysis: interrater agreement (IRA, or $r _ { { \scriptscriptstyle W G } ( J ) } )$ , intraclass correlation (or ICC), and average deviation index (or AD). The three tests assess with different approaches the congruence among team members on their response to measured items. The $r _ { W G ( J ) }$ index compares the observed within-group variances to an expected variance from random responding [29, 30], the ICC index is based on a nested analysis of variance (ANOVA) design and analyzes within-team and between-team variances [28], and the AD measures the absolute deviation of individual responses from group means [6].<sup>1</sup> The use of the three different statistic tests provides methodological and statistical triangulation [17] and its conclusion will be more rigorous.

The results of the aggregation analysis for each construct are reported in Table 4. The IRA values of the three multi-item instruments—awareness of expertise location, shared task understanding, and team performance—were very high $( r _ { _ { W G ( J ) } } > 0 . 7$ is often used as a heuristic for judging high versus low within-group homogeneity [9]), suggesting a high level of homogeneity among responses within each team. In contrast, the ICC values were moderately acceptable [28] and comparable to other similar studies (e.g., [17]). The AD values were much smaller than the mean values of the associated measures, suggesting that the absolute values of individuals’ responses deviated little from group means [6].<sup>2</sup>

In summary, the calculation of three indices suggests a reasonable level of agreement within all the teams. Therefore, aggregating individual responses to the team level is justified.

## Construct Validity

In this study, three core constructs—awareness of expertise location, shared task understanding, and team performance—were assessed by multi-item instruments. The validity of these constructs was assessed in terms of their internal consistency of measurement, convergent validity, and discriminant validity.

Table 4 presents the Cronbach’s alpha levels of the three constructs at both individual level and team level. The Cronbach’s alpha levels are all greater than the commonly used 0.70 level, demonstrating internal consistency of measurement.

The analysis of the convergent and discriminant validity was conducted using principal factor analysis of the three constructs (Table 5). Using a varimax rotation, three factors that emerged explained 82.45 percent of the total variance. All items loaded high on their planned factors (with the exception of one item labeled as TU04 having a loading of 0.634) generally demonstrating satisfactory convergent validity. In contrast, the cross-loadings were low (all were less than 0.45), providing evidence that all factors were measures of different concepts. This was further evidenced by the correlation matrix (Table 5), in which the correlations between variables were significantly lower than 1 (the highest correlation coefficient of 0.69 was significantly lower than 1 at $p <$ 0.001 level). Discriminant validity was therefore readily concluded.

## Hypotheses Testing

Analysis was conducted on the team-level data set. As our purpose is to assert the trend among, rather than comparing the difference between, the levels of team cognition measured at different time points, the test needs to result direct statistics of time effect to assess the trend as increasing or decreasing over time. For this reason, we selected multilevel models of change as the testing method in order to incorporate time effects explicitly in the model [64]. The multilevel models of change is considered appropriate<sup>3</sup> because it formulizes time in a model as a variable so that its effects can be concluded from the resulting statistics. More specifically, we tested the hypothesis using the following multilevel model:

Level 1

$$
Y _ {i j} = \pi_ {0 i} + \pi_ {1 i} T i m e _ {i j} + \pi_ {2 i} E _ {i j} + \pi_ {3 i} C _ {i j} + \pi_ {4 i} M _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}
$$

Level 2

$$
\begin{array}{l} \pi_ {0 i} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \gamma_ {0 1} F _ {i} + \gamma_ {0 2} G _ {i} + \gamma_ {0 3} B _ {i} + \xi_ {0 i} \\ \pi_ {1 i} = \gamma_ {1 0} + \gamma_ {1 1} F _ {i} + \gamma_ {1 2} G _ {i} + \gamma_ {1 3} B _ {i} + \xi_ {1 i}, \end{array}
$$

where $Y _ { i j }$ is the dependent variable (awareness of expertise location or shared task understanding) measured for team i at time j; $E _ { i j }$ is the number of e-mails measured for team i at time $j ; C _ { i j }$ is the number of calls measured for team i at time $j ; M _ { i j }$ is the number of meetings measured for team i at time j; $F _ { _ i }$ is member familiarity of team i; $G _ { _ i }$ is gender diversity of team i; $B _ { _ i }$ is background diversity of team i; $\varepsilon _ { _ { i j } } , \xi _ { _ { 0 i } } ,$ and $\boldsymbol { \xi } _ { 1 i }$ are residuals at cross-team and within-team levels.

The level 1 submodel hypothesizes that team cognition results from team communication and evolves over time; the level 2 submodel assumes that time-caused changes vary across teams due to their different team characteristics. The two levels are methodologically distinguished by their associated residuals, which indicate the extent to which cross-team and within-team variances are explained by the model. By substituting for $\pi _ { _ { 0 i } }$ and $\pi _ { _ { 1 i } }$ from the level 2 submodel into the level 1 submodel, we arrived at the full or composite multilevel model for change described below:

$$
\begin{array}{r} Y _ {i j} = \gamma_ {0 0} + \gamma_ {0 1} F _ {i} + \gamma_ {0 2} G _ {i} + \gamma_ {0 3} B _ {i} + \pi_ {2 i} E _ {i j} + \pi_ {3 i} C _ {i j} + \pi_ {4 i} M _ {i j} \\ + \gamma_ {1 0} T i m e _ {i j} + \gamma_ {1 1} F _ {i} \times T i m e _ {i j} + \gamma_ {1 2} G _ {i} \times T i m e _ {i j} + \gamma_ {1 3} B _ {i} \times T i m e _ {i j} \\ + (\zeta_ {0 i} + \zeta_ {1 i} T i m e _ {i j} + \varepsilon_ {i j}). \end{array}
$$

Note that the residual of the composite model (in parentheses) has an occasiondependent component, $\zeta _ { 1 i } T i m e _ { i j } ,$ the value of which, although unexplained by the model, is dependent on the time of measurement. The mathematical form of the composite residual reveals two common properties of occasion-specific residuals—autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity. Autocorrelation means that the residuals are correlated with each other across repeated occasions. Heteroskedasticity means that the residuals have unequal variances across occasions of measurement. These conditions require special treatment in model estimation. In this study, we use full maximum likelihood (FML) to estimate parameters (including both regression coefficients and variance components). FML computes goodness-of-fit statistics that describe the fit of the entire model. In contrast, another commonly used method, restricted maximum likelihood (RML), assesses the fit of only the stochastic portion of a tested model. Although estimates computed from the two methods are practically identical, their model-fit statistics have different meanings. Under FML, the goodness-of-fit statistics describe how a model fits with the sample data; while under RML, the goodness-of-fit statistics describe how a model fits with the sample data residuals. Therefore, RML cannot be used to compare models that differ both in fixed effects and variance components [64, pp. 87–90].

The sample size (51 teams measured at four different time points, yielding a sample size of 204 data points) is adequate for multilevel models of change. (For a general multilevel model, Snijders and Bosker [67] considered samples of 30 or more to be sufficient.) The models were estimated using SAS version 8.02 for data analysis. The results are reported in Tables 6 and 7.

Table 6 presents testing results regarding the two team cognition elements—awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding. The testing included both the full models (a complete test of all relevant hypotheses) and the optimal models (subsets of the full models constructed by dropping insignificant parameters). Optimal models were evaluated based on deviance (–2 log likelihood), Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), and Schwarz’s Bayesian criterion (BIC) statistics (the smaller, the better). Pseudo R<sup>2</sup>s were calculated for each model by comparing its residual variance with that of an unconditional model estimated by using sample mean as the only parameter [64, pp. 102–103]. Thus, pseudo R<sup>2</sup> statistics should be interpreted as the proportional reduction in residual variance.

This result supports H1, that member familiarity is beneficial to team cognition, and the time effect hypotheses H7a and H7b, that the impact of initial member familiarity on team cognition will fade over time in software project teams.

Gender diversity shows a significant but positive effect on both awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding. Background diversity is not significant in the model. In addition, these effects do not change over time. Therefore, neither H2 or H3 nor their associated time effects (H7c, H7d, H7e, and H7f) were supported.

As to communication frequency, the number of calls and the number of meetings have significant and positive effects on both awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding, supporting the prediction of H4, that frequent communications would lead to higher levels of team cognition in software project teams.

The results shown in Table 6 suggest that initial member familiarity strongly influences awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding within a team. “Familiar teams”—those with high levels of familiarity—had higher levels of team cognition than unfamiliar teams. However, the favorable effect of familiarity on team cognition faded over time, as shown in Figure 3. At Time 4, familiar teams and unfamiliar teams achieved about the same levels of team cognition. Faraj and Sproull considered it to be a challenge that most software project teams are unfamiliar teams, in that they are often formed “depending on project requirements and who is available” [17, p. 1554]. Our analysis indicates that while familiarity is important in the early stages, teams are able to achieve a similar level of team cognition as time passes.

Background diversity did not have a significant impact on the development of team cognition. As shown in Figure 4, while both awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding increased over time, the difference between “same major” teams and “mixed major” teams was not significant. It is possible that since all students were enrolled in various majors in a college of business, they did not have sufficient variance in background. The requirement for students to take business core courses along with their major requisites has introduced a significant overlap of education across business disciplines. If students of business majors have essentially similar knowledge, the expected heterogeneous knowledge base may not exist in teams. Future research should consider the impact of background diversity measured in other terms.

Gender diversity positively affected team cognition. As illustrated in Figure 5, mixed-gender teams tended to develop higher levels of team cognition, and this effect remained strong and stable over time. This finding is contrary to the predictions suggested by the team diversity literature [46, 62]. According to the social categorization theory, people consciously or unconsciously categorize others into different reference groups with certain social statuses. Perceived deviations from equal status among the participants tend to increase anxiety in their work units. In our study, the research subjects were students who might not associate gender-caused reference groups with distinct social statuses. Another possible explanation is that most of the prior empirical studies were done in an era in which women were something of an anomaly in the computer workplace. Today, women are routinely involved in teams of all varieties, so that these results may reflect the realities of this era versus previous ones. The third possible reason might be that the presence of mixed genders helps reduce the tension in teams, especially in software project teams where team members continuously deal with nonroutine problems. Research on group communication suggests gender differences in communication, interpersonal styles, and conflict strategies [23]. Generally, women are more likely to compromise and men more likely to compete in task-oriented settings [60]. Thus, when dealing with conflicts, mixed-gender teams may be more likely to accommodate different perspectives and compromise on a solution than are same-gender (especially all male) teams in which members are more likely to compete.

Understanding the effect of gender diversity among information technology (IT) professionals has significant practical implications for team construction and project management. Future studies are needed to explore and clarify the underlying mecha nisms for better management of IS projects.

The results also reveal that different communication methods employed in the teams had different effects on team cognition. The number of e-mails had no effect on team cognition or team performance. Meetings and phone calls were associated with both elements of team cognition. Similar results have been reported in recent cognition studies. For example, in a study of the emergence and development of transactive memory systems in MBA consulting teams, Lewis [40] concluded that face-to-face communication was the most helpful communication mode. According to information richness theory [15], e-mail is less effective than phone calls and meetings in that it is the most limited communication method of the three in terms of the amount of information being conveyed. In addition, modes of team communication that rely on face-to-face contact, such as meetings, are more likely to create opportunities for team members to learn from peripheral participation [78]. Overall, this suggests that although e-mail may be a valuable communication tool, software project teams must recognize that relying too heavily on e-mail may ultimately undermine the team’s ability to form team cognition structures, and as a result, they may find it more difficult to perform effectively.

Team cognition evolves over time. As presented in Table 6, the data analysis concluded significant and positive coefficients for the time variable. The results provide evidence that team cognition evolves over time as team members work together. Although this is common wisdom, the positive effect of time on the development of team cognition was not observed in Levesque et al. [38], motivating the design of this study. Both studies employed student software project teams. One difference between the two studies involves the operationalization of team cognition. Levesque and colleagues calculated team agreement on project-specific issues as the indicator of shared mental models. In contrast, we applied context-independent instruments to assess awareness of expertise location and shared task understanding. Another difference is on the analysis method. Levesque et al. compared the mean levels of shared mental models at different time points. To conceptualize and test time effects, we used a multilevel model of change, a statistical method suggested particularly for time effect testing [64, 67]. However, as results from single studies cannot serve as the basis for strongly generalizable conclusions, future studies are needed to better characterize the effects of time on team cognition.

## Summary and Limitations

THE DYNAMIC NATURE OF TEAM COGNITION has been discussed but rarely tested in the literature. Of the two exceptions, Levesque et al. [38] and Mathieu et al. [44] proposed a maturing of team cognition through the team life cycle, but failed to find empirical support from their sampled teams. To our knowledge, this study is the first attempt that explicitly confirms the evolution and development of team cognition in software project teams.

As with all empirical work, this study is subject to limitations. While the subjects were engaged in a relatively extended project (five weeks) and had a vested interest in the outcome of the team’s work, the use of student subjects raises the possibility that findings may not accurately reflect the behavior of software project teams working in a business organization. However, most prior studies share this characteristic and there is evidence that students are good proxies for “real-world” people in many contexts [34]. Further studies of team cognition “in the rough” are needed to better understand how the factors considered in this study ultimately play out in the other settings.

In addition, because all teams were working on similar projects with the same technology (Microsoft Access databases), it was not possible to examine the role that task and technology characteristics might play in the formation of team cognition. Again, additional studies that consider a range of technologies and tasks would provide useful insight into the role of these factors in this process.

In this research, the constructs were measured by asking students to take an online survey on a weekly basis. Thus, common method variance could be a concern because of the use of self-reported responses as the sole source of sample data. However, by aggregating individual responses to team levels, this concern is alleviated. Evidence can be found from the grade and team performance correlation matrix (Table 3). Team performances correlate the highest when they are measured at close time points. As the gap between times of measurement increases, correlations decrease to reasonable levels, indicating small common variance that may otherwise lead to much inflated correlations [55] among these team-level data.

The use of single-item measures may be another limitation of this study in that constructs measured by single items may not be assessed on their reliability. We have addressed the concern in our aggregation analysis. For example, communication frequency was assessed by single questions asking for the frequency of using different modes of communication in a certain time period. Aggregation analysis using average deviation revealed that the deviation among individual responses was reasonably small within teams. This may also provide some confidence on the reliability of these measures.

Software project teams’ ability to operate as a team arises from who they are (preexisting team characteristics) and how they interact (team communication). Team cognition concepts provide potential levers for managers seeking to improve the performance of these teams. An arbitrary collection of people can, with a great deal of effort and some luck, accomplish significant goals. However, by forming team cognition, those individuals can increase their chances of success. The more we understand about the formation of team cognition, both within software project teams and in general, the more effective we will be at explaining and supporting the formation of effective teams.

1. AD is proposed by Burke et al. [6] as an index of within-group interrater agreement; its calculation involves determining the deviation of individual responses from the group mean, summing the absolute values of these deviations, and dividing by the number of deviations. The use of AD does not require explicit modeling of response distribution, making it appropriate for the aggregation analysis of open-ended measures, such as the communication frequency of the three communication methods in this study.

2. AD can be interpreted in terms of the metric of original scale; the small values indicate strong agreement among individual responses in groups [6]. For the assessment of AD values, Burke and Dunlap [5] proposed a criterion for the upper range of acceptable AD values as c/6, in which c is number of response options. All of the AD values of Likert-type measures meet this criterion. But the open-ended measures of communication frequency cannot readily use this criterion for a lack of a priori range of answers. If we assume a rectangular or uniform distribution for chance responding (the same assumption underlying the testing of $r _ { w G ( J ) }$ and Burke and Dunlap’s [5] criterion of c/6 for AD), c can be approximated as the double value of a group mean. Results from this approximate testing also suggest an acceptable level of withinteam agreement of these measures.

3. To test longitudinal data, one approach is to use repeated measures of ANOVA and test the differences of interested constructs measured at different time points; a trend (increasing or decreasing) may be suggested, but not readily tested, from the resulting differences. Another common approach of testing longitudinal data is to conduct between-wave analyses using partial least squares (PLS).

4. Baron and Kenny [3] discussed three steps as necessary to assess the mediating role of M on the relationship of $X  Y \colon$ step 1, regressing M on X; step 2, regressing Y on X; and step 3, regressing Y on both X and M. The correlation analysis (as shown in Table 4) between involved constructs (i.e., the two team cognition elements as of M, preexisting conditions as of X, and team performance as of Y) is equivalent to the tests of steps 1 and 2; the regression analysis conducted here can be viewed as the test of step 3. The involved correlations are significant at the $\alpha = 0 . 0 5$ level; after controlling for the effects of the two team cognition elements, none of the predictors (or the preexisiting conditions) has a significant effect on team performance. Thus, a fully mediated model of team cognition can be concluded.
