# The role of IT in crisis response: Lessons from the SARS and Asian Tsunami disasters

## a b s t r a c t

Research on crisis management recognizes the important role of information although few studies of crisis response deal explicitly with information systems. In this paper, we present a case study of Singapore’s response to the SARS and Asian Tsunami disasters. Using the resource-based view of the firm as our theoretical lens, we examine three research questions: what IS resources are needed in crisis response, how are these IS resources bundled with other non-IS resources, and how are they effectively coordinated? Our analysis of the case suggests that existing assets such as information technology infrastructure, leadership, and collaborative networks and existing capabilities such as the ability to build and apply IT, the ability to recognize signals and the ability to see the big picture are critical during crisis response. The actions taken using these assets and capabilities include informing resolutely, gaining stakeholder commitment and agile mobilizing of people and IT. Our analysis further suggests that coordination mechanisms, namely the crisis response organizational, informational, and IT structures, are important facilitators of the response actions. The resulting framework of resource deployment during crisis response extends the resource based view of the firm into a cooperative setting, aiming to understand the nature of IS resource value in a cooperative context, and considers not just the resources per se, but the means of coordination the resources.

## 1. Introduction

Crises. be they societal or organizational, are characterized by non-trivial threats to life. well-being, or other significantly held values, by unpredictability, and by urgency (Falkheimer and Heide, 2006). Societal crises – those having macro-level social and economic impacts – typically involve multiple private and public organizations and agencies working together to achieve crisis resolution (Turoff et al., 2004). As such, the coordination of information collecting, synthesizing, interpreting, and communicating across the multiple organizations becomes a central challenge in crisis response (Dantas and Seville, 2006; Zhang et al., 2002). Yet the very process of gathering and disseminating information during crises often results in unacceptable delays in crisis resolution: for example, during response to the Katrina disaster in the United States, key deci sion makers tergiversated in the presence of what they felt was too sparse or too difficult to interpret information rather than acting quickly to minimize the repercussions of the disaster (Dearstyne, 2006). Common across crisis types, this duality of information impedes crisis response: decision makers need to gather information to assess the scope and nature of the crisis prior to response and yet decision makers also need to reduce information and communication channels in order to respond quickly, often ignoring important information as a result (Turoff, 2002). Thus, both the absence of and presence of information poses challenges during crisis response resulting in a tension between the need to act and the need to gather information (Hale et al., 2005).

In spite of this centrality of information, and the systems used to gather, synthesize and distribute information during crisis response, information and information systems often appear as a tangential theme in research on crises (notable exceptions include Turoff et al. (2004), Lee et al. (2006), and Zhang et al. (2002) which focus on the design principles of crisis information management systems). Research on crises runs a gamut from describing types of crises (Pollard and Hotho, 2006), phases of crisis management (James and Wooten, 2005), and crisis management decision process (Hale et al., 2006; Boin et al., 2005), describing communication strategies (Lee, 2004; Stephens et al., 2005; Krieger, 2005), to interpreting communication strategies (Falkheimer and Heide, 2006), creating crisis network designs (Lin et al., 2006), and learning from crises (Moynihan, 2008; Birkland, 1997). Many of these studies mention information-related challenges, but few attempt to uncover the mechanisms through which IS facilitates crisis response nor the necessary preconditions to the use of IS during crises. Our case study of Singapore’s responses to the SARS and Asian Tsunami crises – crises which can both be considered as societal level crises – seeks to address this gap. Toward this end, we employ the resource-based view as a theoretical lens.

The resource-based view of the firm provides an effective theoretical lens within which to understand the means by which IS facilitates crisis response for several reasons: (1) it enables us to uncover those aspects of IS that are central resources in crisis response, (2) it enables us to view IS within the context of other central resources needed to effectively respond to crises and as such to uncover the complementarities needed for IS to pave the way toward quick crisis response and (3) it provides us a framework within which to examine the role of IS in coordinating the deployment of the entire bundle of resources in crisis response. While the RBV has been mostly considered at the firm-level and has been used to examine the resources needed to help a firm achieve short and long-term competitive advantage (Wade and Hulland, 2004), our work represents a theoretical extension of RBV into a cooperative, rather than competitive, environment with a view towards understanding the resources that provide value in an environment where organizations must work together to achieve a common goal, as in crisis response. Although at first glance, an extension of RBV into a cooperative context might appear to contradict the competitive underpinnings of the theory, the ultimate aim of RBV is to help understand firm performance – why one firm outperforms others. By extending RBV to a cooperative environment, we remain true to the implicit goal of understanding performance, but rather than understanding why one firm outperforms others, we are interested in how a constellation of firms cooperating may perform effectively.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. The resource based view in is research

The resource based view of firms describes a firm as a specific collection of resources and capabilities that can be deployed to achieve competitive advantage (Barney, 1991). Firm resources are defined as all assets (tangible or intangible) and capabilities belonging to or controlled by a firm that can be used to implement competitive strategies (Teece et al. 1997). A firm’s combination of resources forms the basis of competitive heterogeneity, where the scarcity of resources results in maximum rent generation (Grant, 1991). Firm-specific resources and capabilities must be protected and made difficult to transfer, imitate or replicate (Barney. 1991) Therefore by protecting their valuable resources against imitation or substitution, firms can sustain existing advantages. IS research using RBV has largely focused on classifying informationbased resources along the attributes of resources –value, rarity, appropriability, imitability, substitutability, mobility –posited by RBV with a view towards understanding which IS resources are most likely to contribute to competitive advantage (Wade and Hulland, 2004; Mata, Fuerst, and Barney, 1995). It has been argued that IS managerial skills, as opposed to technical IS skills or infrastructure, are more likely to be the source of competitive advantage (Wade and Hulland, 2004; Mata et al., 1995). Specifically, Wade and Hulland (2004) propose that outside-in (external relationship management and market responsiveness) and spanning (IS-business partnerships and IS planning and change management) will have greater impact on short and long-term competitive position than will inside-out (IS infrastructure, IS technical skills. IS development, and cost effective IS operations). However, when the objective is cooperation to achieve a maximum benefit for society, rather than competitive edge for a given firm, these relationships may vary as may the attributes of resources that are valuable. This leads to our first research question: what IS resources (assets and capabilities) are valuable in crisis response situations?

Even while the IS work using RBV has focused largely on identifying specific IS resources, there is recognition that the value of IS resources might have less to do with the particular IS resource per se, than with the IS resources’ interaction, or bundling, with other non-IS resources (Ravichandran and Lertwongsatien, 2002; Wade and Hulland, 2004). Resources may influence other resources in one of three ways: (1) compensatory – a change in the level of one resource might offset the change in a level of another; (2) enhancing – a change in the level of a resource may magnify the impact of another resources; (3) suppressing – the presence of particular resource might lessen the impact of another resource (Wade and Hulland, 2004). In spite of this recognition that the IS resource might serve to enhance the impact of other non-IS resources, there has been very little work looking at the importance of IS resources in the context of a larger bundle of other non-IS resources, nor the nature of the complementarity (Wade and Hulland, 2004). This leads to our second research question: how are IS resources bundled with other resources to enable crisis response?

RBV has been applied in many industry contexts. Early applications of RBV theory were criticized as having limited application to hyper-competitive environments where rapid and sudden changes are common (Teece et al., 1997). To address this issue, the dynamic capability emerged to explain how firms react, adapt and respond to changes in volatile environments (Winter, 2003). The dynamic capability perspective suggests that new forms of competitive advantage are achieved by creating new resources to achieve congruence with the changing environment (Eisenhardt and Martin, 2000) and emphasizes understanding how organizations develop new resources and capabilities to support business strategy in rapidly changing environments (Montealegre, 2002). While the RBV and dynamic capability model are intended to predict a firm’s ability to achieve competitive advantage and a firm’s ability to achieve superior long-term performance (Wade and Hulland, 2004) in stable or hyper-competitive environments, we offer an extension into a crisis setting involvement cooperative response rather than competitive positioning. Although it might be possible for a single firm to acquire new assets and develop new capabilities in response to rapidly changing high-velocity environments, crisis situations require not just fast response, but immediate response: delay in crisis response may result in a humanitarian crisis of a far larger magnitude than the damage caused by the crises itself (Schneider, 2005). A societal crisis situation requires that various organizations and agencies coordinate the deployment of existing assets and capabilities in new combinations in order to quickly respond to the crisis.

Coordination is the process by which resources and activities involving more than one entity are managed (Crowston, 1997). Recent research suggest that many organizations are moving away from conventional modes of coordination such as standardization, hierarchies and routines. to informal coordination and communication in order to satisfy new demands for speed, uncertainty and flexibility (Kellogg et al. 2006). Moreover, research points to considerable differences between coordination in stable and unstable environments (Faraj and Xiao, 2006; Majchrzak et al., 2007; Bigley and Roberts 2001): whereas in stable environments, group membership, tasks, roles, and knowledge tend to be specified ex ante, in unstable environments, antecedent conditions that facilitate coordination are limited, irrelevant, or in some instances, non-existent (Majchrzak et al., 2007). Crises situations are, by nature, unstable. In crises settings, the coordination of resources and activities is an arduous task (Majchrzak et al., 2007; Thomas and Fritz, 2006). Unlike in non-crises situations where team membership, tasks and roles can be determined well in advance, in a crisis, response team members need to adapt to uncertain task definitions in pursuit of multiple simultaneous purposes (Majchrzak et al., 2007). Effective crises management thus necessitates appropriate and coordinated actions among stakeholders (Bigley and Roberts, 2001). This leads to our third and final research question: how are resources coordinated across agencies/organizations during crisis response?

To summarize, our review suggests that little is known about IS asset and capability deployment as part of a bundle of crisis response resources, nor their coordination mechanisms, in crisis responses. By understanding how assets and capabilities are coordinated during crisis, and the central role played by IS both as a resource and as a coordination mechanism, organizations and government agencies can be better positioned to mount a swift response to future crises. It is our aim to address these issues by undertaking an analysis of Singapore’s responses to the SARS and Asian Tsunami crises. Our research question is threefold: what IS resources are needed in crisis response, how are these resources bundled with other resources, and how are they effectively coordinated?

## 2.2. Research approach

## 2.2.1. Data collection and analysis

Our study uses a qualitative case study approach (Eisenhardt, 1989) referred to as soft positivism (Madill et al., 2000; Kirsch, 2004). Using soft positivism enabled us to bring certain a prior expectations to the data analysis, consistent with positivist research, and yet also allowed certain explanations to emerge from the data, as is characteristic of interpretive research. Consistent with a positivist view (i.e., Dubé and Paré, 2003; Benbasat et al., 1987), we assume the phenomena to be relatively objective and as such, assume that we can present a factual account of the case. Yet, in order to examine constructs that are not easily separated from the contexts, we also draw from interpretivists (i.e., Klein and Myers, 1999) in building our model of resource deployment in crisis response. In particular, RBV theory would lead us to look for assets and capabilities as well as the activities taken using the resources such as presented in Montealegre (2002). Yet, the specific assets, capabilities and actions emerged from our analysis of the transcripts. This hybrid approach has been used in recent IS research (see, for example, Kirsch, 2004 and Ravishankar et al., 2009) and helps overcome constraints associated with relying exclusively on positivist, or interpretivist, case studies. Our form of soft positivism can be characterized as a multi-method research design employing a dominant type in which one method serves as the main approach – positivist case – with contributions from another approach – interpretive case analysis (Mingers, 2001).<sup>2</sup> Such multi-method designs recognize that research situations are complex and can be better understood by drawing upon different research paradigms (Mingers, 2001)

The study focuses on reconstructing how Singapore successfully deployed resources and capabilities in response to the SARS and Asian Tsunami crises and formulating an explanatory crisis response resource and capability deployment framework. We first interviewed 18 executives from Singapore’s Defense Science and Technology Agency (DSTA) between August and November 2003, to find out how capabilities were deployed during the SARS outbreak. In August 2005, we re-entered DSTA for two months to collect further information regarding crisis response to the Asian Tsunami disaster. During this time, we interviewed 15 interviewees that include senior managers and members from the crisis response team from both DSTA and Singapore Armed Forces (SAF).

Most interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed, with additional notes being taken when necessary. Interviews were based on topic guides customized for each interview. The list of sample interview questions is shown in Appendix A. These texts became the main corpus of the data used for subsequent analysis. Secondary data included reading the extensive news coverage of the crisis response efforts in Singapore, press releases from different government agencies, advisories for precautions and articles from multiple newspapers and other Internet publications in Singapore. We gained access to some internal reports (i.e., Leung and Ooi, 2003; Koh et al., 2003) which provided insights to the discussions during the crisis response meetings. For example, a key discussion during one of the meetings was to decide whether to involve the Ministry of Defense immediately in supporting the crisis response or simply rely solely on the auxiliary police force, which was traditionally designated to handle major crises. Various sources of secondary documentation are summarized in Appendix B.

For analysis, we first open-coded the transcripts, labeling statements as pertaining to some asset, capability, or action. Assets refer to human capital resources (e.g., managerial skills), organizational assets (e.g., culture) or physical capital assets (e.g., technology) (Barney, 1991) whereas capabilities refer to the ability to use some asset (Amit and Schoemaker, 1993). Just because one has the ability to do something does not imply that one will do it nor does it imply how one does it. Thus, ‘‘actions” describes the actual activities undertaken in the performance of some capabilities using some resources.<sup>3</sup> After this first general coding, we then made tables, one for each of the three general categories that listed the particular asset, capability or action discussed in one column and the quote or reference in the second column We also created a table for other statements that appeared significant to informants but could not be classified into the asset, capability or action categories. We then re flected on the tables and agreed upon meta-labels to describe some of the more specific assets, capabilities, and actions described by informants (for example, the ability to design a system using Visio as described by an informant would be given the more general label ‘‘ability to build and apply IT”). During our reflection of the tables, we also discovered that many of the comments in our ‘‘other” table were referring to coordination mechanisms, largely governance related. We then coded these more specifically into the crisis response organizational structure, the IT structure, and the informational structure categories. The resulting categorization scheme is depicted in Table 1. Our data analysis is thus positivist in the sense that we purposely set out to examine existing IS-related assets, capabilities, and actions, but interpretive in the sense that the specific bundle of resources and the coordination of the resources emerged from the data.

These elements of crisis response will be described in greater detail in our case analysis, following the case description below.

## 2.2.2. The SARS outbreak in Singapore: a national health crisis

The SARS outbreak in Singapore began on March 1st, 2003 when three travelers fell ill and were hospitalized, two in one hospital and one in another, with high fevers after returning from Hong Kong. Table 2 summarizes some of the key dates and events. For the first few days, the patients were treated in an open ward with no infection precautions because the highly infectious nature of the illness was not yet known. However, suspicions arose in one of the hospitals and on the 6th, the Ministry of Health (MOH) was informed by Tan Tock Seng Hospital (TTSH) that the two patients had developed atypical infectious pneumonia after traveling to Hong Kong. Within two days of being notified of the patients, the MOH set up a ninemember high level inter-ministry SARS task force, led by the Home Affairs Minister, Mr. Kan Seng Wong.

At the request of the MOH, the People’s Association, a statutory board that is in charge of over 2000 grassroots organizations, assembled 84 task force teams to assist in the crisis response. On 24th March 2003, contact tracing of SARS patients was begun, involving the identification of all places a patient had visited, and locating every person who had visited these places to assess their health. Tracing required the participation of many government agencies in Singapore: the Ministries of Education (MOE) and Manpower (MOM) provided contact information and promoted crisis containment awareness in schools and businesses, the Ministry of Community Development and Sports (MCDS) guided childcare centers on preventive and tracking measures, the National Environment Agency (NEA) helped manage the data on identifying and tracing contacts and Certis CISCO, a security service agency under the Ministry of Home Affairs (MHA), carried out the quarantine orders.

By the end of March 2003, Singapore had had more than 80 cases and four fatalities. The Ministry of Health requested the help of the Defense Science and Technology Agency (DSTA) to set up an operations center to support the 150 staff handling the contact tracing operations. DSTA was selected because of its prior experiences in developing and implementing national IT projects. Within two weeks, DSTA had developed an information system to support the large-scale contact tracing and data management activities at the operations center.

Several IT applications were adopted to track the movement of SARS patients. their contacts, and the health conditions of relevant staff and in-patients. Microsoft Visio<sup>TM</sup>, a diagramming program used to document and organize complex processes, was used to plot linkages between contacts at hospitals. Tan Tock Seng hospital was experimenting with Radio Frequency Identification tags (RFID) for tracing contacts. Using this system, every patient was tagged with RFID. Every time the patient came in contact with a reader at various points in the hospital, the location and time of visit was recorded. Webcams were installed at the citizens’ homes. As part of the quarantine order, MOH monitored the citizens by requesting them to appear before their in-home webcam on-demand.

The data collected by various agencies were kept in separate databases. An updated centralized database was crucial since the security office would rely on the updated list to issue the quarantine orders. As a result, DSTA developed a Case Management System (CMS) to manage data exchange across agencies. The CMS needed information from hospitals, MOE, MOH, general practitioners and Traditional Chinese Medicine practitioners. DSTA faced several obstacles in collecting the information, including identifying data sources, establishing formats, and resolving security issues associated with the information. Moreover, the handling of sensitive information such as the patient information, infection status, and relation to other patients or contacts had to conform to strict procedures and guidelines. For privacy protection, only the SARS crisis operations center and the data management group at MOH could access the CMS

To prevent the entry and exit of SARS patients into and out of the country, thermal imagers were used to detect fever, one of the earliest detectable symptoms of SARS. DSTA, Singapore Technologies Electronics, and Chartered Electro-optics developed a prototype of the Infrared Fever Screening System based on the thermal imagers widely used in the military as a screening device. Within a week, the equipment was deployed at various checkpoints. As a person walked past the system, an infrared camera captured the thermal emissions from the skin and the processed video images were displayed in real time with distinct colors set to indicate normal, feverish, or high fever. Probable patients were asked to go for a medical examination.

On May 30th, 2003, Singapore was removed from WHO’s list of SARS-affected countries. In all, there had been 238 SARS cases and 33 deaths. By most accounts, the Singapore government and the constellation of organizations that formed the response did an effective job in managing the spread of SARS and minimizing the damage. Nevertheless, not all were satisfied with the government’s efforts. Some lamented the tough quarantine measures following the government’s decision to invoke the Infectious Diseases Act,<sup>4</sup> allowing people who had contact with an infected person to be quarantined and monitored for possible signs of SARS for ten days – the virus’s incubation period. Even in a country where criticism of the government is conducted surreptitiously if at all, prominent journalists voiced their concern of hyper-response and ‘‘overkill” (see Chua, 2003; Menon, 2006). The Singapore government also faced criticism over the thermal scanners, with some questioning their accuracy (Chua, 2003) and the lack of attention to patient privacy concerns (Curley and Thomas, 2004). Others took issue with the intensive communication and the establishment of the SARS Channel,<sup>5</sup> wondering if such massive communication campaigns would be undertaken every time an issue garnered senior governmental attention. And yet others decried the seeming hysteria that the government’s response had invoked, with real estate agents passing out masks to clients, healthy individuals confining themselves to their homes, and medical professionals being restricted from practicing in more than one hospital. While the criticisms may have been warranted, the massive campaign to increase SARS awareness and enforce tough measures to address SARS appears to have created a herd mentality in which the majority of Singaporeans willingly followed the government’s directive and ignored the naysayers.

## 3. The asian Tsunami disaster

On December 26th 2004, an earthquake struck under the Indian Ocean, 250 kilometers northwest of Indonesia’s Island of Sumatra and triggered a Tsunami. The Tsunami devastated thirteen countries’ coastlines, leaving more than 280,000 people dead and millions homeless. Soon after the Tsunami struck those areas, more than 160 aid organizations and UN agencies began operations in these affected areas. The UN proposed to set up a coordination center in Singapore to coordinate all relief activities in the region. Table 3 summarizes the major dates and events in Singapore’s coordination of the relief activities.

On January 1st 2005, the Singapore Government organized a meeting with several government agencies to discuss issues concerning the set up of a Singapore Regional Coordination Center (SRCC). The DSTA was assigned the task of setting up SRCC’s IT infrastructure and applications to store and disseminate information. Even before the UN had agreed to the selection of Singapore Expo as the official site for the regional coordination center, the Singapore government decided to proceed with the system development, using the DSTA facility to store central servers. Within two hours of the Singapore Expo being officially confirmed as the SRCC location, DSTA had designed the network and server logic. And within 4 h, DSTA had completed equipment selection and purchase (e.g., network, server hardware and workstations). SRCC’s IT infrastructure provided a variety of services which includes the Internet, the electronic mail, the World Wide Web, the Directory Name Service, the Directory Services and Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol. The substantial network bandwidth, the high level of performance and functionality of desktop systems and servers enabled SRCC users to exchange updated crisis related data, such as the aid supplies delivery schedule and ground situation report, and collaborate with one another in carrying out the crisis relief activities

A maior IT application developed by SRCC was the web-based Crisis Case Management System (CCMS) which monitored the availability and demand of aid supplies and assessed how these supplies could be distributed. The CCMS could also be used to track SAF’s status of relief personnel deployment in those Tsunami-hit countries by using RFID to track personnel movement, send text alerts as well as messages to and from devices in the field. The urgency of responding to the crisis motivated the DSTA project team to work long hours and compress development processes in order to deliver a solution within 2 days. Installation work occurred between 2 a.m. and 9 a.m., when traffic volume was lowest, in order to minimize disruption to the relief operations.

While the development of the systems was ongoing, the SRCC set up a contact center to manage enquiry calls from victims’ families and friends. Psychologists from the Ministry for Community Development and Sports were at the center to provide counseling service to the needy. The National Volunteer & Philanthropy Center worked closely with the Ministry for Community Development and Sports to provide proper management of volunteering services. As the national coordinating body for volunteerism and philanthropy, the center was providing relevant information for public queries through a ded icated hotline.

On January 11th 2005, approximately 20 Non-Governmental Organizations began their operations in the SRCC.<sup>6</sup> Throughout the entire period, SAF’s 6th Division’s officers supported these offices’ operations which included receiving phone calls from donors, spreadsheet inputs, visual matching of supply and demand of aid supplies, and packaging and shipping of aid supplies. Besides the non-governmental offices, SAF also set up several operational centers to coordinate the crisis relief activities. The operational centers included the Ops Center, the Plan Center, the Situation Center and the Call Center. The Ops center provided effective monitoring and timely update of world-wide information on the Tsunami crisis, and updates of the aid supplies delivery schedule. For example, frequent communications were exchanged between the Singapore government and both the Indo nesian Military, and the Thai civil and military authorities to gather updates of the ground situations. Therefore, properly organizing the delivery of aid supplies by having updated information at SRCC emerged as an extremely important task. The Plan Center matched aids supply and demand, improved packaging of relief supplies and scheduling relief transportation. The Situation Center obtained situational updates from the ground and disseminated the situational updates to the relief personnel. The Call Center mainly provided information updates (e.g., aid requests and donation calls) received from the public. In the end, SRCC had handled close to 2000 out of the 10,000 tons<sup>7</sup> of aid supplies to Tsunami survivors in Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Maldives, Myanmar, and Thailand by UN during the first 20 days of the crisis

Not all of the decisions and actions taken in the Tsunami proceeded smoothly. Despite the updated information provided by SRCC, informants revealed that tons of medical and food supplies had piled up in the Banda Aceh’s airport for weeks because of distribution problems related to an inadequate supply of transportation and manpower. Informants also revealed that there were overcrowding problems at the airports in the northern area of Sumatra, Medan and Banda Aceh.<sup>8</sup> To make matters worse, US Marine helicopters buzzed into Medan, not to bring food in, but to take supplies away: aid had flooded into Medan and the supplies were stacked in disorganized piles near a warehouse at the city’s airport, an overwhelming amount beyond what was immediately needed in the area. Desperate to get the supplies to hard-to-reach areas on Sumatra’s west coast, CH-46 helicopters from the USS Bonhomme Richard launched an airlift to bring the aid back to their ship. The fragility of relief efforts was underscored by the temporary closure of the main, overstretched airport in Sumatra. On one occasion, a load of aid supplies fell from a US helicopter over the island’s city of Medan, hitting a shopping mall. Luckily no one was hurt. Also, the response organizations had difficulty coordinating with the many private organizations raising money and donations, such that there were over-supply of some supplies and undersupply of others.<sup>9</sup> According to an informant, ‘‘While we could take heart that there was a strong response to the tremendous humanitarian needs, there was a need for a more systematic, streamlined and efficient approach to private sector participation in humanitarian work. Perhaps an infrastructure for private sector for partnership could be created and be prepared for the next crisis.”

## 5. Key assets in the crisis response resource deployment framework

## 5.1. Information technology infrastructure

Several types of IT resources were utilized, ranging from equipments such as the laptops, electronic mail servers and data base servers to IT security software such as the firewalls and the anti-virus software. Without the existing infrastructure, the information would have had little reach. According to an informant, ‘‘several Singaporean government agencies utilized the e-government infrastructure and related resources to quickly bring the SARS outbreak under control. In particular, the system streamlined communications, information exchange, and data flow, and significantly eased collaboration among government agencies, private businesses, international agencies, and the public.” Several agencies already had in place video conferencing links that proved useful in coordinating between the Ministry of Health and the Ministry of Defense. One of the e-government infrastructures, the e-Citizen Portal, acted as a key information repository that possessed most of the initial contact information. Nevertheless the handling of such sensitive information had to conform to strict procedures and guidelines that had been developed as part of the e-government infrastructure. For privacy protection, only the SARS crisis operations center and the data management group at MOH could access the CMS. The CIO explained: ‘‘It was not a free-forall as far as the data was concerned. We had to conform to certain norms when handling such data. All data exchange is guided by government policies to protect the privacy of individuals. Data access in the system is tiered into multiple layers and access to tiers is controlled by levels of authority.” Overall, Singapore’s existing IT infrastructure provided the foundation for the crisis management effort, and not just in terms of its technical underpinnings. The IT infrastructure resources proved to be a critical antecedent for engendering collaboration, coordination, and IT knowledge across the involved entities to quickly develop capabilities in response to the crises.

## 5.2. Leadership

An important resource in the Singapore government’s response to SARS and the Tsunami was the leadership exhibited within various government agencies. While leadership often appears as an important resource (Preble, 1997; Montealegre, 2002), what is unique in a large-scale crisis is that multiple leaders are required. As such, unity and shared vision become paramount so that multiple leaders can act as one. According to a senior government official, ‘‘the containment of SARS was a result of distributed leadership at all levels, from the health care workers who had to make important decisions, to government officials who had to implement policy changes and coordinate with other agencies to ensure the new policies were successfully implemented. For instance, a critical decision made by the CIO of DSTA when asked to support the contact tracing operations was to suggest a system that would be scalable enough to support long-term needs. Even though the decision to develop and implement the Case Management System was risky in terms of meeting the demanding schedule, there was no doubt that the decision had eventually paid off.” In the case of Tsunami response, the Applications and System Development team manager also emphasized the importance of leadership: ‘‘There was strong sense of ownership in each working team. Even though there was more than one group performing the task, there was always one owner for each task. Such arrangement helped to eliminate any confusion and conflict among the groups.”

## 5.3. Collaborative network

The collaborative networks across government agencies proved important in addressing the SARS outbreak. The Singa pore government collaborated extensively with international agencies, such as the WHO, to better understand the nature of the disease – how it spread, how it could be contained, and how to help patients recover from it. Similarly, the response to the Tsunami disaster reinforces the important role played by close collaboration among organizations in humanitarian relief. Generally, aid organizations cultivate expertise in crisis intervention and develop extensive experience on the front lines of disasters through long-term development initiatives (Thomas and Fritz, 2006). Such networks, relationships, and know-how may reach into many parts of the world. For example, in coordinating the relief aids for the Asian Tsunami victims, DSTA was able to swiftly assemble SRCC’s IT infrastructure and applications because it was positioned in numerous collaborative networks that have been nurtured over the years: according to a DSTA manager: ‘‘We participated extensively in several collaboration projects, and leveraged our partner organizations’ expertise. One notable project was with several US partners (Boeing, Lockheed Martin, Longbow Limited, General Electric Engines and the US Army) to develop the AH-64D Apache Longbow Helicopters in 2002. The collaboration helped achieve a build-up of technological capability in Singapore with the technology transfer from the US. Such collaborations are important in helping to bring our IT expertise to the next level”. Thus, the leadership and collaborative network assets were critical to reaping benefit from the IT infrastructure asset.

## 6. Key capabilities in the crisis response resource deployment framework

## 6.1. Ability to build and apply IT

While the IT infrastructure was a critical asset deployed in the handling of both crises, the infrastructure in and of itself would have been inconsequential without the skills to quickly build and apply IT solutions. Existing solutions are unlikely to be available on the market for crisis response, namely because the nature of each crisis is so unique that vendors would not have a wide market for a specific solution. DSTA was able to build a system to trace individuals who had come in contact with infected SARS patients and a system to monitor the quarantined individuals. Another important IT application built by DTSA was the Infrared Thermal Fever Scanner which was deployed to detect symptoms of SARS. The CIO of DSTA commented: ‘‘The development of the Infrared Fever Screening System demonstrated how, as a community, we rose to the challenge and made the critical difference in a time of crisis. The team developed the concept for the system into a working reality in the shortest possible time. The work was urgent and intense, a test not only of our technical knowledge but also of our innovativeness and responsiveness.”

In the Asian Tsunami crisis, DSTA’s IT expertise also contributed significantly towards the development of SRCC’s IT applications. The DSTA supported the crisis relief operations center by developing applications to track the deployment of relief supplies and personnel. These applications helped to measure the effectiveness of the relief work and provided regular feedbacks to the relief agencies. One of the main reasons why DSTA was able to develop the ability to build and apply IT was because of its extensive participation in several collaborative projects with various partner organizations, leveraging each partner’s strengths, core competencies and expertise. An example would be the online portal project, ‘Government Electronic Business’ managed by the Ministry of Finance, Infocom Development Authority of Singapore and Singapore Computer Organization to facilitate procurement and revenue tender activities between the Singapore Government and her local and overseas suppliers.<sup>10</sup> Overall, we believe the key reason why DSTA was able to swiftly assemble SRCC’s IT infrastructure and applications is because it was well-positioned in numerous collaborative networks and these networks were important in developing DSTA’s overall IT competence. Thus the capability to build and apply IT was contingent upon the IT infrastructure and collaborative network resources.

## 6.2. Ability to recognize signals

The capability to recognize signals, which is based on attentiveness in assessing and detecting the risks associated with unexpected and potentially widespread threats (Ouarantelli. 1988), is crucial in influencing the speed of responding to a crisis. It reflects the ability of an organization’s alertness in looking out for possible danger or simply adopting ‘‘a policy of openeyed awareness” (Billings et al., 1980). Effective identification of the trigger for crisis is thus a key determinant of the organization’s ability to be vigilant during crisis response. During the SARS crisis, the early warnings of a pneumonia outbreak b WHO, the detection of SARS cases in Guangzhou and Hong Kong, and the alerts provided by TTSH of patients potentially developing pneumonia all served as warning signals. Although it would still be easier to ignore these signals than act upon them, deliberately choosing a wait-and-see attitude and hoping to stay clear of any involvement (Schneider, 2005), the Min istry of Health recognized the signals as significant threats requiring action, and, in the words of a health official. “immedi: ately issued a public warning to advise residents and visitors that if experiencing a sudden onset of high fever, along with coughing episodes or any difficulty in breathing, they were strongly advised to consult a doctor immediately." Likewise, in the early stage of Asian Tsunami crisis, the Singapore government raised its alertness and immediately prepared for crisis relief. According to one of the DSTA managers who was involved in the Tsunami response mission, ‘‘once the Tsunami struck and the damage was catastrophic, the Singapore government responded by immediately setting up a contact center to manage enquiries from victims’ families and friends. Psychologists from the Ministry of Community Development, Youth and Sports were on hand to provide counseling. Furthermore, several public sector agencies were gathered to discuss the setting up of a Crisis Coordination Center.”

In summary, vigilantly observing early signals is crucial since in some instances, governmental actors and organizational leaders do not readily perceive the need to intervene. Failure to recognize the signals can exacerbate the crisis and response (Watkins and Bazerman, 2003).

## 6.3. Ability to see the big picture

Related to the ability to recognize signals is the ability to see the big picture. Seeing the big picture involves an understanding that the tribulations of a small group may have significant repercussions well beyond the initially affected group It also entails understanding the risks of failing to act. Despite robust planning processes, many well companies are caught off-guard by disastrous events when a threat is recognized but not given priority (Watkins and Bazerman, 2003). In our case study, the Singapore government recognized its vulnerability and constraints as a small nation and while the initial SARS cases were few, the potential for widespread panic and potential harm was large, as were potential consequences to its economy. According to a government official, ‘‘Fighting SARS was a total government effort. All ministries were involved. But apart from the government, we also needed the key stakeholders to play their part in protecting themselves and their assets as an extended SARS period could have severe implications on the economy.” Similarly in its response to the Tsunami crisis, the Singapore government identified that the coordination of relief for all the millions affected was itself a major challenge that could leave even greater numbers dead or injured than the initial Tsunami.

In both instances, Singapore leaders recognized the signals and understood the big picture, and reacted quickly to avoid being trapped in a ‘failure of prioritization’ (Watkins and Bazerman, 2003). Overall, the Singapore government relied heavily on its existing assets and capabilities during crisis response. We next discuss how these resources and capabilities were acted upon during the crises responses.

## 7. Key processes in the crisis response resource deployment framework

This section presents the process through which the existing resources are acted upon during a crisis response. The three key actions are: the process of resolute informing, the process of gaining stakeholder commitment, and the process of agile mobilization of people and systems.

## 7.1. The process of resolute informing

The examples of SARS and the Tsunami both suggest that a critical aspect of large-scale crisis response involves the pro cess of communicating. Zuboff (1988) introduced the terms and notions of informating up and down, by which she meant that organizations can establish systems and processes to notify people upwards in an organization what is occurring downwards; and vice versa. With crisis management, the involved entities must report up, down and all around. The communicating practice must be resolute, by which we mean that information search and distribution time is limited and as such, the process must be highly focused and yet comprehensive. Each entity involved in the crisis response must take responsibility for specific information needs and then undertake a comprehensive gathering of information. In the SARS example, the Singapore government faced a considerable challenge in disseminating information given the sheer number of patients and potential individuals infected. One interviewee commented, ‘‘several communication challenges were presented. For example, little was known about the disease in the early phase of the outbreak, leading to considerable fear in the community. With few knowing much about the disease, people avoided public places and businesses were adversely affected. With the confusion, there were foreign reports that grossly exaggerated the crisis in Singapore. Such reports fueled the drastic fall in num bers of tourists visiting Singapore.” To address these challenges, the government set up a high level ministerial committee to coordinate across different agencies. The different agencies were able to focus on specific informing issues. For example, the Ministry of Finance provided the information on Central Provident Fund contributors, which accounts for virtually everyone in the workforce in Singapore. The Ministry of Education led every primary, secondary and tertiary institutions in their battles against SARS by providing daily instructions such as cleaning the schools, performing daily temperature checks and managing education efforts on SARS. The Ministry of Community Development and Sports provided public hygiene education via advertorials in the four official languages of Singapore to inform people about SARS. The Ministry of National Development conducted a survey to assess the level of public confidence in fighting SARS.

This process of resolute informing was enabled by the existing IT infrastructure as well as the ability to build and apply IT. In the Asian Tsunami crisis, the relief center in Singapore was essential in coordinating efforts across the Indonesian and Thai borders. This center served as an information hub, keeping track of aid pledges, tracking the arrival and distribution of supplies, and tracking the deployment of relief personnel. The four relief centers—the operations center, the plan center, the situation center, and the call center – managed to keep information flowing across hundreds of agencies. Here too, informing did not occur without problems. One informant mentioned, ‘‘The biggest challenge here is about making it possible for team members regardless of where they are located to gain immediate information about what is going on in the crisis event and the response. To the extent where they could not be much better informed if they stood in the relief centers themselves.”

Nevertheless, through the process of resolute informing, the Singapore government built public trust which helped legitimize the role of the Singapore government in addressing both crises. This is important since mishandling the situation might turn into a ‘‘legitimacy crisis,” where followers may withdraw support and loyalty to key decision makers (O’Connor, 1987). Through its active role in informating up, down, and all around, the Singapore government laid the groundwork for obtaining stakeholder commitment

## 7.2. The process of gaining stakeholder commitment

By having a transparent and full information disclosure policy, the Singapore government was able to make the stakeholders aware of the gravity of the situations and to build trust in its handling of the situation. For example Singapore’s Minister for Home Affairs commended Singaporeans’ commitment in response to SARS crisis in one of his public speeches: ‘‘The way Singaporeans reacted to the SARS crisis was also heartening – the courage and sacrifice of our healthcare personnel and the manner in which everyone, from taxi drivers to professionals, pulled together in the response effort.” In the Tsunami case, in addition to the public agencies, many private organizations galvanized around the crisis due to several reasons. Be: sides being a good samaritan, many of these organizations had witnessed how major disasters might interrupt the flow of business and inflict staggering economic losses. Therefore, they were determined to alleviate the economic impact of such disruptions. According to an informant, ‘‘It makes a lot of sense for private sector organizations who have major investments in these affected countries to participate in the crisis recovery efforts. They are more susceptible to any economic downturn in these countries arising from the Tsunami impact.” These organizations also experienced escalating pressure from the Singapore public and the international community to offer relief assistance and as a result, they had to act accordingly in order to be socially responsible and demonstrate a good global citizenship (Thomas and Fritz, 2006).

## 7.3. The process of agile mobilizing

After the crisis is identified, intervention must be timely (Augustine, 1995): the longer decision makers are engaged in searching for optimality, the larger the risk that events will run out of control (Preble, 1997). As such, the agile mobilization of people and resources becomes a crucial process in crisis response. One can mobilize people and resources without informing or gaining commitment, but it is highly unlikely that one can do this with any speed or agility without the preceding processes of informing and gaining commitment. Within a week of hearing about the initial SARS cases, the government had formed a task force and within 10 days of learning of six more cases, the task force had begun the process of contract-tracing and within two weeks of the outbreak, 84 task force teams had been established and were gathering and distributing information. Likewise, in the Tsunami crisis, within a week after the crisis, the DSTA had developed the infrastructure for the relief center and within two weeks, the relief coordination had begun with some 160 aid organizations. The agile mobilizing of people as well as technology was crucial to the response efforts. Interestingly, in the SARS crisis response, the initial emphasis was on the mobilization of people with the DSTA not brought into the response until almost a month of activity whereas in the Tsunami response, the mobilization of technology resources took an immediate role with an apparent recognition that the mobilization of people and aid would be more effective if the technology and systems were already in place.

## 8. The enhancement of existing assets and capabilities in the crisis response resource deployment framework

The actions described above forming the crisis response were instrumental in enhancing the existing base of resources and capabilities. Specifically, we noted enhancements to the collaborative network asset and to the IT development capability.

## 8.1. Collaborative network growth during crisis response

While the existing collaborative networks across government agencies were critical, these networks were also strengthened and expanded during the crises response. The knowledge gained from working together gave rise to the development of shared understanding among the agencies and helped coordinated the subsequent response activities. For example, learning to work with foreign agencies such as the World Health Organization who provided information on the SARS threat and several government agencies in several joint projects enhanced the process of resource mobilization during the Tsunami response. The collaboration between DSTA and several agencies during the SARS response allowed DSTA to locate the IT resources (expertise and applications) both within the organization and interorganizationally during the Tsunami response According to an informant, ‘‘DSTA was a member of a clinical consortium of Singapore laboratories that provided the systems for validation of diagnostic kits for the SARS virus and the diagnosis of blood samples. During the response process, DSTA collaborated with the World Health Organisation and several other government agencies, to exchange capabilities and data on infectious diseases activity. Then again during the Tsunami disaster, DSTA was the first field public health laboratory to be deployed at Banda Aceh as part of Singapore’s humanitarian relief efforts in response to UN’s aid appeal. The laboratory’s main role was to set up a system to monitor infectious diseases activity in Banda Aceh in support of the medical relief work and to protect the public health of the aid workers from several international aid organizations and Tsunami victims.”

## 8.2. Ability to build and apply IT to the ability to apply modular IT

Modularization, made possible through a system of codification, standardization, and an infrastructure to facilitate knowledge sharing and software reuse (Oshri and Newell, 2005), enables system designers to reuse, mix, and match software modules as the user requirements in heterogeneous systems evolve (Oshri and Newell, 2005). However, prior to the SARS crisis, the DSTA had not developed software using this approach. In the SARS outbreak, with the pressure of quickly developing the CMS system, the developers realized that they had to learn new development techniques. Said one of the development team members, ‘‘The team completed the designs of the network and server logic in less than two hours. We managed to complete the tasks within a short time because we were able to re-use the standard designs and components adopted in prior projects”.

DSTA mobilized developers with extensive prior experience working for the Singapore military who were able to develop the CMS using modules from the SAF Casualty Management System. Similarly, the DSTA, Singapore Technologies Electronics, and Chartered Electro-optics developed the Infrared Fever Screening System’s prototype in less than a week by adopting several modules of the thermal imager used in the military as a screening device. The modularization development technique used in response to the SARS crisis was then applied in the Asian Tsunami crisis response effort. DSTA’s software engineers created several templates during the development of CMS (for the relief agencies) adapted from the previous SARS project. According to one of the DSTA’s publication,<sup>11</sup> ‘‘Supernet proved to be an invaluable homeland security system used in earlier crisis response situations. It provides users with interoperable communication to effectively support mission planning and management of emergencies. Its purpose-built integrated command and communication vehicles, equipped with Supernet solutions, serve as mobile centers that can be quickly deployed for onsite management of crisis situations.” Learning a modular software development technique helped DSTA to reduce the duration of system development and allowed DSTA to deliver the IT infrastructure and applications much faster. The existing ability to build and apply IT, combined with the collaborative network and IT infrastructure, laid the foundation for the enhanced capability of modular software development.

## 9. Coordination mechanisms in the crisis response resource deployment framework

Coordination is the mechanism that enables an organization to transform existing resources into actions. While the model suggests that is the bundle of assets and capabilities as well as the set of actions, undertaken in a particular order, that are necessary for the crisis response, it is also informative to consider these specific relationships as presented in Table 4. The Table extracts from the previous analysis the coordination mechanisms that prove essential in binding the aspects of the model together.

Based on our analysis, the major coordinating mechanisms are organizational, informational, and IT structures. While standard operating procedures and face-to-face meetings might form the primary coordination mechanism in small scale, local crisis or during the very early phases of a crisis, as the number of involved parties increases and in time-sensitive, non-routine crises, coordination will largely involve structural techniques (Moynihan, 2008). Whereas most prior research focuses on the organizational structure (Moynihan, 2008; Lin et al., 2006; Dantas and Seville, 2006), our data suggest that the organizational structure of the crisis response effort interacts with information and IT structure to coordinate the response activities.

## 10. Crisis response organizational structure

The crisis response organizational structure refers to the coordination mechanism for organizing the people involved in the response. In our case, the crisis response organizational structure played a major role in the deployment of the resources and capabilities. This structure was not a permanent feature of the Singapore government, but was rather established specifically for the response. In the SARS case, the task force made the decision to have a simple ‘‘command and control” structure. This structure meant that all involved agencies would report directly to the SARS task force. The newly designed structure removed major portions of the extensive layers of bureaucratic government structures. Singapore’s flat ‘‘command and control” structure had all agencies reporting and sharing information through the crisis task force and eliminated delays caused by bureaucratic structures (Bailetti et al., 1994). According to an informant, ‘‘having the task force was useful in ensuring the timely and consistent flow of messages and information out to our local and overseas trade partners and citizens. This would ensure that the most accurate and relevant information on SARS was being conveyed. The task force had regular sessions with the media to update and share new information.” Also in the Asian Tsunami crisis response, the crisis management command team eliminated formal consultation, deliberation, and complex accountability procedures, allowing bureaucracy and exceptional flexibility to co-exist (Bigley and Roberts. 2001). The choice of the command and control struc: ture enabled the agile mobilizing of people and resources. It was a critical enabler of the resolute informing process. More over, such structure allowed information to be coordinated through a single body and quickly spread to others. The rapid information dissemination allowed stakeholders to be informed of the government’s interpretation of the signals. Stakeholders then chose to act in a manner consistent with the government’s warning, in essence, adopting a ‘‘shared understanding”. According to an informant, ‘‘Quite honestly, I thought the coordination of crisis relief activities was going to be a disjointed exercise, with people running everywhere and not sure of what to do. I was delighted with the leadership at the command center and with how well the coordination had turned out.”

## 11. Crisis response informational structure

Related to the crisis response organizational structure is the information structure. The informational structure refers to the protocol for information gathering, sharing and transparency across the disparate agencies and stakeholders. A high leve committee was set up so that information could be coordinated through one body and quickly disseminated to others. This structure promoted a communication protocol that enhanced Singapore’s agility in mobilizing its agencies and people (Faraj and Sproull, 2000). And by putting in place a policy of keeping information transparent at all times, stakeholders were aware of the gravity of the crisis situations and the significance of individual roles in the response, and this encouraged their commitment in the relief activities. One informant mentioned, ‘‘I felt one of the things we probably had done right in terms of communicating SARS information was perhaps never to provide over-reassurance. Also, to acknowledge uncertainty rather than claiming to be confident.” In addition, by gathering information from existing networks (i.e., WHO, UN, etc), the Singapore government managed to keep itself updated about the latest development of the crisis (i.e., the Singapore government was informed of the nature and possible symptoms of SARS) and the response progress.

The full information disclosure policy enrolled most if not all stakeholders into participating in crisis response (Turner, 1976). Heeding warnings from UN, WHO and the detection of SARS in Guangzhou and Hong Kong as well as the initial alerts provided by TTSH in the respective crises served as information inputs and triggered Singapore’s crisis information gathering process. By synthesizing the information collected and keeping it transparent, it further enhanced Singapore’s crisis planning and response, and more importantly, generated crisis awareness among the public. The involved agencies paid close attention to the overall information flow and extracted information that might affect their own functioning (Vaughan, 1990) Information structure had also acted as the coordination mechanism for developing Singapore’s ability to apply IT through resolute informing. For example, DSTA kept close contact with users in various agencies and as such, was able to rapidly build and modify systems as needed. A member of the Applications and System Development team also commented: ‘‘We traveled to SRCC to talk to the system users to gain a deeper understanding of the system requirements. We gathered information regarding Singapore Armed Forces’ work procedures which helped us to identify the features to be incorporated within crisis management system. In addition, our design also considered the public’s needs. For example, the call-ins that inquire about the status and locality of aid supplies.” Finally, the information transparency policy and open approach helped improve Singapore’s agility in mobilizing resources and created public awareness of the crises (Roberts, 1990). For example, the Singapore government set up a series of crisis communication programs and several agencies were asked to launch cam paigns to remind Singaporeans of the importance of keeping a SARS-free environment.

## 12. Crisis response IT structure

Similar to the previous two coordination structures, the IT structure also played a primary role in deploying resources and capabilities. IT structure (also referred to as IT governance) refers to the way that decision making rights concerning IT are distributed across enterprise stakeholders and the procedures followed in making and monitoring IT decisions (Peterson, 2004). Historically, decisions about IT were made through steering committees and working groups. In fact, the decision making process at DSTA was typically very slow. In the past, to implement even a small change in the system (e.g., add an e-transaction), one had to write a proposal and submit it for approval. However, during the crisis response, the governance followed was more akin to what Weill (2004) labels an IT monarchy: aided by its close relationships with vendors, DSTA was allowed to make quick decisions without having to follow the usual procurement procedures, which could take months, before a tender can be called. According to a DSTA manager, ‘‘With our webs of partners, suppliers and agencies, we were able to act quickly. Such relationships were crucial in times of crisis where rapid collaboration and exchange of information were essential.”

IT governance is typically very difficult to change and certainly to change abruptly (Weill, 2004). What made the governance change possible were two enablers: (1) the collaborative network of the DSTA staff and (2) the nation-wide IT policies over the preceding decades. In terms of the first enablers, the pre-existing knowledge of individuals within DSTA garnered through their collaborative networks was crucial in helping DSTA develop and evaluate alternative solutions. Having developed a complex IT ecosystem that included webs of public and private sector agencies, and people, DSTA leveraged the network to decide upon vendors and solution. In terms of the second enabler, the Singapore government’s strategic IT vision and nation-wide IT policies over the years were instrumental in convincing governmental agencies and leaders of the value of IT resources and capabilities. This vision and history helped thwart skepticism concerning the need to apply IT to the crisis response, enabling a more rapid mobilization of technology.

The move to an IT monarchy during the crisis response proved essential in linking the ability to build and apply IT with the transforming activity of resolute informing. Without the quick IT decisions, DSTA would not have been able to supply the tools that underscored the resolute informing process. For example, DSTA built the contract-tracing and Thermal Fever Scanner systems during SARS and aid-tracking systems during Tsunami crisis, both of which provided the information needed to respond but neither of which would have been developed so quickly had DSTA followed its normal processes for IT approval. Thus the flexible IT governance was central in enabling Singapore to deploy its IT resources and capabilities in response to the crises.

## 13. Summary of findings

We set out to examine the specific IS resources important to effective crisis response (research question 1), the way that the IS resources were bundled with other resources (research question 2), and the coordination mechanisms used to deploy the resources during crisis response (research question 3). In response to the first question, our findings suggest that IT infrastructure is a central resource in crisis response. Whereas past research using RBV in a competitive context suggests that infrastructure is not a valuable source of competitive advantage (Mata et al., 1995; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997), in the cooperative context where disparate organizations need to coordinate information, a strong and flexible IT infrastructure is highly valuable. Likewise, the development skills of IS personnel has largely been considered as a mobile and imitable resource and therefore, of relatively low value in competitive environments except where those skills involve organizationspecific knowledge (Wade and Hulland, 2004; Bharadwaj, 2000; Feeny and Wilcocks, 1998). However, in the cooperative context of crisis response, the skills of the IS personnel (the DTSA staff) was essential in enabling quick crisis response.

In response to our second question, the bundling of the assets and capabilities was important in increasing the value of the individual IS resources. For example, absent the IT development capability, Singapore would have had to bring in external IT staff, but external IT staff, no matter how skilled, would have lacked the extensive collaborative network that facilitated the fast development of crisis information systems in both the SARS and Tsunami crisis response efforts. Thus, the IT skill itself has greater value in the context of the collaborative network just as the infrastructure was dependent upon having leadership that recognized the value of IT. In addition, refusal or inability to recognize signals often leads to inadequate design of crisis response systems (Turoff, 2002). Having leadership that was willing and able to recognize the signals early and see the big picture, enabled the IT staff to begin development of crisis response systems early in the crisis, which facilitated the processes of resolute informing, gaining stakeholder commitment, and agile mobilizing. Thus, the IT assets and capabilities were inextricably tied to the non-IS-related assets and capabilities, exhibiting the characteristics of enhancing complementarity (Wade and Hulland, 2004).

And concerning our third question on the coordination of resources in crisis response, our findings suggest that quickly establishing a crisis response structure is very important in linking existing assets and capabilities to the response activities and that IT and organizational structures complement each other and work hand in hand during crisis response. Traditionally, disaster planners and managers advocate top–down, centralized command and control during emergency situations (Moynihan, 2008; Rosenthal and Kouzmin, 1997). However, some studies have argued that the centralized command and control may not be a good model, instead loosening rather than tightening the command structure may be more appropriate (Lin et al., 2006). In the SARS and Tsunami examples, the agile mobilization of people and technology was enhanced by the single-layer structure. Moreover, the top–down command structure also improved the overall efficiency and interoperabilit of deployment and distribution-related activities.

## 14. Implications

There are several theoretical implications of our research. First, our paper provides compelling evidence of the central role of IS resources in crisis responses. IS figures prominently as an asset (the IT infrastructure), a capability (development skills), a transforming activity (resolute informing), and a coordination mechanism (the IS and informational structures). Past research on the role of information systems in crisis response has focused on design principles to ensure the most accurate collection and dissemination of information (Turoff et al., 2004; Zhang et al., 2002; Zerenier et al., 2007) as well as strategies to achieve information sharing (Dantas and Seville, 2006) and challenges related to information flow (Hale et al. 2006) Our research provides a holistic framework within which one sees the multi-faceted role of information and information systems in crisis response. Our framework of resource deployment during crisis response might serve as a means to evaluate crisis preparedness in organizations and as a springboard to future research into intra as well as inter-crisis learning.

Second, our study extends research on the resource based view (RBV) of the firm to a cooperative context, demonstrating that certain resources considered less valuable in competitive contexts are highly valuable in cooperative contexts. Wade and Hulland (2004) propose that certain resources are more valuable to firms in stable environments whereas other resources are more valuable in unstable (dynamic, hypercompetitive, volatile) environments. As such, researchers need to examine the attributes of value in different environments – resource mobility, for example, may have high value in one context but low value in another, as might rarity or imitability. Indeed, in a crisis setting involving cooperative response across agencies, resource mobility has value – if a resource is inextractable from its given agency, it cannot be of use to the crisis response effort. Moreover, resource imitability also provides value, such that as one agency can intentionally be called to imitate what another is doing. Our research was concerned with the bundle of resources and the coordination of the resources as applied to crisis response. Future research might consider this issue of the value of the resources in cooperative settings, with a view towards defining attributes of resource value as applied in crisis response settings.

Third, past research has identified the important role of resource complementarity, but RBV itself has not shown how assets and capabilities are acted upon and coordinated in affecting firm performance (Wade and Hulland, 2004). Our research makes progress in filling this gap by linking coordination mechanisms with the actions taken on bundles of assets and capabilities. Some of the assets and capabilities that are important in crisis response might be largely dormant during non-crisis periods. Existing, well-understood, coordination mechanisms provide an organization the ability to quickly pull these resources and capabilities into action. This is an important issue since it is reported that many organizations underestimate the importance of coordination and neglect to take steps to minimize the coordination problems (Heath and Staudenmayer, 2000) that may hinder themselves in meeting the urgent demands for flexibility, speed and uncertainty in a dynamic environment. Our case highlights that, in crisis response settings, outside-in and inside-out resources are dependent upon one another. The outside-in resource (collaborative network asset) was closely tied to the inside-out resource (IS development capability). Whereas research in the competitive context suggests that outside-in resources provide more value than inside-in resources, particularly in unstable environments (Wade and Hulland, 2004), our work suggests that these two work in tandem in a cooperative context. Future work might delve more deeply into the relative importance of outside-in as opposed to inside-out or spanning resources in cooperative yet unstable environments or in other types of crisis such as financial crises, terrorism, or corporate sabotage.

Fourthly, whereas previous research emphasizes some of the resources found central in our study, such as leadership (Augustine, 1995) and recognizing signals (Quarantelli, 1988), our study examines the actions made possible from the assets and capabilities and how the bundle of resources and capabilities are deployed during crisis responses. Assets and capabilities must be put into action in order for a crisis to be addressed. We found that three specific actions enabled effective response: resolute informing, gaining stakeholder commitment, and mobilizing people and technology. While in noncrisis situations, an organization might have the time to assess what capabilities it needs to develop and set out purposefully to develop these (i.e., Montealegre, 2002), in a crisis situation, the new capabilities are created more surreptitiously, as part of the response effort. In the absence of the resources present in our cases, one might question whether new assets and capabilities must be swiftly acquired and learned, or whether an alternative bundle of assets and capabilities could generate the same set of actions. Future research should consider these dual alternatives. Our cases do present evidence of swift learning, but the learning as mentioned was neither intentional nor radical, but rather incremental enhancements to the existing base of IT resources and capability.

Lastly, our work provides insight into the governance mechanisms appropriate for coordinating crisis response. Coordination theory, which resembles and builds upon systems theory and cybernetics, is concerned with identifying the interdependencies among actors, goals, and activities and establishing mechanisms to improve task performance (Malone and Crowston, 1990; Crowston, 1997). Assessing interdependencies and establishing processes to manage dependencies are primarily information processing activities; as such coordination theory provides a conceptual link between the use of IS and organizational form (Crowston, 1997). Our work suggests that governance forms the backbone of the ad-hoc coor dination required in crisis response. Whereas governance is typically considered a stable construct within a given organization (Weill, 2004), the same organization might use different governance mechanisms for different types of decisions concerning IT (Xue et al., 2008). Our results highlight that during crises, responding organizations must put into place a crisis structure, information structure, and IT structure. In both cases, the Singapore government relied upon much flatter governance structures than was typical for the government agencies involved, and allowed the IT unit (the DSTA) to make independent decisions concerning the needed technology solutions. Moreover, from the first crisis (SARS) to the second (Tsunami), the Singapore government appears to have learned that rather than first establishing the crisis organizational structure and information structure, and then soliciting the help of IT to establish a complementary IT structure (as was the case in the SARS response), that the response was able to proceed more expeditiously by first setting up the available IT structures and then developing the information and organizational crisis response structure around the IT structure (as in the Tsunami response). Future research might explore the conditions under which crisis decision makers should establish an IT structure around an organizational structure or the organizational structure around the IT structure. Factors such as the strength of the existing ties across involved agencies, the flexibility of the existing technology, and the skill of involved IT personnel are certainly important considerations.

## 15. Limitations and conclusion

The findings of this study should be viewed within the context of its limitations. First, we relied upon the interviewees retrospective view of events (memory traces). A disadvantage of retrospective responses is that they might be affected by errors of recall (Glick et al., 1990). To minimize errors of recall, we have observed the following procedural safeguards: (1) interviews explicitly focused on major events that could be recalled more reliably. (2) We used cross-checking of reports between the stakeholders without privileging one over another. Both of these safeguards have been recommended to minimize errors of recall (Glick et al., 1990). Second, our case focuses on two crises, both of which can be considered natural disasters. Mitroff (1994) identifies seven forms of major crisis events, one of which is natural disasters. Other of the six forms might necessitate different resources, activities, and coordination mechanisms. Lastly, we have developed a model of crisis response resource deployment. Our model covers many components of crisis response, any of which could themselves form the basis of a full study. Our intent was to show the interrelationships among the multiple components in the model rather than to delve into great depth on any single aspect of the crisis response. By providing a broad model, we hope to encourage future research to examine the individual components within the model in greater detail.

Despite its limitations, we believe that our study should be of interest to managers in both the public and private sectors that are directly involved in handling emergency situations: crisis management has become part and parcel of today’s organizational operations and organizations must be prepared for rare and unexpected situations. Our analysis revealed the existing assets of information technology infrastructure, leadership and collaborative network and the existing capabilities of the ability to build and apply IT, the ability to recognize signals and the ability to see the big picture are central to the crisis response. Coordinated via the given crisis response organizational, informational, and IT structures, these key assets and capabilities are converted into concrete actions. The key actions taken during the crisis response were the acts of resolute informing, gaining stakeholder commitment and mobilizing people and IT. These actions then created enhanced resources and capabilities, via the learning that occurred during the response as well as across crisis response incidences. We hope that the analysis, framework and insights presented in this paper will spark interest among other IS scholars to study the role and management of information and information systems in crisis response. Researchers could look more specif ically at a particular element in the framework developed in our paper or the interactions among elements. For example, one could examine the process of informing during crises, and look deeper into the relationship of quantity and quality of information disclosure and the impact on stakeholder response, both positive and negative. Or one might investigate different information network structures and how the form of the network, both social and technical, influence the agility of the mobilization process. In short, many research opportunities exist in the area of information, IT and crisis response. Given the importance and relevance of crisis response to organizations and society, we encourage scholars in IS to pursue research in this area.
