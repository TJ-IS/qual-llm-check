# Solving misfits in ERP implementations by Solvin

Joost A. A. van Beijsterveld\* & Willem J. H. van Groenendaal<sup>†</sup>

\*BDO Audit & Assurance B.V., PO Box 757, 5000 AT Tilburg, The Netherlands, email: joost. van.beijsterveld@bdo.nl, and <sup>†</sup>Tilburg University, PO Box 90153, 5000 LE Tilburg, The Netherlands, email: w.j.h.vgroenendaal@uvt.nl

Abstract. The gap between the organizational needs and the extent to which an ‘off-the-shelf’ enterprise resource planning (ERP) system can meet these is called a mis<sup>fi</sup>t. A framework is developed to distinguish actual from perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. This is used to analyse the ERP implementation at four small-sized and medium-sized enterprises. The results show that they prefer to adjust the ERP system to their business processes when needed but often unnecessarily change the system to solve perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The framework is a <sup>fi</sup>rst step to prevent this unnecessary work in the future.

Keywords: ERP, mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, customization, workarounds, process adaptation, mis<sup>fi</sup> acceptance

## INTRODUCTION

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) is the most comprehensive of all enterprise wide systems a company can implement, and failing to realize all its bene<sup>fi</sup>ts can be very costly. A topic discussed from the start has been ‘when should an implementing company adjust its business processes to the ERP system and when should it adjust the ERP system to the business pro cesses’. This gap between the organizational needs and the extent to which an ‘off-the-shelf ERP system can meet these is called a mis<sup>fi</sup>t, and implementations are often unsuccessfu because of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (Yen et al., 2011).

Resolving mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can be achieved by either customizing the ERP system or by adapting the processes of the organization to <sup>fi</sup>t the requirements of the ERP system (Hong & Kim, 2002). Many studies (mostly on large organizations) advocate adjusting the processes, because cus tomization is risky, costly and reduces maintainability (Zach & Munkvold, 2011). Another often used argument is that the ‘off-the-shelf’ solutions contain best practices (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2002) and that it is wise for a company to use these. However, a best practice is a vendor’s description of how a speci<sup>fi</sup>c business process is to be performed in an optimal way (Holsapple et al., 2005; Ekman & Erixon, 2009). These are, however, not general standards on how to run your business, but based on working with previous customers. One of the predecessors of ERP,

Material Requirements Planning (MRP) systems, led to the same discussions between users and developers and to serious mis<sup>fi</sup>ts between company needs and what MRP packages had to offer (Swan et al., 1999). Then too it was argued that companies should adjust to the system, which cost many implementing companies dearly.

Off-the-shelf ERP has several advantages: quick delivery, incorporation of commonly used practices, professionally produced technical solutions, broad testing and fewer errors (Holsapple et al., 2005). However, because of these incorporated practices, there is hardly ever an exact <sup>fi</sup>t between the ERP package and the processes of the implementing organization (Holsapple et al., 2005; van Groenendaal & van der Hoeven, 2008).

Fosser et al. (2008) argue that solving mis<sup>fi</sup>ts by modifying the business processes should not lead to losing uniqueness or (in other words) competitive advantage, that is, having unique business processes for their speci<sup>fi</sup>c environment (Olsen & Saetre, 2007). This holds especially true for many small-sized and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) working in niche markets; adapting those business processes that make the company unique to the standardized ERP system could be fatal for SMEs. This view is supported by contingency theory of organizationa adaptation, which claims that there is no single best way to design organizational structures (Donaldson, 2006). The best way is contingent upon the internal and external situations of the company. Companies constantly adapt their organizational structure by moving out of mis<sup>fi</sup>t (low performance) to <sup>fi</sup>t (performance and effectiveness), by adjusting the way they do business to changes in their environment. This will differ per company, which would be an argument for more customization instead of less.

According to Haddara & Zach (2011), little research has been carried out on ERP system use in SMEs. However, according to Buonanno et al. (2005) and Newman & Zhao (2008), <sup>fi</sup>ndings on ERP implementations in large enterprises (LEs) cannot directly be applied to SMEs. Impor tant differences between SMEs and LEs are as follows: SMEs have fewer resources, SMEs have limited information technology (IT) expertise and SMEs acquire their strength from being unique (Buonanno et al., 2005). Research on how SMEs handle mis<sup>fi</sup>ts is limited.

There is another important problem that has not been solved yet, especially for specialized SMEs. When a company identi<sup>fi</sup>es a mis<sup>fi</sup>t because the ERP system requires a different way of working, it is unclear whether this is an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t – the system does not do what is required – or a perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t, the company does not properly understand the way the ERP system works. This distinction is important to avoid excessive spending. This research will show that this risk can be minimized by applying an appropriate decision procedure. The aim is to provide insight into mis<sup>fi</sup>t resolution strategies of SMEs by identifying the causes of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and understanding how SMEs handle these misfits.

Mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are a clear sign that there is a lack of alignment. To minimize organizational resistance and thus decrease the chance of an unsuccessful implementation, a company should choose a system that best <sup>fi</sup>ts its organizational structure (van Everdingen et al., 2000; Morton & Hu, 2008; and Somers & Nelson, 2003). It is evident that an appropriate selection minimizes the number of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts.

The focus will be on mis<sup>fi</sup>ts between business processes and the ‘traditional’ off-the-shelf ERP system within SMEs in the Netherlands, where about 93% of the installed base are ‘off the-shelf’ solutions. To rule out bias due to differences between packages, the four case companies studied all use the Microsoft Dynamics NAV ERP system (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Furthermore, to guarantee contemporary results, only cases where the ERP system was implemented less than 5 years ago were considered.

First, a literature review will be conducted. The theory resulting from this review will be tested by conducting a multiple case study where each case can be viewed as an individua ‘experiment’ that con<sup>fi</sup>rms or discon<sup>fi</sup>rms the theoretical framework (Eisenhardt, 1989).

The results of this research will allow SMEs to be better prepared for an ERP implementation. Implementers will be able to discover actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts earlier, and by distinguishing actual from perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, companies can avoid organizational stress and save costs. If an implementing company is knowledgeable and wants to take the correct decisions, the current literature does not offer a clear methodology to support these decisions. This research tries to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by developing a decision strategy for implementing companies.

The second section discusses the literature on mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The third section discusses perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The section on Framework for Analyzing Mis<sup>fi</sup>ts develops a framework to identify different types of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The <sup>fi</sup>fth section discusses resolution strategies. Section on Research Design describes the empirical research design. The section on Case Analyses uses multiple case studies to test the usefulness of the framework. The eighth section discusses the <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and the last section contains the conclusions.

## LITERATURE REVIEW OF ENTERPRISE RESOURCE PLANNING MISFITS

There is no common understanding of mis<sup>fi</sup>t in ERP implementations (Pries-Heje, 2006). According to Rosemann et al. (2004, p. 440), a mis<sup>fi</sup>t manifests differences between two worlds. One world re<sup>fl</sup>ects the organization’s needs, the other the package’s capabilities. A mis<sup>fi</sup>t can be viewed from two perspectives, functionality really needed is missing – a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency – or the ERP system imposes an inef<sup>fi</sup>cient way of working – an imposition (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). In both cases, a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is actual and a legitimate issue. Current literature contains various de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of an ‘ERP-related mis<sup>fi</sup>t’.

The way a company organizes its processes is determined by its business strategy, and a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is a poor <sup>fi</sup>t between the IT strategy supported by, and implemented in, the ERP system and the business strategy pursued by the company. This is in line with Soh et al. (2003) who de<sup>fi</sup>ne a mis<sup>fi</sup>t as a gap between the requirements of the organization and the features of the ERP package. This de<sup>fi</sup>nition is neutral in the sense that there is no value judgement. Wang et al. (2006) and Maurer et al. (2012) on the other hand de<sup>fi</sup>ne a mis<sup>fi</sup>t as a mismatch between the ‘best practice’ of the ERP system and the practices used by the organization. Liang & Xue (2004) speak of a mis<sup>fi</sup>t when there is a gap between the values brought in by the best practices of an ERP system and the existing values of an organization. The use of the term ‘best practices of an ERP system’ clearly is a value judgement. A clear de<sup>fi</sup>nition of a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is needed to understand what a misfit is and how it should be handled

All de<sup>fi</sup>nitions speak about a mis<sup>fi</sup>t, misalignment or mismatch (all synonyms) between the ERP system and the business. But what exactly is meant by the ‘ERP system’ part and by the ‘business’ part? Some de<sup>fi</sup>ne a mis<sup>fi</sup>t as a gap between the organization ‘as it is’ and the ‘to be’ implemented ERP system, while others de<sup>fi</sup>ne a mis<sup>fi</sup>t as a gap between what the orga nization wants and the possibilities the ERP system offers. An important reason to implement an ERP system is to obtain new functionality, which the organization needs to overcome another mis<sup>fi</sup>t, the mis<sup>fi</sup>t between its current way of working and the demands of its (future) environment. Therefore, the ‘business’ part is broader than the organization ‘as it is’; Soh et al. (2003) call this ‘structures embedded in the organization’. These are the assumptions, norms and values of the organization. Soh et al. (2003) de<sup>fi</sup>ne the ‘ERP system’ as the structures embedded in the ERP system, which are the assumptions, norms and rules built into the technology by the developer.

Key to a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is that an exact match between the embedded structures of an ERP system and those of an organization is missing, ranging from minor inconveniences to critical de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in functionality (Maurer et al., 2012). However, practitioners have to deal with another problem also: is a mis<sup>fi</sup>t an actual one or is it only perceived as such (normally) by end users? Actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts require a different solution strategy than perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts do.

The unit of analysis of an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t has to be the organization, not the individual user (Seddon et al., 2010; Maurer et al., 2012). More ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency on the organizational level (no mis<sup>fi</sup>t) may lead to some users needing more time to complete their tasks, which they could easily perceive as a mis<sup>fi</sup>t. Therefore, a concise de<sup>fi</sup>nition of actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t is a mismatch between the structures embedded in the ERP system and the structures embedded in an organization utilizing the system (as re<sup>fl</sup>ected by its strategy, procedures, rules and norms), leading to inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency or missing important functionality on the organizational level.

Note that features of the ERP system that are present but the company does not require are not problematic and thus not mis<sup>fi</sup>ts as long as they do not interfere with using the system.

## Identifying actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts

Sia & Soh (2007) made a typology of actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts based on institutional theory and system ontology, and differentiate between ‘imposed structures’ and ‘voluntary structures’.

Imposed structures are structures based on external demands on the organization by, for example country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c or industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c authoritative sources. Industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are structures imposed by professional and/or industry institutions that exert normative authority on organizations through guidelines on professional conduct, industry accreditation or recogni tion. Imposed country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c mis<sup>fi</sup>ts arise because of unique regulatory, social and/or cultural practices that are not supported by the ERP system, for example not meeting tax compliance requirements (Sia & Soh, 2003; Soh & Sia, 2004).

Voluntary structures are structures where the organization has a choice. Its creation is based on the strategy chosen to solve the mis<sup>fi</sup>t between the organizational structure and the require ments of its environment. For a company, this will normally be based on its market approach, using history, experience, strategy and management preferences. For this, organizations develop distinctive routines and structures deemed crucial for its competitive advantage. This results in company-speci<sup>fi</sup>c mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, where the structures embedded in the ERP system do not support the company’s voluntary structures.

Using Wand & Weber’s (1993, 1995) systems ontology, Sia & Soh (2007) also distinguish ‘deep structures’ and ‘surface structures’ in ERP systems. The deep structural elements are considered core, and the absence of such elements in a system leads to major de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies. A deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t occurs when ‘real-world things, properties, states, and transforma tions are missing or incorrectly represented in the system' (Sia & Soh. 2007. p. 572), Examples are inventory items, order number and inventory replenishment options. A surface structure ‘is concerned with how real-world meanings are conveyed through the interface between the information system and its users’ (Sia & Soh, 2007, p. 572). A surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t occurs when the interface of the ERP system differs from the way users access, view and input data. These mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are less severe and are often – but not necessarily – related to such issues as convenience. Examples are format of documents and screen <sup>fl</sup>ows. Com pared with deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, it is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to determine whether or not a surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t is an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t.

"Combining the institutional and ontological perspectives leads to four types of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts: imposed-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, imposed-surface mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, voluntary-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and voluntary-surface mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. According to Sia & Soh (2007), these are in order of severity from high to low. How ever, from a business point of view, a voluntary-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>t can easily be more severe than an imposed-surface mis<sup>fi</sup>t, because it may be crucial for a company’s competitive advantage. Imposed only implies that the company has to solve it, not that it is crucial from a business point of view.

Soh et al. (2000) clustered the most common mis<sup>fi</sup>ts into three categories based on a traditional software application perspective: data, process and output. This categorization is widely used by other researchers of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts too (Hong & Kim, 2002; Wang et al., 2006; Sia & Soh, 2007; Wu et al., 2007; Yen et al., 2011). Note that all three can be either a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency or an imposition.

A data mis<sup>fi</sup>t is the inability of the ERP system to capture various object attributes or documents into the database of the ERP system (Yen et al., 2011). A data mis<sup>fi</sup>t therefore is a deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t.

A process mis<sup>fi</sup>t or functional mis<sup>fi</sup>t occurs when there are incompatibilities between the orga nizational and ERP system requirements in terms of processing procedures (Soh et al., 2000). An example is the incapability of an ERP system to model the work<sup>fl</sup>ow of the organization. A process mis<sup>fi</sup>t is a deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t also.

An output mis<sup>fi</sup>t occurs when there is an incompatibility between the organizational and ERP system requirements in terms of the data presentation and information content of the output. An output mis<sup>fi</sup>t clearly is a surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t

Strong & Volkoff (2010) have augmented the categorization of Soh et al. with three more categories: ‘role mis<sup>fi</sup>t’, ‘control mis<sup>fi</sup>t’ and ‘organizational culture mis<sup>fi</sup>t’. These are part of the latent structure of Wand and Weber. They use the term latent, because the latent structure is a second-order structure that arises from the physical, deep or surface structures of the ERP system. For example, functionality programmed in the ERP system leads to certain roles, control structures and/or organizational culture. They do not take material form but are also part of an ERP system (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). The latent structure has a substantial impact on ERP system implementation success/failure.

## Consequences of actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts

A good <sup>fi</sup>t between the requirements of the company and those of the ERP system increases the chance of a successful implementation (Hong & Kim, 2002; Wang et al., 2006; and Morton & Hu, 2008) and also increases the organizational bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Seddon et al., 2010). Actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can have various negative effects, ranging from lack of integration, limited visibility and loss of control to decreased productivity and increased costs (Maurer et al., 2012).

Mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can have various consequences at the strategic, tactical and operational levels of an organization (Table 1). The consequence of a mis<sup>fi</sup>t depends on the type of mis<sup>fi</sup>t that occurs. For instance, an incompatible input data format mis<sup>fi</sup>t may lead to an employee not working properly and/or generating incorrect data, leading to incorrect decisions on a higher organizational level (Yen et al., 2011). Wei et al. (2005) call this the cascading effect of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. Table 1 combines and augments the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of Yen et al. (2011) and Wu et al. (2007) on the consequences of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts for each organizational level. According to Wu et al. (2007) and Yen et al. (2011), the consequence of a mis<sup>fi</sup>t on the strategic level is the need to change business processes or organizational structure. So, it is assumed that the organization should be adjusted to the ERP system and not the other way around. However, according to the contingency theory of organizations (Donaldson, 2006), this is a premature conclusion; for an SME’s distinguishing processes, the ERP system has to be adjusted in order to maintain its competitive advantage.

## PERCEIVED MISFITS

Gattiker & Goodhue (2005) state that it is important to make a distinction between actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and those that are only perceived as such but do not expand further on this point. For an implementing company, it is important to know whether a mis<sup>fi</sup>t quali<sup>fi</sup>es as actual or not. Legitimate issues are actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and will occur in every organization, while perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts depend on the ability of the people within the organization and the implementation team to identify mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. Identifying mis<sup>fi</sup>ts that are actually not can jeopardize the implementation and/or the acceptation of the system, and solving those costs money and results in unnecessary adjustments now and in the future. Not handling them properly might result in users rejecting the system.

## Causes of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts

Assume that an implementing organization identi<sup>fi</sup>es a collection of mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The empirical prob lem then is to identify which are actual and which are perceived. There is a perceived misfit in case some stakeholder in the implementing organization wants to change the system in order to make it <sup>fi</sup>t the process – or the other way around – while this is not necessary to actually achieve the performance required. In the literature, there is evidence for the following sources of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts: resistance to change, ignorance and wishes.

## Resistance to change

Because ERP system implementations are more or less disruptive changes, every implementation faces resistance that might lead to perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. No matter how good the technical ERP system performs, people have to be motivated to use it and have the knowledge to work it effectively (Seddon et al., 2010).

Resistance to change is when employees refuse to work according to the structures embed ded in the ERP system because these deviate from the way things were performed before, regardless of whether the new way of working leads to bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the company as a whole. Studies that mention this difference between actual and perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts all address this to resistance to change (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Pries-Heje, 2006; Haines, 2009). Haines (2009) found evidence that a relationship exists between change management and the amount of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts caused by resistance to change and concludes that not addressing organizational resistance leads to more perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. When not properly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such, perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are solved by customizing or working around the ERP system, while this is actually not needed.

## Ignorance

Proper knowledge sharing between experts (consultants, key users, etc.) and end-users will decrease the amount of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts caused by not understanding the business processes and the processes embedded in the ERP system (Xu & Ma, 2008), in other words, reduce ignorance. Ignorance can be prevented by involving (key) users and having a proper knowledge transfer (training) between consultants, key users and the end users.

Not having suf<sup>fi</sup>cient knowledge about the ERP system leads to users not being able to develop and evaluate design suggestions during the implementation, users not knowing how to use the system correctly after the system goes live and users not knowing what the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts are (Pries-Heje, 2008). So, ignorance is a source of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts in ERP implementations.

Pries-Heje (2008) and Wu & Wang (2006) state that the knowledge-level end users affect their attitude and satisfaction, possibly leading to resistance to change also. Being more experienced and knowledgeable about the new ERP system is likely to cause more positive attitudes towards the ERP system (Abdinnour-Helm et al., 2003). This indicates that there is a link between mis<sup>fi</sup>ts caused by resistance to change and mis<sup>fi</sup>ts caused by ignorance.

## Wishes

Users have wishes with respect to the way the ERP system should work. If these wishes are not ful<sup>fi</sup>lled, users may indicate this as a mis<sup>fi</sup>t, which is not necessarily true. If a wish for additional functionality does not increase ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency (de<sup>fi</sup>ciency) or not result in missing functionality (imposition), there is no actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t. So, wishes for new functionalities my lead to perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Wishes may be partially attributed to resistance to change, when a user wants the new system to function more or less as the legacy system did. Current literature does not explicitly ad dress wishes as a source of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. The question ‘why it is needed’ is important here (Daneva, 2004). So, wishes can also cause resistance to change, leading to perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## F RAMEWO R K FO R AN A LY SI NG MIS FI TS

The various perspectives on mis<sup>fi</sup>ts discussed in the preceding text have been used to formulate an analytical framework to categorize mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. There are three sources of actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and fou mis<sup>fi</sup>t categories, of which three – deep structure, surface structure and latent structure – are related to actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and one covers perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (Figure 1).

An actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t affects the deep, surface or latent structure of the system and can therefore be categorized as such. Within the deep structure, input data and process mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can be distinguished; within the surface structure, output data mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can be distinguished; and within the latent structure, all mis<sup>fi</sup>ts related to the environment can be distinguished. Figure 1 combines the mis<sup>fi</sup>t framework of Yen et al. (2011) with the typologies of Sia & Soh (2007) and Strong & Volkoff (2010). Yen et al. proved that their framework is applicable, but the framework of Figure 1 contains valuable additions based on our own research.

Based on the framework of Figure 1, an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t between the embedded structures of the ERP system and those of the organization has a source and belongs to a category. This classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is important, because different actions are required for different sources and categories. With the three categories, deep, surface and latent structures, and the three possible sources, country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and company-speci<sup>fi</sup>c, nine combinations are possible, and each of these actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts can be either imposed or a de<sup>fi</sup>ciency (Section on Literature Review of ERP Mis<sup>fi</sup>ts).

## RESOLUTION STRATEGIES

There are two major ways to solve mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. An organization can choose to restructure the embedded structures of the ERP system (customization), and an organization can choose to change its own organizational structures and adapt to the structures of the ERP system (Brehm et al., 2001; Soh et al., 2003). Apart from these two extremes, an organization can choose to accept a shortfall in ERP functionality, or it can create a workaround to provide the functionality needed without touching the script of the ERP system (Soh et al., 2000). Actual implementations normally use a mixture of all four strategies. These four options will be discussed next.

## • Customization: changing the embedded structures of the ERP system

Alignment can be achieved by adapting the system to the organization, also known as cus tomization (Luo & Strong, 2004), so reversing the choices made by the vendor (Wang et al., 2006). Brehm et al. (2001) list nine different forms of ERP adjustment. Two – con<sup>fi</sup>guration and bolt-ons – do not require any modi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the package. Con<sup>fi</sup>guration is setting the system parameters and thus is part of the embedded structure of the ERP system. A bolt-on is a standardized piece of software on top of the ERP system, which together can be seen as a standardized solution, so it is actually part of the embedded structure of the ERP system.

The other seven ERP adjustments are customizations ranging from relatively small adjustments, such as creating new screen masks for input/output data to the most severe form of package code modi<sup>fi</sup>cation. This is in line with the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t; that is, there is a gap between the embedded structures of the ERP system and the embedded structures of the organization, leading to the system becoming inef<sup>fi</sup>cient or missing important functionality.

Despite the growing awareness that ERP customizations are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult and have cost implications, many organizations perform at least some customization (van Groenendaal & van der Hoeven, 2008; Haines, 2009), because these structures embedded in the organization are strategically important (Haines et al., 2006; Zach & Munkvold, 2011).

Customization downsides are longer implementation time, problems with upgrades, vendor software maintenance and having minor bugs (Davis, 2005), and increases in complexity of the system, because all processes are interconnected (Wang et al., 2006). Furthermore, if not carried out properly and for the right reasons, they result in budget overruns, delays and regularly even in project failure (Haines et al., 2006). However, they are also the main source of ERP-system improvements for ERP developers.

When applied properly, the main advantage is that the way of working through which a company distinguishes itself from its competitors does not change, so the uniqueness of the organization’s market approach can be retained (Buonanno et al., 2005; Fosser et al., 2008; Haines, 2009), which supports the contingency theory of organizations mentioned in the preceding text

## • Workarounds

A workaround uses the functionality of the ERP system different than the developers intended to achieve functionality required (Boudreau, 2003). Although workarounds do not change the ERP system or the business processes, ‘workarounds usually have negative impacts on productivity and organizational controls and undermine potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from integration’ (Soh et al., 2003, p. 82), and often slow down processes and increase the possibility of making errors (Haines et al., 2006).

## • Accept mis<sup>fi</sup>t

Accepting a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is in fact compromising on the requirements of the organization (Soh et al., 2000). It leads to missing the required functionality but also does not require making adjust ments to the ERP system or business processes, or creating workarounds.

## • Changing embedded structures of the organization

According to Gattiker & Goodhue (2002), there are two reasons for changing existing busi ness processes. First, the ERP system is incapable of modelling certain business practices used by the organization. Second, the implementing organization believes the processes embedded in the ERP system are superior to the existing business processes. A distinction has to be made between strategic and nonstrategic business processes. For the latter, adapting to the ERP system could lead to resistance to change but will not impact performance. However, a strategic business process should be aligned. In case a core business process is changed to accommodate the embedded structures of the ERP system, the alignment between the business process and the business strategy decreases, which will impact the ERP implementation, as well as the business performance, negatively (Gattiker & Goodhue, 2002; Buonanno et al., 2005).

According to Somers & Nelson (2003) and Haines (2009), adapting the processes to the ERP package promises a higher return on investment, compared with customizations. However, these conclusions are not based on solid empirical research. Holsapple et al. (2005) and van Groenendaal & van der Hoeven (2008) disagree and mention the risk of losing competitive advantage.

## Choice of resolution strategy for actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts

A company is most likely to customize the ERP system for imposed-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>ts and most likely to adapt the business processes for voluntary-surface mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (Soh & Sia, 2004; Sia & Soh, 2007). Their research shows that imposed mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are more severe than surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, and therefore, a customization is easier to justify for imposed structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts than for voluntary structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. Customizations for imposed-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>ts often cannot be avoided because, for example a country’s regulations have to be obeyed. Therefore, ERP vendors normally adapt the system to imposed mis<sup>fi</sup>ts quickly because it affects many of its customers. For voluntarily acguired structures. an organization has more freedom to change the reguirements

Also imposed-surface mis<sup>fi</sup>ts cannot be ignored, and therefore, a company has to customize or create a workaround. The choice depends on whether the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of a customization outweigh the workaround efforts needed to ful<sup>fi</sup>l the imposed requirement.

For voluntary-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, organizations have the option to adapt to the ERP system. The choice should depend on whether the strategy of an organization is at risk. If so, the organi zation should choose to customize (Yen et al., 2011). So, whether a process is strategic or not (as discussed by Gattiker & Goodhue (2005) and Haines et al. (2006)) only plays a role for mis<sup>fi</sup>ts where an organization has a choice.

Because solving mis<sup>fi</sup>ts in the deep structure by customizing the ERP system has larger implications than customizations in the surface and latent structures, an organization has to avert customizations in the deep structure as much as possible, emphasizing the importance of a good <sup>fi</sup>t between the (wanted) organizational structure and the structure embedded in the ERP system. Only when the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of customizing the deep structure are greater than the costs and dif<sup>fi</sup>culties associated with it, or when an organization has to comply with an imposed structure, customizing the ERP system can be justi<sup>fi</sup>ed. Note that for surface structures, customization is normally less risky, costly and complex.

## RESEARCH DESIGN

The data for the case study were collected by reading documentation and conducting semi structured interviews at each company. For each case, data triangulation was applied to increase validity (Guion, 2002; Yin, 2003). A case study protocol was made upfront, in orde to increase the external validity and reliability of this research (Yin, 2003).

Four cases – called A, B, C and D – were analysed; for details, see Table 2. Case A was used to determine the usability of the protocol, but no adjustments were needed.

The aim was to interview for each case the head of IT, the controller and at least one key user of the implementing company, the ERP vendor and the consultant. This was achieved with the exception of company D (Table 2). Each interviewee received a list of topics and related ques tions upfront. Based on the course of the interview, additional questions were asked, so the interviews were semi-structured. Each interview was reported back to the interviewee in order to avoid misinterpretation and receive feedback. To increase reliability, a case study database was created. and a chain of evidence has been maintained: this to be able to trace back the steps taken during the research.

Because the written documentation kept by companies about the mis<sup>fi</sup>ts reported during the implementation is limited and interviewees do not remember all mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, it was not possible to obtain a complete list of all individual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, for each case, a complete picture of the most important and the most occurring mis<sup>fi</sup>ts could be established. (A complete list is available from the authors.) For cases A to D, 15, 18, 32 and 17 (groups of) mis<sup>fi</sup>ts were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, respectively. Interviewees remember, and reports contain, detailed information on the most important mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, that is, those crucial for business. Those that occurred many times are remembered as groups, think of reporting options, layouts and coding. As such, the mis<sup>fi</sup>ts collected have a bias towards those considered more serious.

Studies on large organizations often advocate that an ERP system should not be customized, because it is risky, costly and leads to limited maintainability (Zach & Munkvold, 2011); process adaptation is the preferred solution for solving mis<sup>fi</sup>ts. However, an important reason for mos mis<sup>fi</sup>ts at SMEs is that SMEs acquire their strength from distinguishing themselves from competitors (Quiescenti et al., 2006). This may require a set of unique business processes (deep structure) that are not supported by the standard ERP system, even not in combination with industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c standard software.

The case companies solved actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts mainly (54.5%) by customization and workarounds, not by process adaptation (only once), despite the disadvantages. The four SMEs studied here all preferred a vanilla implementation (Table 2), but none managed to achieve this. Workarounds (25%) seem to be a good solution when the functionality is really needed, but customization is too expensive or not feasible. Keeping their uniqueness by customizing the ERP system or using a workaround is in line with the contingency theory on organizational adaptation that states that companies are constantly solving another companyspeci<sup>fi</sup>c mis<sup>fi</sup>t, meeting changing environmental/market requirements. This corresponds with the reason most mentioned for customization: because the functionality is needed for our way of doing business. For instance, company C, one of the leading valuation <sup>fi</sup>rm(s) in the Netherlands, stated ‘If you are the best, why should you change your way of doing business? However, other factors could play a role also, like suf<sup>fi</sup>cient internal knowledge to handle customization.

As customization does, a workaround leads to having the required functionality withou changing the business processes or customizing the ERP system. Despite the down sides, the case companies consider the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of workarounds to outweigh its disadvantages. The most cited reason to solve an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t by creating a workaround is that it provides an acceptable – not optimal – solution, and customization is not needed, indicating that a workaround is a cheaper but less <sup>fl</sup>exible alternative.

The surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts show a different pattern. These are by de<sup>fi</sup>nition output related. Poor output quality or accuracy (56%) and incomplete output data formats (31%) are the main reasons for surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, and almost all (88%) are at the tactical level and none at the operational level. A bit more than one-third of these mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are accepted, and more than half is solved by customization.

Overall, actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts were solved mostly by customizing the ERP system (55%), followed by creating a workaround (25%). Both resolution strategies lead to achieving the required functionality, so it can be concluded that for most actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (80%), the case organizations wanted to keep their ‘unique’ way of doing business. Only for a limited number of actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (14%) – all surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts – companies compromised on the requirements and accepted it.

According to Sia & Soh (2007), a deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t should be solved by customization or process adaptation. However, the results of this study of SMEs show that 96% of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed mis<sup>fi</sup>ts in the deep structure were solved by either customizing the ERP system or creating a workaround, not by adapting the business process. Yen et al. (2011) found that customizations in the deep structure have larger implications than customizations in the other structures. Therefore, if companies are willing to deal with these implications, they belief the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the customization outweigh the disadvantages. Only for surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts (output) are organizations willing to compromise on requirements and accept some surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts.

However, when not accepting a surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>t, the case companies did not use process adaption. These results contradict the conclusions of Somers & Nelson (2003) and Haines (2009) that adapting the processes to the ERP package promises the highest return on investment, compared with customizations.

One should, however, keep in mind that ERP systems for SMEs are less complex than those for LEs, which have a much larger product and geographical spread, so SMEs have a smaller span of control. This will affect the decision-making on adjusting the ERP system or adapting the processes.

All four cases show perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts caused by the main drivers: resistance to change, ignorance and wishes. An example of the latter is the fact that company D was used to having many different overviews that were not available in Dynamics NAV. The system developers had to program and adjust (customization) the standard overviews to meet these user demands. The experts quali<sup>fi</sup>ed this as perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, and in hindsight, the company did the same when interviewed.

Although there was considerable agreement about the categorization of actual and perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, the experts had dif<sup>fi</sup>culties determining to which category a perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t belongs. A possible reason for this is that the mutual relationships between the categories are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to assess by experts. Almost every perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t was placed into one of the three main categories ‘resistance to change’, ‘ignorance’ and ‘wishes’, so the extension of the analytical framework for actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts with the perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t drivers, resistance to change, ignorance and wishes, seems a valuable one. However, the experts were not able to identify moderating effects.

The empirical evidence indicates that the companies understood that some mis<sup>fi</sup>ts were only perceived as such, which is supported by the fact that more than half were solved by unconsciously choosing no solution (29%) or consciously accepting the mis<sup>fi</sup>t (26%). However, 45% were still solved, work that was unnecessary and could have been prevented. So, if the case companies had identi<sup>fi</sup>ed these mis<sup>fi</sup>ts as perceived from the start, they could have saved time, resources, complexities and so on. Unfortunately, no information is available on money, time and effort spent on this. Furthermore, this result is mitigated by the fact that the case companies sometimes chose to solve a perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t by customizing the ERP system or creating a workaround to accommodate the resistance to change or to prevent users having to adapt to something new.

## CONCLUSIONS

Small-sized and medium-sized enterprises implementing ERP clearly choose adaptation of the ERP system over adapting business processes as recommended in the IM literature. This supports the contingency theory of organizations that states that companies are constantly adapting their processes to meet changing market conditions, that is, solving the mis<sup>fi</sup>t between its way of working and what the market requires. To regain <sup>fi</sup>t and performance, an organization will adjust its voluntary structures. Our results suggest that the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties with implementing an ERP system by SMEs may be caused by the ‘best practices’ embedded in the ERP system being a mismatch with the voluntary structures of the company and not necessarily the company not understanding what the ERP system has to offer. Therefore, distinguishing actual mis-<sup>fi</sup>ts, that is, mis<sup>fi</sup>ts that need to be resolved, from perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts is of the utmost importance.

For imposed mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, which are always actual, companies are required to <sup>fi</sup>nd a solution because they have to comply. In the empirical part of the research, only one imposed-deep mis<sup>fi</sup>t based on municipal requirements (similar for cases B and D) was identi<sup>fi</sup>ed and solved by customization. All other mis<sup>fi</sup>ts were due to company-speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements and as such are voluntary structures. The ERP package chosen meets to a large extent the country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c and industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c requirements, which seems reasonable because doing so is a clear market advantage for the ERP vendor.

For mis<sup>fi</sup>ts based on voluntary structures, companies have a choice, and at the start, they all preferred a vanilla implementation. However, their business strategy and market requirements required the companies to customize the ERP system to meet these. Once the goal of a vanilla implementation has been abandoned, a company has no clear lead on where to stop. With the use of the framework in Figures 1 and 2, experts reached substantial agreement – according to Fleiss’ kappa – when classifying mis<sup>fi</sup>ts as actual or perceived, making it easier to select a resolution strategy. If a mis<sup>fi</sup>t is the result of a mismatch between the voluntary structures and the ERP system, the company needs to evaluate if this voluntary structure is needed to regain or maintain <sup>fi</sup>t. If not, the structures embedded in the ERP system should be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient (and thus, it is a perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>t). In this way, the amount of work can be minimized, which has clear advantages. The companies chose customization to solve voluntary-deep structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts relatively more often, than for surface structure mis<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Roughly half of the mis<sup>fi</sup>ts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed across the four cases were actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, and there was substantial agreement between the <sup>fi</sup>ve experts who categorized the mis<sup>fi</sup>ts about the category each actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t belongs to – deep structure, surface structure and latent structure. This indicates that the different drivers of an actual mis<sup>fi</sup>t are clear and applicable. Therefore, it can be concluded that the analytical framework is able to distinguish actual from perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts in ERP implementations at SMEs. When used properly, this can save substantive money and time.

With perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as such, implementers are better equipped to give arguments against the system changes asked for to solve these. This can be tackled by appropriate change management and asking the question ‘How does this add to realizing our market strategy?’ Because there was only fair agreement among the experts on the causes of perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts – resistance to change, ignorance and wishes – and no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant support for moderation between causes, formulating the appropriate change management strategy remains challenging.

The framework clearly helps an implementing company to better understand its own choices. It contradicts the general belief that the voluntary structures need to change to meet the structures embedded in the ERP system. The consequence for IM research on ERP (and other enterprise wide) systems is that the contextual conditions for implementations should be based on the contingency theory of organizations in order to determine whether the structures embedded in the ERP system or the voluntary structures of the company need to be adjusted.

## Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First of all, the list of identi<sup>fi</sup>ed mis<sup>fi</sup>ts is not exhaustive. It can be assumed that ex post most actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, because these are legitimate issues leading to the problems mentioned during the interviews and/or documented. However, the information about perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to come by. The perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts identi<sup>fi</sup>ed for the case companies strongly depend on the limited number of interviewees. Documentation was only of limited help, because perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts are less often documented. Therefore, not all perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed.

Second, the resolution strategy ‘process adaptation’ is possibly underexposed in this research. When asked about process changes to accommodate the embedded structures of the ERP system, interviewees often could only indicate that some processes have been changed but were not able to give concrete examples. This is also re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the available documentation, changes in the software were better and more often documented. Therefore, for actual mis<sup>fi</sup>ts, there is most likely a bias towards the strategy customization and creating a workaround, and for perceived mis<sup>fi</sup>ts towards accepting the mis<sup>fi</sup>t.

Because the results of this study are based on only four cases at SMEs in the Netherlands using MS Dynamics NAV, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings cannot be generalized to all SMEs. However, clear guidance for future research as well as for handling mis<sup>fi</sup>ts in implementations has been given.
