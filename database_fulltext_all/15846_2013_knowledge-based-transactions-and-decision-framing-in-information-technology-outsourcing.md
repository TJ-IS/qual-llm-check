# Knowledge based transactions and decision framing in Information Technology Outsourcing

Amit Jain <sup>a,1</sup>, Raymond-Alain Thietart <sup>b,</sup>⇑

Keywords: Expropriation Stickiness Capabilities Knowledge-based transaction costs Prospect theory IT outsourcing

## a b s t r a c t

Knowledge based transaction costs (KTC) are knowledge related costs associated with the transfer of an information technology (IT) firm process outside its boundaries to a contractual partner. In this paper, we show that these knowledge based transaction costs are omitted variables in studies of firm boundaries that may reduce the likelihood of outsourcing. KTC arise from the risk of expropriation of firm knowledge by their contractual partners. In addition, KTC arise from the stickiness of knowledge, as transfer tacit and complex knowledge is both difficult to transfer and difficult to assimilate. Finally, KTC decrease with the knowledge and capabilities that contractual partners have, as this increases the partner firm’s ability to assimilate knowledge transferred. While all three sources of KTC lower the likelihood of outsourcing, the way managers frame the outsourcing decision is also important. As a result, managers may engage in outsourcing of IT processes even though they risk greater expropriation hazards in order not to lose out on higher expected outsourced performance. We validate these arguments based on a study of 180 IT processes from firms listed on the French (CAC40) and British (FTSE100) stock exchanges.

## 1. Introduction

Industry surveys and prior research (Hirschheim and Lacity, 2000) indicate that resources and core competencies (Jain, 2011; Prahalad and Hamel, 1990), information systems costs (Aspray et al., 2006; Rottman and Lacity, 2006), mitigation of contractual hazards (Aubert et al., 2004; Susarla et al., 2009, 2010), skills, activities interdependencies and firm-level characteristics (Aubert et al., 2012), knowledge (Dibbern et al., 2008; Kogut and Zander, 1992), and production costs (Ang and Straub, 1998) influence information technology (IT) sourcing. Even though a lively debate has taken place regarding the relative importance of knowledge and of contractual hazards as determinants of the boundary of the firm (Foss, 1996a,b; Kogut and Zander, 1992), transaction cost studies do not provide consistent evidence in its support (David and Han, 2004; Karimi-Alaghehband et al., 2011), and empirical knowledge based studies remain relatively sparse. Furthermore, a number of scholars have recently called for an endogenous Information Technology Outsourcing (ITO) theory where practice and behavioral considerations are also taken into account (Lacity et al., 2011). Accordingly, in this study, we propose that knowledge based transaction costs and managers’ decision framing affect the decision to outsource and the performance that follows.

We contribute to ITO literature by developing a model of knowledge based transaction costs. We define knowledge based transaction costs as costs related to the transfer of knowledge during the process of outsourcing. We identify three types of knowledge based transaction costs (KTC). First KTC are determined by the stickiness of IT knowledge as tacit and non-codified knowledge is costly and difficult to transfer (Conner and Prahalad, 1996; Kogut and Zander, 1992; Szulanski, 1996). Second, knowledge based transaction costs increase when a partner firm has relatively less knowledge and expertise, or absorptive capacity (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990; Dibbern et al., 2008; Szulanski, 1996), and it is more difficult to transmit information from one firm to another. Finally, knowledge based transaction costs are related to the perceived risk of expropriation of knowledge transferred (Liebeskind, 1996), as the outsourcing firm will incur costs to safeguard itself. Through a consideration of expropriation, our model of IT outsourcing embraces the possibility of opportunistic behavior and the behavioral assumptions of transaction costs economics. Further, our model gives due credit to the rational of the knowledge based view through a consideration of stickiness and firm capabilities. Using this model of KTC, our research reveals that what are commonly understood to be two opposing theories: transaction costs economics (TCE) and the knowledge based view (KBV), are in fact complementary to each other (Heiman and Nickerson, 2002).

Our second contribution to information systems research is that we incorporate the behavioral assumption of decision framing into a firm’s boundary decision. Consider our baseline argument that rational managers economize on their knowledge based transaction costs by insourcing IT processes within the firm. Given this, we observe that managers may violate rational choice to outsource IT processes with high knowledge based transaction costs. We propose and show that this observation is consistent with risk taking behavior in a situation of a perceived loss (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). Managers may decide to outsource activities with higher knowledge based transaction costs in order to avoid the expected performance loss from in-sourcing relative to outsourcing. We show that the framing effects of a potential lost opportunity for higher performance may offset any savings on future higher costs of knowledge related transactions and that this results in outsourcing

We believe these additions, of knowledge based transaction costs and of managers’ decision framing contribute important and new perspectives to ITO, and that they will help decision makers in their reflections on ITO in a number of ways. For one, in addition to taking into consideration production costs and opportunism, managers need to address strategic knowledge based concerns. For example, our KTC framework pushes managers to investigate the characteristics of knowledge that flows out of firm boundaries following outsourcing, and how this affects firm competitiveness. The KTC framework pushes man agers to envision the challenges that will arise with respect to the transfer of IT processes to contractual partners. Is IT process knowledge ‘‘sticky’’, that is, complex and tacit, and difficult to communicate to contracting firms? Is communication of sticky IT process knowledge facilitated by the absorptive capacity and capabilities of supplier firms to rapidly get up to speed and become productive? Even though there may be no contractual hazards and the perceived cost related benefits of ITO are high, outsourcing may be inadvisable if transfer of processes to the supplier is characterized by high knowledge based transaction costs. Finally, our study indicates that managers need to weigh KTC concerns against the framing of outsourcing as a gain in productivity and performance in the future. Managers need to be aware of the framing of their choice, and of its competitive implications. Framing might induce risk taking behavior that leads to long term economic suboptimal outsourcing decision.

In sum, this research brings into focus a number of fundamental knowledge based and behavioral concerns pertaining to outsourcing that escape analysis when the focus is limited to production costs and to the threat of contractual hazards.

## 2. Theoretical foundations and hypotheses

TCE provides a powerful explanation of sourcing decisions in which contractual hazards and risk of opportunism are key drivers of outsourcing. Contracts cannot specify all possible contingencies, and since environmental conditions change, this results in contractual hazard and risk of opportunism. Firms anticipating greater contractual hazards incur greater costs exante to safeguard their interests while outsourcing. According to TCE, when costs of governance and production are lower in the market, outsourcing is the preferred option; else in-sourcing is preferred. Furthermore, transaction costs increase with asset specificity as a firm makes transaction specific investments (Williamson, 1999).

A number of studies have used arguments from transaction cost economics in the study of ITO and boundaries of the firm For one, characteristics of IT activities, notably asset specificity and uncertainty, and firm level particularities such as knowledge intensity, influence the level of outsourcing (Aubert et al., 2012, 2004). Second, while transaction costs play an important role in the ITO decision, production costs play an even bigger role, as shown in a study of ITO by 243 US banks (Ang and Straub. 1998). This suggests a shift in focus from transaction costs to production costs, In this vein, a study using transactioncost, knowledge-based, and measurement theories (Poppo and Zenger, 1998) analyzed outsourcing of information services in 152 companies and concluded that a theory of firm boundary choice and of outsourcing needs to integrate arguments from each of these theories and that a model emanating from one theory only is insufficient, In addition, a study of 190 information system service projects (Mayer and Nickerson, 2005) found that contracting difficulties arising from opportunistic concerns, measurement costs, and interdependence affect the decision to hire or to contract for knowledge workers. Another study, based on 405 service contracts from an information technology firm (Mayer and Salomon, 2006), stressed the interrelationship between capabilities and transaction costs

While contractual hazards encourage internalizing transactions, strong technological capabilities improve a firm’s ability to govern transactions and thus increase the likelihood of outsourcing (Mayer and Salomon, 2006). Consistent with review studies (Carter and Hodgson, 2006; David and Han, 2004; Karimi-Alaghehband et al., 2011; Lacity et al., 2009), these researches indicate that TCE provides a limited explanation of empirically observed outsourcing decisions and that there may be other omitted variables in the analysis of ITO decisions. In this study, we propose that knowledge based transactions costs and managers’ decision framing are significant omitted effects that could potentially improve the predictability of ITO

We define knowledge based transaction costs as knowledge related costs that are associated with the transfer of a firm IT process outside its boundaries to a contractual partner. As Conner and Prahalad (1996: 484) point out ‘‘Frictions between economic actors can occur without opportunism, because of inevitable, irreducible differences in their knowledge.’’ These frictions, we postulate, are knowledge based transaction costs. We identify three sources of information systems knowledge based transaction costs: (1) Expropriation of IT based knowledge processes; (2) Stickiness of IT processes; (3) Relative knowledge and capabilities of IT supplier firms. By taking into consideration these three effects and in particular the risk of expropriation of valuable knowledge, our framework integrates opportunism with knowledge based effects (Heiman and Nickerson, 2002) for ITO. In this way, our theoretical framework reconciles with critics of the knowledge based view (Foss, 1996a) and differs from classical knowledge based arguments. We develop below the influence of each of these knowledge based transaction on the ITO decision.

## 2.1. Expropriation

Each time a firm outsources an activity, it incurs the risk of expropriation of proprietary firm knowledge. For instance, a firm transfers valuable knowledge to a supplier firm following outsourcing. Given this, what stops the supplier from capitalizing on this knowledge to further its own ends?

Contracting is particularly effective in safeguarding rents originating from those assets where ownership can be assigned unambiguously by property laws and where the assets are clearly observable and have a finite productive capacity. In these cases, expropriation can be easily detected (Liebeskind, 1996). Not all firms’ IT assets, however, are characterized by clearly defined property rights and are observable with respect to their usage. Property rights to knowledge such as patents, copyrights and trade secrets are costly to write and enforce, and they suffer from a narrow definition under law (Shapiro and Varian, 1999). Since transacting involves the transfer of valuable and proprietary knowledge to one’s contractual partner firm, it opens the door to the risk of expropriation. Given this, the governance mechanism that provides better protection from expropriation has a greater likelihood of being adopted.

Information technology based processes are particularly vulnerable to expropriation as IT process knowledge is largely intangible and weakly protectable by property rights (Teece, 1986). Given this, firms have certain organizational capabilities that make them particularly effective towards protection of proprietary knowledge from expropriation as compared to market contracting (Liebeskind, 1996; Mayer and Nickerson, 2005). For one, firms provide for better incentive alignment mechanisms and knowledge protection given incomplete contracting. Since knowledge is characterized by lack of contractibility, when residual rights to control (Grossman and Hart, 1986; Hart and Moore, 1990) are distributed among different transacting parties, these parties have incentives to use them opportunistically in their own favor. Given this, firm governance unifies ownership of knowledge with other kinds of assets and provides for better alignment of the incentives of different concerned parties, decreasing the likelihood of opportunistic behavior.

A second organizational mechanism that enables firms to be particularly effective against expropriation is the ability to write employment contracts that give rise to an authority relationship between an employer and an employee (Liebeskind, 1996; Simon, 1951). Through this authority relationship and rules governing the activities of organizational members, actions of individual employees can be restricted in ways not possible by a market contract for human capital services (Masten, 1993). For one, IT employee contracts may limit the activities of employees uniquely to the current employer. This restricts spillovers of knowledge outside firm boundaries. In addition, confidentiality and non-disclosure clauses place restrictions on how freely employees may discuss firm activities with non-firm personnel. A firm may, in fact, place limits to the extent to which knowledge is shared between employees within the firm itself, thus limiting the extent of expropriation. Finally, noncompete clauses in employment contracts limit the possibility of organizational members to work with competing firms, and by limiting employee mobility, also limit the risk of opportunistic expropriation of proprietary firm technology and knowledge.

A third organizational capability possessed by firms which enables the protection of IT process related knowledge from expropriation is the ability of the firm to ‘‘reorder awards over time’’. The employment contract of a firm may contractually bind an employee with ‘‘golden handcuffs’’ (Milgrom and Roberts, 1992) by deferring payments an employee receives for her efforts. Stock options and pension plans with delayed encashment of benefits are two forms of such limitations to employee mobility, and thus barriers to leakage of firm knowledge.

In sum, firm governance reduces the hazard of expropriation of valuable firm knowledge by providing for better incentive alignment, for employment contracts and control, and for deferred payments of compensation. This makes it the preferred mechanism of governance when there is a risk of expropriation of valuable knowledge by a contracting partner. This is important as contracting partners may be better positioned to use expropriated IT knowledge, technology and other assets, as they usually possess complementary assets in the form of an established base of customers. Not only does this raise the incentive of the contracting partner to opportunistically engage in expropriation, it also raises the additional risk of leakage of proprietary information to rival firms. The expectation of this opportunistic behavior and of expropriation of rents reduces the incentive to engage in contracting valuable knowledge based and other intangible assets. Thus, the likelihood of outsourcing an IT process increases when outsourcing carries with it a relatively lower risk of expropriation of rents. This leads to the first hypothesis.

H1. The likelihood of outsourcing a firm’s IT process decreases as the hazard of expropriation of a firm’s intangible and knowledge based assets increases.

## 2.2. Stickiness

ITO involves transfer of knowledge and capabilities from the outsourcing firm to a partner. Prior research indicates that this transfer of knowledge and capabilities within a firm is characterized by ‘‘stickiness’’ (Szulanski, 1996). Within a firm, stickiness of knowledge makes transfer of best practices difficult (Szulanski, 1996). Given these observations of knowledge transfer within a firm, it has been theoretically proposed that difficulty of transfer of knowledge increases when transfer involves a different firm (Kogut and Zander, 1992). Since transfer of expertise and knowledge related to IT outsourcing is dif ficult and not costless, stickiness of knowledge is a form of knowledge based transaction costs that introduces friction in an otherwise feasible contracting decision (Conner and Prahalad, 1996). Not all IT processes involve equivalent levels of stickiness. For instance, outsourcing maintenance of a firm’s computer hardware assets would involve a significantly lower level of stickiness and KTC than would that of software development linking different activities of a firm’s value chain.

At least three factors influence stickiness of knowledge and capabilities of IT processes. First, the complexity of an IT process leads to greater knowledge-based transaction costs. Complexity arises when a given firm activity is associated with a large number of technologies, organizational routines, and individual or team-based experience (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). As a result of complexity, very few if any individuals and departments within an organization possess comprehensive knowledge pertaining to an organizational process (Nelson and Winter, 1982) that is a candidate for transacting. For instance, development of banking software is a complex process which involves not only knowledge of client back office transacting procedures, but also integration of several different information technologies such as relational databases, high level programming languages, internet communication protocols, security and encryption technologies, and a multiplicity of other knowledge inputs. As a consequence, complexity leads to greater knowledge based transaction costs, as shown by Dibbern et al. (2008), owing to the difficulty to communicate the capability to a partner firm.

A second factor resulting in greater stickiness and knowledge based transaction costs is the degree of codification of capabilities and knowledge requisite for execution of an IT activity (Tiwana and Bush, 2007). Even if knowledge and capabilities are complex, documentation and codification influence how easily capabilities are taught and will lead to lower transfer costs (Zander and Kogut, 1995). In the absence of such codification and in particular if knowledge is tacit, communication is impeded. Such tacit knowledge includes skill-based competencies that are acquired by learning by doing (Polyani, 1967). Since tacit knowledge is accumulated over time with experience, the greater the tacit knowledge component of transaction related knowledge, the greater the extent to which expertise need be communicated to transaction partners, and the more difficult it is to communicate. These two effects result in greater knowledge based transaction costs. For instance, consider the case of a firm that outsources maintenance of internally developed software. Assuming this software was developed over time and embeds a significant degree of complex and tacit knowledge about firm activities, it would be difficult for a service supplier to troubleshoot and effectively maintain the software if there is no documentation available and the software code has to be decrypted.

Finally, when degree of observability of the product or service rendered by an activity is high, knowledge based transaction costs are lower. In particular, if partner firms and rivals can replicate a capability by observing its output products and services, then the extent to which knowledge and capability transfer is necessary decreases. The possibility to reverse engineer the product or service produced by an organizational activity mitigates knowledge based transaction costs. Thus, complexity, tacitness, and reverse engineering jointly influence the magnitude of stickiness and of knowledge based transaction costs, and whether a transaction is outsourced or not. This leads to the second hypothesis:

H2. The likelihood of outsourcing a firm’s IT process decreases when stickiness increases.

## 2.3. Supplier relative knowledge and capabilities

It is critical for the success of ITO that the information capabilities of partner firms match the requirements of outsourcing exchange (Mani et al., 2010). The influence of relative knowledge and capabilities on knowledge based transaction costs is twofold: it influences stickiness and the risk of expropriation.

Supplier knowledge and capabilities facilitate the absorption of ‘‘sticky’’ knowledge (Dibbern et al., 2008). For one, the ability of organizations to effectively carry out an activity comes from experience and by learning-by-doing over time. Firm capability developed in this way is routinized (Nelson and Winter, 1982), often tacit (Polyani, 1967), and difficult to transfer. When greater differences exist in the knowledge and capabilities of a firm and a potential supplier, more effort has to be made to educate the partner firm to bring it up to speed. This transfer of information and tacit knowledge to the supplier firm is costly and time consuming (Zander and Kogut, 1995). Supplier knowledge serves as absorptive capacity that facilitates firm knowledge transfer (Cohen and Levinthal, 1990) and results in lower knowledge based transaction costs. It thus leads to a greater likelihood of outsourcing.

Second, supplier knowledge and capabilities decrease the risk of expropriation of firm knowledge. The risk of expropriation exists if an outsourcing firm and its partner possess substantially different levels of knowledge and capabilities. If supplier has equivalent skill sets or even better knowledge pertaining to the execution of an IT activity, it will not expropriate firm knowledge, rather, it will use its own skill sets in the event that outsourcing occurs to enhance performance. Thus, as supplier knowledge and capability increases, knowledge based transaction costs decrease and likelihood of outsourcing increases.

Arguments that supplier knowledge facilitates assimilation of new knowledge and decreases expropriation hazard are independent of the classical resource based proposition that supplier relative knowledge and capabilities result in lower production costs (Rumelt, 1984; Wernerfelt, 1984) and to a greater likelihood of outsourcing (Ang and Straub, 1998; Dibbern et al., 2008; Tiwana and Bush, 2007). All in all, we propose that greater supplier knowledge and capabilities relative to an outsourcing firm corresponds with lower knowledge based transaction costs as the risk of expropriation is lower and the difficulty of knowledge transfer (stickiness) is also lower. This will lead to a greater likelihood of outsourcing. The third hypothesis follows.

H3. The likelihood of outsourcing a firm’s IT process increases when the level of supplier knowledge and capabilities relative to the outsourcing firm is greater.

## 2.4. Manager expectation on supplier’s performance and framing of the decision

The logic of the strategic outsourcing decision developed so far is consistent with the argument that managers are rational fully informed actors actively pursuing the goal of maximizing firm performance. Managers optimize performance by making strategic boundary decisions taking into consideration governance costs, production costs, and knowledge-based transaction costs. The first three hypotheses predict that the risk of expropriation, the degree of stickiness, and supplier relative knowledge and capabilities influence the choice of the governance mechanism that optimizes performance.

However, just as transactions differ in the risk of expropriation of knowledge, in stickiness, and in relative knowledge and capabilities of partner firms, they also differ in their intrinsic potential to contribute to firm performance. Thus, while risk of expropriation of knowledge and stickiness may advise against outsourcing due to KTC concerns, an expected disproportionate increase in performance following outsourcing may swing the balance in favor of outsourcing. For instance, managers may risk divulging knowledge of vital firm processes and routines key to firm competitive advantage when they outsource the development and maintenance of their enterprise resource planning system to a contractor. In this vein, Jain and Thietart (2007) demonstrated that managers may increasingly outsource resources core to their organization when their organizations are faced with performance declines and with increased product market competition. Given this risk, they may still engage in this operation as outsourcing promises significant performance increments. Thus, the expectation of superior performance following outsourcing may push the managers to risk outsourcing an IT process that would not be outsourced following KTC arguments.

Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) offers a theoretical explanation of this risk taking choice. The theory has already been thoroughly investigated. It has been applied to a variety of situations, as reported by Camerer (2000), from stock market decision, consumer behavior, work effort to insurance purchase and lottery betting. It challenges the traditional expected utility theory to describe decision under risk, and shows that people tend to underweight probable outcomes to the benefit of certain outcomes, a tendency called the certainty effect. This effect induces risk aversion in situations of sure gains and risk taking behavior in situation of sure loss. In the present case, there is a choice between a sure loss of higher performance if IT is not outsourced, and a lottery between probable higher costs due to transactional hazards and expected higher performance (outsourcing). Confronted with a sure loss (opportunity cost of foregoing an outsourced superior performance), managers take the risk to lose proprietary knowledge if they expect to be compensated by higher short term performance.

Consider, for example, a firm IT such as an ERP system, the outsourcing of which is associated with a risk of losing trade secrets and knowledge about firm processes. Outsourcing may not be prescribed based on KTC arguments. Outsourcing, however, may still occur if the expected cost reductions and performance ex-post compensate the firm for the expropriation hazard. Similarly, if an IT process is characterized by high stickiness and it is difficult to transfer to a partner firm, managers may still go to great lengths to outsource it if they expect higher performance. Contingent on a process being outsourced, this suggests that the expected performance of an outsourced IT process should compensate for the level of expropriation hazard and the level of stickiness. This leads to the following last hypotheses:

H4a. The outsourced performance of an IT process associated with high expropriation hazard will be higher than for the average IT process.

H4b. The outsourced performance of an IT process associated with high stickiness will be higher than for the average IT process.

It is important to note that these arguments do not in any way suggest that activities with greater expropriation hazards and knowledge-based transaction costs should be outsourced because they will result in greater outsourced performance; rather they are outsourced only if the expected benefit ex-post is perceived to compensate for knowledge-based transaction costs.

Taken together, these hypotheses suggest that self-selection and endogeneity play a significant role in the estimation of in-sourced and outsourced performance. Managers self-select those IT processes to outsource for which suppliers with relevant capabilities exist, where expropriation hazards are low, and which are relatively easy to transfer and associated with low knowledge-based transaction costs. However, activities associated with high KTC may still be outsourced if expected performance ex-post seems to compensate for greater expropriation hazard and knowledge stickiness ex-ante.

## 3. Research method

## 3.1. Sample and data collection

The sample is drawn from a list of companies comprising the French (CAC40) and British (FTSE100) stock indices. Senior executives in the sampled firms were invited to contribute to the research by (1) identifying at least two IT processes and the extent to which they were outsourced; and (2) proposing the names of executives in their firms that had experience with these IT processes and their outsourcing decisions. Follow up letters were sent to managers who did not respond to the initial mailing. Of the 1307 executives contacted, 140 (10.7%) provided contact information of 289 executives experienced in outsourcing. These 289 executives were in turn contacted and invited to participate in the research by answering to the survey questionnaire on the processes identified by their senior executives. 96 (33.2%) participated in the survey. This gave a total of 192 observations of outsourcing decisions. However, only 180 of these observations from 86 firms were usable, as 12 obser: vations had a great proportion of missing data. It is worth noting that a number of firms in the sample provided data on more than two IT processes, resulting in 2.09 observations per firm on the average.

Before the questionnaire was sent to interviewees, in a preliminary phase, we conducted 14 semi-directive interviews with senior IT executives of leading firms in order to obtain a better understanding of the outsourcing decision. These interviews provided insight into of a wide range of IT based outsourcing decisions. The questionnaire, then, was pre-tested (Sheatsley, 1983; Spector, 1992) with a set of 12 executives experienced in IT and the decision to outsource. Responses collected during this pre-test period were not included in the final data set. The questionnaire was modified based on the feedback of these executives through several iterations. In this way, it gained in clarity and was reduced in length to increase response rate (Dillman, 1978).

## 3.2. Measure development

Survey items were operationalized using a 7 point semantic differential scale (unless otherwise reported below). To develop the latent variables, we submitted the survey items to principle component factor analysis (Kim and Meuller, 1978) positing a single factor. Only factors with Eigen values greater than 1 were retained. Following Kendall’s (1975) recommendations, we computed latent variables by applying the weights obtained for principal component analysis to each item.

## 3.2.1. Dependent variables

In keeping with research studying, make, buy, or make and buy (Parmigiani, 2007; Veugelers and Cassiman, 1999), we used the degree to which an IT process was outsourced as a measure of outsourcing. This ranged from zero to one hundred percent. The dependent variable ‘‘Outsourced’’ takes a value of one if an activity was completely or partially outsourced, and zero otherwise. The binary measure of outsourcing is bi-modal, with 43% of the observations corresponding to completely insourced firm activities, and remaining 57% of the observations pertaining to partially or completely outsourced firm activities.

The second dependent variable, performance, encompasses a broad range of performance features, across both market and hierarchical exchanges. Several studies (Goodman et al., 1995; Mohr and Spekman, 1994) have developed measures of exchange performance that distinguish whether the vendor has realized pre-established performance expectations based on cost, quality and responsiveness to problems or inquiries. In this vein, the performance variable consists of five items. It provides a measure of the extent to which the performance of an IT process contributes to performance in terms of quality, cost effectiveness, innovativeness and agility. Further, a fifth item provides a direct measure of the extent to which respondents perceived of the IT process as contributing to firm performance. These five items loaded onto a single factor with an Eigen value greater than one for which the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.63.

## 3.2.2. Independent variables

Expropriation is operationalized by assessing the extent to which a focal IT process creates conditions for opportunistic behavior by a partner firm. In particular, when knowledge pertaining to IT processes that is highly valuable to a firm, it is particularly susceptible to expropriation following outsourcing. The latent variable for expropriation is comprised of three scale items resulting in a unique factor with an Eigen value greater than one $\left( \alpha = 0 . 7 0 9 \right)$ . The scale assesses the extent to which respondents consider a focal IT activity as enabling the organization to differentiate, as strategic to firm activities, as permitting direct contact with firm clients.

Stickiness results from complexity, tacitness, and non-observability of knowledge associated with a firm IT activity (Reed and DeFillippi, 1990). The latent variable for stickiness is operationalized through four scale items resulting in one factor with an Eigen value superior to 1 (a = 0.701). These items respectively provide a measure of the degree to which a firm IT process is perceived to be complex, the extent to which it draws upon several skills and technologies, the extent to which knowledge is not documented and is tacit, and the extent to which the activity can be reverse engineered from its product and service outputs.

The latent variable for supplier relative knowledge and capabilities measures the knowledge and capabilities of partner firms relative to the outsourcing firm using four different scale items which load onto a unique factor (a = 0.767). The first item measures the extent to which supplier firms possess knowledge and skills relevant to an IT process relative to the focal firm. The second item measures the extent to which suppliers are equipped with physical assets and equipment that are relevant to the IT process relative to the focal firm. The third item measures the extent to which suppliers have the capacity to meet fluctuating transactional demand, relative to the focal firm. The fourth item measures whether suppliers benefit from economies of scale in the production process relative to the focal firm.

## 3.2.3. Control variables

Several other variables potentially affect whether an IT process is outsourced or not. We control for three such variables in the statistical analysis. First, when there is a scarcity of competent suppliers for a transaction, an outsourcing firm becomes dependent on its supplier and is exposed to opportunism. Small numbers of suppliers was operationalized by an item that measured whether a sufficient number of competent suppliers were available at the time of the outsourcing decision. The binary variable for small numbers was coded 0 if there is a sufficient choice of suppliers to choose from at the time of the outsourcing decision, and 1 otherwise.

Second, we controlled for whether a firm has excess capacity with respect to a given IT process. Since sunk costs and investments may prove to be a barrier to outsourcing when a firm has surplus capacity (Williamson, 1999) it was also controlled for. Excess capacity was operationalized as an item on a seven point scale asking respondents if their firm had sig nificant excess capacity with respect to an IT process under consideration (Campbell and Fiske, 1959), or was significantly short of capacity (Anderson and Schmittlein, 1984). Firm size was controlled for as well.

Finally, we controlled for firm size. Respondents provided the number of employees in their division for the last fiscal year, and size is the logarithm of this value.

## 3.2.4. Measurement issues

Latent variables for expropriation, stickiness and supplier relative capabilities have high convergent validity, substantially greater than the threshold of 0.70, and factor loadings of scale items and proportion of variance explained by the latent variables is more than 0.5. Furthermore, discriminant validity value of each latent variable is superior to the off diagonal corre lation it has with other latent variables. This indicates good discriminant validity.

A problem with survey based data is common method bias (Campbell and Fiske, 1959). What common methods bias suggests is that survey respondents justify their own answers. We attempted to mitigate the effect of common method bias using two strategies. First, we conducted the survey in two steps. In a first step, senior executives in each firm identified a number of firm IT processes of interest, along with the extent to which these were in-sourced or outsourced. They also provided the names and contact information of executives knowledgeable about these processes. In a second step, the identified executives were contacted and they provided input to the survey instrument. Thus, to avoid hypothesis guessing, we separated the identification of IT processes and the extent of outsourcing from the rest of the survey instrument. Second, like Ang and Straub (1998), we tried to avoid common method bias by not making obvious the nature of our research question to respondents. Survey respondents were not informed that this study was about outsourcing decision. They were uniquely informed that we wished to collect information on a number of firm IT processes identified by their senior executives.

Finally, we checked empirically whether common method bias was a problem using Harman’s one-factor test by entering all the principal constructs into a principal components factor analysis (Podsakoff and Organ, 1986). The data loaded onto four constructs with Eigen value greater than one, and the principle factor explained 28% of the variance. Since there is no dominant factor, the Harmon one-factor test indicates that common method bias does not pose a significant problem. Non-response bias was controlled for by comparing early with late respondents (Armstrong and Overton, 1977). Since constructs for expropriation, knowledge-based transaction costs, and supplier relative knowledge showed no significant differences between early and late respondents, non-respondent bias does not appear to constitute a problem

## 3.3. Statistical method

The statistical analysis comprises two distinct steps. In the first step, we used a Probit model to investigate the influence of expropriation, stickiness, and supplier relative knowledge and capabilities on the outsourcing decision. This allowed us to test the first three hypotheses. To test the last hypotheses 4a and 4b, in-sourced and outsourced performance was estimated using the Heckman selection model to control for unobserved heterogeneity and self-selection bias.

## 3.3.1. Probit estimation

Binary choice Logit and Probit models have been extensively used in the make or buy literature in order to investigate the influence of independent variables on the binary dependent variable (Anderson and Schmittlein, 1984; Mayer and Salomon, 2006; Monteverde and Teece, 1982; Poppo and Zenger, 1998). Here, we assume that managers analyze a given strategic outsourcing decision by taking into account the different factors that influence it, such as the expropriation hazard, knowledge based transaction costs, and firm relative capabilities. These predictor variables and the control variables were combined into a single Probit model where the coefficient vector $\beta$ is derived to maximize the following log likelihood function:

$$
L = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} y _ {i} \ln F (X _ {i} ^ {\prime} \beta) + \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} (1 - y _ {i}) \ln [ 1 - F (X _ {i} ^ {\prime} \beta) ],\tag{1}
$$

where $y _ { i }$ is 1 if the ith activity is outsourced, and 0 if it is not outsourced. $X _ { i } ^ { \prime }$ is the vector of values of the independent and control variables for the ith observation, and $F ( X _ { i } ^ { \prime } { \beta } )$ is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

## 3.3.2. The Heckman model

The variable ‘‘performance’’ is measured with the performance of an in-sourced or an outsourced IT process. We do not observe the ‘‘in-sourced’’ performance of an outsourced process, and vice versa. Given this, making one general OLS regression in order to estimate performance will result in selection bias, unless one of two conditions hold true. The first condition is that strategizing across firm activities is random and no differences in the determinants of performance exist across the two strategies (in-sourcing and outsourcing) (Shaver, 1998). If strategizing is not random, then self-selection becomes a problem, and shall lead to biased coefficient estimates (Masten, 1993). The second condition necessary to eliminate selfselection bias is that there is no unobservable heterogeneity: all factors influencing performance are identified, and taken into account in the regression for performance (Shaver, 1998). Clearly, researchers are not able to observe and measure all influential factors that are observable and important to managers.

The Heckman model provides a solution for problems of unobservable heterogeneity and self selection (Heckman, 1979). This method consists of a two stage estimation procedure. In the first stage, a strategic choice such as the outsourcing decision is modeled using a Probit model. Following this, the inverse Mills ratio is computed and is used in the second stage to account for the part of the decision that is not explicable based on the observed variables used in the model. In this second stage of the Heckman estimation, the sample is split into in-sourced and outsourced sub-samples in order to account for selfselection, and performance estimation is done using ordinary least squares for each independently.

In the current analysis, we used a modified Heckman procedure.<sup>2</sup> According to Wooldridge (2001), a type III Tobit model produces efficiency gains over the standard Probit specification used in the Heckman procedure. Since data for the extent of outsourcing is available, a Tobit formulation for the first stage model is preferred.

The Heckman-Tobit III model consists of two stages. The Tobit regression model discussed in the previous section (Table 4, model 6) consists of the first stage in the two step Heckman estimation. We expect no effect of the knowledge transaction on the in source performance. Models (4) through (6) in Table 4 control for self-selection and unobserved heterogeneity. Taken together, it may be observed that knowledge expropriation, an important source of knowledge based transaction costs, is significantly associated with outsourced performance but are not with in-sourced performance. This shows that contrary to knowledge transaction costs prediction, managers are willing to make boundary decisions which are not necessarily consistent with a transaction based perspective only if they expect a superior performance. Results are consistent with a prospect theory perspective according to which managers take the risk to lose proprietary knowledge by outsourcing, because they don’t want to incur the sure loss of an attractive supplier’s performance.

## 5. Discussion and conclusion

This is the first study to validate empirically the role of KTC on IT outsourcing decisions while controlling for classical transaction cost based effects. To this end, we identified three types of KTC: expropriation hazard, stickiness, and relative knowledge and capabilities. We used data on 180 IT processes from firms listed on the French (CAC40) and British (FTSE100) stock exchanges and corroborated the argument that KTC are important factors influencing the outsourcing decision. Knowledge based transaction costs, in our research, provide an integration of knowledge based and transaction cost based ap proaches to IT outsourcing decisions (Heiman and Nickerson, 2002).

First, knowledge based transaction costs arise from the risk of expropriation of valuable knowledge following outsourcing. Partner firms may opportunistically utilize the knowledge thus obtained to further their own interests during IT outsourcing. Since our framework of knowledge based transaction costs acknowledges the role of opportunism, it differs from classical knowledge based arguments that argue that its consideration is not necessary for contracting decisions (Kogut and Zander, 1992).

Second, given the importance of expropriation risks and opportunism, research points to a number of other key knowledge based effects. On the one hand, stickiness, i.e. tacitness and complexity of knowledge, is a source of knowledge based transaction costs. Tacitness and complexity lead to sticky knowledge, knowledge that is difficult to transfer to partner firms in the event of outsourcing. This deters outsourcing. On the other hand, our framework takes into consideration the relative capabilities of IT supplier firms, or their capacity to absorb knowledge flows from the outsourcing firm. The greater the supplier capabilities relative to a focal firm, the easier is knowledge transfer and the greater the productivity of the supplier. Thus, if supplier relative capabilities are low, KTC will be high, and outsourcing is deterred.

Third, the empirical analysis shows that the governance choice is closely related to the performance that follows. In a second step, we performed a two stage Heckman estimation of the influence of the knowledge based variables on activity performance. This analysis suggests that the influence of knowledge-based transaction costs on performance is contingent on strategizing, as their influence depends on whether an activity is in-sourced or outsourced. Notably, when the sourcing decision is assumed to be random and unobserved heterogeneity to be inexistent, expropriation concerns and stickiness are found to have a positive influence on activity performance. However, when the sample is split into in-sourced and outsourced sub-samples and selection bias controlled for. it is found that managers outsource activities with higher knowledge based transaction costs in the form of greater expropriation hazard only when they expect the outsourced performance will compensate for higher knowledge based transactional costs. Managers prefer taking the risk of higher transaction costs of expropriation hazards instead of losing the opportunity of a high supplier performance

While the risk of expropriation significantly influences outsourced activity performance, it has no influence on the performance of in-sourced firm activities. It is of note that this construct is relational: risk of expropriation arises only when an activity is transacted for. Thus, this construct does not have a significant influence on in-sourced firm performance as there is no risk of expropriation of rents, there are no costs of transfer of firm knowledge, and firm capabilities relative to suppliers and rivals are no longer important.

Despite some potential research limitations, we believe our results have value for ITO decisions. Most importantly, survey instruments give rise to the possibility of various types of bias such as common method bias and non-response bias. Biases, we think to have circumvented. Second, sample size might be perceived as too small for reliably testing our hypothesis. However, our different analysis showed results are robust. Third, we have not taken into consideration one major source of knowledge transaction costs, high partner firm employee turnover. This turnover can reach a 100% level per year in some Indian subcontractor companies as documented and researched by Rottman and Lacity (2006), Kuruvilla and Ranganathan (2008), Lacity et al. (2008) and Luftman and Ben-Zvi (2011). As a result, supplier relational capabilities accumulated through prior transacting with a firm may decay over time, increasing the costs of transfer sticky knowledge. Investigating the effect of such partner firm turnover on transacting and the performance of transacting partner firms remains a fruitful avenue for future investigations.

Assuming we have correctly controlled for all other limitations, we can put forward some recommendations for managers. CIOs and other senior managers interested in making better information systems sourcing decisions need to take into consideration knowledge based transaction costs, such as what type of knowledge will flow across firm boundaries to partner firms and what strategic effect will this have. They’ll also have to evaluate how easy it is to communicate the knowledge associated with a firm process/transaction to a partner firm, and how capable is a partner to absorb the knowledge that is made available to it. Finally, they’ll have to consider the framing of their choice and ponder more carefully short term gain and more uncertain but potentially detrimental long term loss.
