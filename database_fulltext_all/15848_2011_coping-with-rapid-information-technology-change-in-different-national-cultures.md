# Coping with rapid information technology change in different national cultures

Xiang Fang<sup>1</sup>, John “Skip” Benamati<sup>1</sup> and Albert L. Lederer <sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Decision Sciences and MIS, Farmer School of Business, Miami University, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>C.M. Gatton College of Business and Economics, University of Kentucky, U.S.A.

Correspondence: X. Fang, Department of Decision Sciences and MIs. Farmer School of Business, Miami University, 501 East High Street, Oxford, OH 45056, U.S.A. Tel: 513 529-1390; Fax: 513 529-9689

## Abstract

Coping with rapid information technology change challenges IT organizations throughout the world. National culture theory based on the GLOBE study suggests that culture affects the coping with such change in China and the United States, but does not cogently suggest that coping mechanisms differ in effectiveness across the two cultures. Analysis of data from 71 IT executives and managers in China and 246 in the United States suggests that Chinese IT organizations employ coping mechanisms of vendor support, education and training, and internal procedures more extensively than do U.S. IT organizations. IT organizations in both countries apply education and training as well as internal procedures to successfully deal with the problems of rapid IT change. Vendor support additionally predicted success in Chinese but not U.S. organizations, whereas in both U.S. and Chinese organizations endurance predicted lack of success. The research thus extends national culture theory by proposing that culture affects successful coping. Implications for research suggest the identification of actions to overcome the impact of cultural differences. Implications for practice suggest that multinational corporations deal with rapid IT change differently in divisions in different cultures, regardless of whether in China, the United States, Europe, or elsewhere. ## Introduction

Today’s dynamic environment demands that IT organizations must cope better with the challenges of managing rapidly changing information systems (Cegielski et al., 2004, 2005; Marble, 2004; Skok & Tan, 2007). Although change is occurring throughout the world in a variety of cultures, researchers and managers alike cannot assume that studies in one national culture can be generalized to another (Tan et al., 1998; Calhoun et al., 2002; Leidner & Kayworth, 2006; McCoy et al., 2007; Yoon, 2009). In fact, national culture has been shown to play a key role in determining reactions to new consumer products as well as to new information technologies (Png et al., 2001; Yaveroglu & Donthu, 2002). For example, uncertainty avoidance, a dimension of national culture, affects the perceived ease of use of enterprise resource planning systems (Hwang, 2005; Cyr, 2008; Guo et al., 2008). That is, desire to avoid, or at least reduce uncertainty encourages organizations to provide the training that enhances ease of use.

China and the United States are major IT users and participants in the global economy. The two countries also illustrate very different national cultures (Hofstede, 2001; House et al., 2004). What is known about managing IT in one might not necessarily apply in the other. Multinational corporations with divisions in both countries must understand how they do respond and how they should respond to rapid IT change in their divisions in each country.

IT organizations use five possible coping mechanisms to respond to new IT: vendor support, consultant support, education and training, internal procedures, and endurance (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). The purpose of this research is to compare the use of such coping mechanisms in China to the United States, and to assess the success of the coping mechanisms in the two cultures.

In the next section we define the study’s constructs: successful coping, coping mechanisms, and national culture. In subsequent sections we describe the hypotheses, methodology, data analysis, and findings. We conclude the paper with implications for research and practice.

## Constructs

## Successful coping with rapid information technology change

Information technology is rapidly changing. For example, over a typical 3-year period, an organization might face the implementation of many new development products. The evolution of the popular development tool, Microsoft VisualStudio.Net 2005 to the 2008 version, and then from the 2008 to the 2010 illustrates such a timeframe.

Rapid change in information technology provides not only new benefits to its users via new products, but also creates challenges for the managers who select and implement it (Lee & Xia, 2005; Benamati & Lederer, 2008). Vendors rush new products to market before sufficiently testing and debugging them, and the new products therefore sometimes have unanticipated, deleterious defects. The latest products can be incompatible with an organization’s current IT. New IT may confuse managers so that they may take longer than planned to select among competing products. New IT may be surprisingly difficult to learn and hence cause additional work and delays. Rapid IT change may result in the acquisition of new IT that is obsolete even before its implementation (Cegielski et al., 2004).

These challenges from rapid IT change can thus lead to the problems of unexpected work and delays in the implementation of new applications (Benamati & Lederer, 2008). Hence, successful coping is herein defined in terms of the reduction in the problems of unexpected work and unexpected delays, which would have been attributable to new IT (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a).

## Coping mechanisms

An organization’s environment comprises those physical and social factors relevant to its success but outside its boundaries. Because firms face an increasingly demanding and unforgiving environment in which mistakes can be extremely costly (Zahra & O’Neill,

1998), the environment is even more critical to the organization’s welfare (Choudhury & Sampler, 1997; Maier et al., 1997). Organizations must either respond to a changing environment or interact with it to bring about more favorable outcomes. They thus deal with their problems reactively or proactively, and do so through the use of coping mechanisms (Benamati et al., 1997; Tsai et al., 2007; Barrella & Buffington, 2009).

The selection of a particular coping mechanism depends on the specific problem facing the organization or on the environment (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990) and on management’s perception and interpretation of the environment (Weick, 1979). Selection decisions can bring about the success or failure of the organization (Hambrick & Mason, 1984) and as contingency theory asserts, the optimal course of action is dependent on the internal or external environment (Galbraith, 1973). Hence, no single best way to cope exists.

One component of the environment is information technology (Maier et al., 1997). IT managers experience problems due to change in IT such as new generations of hardware or new paradigms in software, and they use coping mechanisms to deal with the problems (Lederer & Mendelow, 1990). Interviews with experienced IT professionals in one study identified specific actions for coping with the problems (Benamati et al., 1997). In another study, a survey instrument with items developed from those actions asked U.S.-based IT professionals, who selected and purchased enterprise-wide software, the extent their organizations had taken each action to better deal with rapid IT change (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a).

That study used factor analysis to derive a set of five categories of coping mechanisms: vendor support, consultant support, education and training, internal procedures, and endurance. In this study, we employed all five coping mechanisms to investigate how Chinese and U.S. IT organizations respond to the challenges of rapid IT change. Table 1 shows the five categories and the items within each (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a).

Vendor support refers to the reliance on IT suppliers for the identification and resolution of problems with new IT. It also includes the customization of new IT, the creation of interfaces with new IT, and the functional enhancement of new IT. IT organizations coordinate communication among multiple IT vendors on complex projects, and sometimes even pressure vendors of new IT to provide support.

Consultant support denotes the engagement of external IT professionals to help plan for and implement new IT. It also includes current problem solving and ongoing support.

Education and training refers to staying informed of new IT as it becomes available by reading about it, taking formal and customized classes, and attending conferences. It entails informing IT professionals about the benefits of new IT and even pressuring them to use it.

Internal procedures denotes using well-defined IT acquisition and implementation procedures. It includes considering only new IT compatible with existing IT or successfully used by other organizations. It also refers to documenting the differences between new and previous IT.

Finally, endurance is defined as ignoring or working around problems. It also includes learning new IT without formal education. On one hand, ignoring and working around problems may be deemed substantially less rational and constructive than the actions associated with vendor support, consultant support, education and training, and internal procedures. Moreover, learning new IT without formal education may be deemed less rational and constructive than doing so with instructors, classes, and integrated texts and exercises. This unfavorable connotation is supported by a prior study showing that endurance had a statistically significant negative correlation with reduction of problems (Benamati & Lederer, 2001b). On the other hand, endurance is a way of coping, perhaps much like muddling through (Lindblom, 1959).

Researchers have lodged several criticisms against Hofstede’s work. For example, the study was based on the needs and interests of a single U.S. company (IBM) in one industry, surveying 116,000 of its employees in 40 countries. The resulting framework was thus oriented around one culture, that of the U.S., in that all subjects worked for the U.S.-based company (Javidan et al., 2006). Moreover, Hofstede’s constructs have been characterized as flawed. For example, the Hofstede theory creates an unrealistic sense that all dimensions of national culture have been identified, and, being over 35 years old, it may be seen as somewhat dated (Javidan et al., 2006).

Moreover, recent findings have contradicted Hofstede’s work. Unlike Hofstede, Jenner et al. (2008) did not find differences in Power Distance between the U.S., Mexico, and Canada. Mexico did not have greater Uncertainty Avoidance than the U.S., but the U.S. had greater Uncertainty Avoidance than Canada. Canada and the U.S. did not differ on Individualism/Collectivism.

In the GLOBE study (House et al., 2004), 170 researchers collaborated to survey 17,000 managers in the telecommunications, food, and banking industries in 62 national cultures. The authors extended Hofstede’s work by identifying nine national culture dimensions and distinguishing each in terms of values (i.e., the way things should be) and practices (i.e., the way things are). They referred to the dimensions as in-group collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, institutional collectivism, gender egalitarianism, humane orientation, power distance, performance orientation, future orientation, and assertiveness. The Chinese and U.S. national cultures differed at least to some extent on all nine, and several could be used as the theoretical foundation for research comparing how Chinese and U.S. IT organizations cope with rapid IT change. In this study, we used the three with the greatest differences: in-group collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, and assertiveness. Table 2 shows the practices scores for all nine dimensions for each culture as well as the differences between them with each multi-item dimension measured by a seven point Likert scale such as strongly agree to strongly disagree (House et al., 2004).

In-group collectivism refers to how strongly individuals express pride (a high or inordinate opinion of one’s organization whether in one’s mind or as outwardly displayed), loyalty (a feeling of devotion or allegiance to one’s organization), and cohesiveness (the connection group members feel with other group members) in the organizations they work for (Javidan et al., 2006). High in-group collectivist societies make use of team-oriented leadership. Turkey (5.88) and Morocco (5.87) are examples of particularly high in-group collectivist cultures, and New Zealand (3.67) and Switzerland (3.85) are examples of low ones (House et al., 2004). House et al. did not explain the historical, geographical, or other events that may have caused national culture differences.

Uncertainty avoidance refers to the degree that a society, organization, or group depends on social norms (behavioral expectations within the organization), rules (principles or regulations governing conduct), and procedures (specified sequences of actions) to reduce the unpredictability of future events (Javidan et al., 2006). In high uncertainty-avoidance cultures people seek to reduce uncertainty by applying elaborate formal procedures and detailed strategies that enhance orderliness, consistency, and structure. In contrast, low uncertainty-avoidance cultures take risks, act opportunistically, and prefer broad strategies with simple processes. Singapore (5.31) and Switzerland (4.98) illustrate particularly high uncertainty-avoidance cultures; Bolivia (3.35) and Guatemala (3.30) exemplify low ones.

Assertiveness is the degree that individuals are confrontational (tending toward facing with hostility) and aggressive (boldly pursuing one’s ends) (Javidan et al., 2006). High-assertiveness cultures are competitive and display can-do attitudes, while low-assertiveness cultures favor loyalty, solidarity, and harmony. El Salvador (4.62) and South Africa (4.60) are high-assertiveness cultures, and New Zealand (3.42) and Switzerland (3.47) are low ones.

This study compared coping with rapid IT change in Chinese and U.S. IT organizations. It categorized national culture as either Chinese or U.S. with distinctions based on the GLOBE study (House et al., 2004). Because it examined the organizational practice of coping, it applied the GLOBE study’s practices interpretation of the dimensions rather than values interpretation.

## Hypotheses

## Extent of use of coping mechanisms

According to the GLOBE study, Chinese culture exhibits greater in-group collectivism, greater uncertainty avoidance, and less assertiveness than American culture does (Javidan et al., 2006). With greater in-group collectivism, the Chinese express greater pride, loyalty, and cohesiveness (Gelfand et al., 2004). Hence, to maintain the pride and group cohesiveness that might have suffered because of unexpected work and delays resulting from rapid IT change in the past, they would more extensively apply the four constructive coping mechanisms – vendor support, consulting support, education and training, and internal procedures – to minimize unexpected work and delays from such change in the future.

Having greater uncertainty avoidance, the Chinese rely more on social norms, rules, and procedures to alleviate unpredictability (De Luque & Javidan, 2004). Hence, they are expected to rely more on the established practices and procedures associated with the four coping mechanisms to reduce the unpredictability of changing IT.

Being less assertive, the Chinese are less confrontational and aggressive, and lack the U.S. culture’s selfconfident ‘can-do attitudes’ (Den Hartog, 2004). Thus, the Chinese would rely more on the use of the constructive coping mechanisms to avoid the user and business manager confrontations that might result from the unexpected work and delays.

In particular, for the vendor support coping mechanism, the Chinese are expected to rely more on IT vendors to provide solutions to avoid unnecessary work and delays (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a; Marble, 2004; Skok & Tan, 2007). Doing so could enable the IT organization to ascribe responsibility for these outcomes to the vendor, thereby reducing internal recriminations and enabling it to maintain pride and group cohesiveness (as in the ingroup collectiveness dimension). The Chinese also would, for example, follow practices to more extensively coordinate communication among multiple vendors to reduce risk and the associated unpredictability (as in the uncertainty avoidance dimension). They would more extensively apply vendor customization of new IT to fit user and business manager needs more rapidly and effectively than if done in-house, and thus reduce potential confrontation with those stakeholders (as in the assertiveness dimension). Hence, we hypothesize:

H1a: Vendor support is used more extensively to deal with the problems of rapidly changing IT by Chinese organizations than by U.S. organizations.

Chinese organizations are also expected to rely more on the consultant support coping mechanism to solve the problems of rapid IT change. The theoretical basis for this assertion is similar to that of H1a (Benamati & Lederer, 2001b; De Luque & Javidan, 2004; Den Hartog, 2004; Gelfand et al., 2004; Marble, 2004; Javidan et al., 2006; Skok & Tan, 2007). Thus, similarly to the vendor support coping mechanism, Chinese organizations would engage consultants more to help deal with rapid IT change and thus would be better able to ascribe responsibility to the consultant for any unexpected work or delays, again, to help maintain pride and group cohesiveness (as in the in-group collectiveness dimension). They also would, for example, more extensively engage consultants to help plan for new IT to reduce risk and unpredictability (as in the uncertainty avoidance dimension). They would more extensively engage consultants to help address user resistance, and thus reduce potential confrontation (as in the assertiveness dimension). Hence, we hypothesize:

## H1b: Consultant support is used more extensively to deal with the problems of rapidly changing IT by Chinese organizations than by U.S. organizations.

Chinese organizations are also expected to rely more on the education and training coping mechanism. Being more concerned about in-group collectiveness, they would rely more on educating IS professionals about new IT through classes to enhance knowledge to deal with the problems, thereby improving self-confidence, and enhancing cohesiveness. To avoid the potential uncertainty of the new IT, they would also strive to keep informed of available new IT through more reading and conference attendance. They would inform IS professionals of the benefits of new IT to deal with resistance and potential confrontation. Hence:

H1c: Chinese organizations will use education and training more extensively to deal with the problems of rapidly changing IT than will U.S. organizations.

Chinese organizations are also expected to rely more on the internal procedures coping mechanism. Because their culture is more concerned about in-group collectivism, they would rely more on using well-defined IT acquisition and implementation procedures so that each IS professional would know what the others are doing, thus fulfilling the need for cohesiveness. To avoid the potential uncertainty of the new IT, in addition to applying such procedures, they would also consider only new IT successfully used elsewhere and new IT compatible with their existing IT. Hence:

## H1d: Chinese organizations will use internal procedures more extensively to deal with the problems of rapidly changing IT than will U.S. organizations.

Whereas H1a–d predict that the Chinese will use constructive coping mechanisms more than the Americans, the nature of endurance suggests that Americans are expected to use that coping mechanism more than the Chinese. In U.S. culture, with its lower in-group collectivism, leaders place less value on group loyalty and cohesiveness, and therefore support their subordinates less. By dealing with the problems of rapid IT change in constructive ways less (H1a–d), they would show greater tendency to force subordinates to work around problems without fixing them, ignore problems, or learn the IT on their own. Simultaneously, because of lower in-group collectivism, U.S. IT organizations would understand less about each other’s activities. Thus, U.S. IT professionals would have greater opportunity to work around or ignore problems without being discouraged from doing so.

Americans, being more comfortable with uncertainty, take greater risks than the Chinese. Thus, they are more likely to work around the problems without fixing them, ignore the problems, or attempt to learn the IT on their own to reduce the unpredictability of changing IT. With their greater assertiveness, Americans also have more self-confident can-do attitudes. Americans would hence be less intimidated by the problems and be more likely to struggle on while ignoring or working around them. Americans’ typical self-confidence would also lead them to attempt to learn new IT on their own. Hence, they are expected to be more inclined to endure the problems of rapidly changing IT, and so we hypothesize:

H1e: U.S. organizations will use endurance more extensively to deal with the problems of rapidly changing IT than will Chinese organizations.

## Effect of use of coping mechanisms on success in dealing with problems

Whereas prior research enables predicting differences in the use of coping mechanisms across national cultures as illustrated in H1a–e (De Luque & Javidan, 2004; Den Hartog, 2004; Gelfand et al., 2004; Javidan et al., 2006), it does not cogently predict differential effects on successful coping across them. Instead, it suggests merely that the coping mechanisms would reduce the unexpected work and delays caused by rapid IT change independently of national culture (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a; Marble, 2004; Skok & Tan, 2007).

To illustrate, regardless of national culture, the use of vendor support is expected to improve the organization’s success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT (Fink, 1998; Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). Vendors would have the best knowledge of their products and services, and can more effectively customize new IT, write interfaces, and provide other general support. Careful coordination among multiple vendors may enhance communication among them, and working with vendors may improve future versions of their IT. As a result, rapid IT change would produce less unexpected work and delay regardless of national culture. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2a: The greater the use of vendor support, the greater the success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT in

i. Chinese organizations, ii. U.S. organizations.

Also, regardless of national culture, the use of consultant support would improve the organization’s success in dealing with the problems (Fink, 1998; Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). Consultants are expected to be chosen on the basis of their specific expertise, and therefore would have much better knowledge of the new IT they are hired to support than would the IT organization. The IT organization could therefore employ consultants to help plan for new IT, aid in the implementation of new IT, and provide ongoing support for new IT to enable the organization to more effectively address the problems. Hence, we hypothesize:

H2b: The greater the use of consultant support, the greater the success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT in

i. Chinese organizations, ii. U.S. organizations.

The use of education and training is expected to improve the organization’s success in dealing with the problems in any national culture (Lee et al., 1995; Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). Education and training would increase the IT organization’s knowledge of the new IT and enable it to solve the problems itself. The IT organization could educate IS professionals about new IT through classes, customize the education to specific needs, send employees to conferences, and encourage them to read to keep informed about new IT. Hence:

H2c: The greater the use of education and training, the greater the success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT in

i. Chinese organizations, ii. U.S. organizations.

Also, the use of internal procedures is expected to improve the organization’s success (Huff & Munro, 1985; Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). Internal procedures would structure the IT organization’s approach to new IT and make the approach more visible and manageable. Thus, using well-defined IT acquisition and implementation procedures, considering only new IT compatible with existing IT and successfully used elsewhere, and documenting differences between new and previous IT would enable managers to monitor progress in new IT implementation. Hence:

H2d: The greater the use of internal procedures, the greater the success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT in

i. Chinese organizations, ii. U.S. organizations.

On the other hand, the use of endurance is likely to lessen the organization’s success (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). Ignoring or working around problems without fixing them leaves the problems intact. Learning new IT informally without classes and thus without the guidance of knowledgeable and experienced instructors could produce more problems. Hence:

H2e: The greater the use of endurance, the less the success in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT in

i. Chinese organizations ii. U.S. organizations.

In summary, the hypotheses predict two outcomes. First, coping mechanism usage is a function of culture with the Chinese culture using all but endurance more. Second, greater usage, independent of culture, will result in greater success for all coping mechanisms except endurance.

## Methodology

This study employed a postal survey to collect data from Chinese and U.S. IT organizations. It used an instrument from the aforementioned Benamati & Lederer (2001a) study, the only instrument identifiable by the authors for studying mechanisms for coping with rapid IT change. The instrument had asked the extent the organization had taken the actions in Table 1 to reduce problems caused by changing IT on major development, implementation, and support efforts underway during the last 3 years. It defined changing IT as any new hardware or software that had been used to build, operate, or maintain an organization’s IS applications and that had become available in the marketplace in the last 3 years. It had also asked how successful each of those same actions had been at reducing unexpected work, delays, and problems attributable to new IT. These questions used a 1–7 scale, where 1 meant to no extent for the coping mechanism items (and very unsuccessful for the success items) and 7 meant to a very great extent (or very successful). ## Analyses and Findings

Previous research using the U.S. data tested and confirmed discriminant and convergent validity for multiitem, coping mechanism factors (Benamati & Lederer, 2001a). The analysis of the Chinese data resulted in dropping three items (learn new IT informally without classes, consider only new IT compatible with existing IT, and consider only new IT successfully used by other organizations) due to extremely low Cronbach’s alphas. The alphas for the resulting constructs exceeded the recommended minimum of 0.70 (Nunnally, 1978) except for endurance in the Chinese data, which was quite close (see Table 4). The sample size for the Chinese data prevented additional tests of discriminant and convergent validity, and necessitated correlation for testing hypotheses.

## Discussion

## Extent of coping mechanism usage

As shown in Table 4, three of the five H1 hypotheses were supported (tinted areas represent statistically significant p values). That is, Chinese organizations used vendor support (H1a, Po0.001), education and training (H1c, Po0.05), and internal procedures (H1d, Po0.001) more extensively than U.S. organizations did. Support for H1a (greater use in China of vendor support), H1c (education and training), and H1d (internal procedures) was consistent with expectations. Greater in-group collectivism, greater uncertainty avoidance, and less assertiveness were expected to inspire greater pride and thus cohesiveness, greater reliance on procedures to alleviate uncertainty, and less self-confident can-do attitudes. As a result, the Chinese organizations would rely more on these coping mechanisms.

Table 4 accentuates the particular coping mechanism items that contribute to the support of H1a–d. In other words, for the vendor support category of coping mechanism, ‘Have vendors customize new IT’ and ‘Engage the vendor to write required interfaces between IT’, both significant at the 0.001 level, illustrate the greatest differences between coping in the two national cultures. For the education and training category, ‘Pressure IS professionals to use new IT’ and ‘Read to keep informed of available new IT’ illustrated the greatest differences. For internal procedures, ‘Use a well-defined IT acquisition procedure’ and ‘Document the differences between new and previous IT’ did so.

The analysis found no similar support for consultant support (H1b) and endurance (H1e). Perhaps the Chinese do not use more consultant support (H1b) because of potential conflicts that are inconsistent with in-group collectivism. Consultant support might reveal a lack of expertise and cause a loss of pride; it might increase internal conflict, leading to uncertainty; and it might make Chinese IT management feel less self-confident.

The interviews with Chinese and American IT managers illustrated such assertion. For example, a senior manager from a telecommunications consulting company described his firm’s effort to help a Chinese client develop broadband Internet services. The consulting company identified many problems in the client company. According to the senior manager, the client realized that the consultant’s help was valuable but the revelation of the problems hurt the pride and self-confidence of the client who eventually refused the consultant’s help.

Although Americans are greater risk takers and more self-confident in general, the threat of IT project failure may still inhibit their risk taking and self-confidence in such projects. ## Extent of coping mechanism success in each culture

H2 predicted the effects of the coping mechanisms on the extent of success by Chinese and U.S. organizations in dealing with the problems of rapidly changing IT. Vendor support predicted success in Chinese organizations (H2ai, Po0.05) as expected. Interestingly as Table 5 shows, no individual coping mechanism item predicted success in China at a statistically significant level, suggesting that all items, as a group, contributed to the effect.

However, vendor support did not predict success in U.S. organizations (H2aii). With national culture as the fundamental difference between the two samples, perhaps with greater individualistic thinking (low in-group collectivism), greater risk taking (low uncertainty avoidance), and greater assertiveness, the U.S. IT organization may use vendor support but adhere less closely to vendor recommendations than it should. An American CIO with an IT service provider described how his employer was installing an ERP system and had contacted the vendor for implementation help. The provider, however, then incurred greater risk by ignoring the vendor’s strong recommendation for more user involvement. Users rejected the system and the project failed.

Consultant support failed to predict success in both Chinese (H2bi) and U.S. organizations (H2bii). Perhaps in the Chinese culture, consultant support increases conflict by drawing attention to IT organization flaws and this conflict impedes the successful use of the coping mechanism. A Chinese systems development manager with an auto insurance software provider described how an outside consultant drew attention to shortcomings of a project leader by suggesting Java be used on the project instead of $\mathrm { C } + +$ as the leader had planned. The organization’s top management declined to use the project leader’s recommendation and instead followed the consultant’s. This decision embarrassed and angered the project leader, and created conflict between the project leader and both the consultant and top management, delaying the project substantially so that it failed to meet expectations.

Perhaps Americans’ greater individualistic thinking, risk taking, and assertiveness de-motivate them from adhering to consultant recommendations. An associate director in a risk and business consulting firm told of a health care services client that estimated a disaster would cause it to suffer significant financial loss each day its systems were down. The client company paid the consulting firm to recommend a continuity strategy, but the client, thinking individualistically, ignored the advice and remained exposed to a potential multi-million dollar loss. In any case, Table 5 shows only one statistically significant consultant support item, ‘Engage a consultant to help plan for new IT’, and its correlation is negative, suggesting, if anything, a possible unfavorable effect in the United States. (In fact. all of the U.S. consultant support correlations are negative.) On the other hand, in both countries, consultant support may have failed to predict success because the organization failed to select knowledgeable consultants. It may also have failed to lead them properly.

## Implications for research

As expected, this study confirmed that Chinese IT organizations apply vendor support, education and training, and internal procedures more extensively than do U.S. IT organizations (H1a–d). We conjecture above as to why Chinese IT organizations do not apply consultant support more extensively (H1b) and why U.S. IT organizations do not apply endurance more extensively (H1e). Future research might test that conjecture or search for other explanations.

This study confirmed that both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations use education and training (H2c) as well as internal procedures (H2d) to deal successfully with the problems of rapid IT change. However, the research did not show that vendor support and consultant support would be effective in both cultures. Instead, the analysis showed vendor support to be effective in China (H2ai) but not in the United States (H2aii) and consultant support to be effective in neither China (H2bi) nor the United States (H2bii). We also hypothesized that endurance would have an unfavorable effect in both cultures and it had such an effect (H2e). Future research might test the conjecture that differences in national culture explain these findings, or it might search for other explanations.

This study also suggested that future research might investigate how both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations could overcome the differential effects of culture. Research might thus ask such questions as: What actions might U.S. organizations take to avoid their failure to effectively use vendor support? What actions might both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations take to achieve favorable effects from consultant support? What actions might both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations take to avoid the unfavorable effects of endurance? Such research might thereby enable IT organizations in both cultures to deal better with rapid IT change.

Three years was a discretionary choice as the criterion for rapid IT change in the American data collection. The same timeframe was used for the Chinese data collection to facilitate comparison. Future research might empirically establish a timeframe to serve as a foundation for later research. Such a timeframe might be shorter or even longer than 3 years.

In order to facilitate data comparison, this study used the same items for success in different cultures, an approach used in past research (Xie et al., 1998; Calhoun et al., 2002; Sagi et al., 2004). However, perhaps cultures perceive success differently. Future research might therefore study different cultures to confirm or refute that information systems success is perceived similarly.

This study investigated rapid IT change from the perspective of IT managers at Chinese and U.S. organizations. Future research might examine coping with rapid IT change in other cultures, and it might also investigate the perspective of vendors, consultants, users, and other managers.

## Limitations

The major limitation in this study was probably the Chinese response rate and resultant sample size. The sample size may have had an impact on the results, but it was sufficient for the analytic techniques in the study. In an attempt to increase sample size, one of the authors, a Chinese national, visited China to work on the data collection. Future research might try a different approach for improving the response rate.

The coping mechanisms in this study were identified based on a U.S. sample. Future research might identify coping mechanisms based on a sample from China or another national culture. Perhaps such a study would identify different coping mechanisms useful in other cultures as well.

Finally, many other elements besides national culture such as resources and general expertise (prior experience) might influence the adoption of new IT. Future research might consider such elements.

## Implications for practice

Correlation is not causation. However, the significant results regarding the effects of the coping mechanisms are consistent with the recommendation that both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations apply education and training as well as internal procedures more, and that Chinese IT organizations apply vendor support more. After all, those coping mechanisms were effective in IT organizations in those national cultures. Managers in those organizations might therefore follow such recommendations.

Consultant support appeared to have no effects in Chinese and U.S. IT organizations. Those organizations might want to reduce their use of that coping mechanism, or try to identify and overcome impediments to using it successfully.

Endurance appeared to have unfavorable effects in both Chinese and U.S. IT organizations. Those organizations might want to reduce their use of endurance, or try to minimize the negative effects when they are forced to use it.

Perhaps the most important implications of this research are for managers at headquarters of multinational corporations with responsibilities for divisions in different national cultures. First, made aware of the differences in the extent of use of the vendor support, education and training, and internal procedures coping mechanisms in China and the United States, such managers might better understand the nature of those differences in their own divisions (regardless of whether in China, the United States, Europe, or elsewhere) and better understand the nature of differences in other practices. With the realization that differences might be attributable to the national culture rather than the peculiarities of individuals, the managers might further be better able to work with their IT managers and professionals at their divisions across different cultures.

Second, multinational corporate managers with responsibilities at divisions in different cultures might want to encourage or even require that those divisions deal differently with rapid IT change. Although education and training and internal procedures were effective in both cultures in this study, vendor support appeared culturally dependent. Thus for example, multinational corporate management might expect coping mechanisms such as vendor support to be more effective in a division in one culture (China in this study) than another (the U.S.). Such management might therefore take from this study the importance of not forcing each division to follow the very same practices, regardless of its national culture.

## Conclusion

Managing information systems in today’s environment of rapidly changing technology is challenging IT organizations around the world, and those organizations need to cope better. This study has confirmed and extended national culture theory to the process of coping with rapid information technology change while enriching our understanding of coping itself. It confirmed the categories of coping mechanisms in the Chinese IT context, and raises awareness of the rapid IT change challenge and of the coping mechanisms that lead to success in a culture other than the U.S. It hence lays the groundwork for future research on the categories in this study in other cultures.

The study has confirmed that Chinese IT organizations apply the constructive coping mechanisms of vendor support, education and training, and internal procedures more extensively than do U.S. IT organizations. It has extended national culture theory with its differential findings that vendor support leads to success in coping with rapid IT change in China but not the U.S. by suggesting that culture plays a role in how effectively their coping actually works, while at the same time not indicating that national culture plays a role in the effectiveness of consultant support, education and training, internal procedures, and endurance.

Recent studies have considered the impact of national culture in information systems in a variety of areas (Hanke & Teo, 2003; Tan et al., 2003; Chai & Pavlou, 2004; Kankanhalli et al., 2004; Ho et al., 2007; Lippert & Volkmar, 2007; Ramingwong & Sajeev, 2007; Siau et al., 2007; Sinkovics et al., 2007; Kock et al., 2008; Reinig et al., 2009). By extending national culture theory to the process of coping, this study gives evidence of the further potential of the theory in information systems research.

The study confirmed that Chinese and U.S. IT organizations are simultaneously different and the same. They are different in that Chinese IT organizations rely on vendor support, education and training, and internal procedures more. Thus, multi-national managers from one culture who are responsible for coping in the other culture need to understand that differences in behavior are likely due to differences in national culture, and they must be especially cautious in attempting to change behavior in the foreign culture.
