# WHEN THE MACHINE MEETS THE EXPERT: AN ETHNOGRAPHY OF DEVELOPING AI FOR HIRING<sup>1</sup>

Elmira van den Broek, Anastasia Sergeeva, and Marleen Huysman

Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam, De Boelelaan 1105,

1081 HV Amsterdam, THE NETHERLANDS

{e.p.h.vanden.broek@vu.nl} {a.sergeeva@vu.nl} {m.h.huysman@vu.nl}

The introduction of machine learning (ML) in organizations comes with the claim that algorithms will produce insights superior to those of experts by discovering the “truth” from data. Such a claim gives rise to a tension between the need to produce knowledge independent of domain experts and the need to remain relevant to the domain the system serves. This two-year ethnographic study focuses on how developers managed this tension when building an ML system to support the process of hiring job candidates at a large international organization. Despite the initial goal of getting domain experts “out the loop,” we found that developers and experts arrived at a new hybrid practice that relied on a combination of ML and domain expertise. We explain this outcome as resulting from a process of mutual learning in which deep engagement with the technology triggered actors to reflect on how they produced knowledge. These reflections prompted the developers to iterate between excluding domain expertise from the ML system and including it. Contrary to common views that imply an opposition between ML and domain expertise, our study foregrounds their interdependence and as such shows the dialectic nature of developing ML. We discuss the theoretical implications of these findings for the literature on information technologies and knowledge work, information system development and implementation, and human–ML hybrids.

Keywords: Machine learning, artificial intelligence, knowledge production, knowledge work, mutual learning, human–ML hybrids, information system development, learning algorithms, ethnography, hiring, human resources

## Introduction

The introduction of machine learning (ML) into the world of management and organizations comes with the promise that learning algorithms will become part of how we come to know and make decisions. Consider, for example, the claim that deep learning technologies surpass radiologists’ expertise in spotting tumors medical images (Aerts 2018), that one’s digital footprint, not financial advisors, determines credit worthiness (Pasquale 2015), and that hiring algorithms promise insights into candidates’ profiles that are unknown to recruiters (Tambe et al. 2019). This so-called machine knowledge production, where knowledge is created out of data through the application of ML techniques, may offer a radically new approach to the production of knowledge (Bonde Thylstrup et al. 2019; Henriksen and Bechmann 2020; Kitchin 2014; Newell 2015). Therefore, scholars call for more attention to how ML changes our understanding of knowledge production in organizations.

How ML improves the creation, sharing, and use of knowledge in organizations is different from how traditional information technologies (IT) attempted to do so. These knowledge systems, such as intranets, expert systems, and virtual networks, were mainly introduced to augment knowledge work and depended on practitioners contributing to them (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Huysman and De Wit 2004; Newell 2015; Ruggles 1998). Knowledge systems had to align closely with the work practices of the domain experts they served to be relevant to them and improve knowledge work (Huysman and De Wit 2004; McDermott 1999; Orlikowski 1993; Schultze and Boland 2000). In contrast, current ML systems attempt to automate knowledge work by learning from data without relying on the involvement of domain experts (Agarwal and Dhar 2014; Agrawal et al. 2018; Domingos 2015; Siegel 2013). Going beyond traditional business intelligence tools that aggregate and mine data to deliver comprehensive knowledge to organizations (Chen et al. 2012), ML systems generate their own predictive models from data to provide insights that are superior to those of domain experts.

Therefore, ML technologies differ from traditional knowledge systems in that they produce knowledge more or less independent of domain experts. Specifically, these systems promise to mitigate the human biases, inefficiencies, and pathdependencies that have plagued experts’ work for decades, discovering “truthful” knowledge on their own instead (Agarwal and Dhar 2014; Agrawal et al. 2018; Domingos 2015; Siegel 2013). However, the literature leads us to expect that the pursuit of independence likely clashes with the need to produce knowledge that is relevant to the domain, as previous generations of knowledge systems failed if they were not useful to domain experts’ work practices. We must update our understanding of how knowledge systems enter organizations and how they manage the novel tension between independence (i.e., producing knowledge without relying on domain expertise) and relevance (i.e., producing knowledge that is useful to the domain). Therefore, this study asks: How do ML developers manage the tension of producing knowl edge independently without losing relevance to the domain?

We address this question with our two-year ethnographic study of an ML vendor that develops an ML system to support the process of hiring job candidates at a large international organization. To trace the knowledge production process, we take a practice perspective on knowledge, that is, a study of the situated practices of actors in the workplace through which knowledge is constituted (Brown and Duguid 2001; Cook and Brown 1999; Orlikowski 2002). We find that ML developers manage the tension between independence and relevance through a process of mutual learning in which developers and domain experts reflect on and adapt the activities through which they come to know candidates by excluding and including domain expertise. As a result, actors’ divergent hiring practices start to converge, unintendedly leading to the emergence of a new hybrid practice in which candidates are judged and selected by relying on a combination of ML and domain expertise. This study thereby contributes to research on IT and knowledge work, information systems (IS) devel opment and implementation, and human–ML hybrids.

## Knowledge Production with Machine Learning

Knowledge production with ML, or machine knowledge production, may offer a superior pathway to knowledge, where machines can discover the “truth” from data (Bonde Thylstrup et al. 2019; Henriksen and Bechmann 2020; Kitchin 2014). ML, one of the central areas of artificial intelligence (AI), refers to a broad set of techniques that can derive and revise knowledge automatically over time by learning from data (Domingos 2015; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). ML usually takes the form of either supervised or unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, the algorithm learns from a labeled training dataset to predict the labels of future data. In unsupervised learning there are no labels to predict, and instead the algorithm groups or clusters the data in some meaningful way (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). We limit the discussion to supervised learning because, with its focus on prediction and classification tasks, this type is most commonly used in organizations nowadays and positioned as being able to take organizationally impactful decisions on its own, such as in risk assessment, predictive policing, and credit scoring (Ford 2018; Lehr and Ohm 2017). By developing predictive models from data, these ML systems can produce knowledge and steer action more or less independent of domain experts, thereby mitigating the undesirable human biases and subjectivities that have plagued knowledge production. However, the pursuit of independence may also render ML’s produced knowledge irrelevant to the domain it serves. To clarify the tension between independence and relevance that surrounds machine knowledge production, we review ML research on the new knowledge production paradigm, and the literature on IT and knowledge work.

## The Machine Learning Paradigm

The introduction of ML to organizations has come with a new set of assumptions regarding how knowledge should be produced in organizations (Elish and Boyd 2018; Kitchin 2014). By transforming data into knowledge, ML systems are positioned as superior means for creating insights, suggesting that machines can produce knowledge independent of domain experts to improve understanding and inform action (Agrawal et al. 2018; Domingos 2015; Siegel 2013). This new paradigm suggests that knowledge can be produced through a set of activities different from traditional IT systems to realize new promises for organizations, as summarized in Table 1.

ML systems attempt to produce knowledge independent of domain experts by collecting training data, training a model on the data, and deploying the model to predict future outcomes. Instead of eliciting experts’ knowledge through communication techniques like interviews, surveys, and observation (Forsythe 1993), developers ensure independence by gathering training data. The data are assumed to be “raw” and neutral, so they represent reality in a way that is more accurate than human experts could (Cukier and Mayer-Schönberger 2013; Jones 2019; Kitchin 2014). For example, to learn how to detect fraud, an ML system is fed with many thousands of examples of transactions that customers reported as fraudulent, whereas a bank employee can handle only a small number of suspicious transactions. Since ML systems’ accuracy improves with exposure to additional training examples, their performance depends on a vast and varied supply of data (Domingos 2015; Ford 2018).

This training data is then fed to a learning algorithm that converts it into knowledge in the form of a predictive model, thereby avoiding the need to codify experts’ knowledge into the system (Forsythe 1993). By computationally detecting patterns in large datasets, algorithms can uncover hidden relationships that are far too large and complex for humans to decipher. These learning algorithms can adapt automatically to changes in the data and revise the knowledge they have created in response (Agrawal et al. 2018; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). In the fraud example, the algorithm learns which conditions are proxies for fraudulent outcomes, such as the time of the penultimate transaction or the presence of other payees at the same postal address, and adapts these learned distinctions to changes in the nature of fraudulent transactions in the dataset.

Finally, using the model to predict future outcomes automatically so as to steer the ML system’s decision-making ensures independence (Agrawal et al. 2018). For example, when a new transaction arrives, the fraud-detection system can predict the likelihood of fraud based on its learned distinctions between fraudulent and legitimate transactions and decide whether to refuse the transaction accordingly. It thereby predicts forthcoming forms of fraud by generalizing from previously observed examples (Siegel 2013). The use of predictive models allows machines to perform tasks that were once considered the exclusive domain of human experts, such as planning political campaigns, grading student essays, and diagnosing mental illness (Siegel 2013). Thus, the ML paradigm represents a radical empiricist mode of knowledge production that attempts to avoid the need for expert work altogether (Domingos 2015; Kitchin 2014; Siegel 2013).

The new activities through which ML systems attempt to produce knowledge give rise to the claim that these systems can create insights that are superior to those of their human counterparts. A first promise of ML is that it helps to overcome many of the irrationalities and biases that plague domain experts, thereby providing more objective knowledge. Experts can suffer from various cognitive deficiencies, such as poor memory, distraction, and prejudice (Kahneman et al. 1982; Mitchell et al. 1986). For instance, experts can make poor hiring decisions because they are easily distracted by information that is not relevant to job performance, such as aspects of candidates’ personalities, appearance, or demeanor (Kuncel et al. 2013). By inferring patterns from data, ML seeks to replace such “gut” instincts and bias with objective and measurable “facts” (Agrawal et al. 2018; Siegel 2013).

The hope is that automated techniques also discover new knowledge that domain experts may have missed (Agarwal and Dhar 2014; Domingos 2015; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). By analyzing large datasets in new ways and factoring in complex interactions between variables, ML systems can uncover novel patterns and relationships that might not otherwise be conceived of or anticipated (Agarwal and Dhar 2014; Müller et al. 2016). In the medical field, for instance, ML-driven microscopic image analysis is used to predict malignant breast tumors. Developers claim that its predictions are at least as accurate as those of human pathologists, and ML has even been able to discover new markers of breast cancer that experts have ignored so far (Beck et al. 2011).

Finally, ML promises more efficient ways for producing knowledge. Whereas domain experts can process onlylimited amounts of information, algorithms can analyze huge quantities of data from multiple sources with the kind of speed and efficiency that far surpasses human abilities (Brynjolfsson and McAfee 2014; Schildt 2017). For example, given a database of patient records, an algorithm is claimed to learn to diagnose a patient’s condition in a fraction of a second, while physicians must rely on years of medical training and practice (Domingos 2015). Thus, ML systems offer organizations a way to discover insights and inform action with unprecedented speed and consistency.

However, despite these promises, emerging research alerts us to the difficulties that arise when producing knowledge independently (e.g., Amershi et al. 2014; Holzinger 2016; Seidel et al. 2020; Selbst et al. 2019). Machines are known to suffer from the “frame problem,” which describes the inability of algorithms to reason about and act on events they are not designed to handle (McCarthy and Hayes 1969; Salovaraa et al. 2019; Seidel et al. 2020). This makes machines incapable of recognizing what is relevant to some particular context of action (Dreyfus 2007). Moreover, by distancing themselves from domain experts and the wider social context in which they work, ML systems fail to account for the broader social system that surrounds the application. For example, by mathematically defining social concepts such as fairness and justice, ML systems fail to recognize their full meaning, which can be at times procedural, contextual, and contestable (Selbst et al. 2019; Van den Broek et al. 2019). This may render ML applications ineffective, inaccurate, and even misguided (e.g., Amershi et al. 2014; Holzinger 2016; Selbst et al. 2019).

In sum, the ML paradigm is characterized by the ultimate goal of developing algorithms that produce knowledge without relying on domain expertise that is often considered biased and inefficient. To sustain this ideal, these systems have to maintain a sufficient degree of independence from the activities through which experts produce knowledge. However, this pursuit of independence can be problematic, as ML systems fail to recognize what is relevant to a particular course of action and the broader domain of which they form part. The literature on IT and knowledge work has long recognized the importance of making tools that are relevant to the domains they serve. It is to this topic to which we now turn.

## IT and Knowledge Work

The literature on IT and knowledge work recognizes the importance of developing tools that are relevant to the domain. Research on knowledge systems that emerged in response to the popularity of IT-enabled knowledgemanagement initiatives like repositories, expert systems, and data warehouses (Ruggles 1998) is particularly concerned with this issue and focuses on whether IT lives up to its expectations of improving knowledge efforts and ensuring organizational learning (Alavi and Leidner 2001; Huysman and De Wit 2004; Newell 2015; Schultze and Leidner 2002). Traditional knowledge systems have similarities with ML systems in that they perceive knowledge as objective “facts” and seek to provide organizations with comprehensive insights by capturing information that is relatively devoid of context (Ruggles 1998). However, while these knowledge systems were popular among management, studies following their implementation and use in organizations often find that these systems fail to deliver on their promises (Huysman and De Wit 2004; McDermott 1999; Schultze and Boland 2000).

Practice-based studies explain that a reason for such failure relates to misalignment between the idea of what constitutes knowledge as embedded in the design of the system, and how knowledge actually manifests in experts’ daily work (Orlikowski 1993; Schultze and Boland 2000; Vaast and Walsham 2005). Rather than understanding knowledge as a decontextualized object, these studies focus on the everyday practices through which workers come to know how to get things done in complex organizational work (Newell 2015; Orlikowski 2002; Swan et al. 1999). By taking this practice perspective on knowledge (Brown and Duguid 2001; Cook and Brown 1999; Orlikowski 2002), these scholars recognize the difficulty developers encounter in building useful tools because of the largely invisible and complex nature of the knowledge work they are expected to support.

A practice perspective highlights that knowledge is socially constructed, contested, and situated in nature. This perspective suggests that developers may overestimate the extent to which knowledge can exist in an objective form, as knowledge requires an active process of meaning construction, making it subjective and open to interpretation (Schultze and Leidner 2002; Tsoukas 1996). The meanings that individuals construct are often intertwined with the norms and values that characterize the social context within which knowledge work is undertaken. For example, experts’ social values and moral judgments may dictate what factors they consider appropriate to measure and embed in a technology (Pine and Liboiron 2015). Therefore, to build useful tools, developers must align their tools with the norms and values of those involved with the knowledge system (e.g., Bresnen et al. 2003; Huysman and Wulf 2006; Swan et al. 1999).

Moreover, practice-based studies alert us to struggles and power conflicts that may arise as a result of the contested nature of knowledge. A practice lens recognizes that what constitutes knowledge is open to dispute and that groups compete for control of what is worth knowing and what should be done to produce knowledge (Contu and Willmott 2003; Nicolini 2011). As a result, dominant groups in an organization often hold power over what knowledge will or will not be included in the knowledge system (Huysman 2000) or decide not to use the knowledge system at all when it threatens their privileged positions (Orlikowski 1993; Schultze and Boland 2000). For example, as Orlikowski (1993) explains, experts avoid contributing their knowledge to groupware when the collaborative premises that underlie the system are at odds with how they are rewarded in the organization, so they fear a loss of power if they share their ideas with others. Therefore, to ensure that knowledge systems are used, developers must create solutions that are consistent with experts’ motivations and interests.

Finally, practice-based work highlights that developers may overestimate the extent to which knowledge is codifiable and independent of context by pointing to its situated nature (Nicolini 2011; Orlikowski 2002). No matter how explicit and well-defined knowledge is in guiding action, the context within which people act requires them to make continuous inferences and judgments (Tsoukas 1996). In their case on a Greek call center, for example, Tsoukas and Vladimirou (2001) explain how employees handle customer concerns, not by simply following explicit rules but by constantly modifying the rules to the particular contexts they face. The “right” response to a customer’s question differs from time to time and from context to context, depending on the unique situation it addresses. Therefore, to be useful, knowledge systems must contribute to and be embedded in domain experts’ situated work practices (Agterberg et al. 2010; Levina and Vaast 2005; Swan et al. 1999).

From this line of research, it follows that, to be relevant to the domain they serve, knowledge systems must align with the norms and values, power structures, and work practices that guide how experts come to know. However, since these traditional knowledge systems differ significantly from ML systems, we cannot directly transfer the lessons learned to the new generation of tools. While earlier systems were introduced mainly to augment knowledge work and depended on experts who contributed to them, ML systems aim to automate knowledge work by producing knowledge more or less independent of experts. Rather than close alignment, these tools seek to bypass domain experts, as it is these experts who can inhibit knowledge work.

Despite these differences between prior knowledge systems and ML systems, the need to remain relevant to the domain still holds, as work on ML interventions indicates (e.g., Amershi et al. 2014; Holzinger 2016; Selbst et al. 2019). Therefore, we expect that ML systems are accompanied by a new tension between maintaining independence from domain experts so they can uphold their claim to superior knowledge and maintaining relevance to the domain they serve. To explore the dynamics of this tension and how to cope with it, we need to update our understanding of how knowledge systems are developed and support knowledge work. In the next section, we report on a field study that focuses on how ML developers and domain experts manage this tension by iterating between excluding and including domain expertise as both groups reflect on the activities through which they produce knowledge.

## Method

We conducted a 23-month field study at the ML vendor “NeuroYou”<sup>2</sup> from January 2018 until December 2019. We employed ethnographic methods, which have shown to be useful in studying novel IS phenomena (Schultze 2000; Vieira da Cunha 2013) and have been called for in studies of knowledge work with ML (Hadjimichael and Tsoukas 2019; Newell 2015). These methods allowed us to trace how the process of machine knowledge production unfolded over time and to unpack the promises, problems, and practices of the actors involved in the process, thereby generating an explanation for how developers manage the independence-relevance tension in machine knowledge production.

## Research Setting

NeuroYou is a scale-up vendor in Europe that develops ML systems to provide insights into job candidates’ fit with the organization to improve hiring decisions. The company, which is recognized as a leader in the market of predictive people analytics, offers a wide range of ML-supported functionalities, including more than 50 online gamified assessments. Unlike traditional selection methods that rely on self-reported data, such as questionnaires and CVs, these games simulate situations where participants’ personalities and behavior are supposed to be reflected. For example, one game asks candidates to inflate a digital balloon with air in exchange for a reward. Since each inflation also presents a risk of bursting the balloon and losing the rewards gained, the game identifies a person’s propensity for risk-taking behavior. Another game measures people’s ability to understand emotions. Candidates are asked to label the emotion they believe is expressed in a video or audio clip, and their score is a direct representation of how many emotions they labeled “correctly”

and, thus, their social perceptiveness. These games, which typically last one to five minutes, promise insights into more than 150 personal attributes, including personality traits (e.g., extraversion), cognitive skills (e.g., short-term memory), and career values (e.g., working conditions). A learning algorithm then predicts candidates’ fit with the organization by analyzing the attributes of existing high-performing employees and matching them against candidates to identify those candidates with the highest overlap in attributes.

NeuroYou has multiple clients of various sizes and from various industries, ranging from hotel chains to universities. The company employs around 30 ML developers. We define the term “ML developer” broadly, using it to refer to a range of employees who work with ML in management, research and development (R&D) and data science, software development, sales, and marketing and design. ML developers have university degrees in such fields as data science, computer science, (neuro)psychology, and business, with only one person trained in the subject area of human resources (HR). Almost half of the 30 ML developers joined the company as recent graduates with little to no work experience. The teams at NeuroYou are described in Table 2.

To trace how the developers used the ML technology to produce insights on candidates, we zoomed in on their work with “MultiCo,” a key client. At the time of data collection, MultiCo belonged to one of the world’s largest fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) companies, with almost 200,000 employees in more than 50 countries. We selected this account because the client was interested in adopting a wide range of functionalities, was the focus of most of the developers’ work activities, and offered expansive site access (reported in another study).

Before the use of the ML system, MultiCo’s hiring process consisted of a screening stage, involving CVs and standardized tests that HR professionals evaluated, and an interview stage, in which senior managers assessed candidates based on a group interview and made final hiring decisions. With the introduction of the ML system, the screening stage was substituted by NeuroYou’s online games, in which a learning algorithm predicted candidates’ fit with the organization based on the input from the games. Candidates who were predicted to be a good fit (i.e., who matched the attributes of high-performing employees with more than 72%) were selected by the algorithm for the final interview stage. Senior managers then had the algorithmic predictions to inform their assessments during the group interview. ## Data Collection

Our data sources included nonparticipant observations, semistructured interviews, and archival records collected over a 23-month period. Table 3 shows a summary of all data collected per period, as discussed below.

The first author conducted 122 hours of field observations, including on-site and online observations. For each on-site observation at NeuroYou, the researcher followed a different ML developer, spending at least a full workday with every team. The researcher documented in-depth and time-specific data related to all aspects of the daily work practices, including modeling activities, design- and data-visualization activities, a client workshop with MultiCo, and internal team meetings that addressed the company’s strategy. The researcher also participated in informal events, such as lunches and dinners, to observe how employees interacted informally. Online observations involved observations of NeuroYou– MultiCo meetings that took place via an online video conference platform. In these online meetings, which included recurrent meetings between the developers and HR professionals who were involved with the project, progress and updates related to the project were discussed, such as updates on the number of employees who participated in data collection. Online meetings were also sometimes spontaneous meetings in which problems or requests of HR professionals were discussed that required immediate action, such as when experts requested explanations of insights the algorithm had produced. Whenever possible, the researcher recorded meetings and transcribed the conversations to complement the field notes. Detailed notes made during the day and during meetings were expanded into a 203-page document of written field notes.

As a complement to our field observations, 47 unstructured and semi-structured interviews were conducted with NeuroYou’s ML developers and MultiCo’s HR team. The interviews provided more in-depth evidence and interpretations related to specific parts of the knowledge production process, and complemented other information we had gathered about the aims, challenges, and work activities around these events. Examples of interview questions were: “What was most challenging when you started collecting data?” and “What was most surprising when you saw the results the system produced?” Interviews lasted 30–90 minutes and were all audio-recorded and transcribed. Finally, we had access to both NeuroYou’s and MultiCo’s shared folders related to the ML project, 814 internal documents, including PowerPoint presentations, project proposals, and brand materials. The company documents allowed us to collect the companies’ statements, vision, and proposed actions, so we could triangulate the data from our field notes and interview transcripts.

## Data Analysis

We used a process research approach to track the flow of events and document the knowledge production process as it happened in practice. We started our analysis by using visual mapping as a preliminary exploratory step to recreate a chronology of how knowledge production with ML unfolded in the field (Langley 1999). We selected evidence from the company documents, interview data, and observation data that indicated the occurrence of any event that, from the informants’ perspective, signaled a critical juncture in the knowledge production process. For example, we reconstructed when and how NeuroYou initially contacted MultiCo, when the pilot study kicked off the knowledge production process, when the collection of training data started, and when the model was deployed at MultiCo. All events were recorded chronologically in Excel and linked to their data source. We began with the events that were indicated in more than one data source (e.g., recorded in PowerPoint and captured in the interview). The final list contained 94 events that occurred between July 2012 and December 2019.

Next, we visually represented the event chronology, indicating whether the event represented an action (e.g., “HR team recruits employees”), a problem (e.g., “HR team cannot supply exhaustive data”), or a strategy to solve the problem (e.g., “developers use selection of attributes”). The visual mapping helped us to move from the raw data to a more abstract conceptualization of the process. Next, we decomposed the event chronology into three phases to structure the description of events: “constructing training data,” “building a model,” and “using the model in practice.” We chose these labels, as there were clear discontinuities at its frontiers (e.g., MultiCo signed off on the model, signaling an end to the model-building period and the start of the model-deploying period). This temporal bracketing allowed us to constitute comparative units of analysis so we could explore theoretical mechanisms (Langley 1999). Figure 3, which shows an interim output of the visual mapping, illustrates the event chronology with key events for the three temporal phases of machine knowledge production.

Then we turned to thematic analysis of our observation and interview data to expand and deepen our documentation of the temporal process. To organize a large corpus of data involving both NeuroYou and MultiCo, we used an exploratory role-by-time matrix (Miles and Huberman 1994). We coded all instances in the field notes and interviews that described the ML developers’ and HR professionals’ expectations and intentions at the start of each period, what actions they took, what problems they faced, and what consequences followed. Table A1 in the Appendix shows a summary of this matrix. After analyzing this role-by-time matrix, we noticed that the process contained critical turning points and deviations from the path that both groups sought to take. For example, the ML developers started with optimistic assumptions about data collection at MultiCo but ran into unanticipated problems, such as the lack of objective performance indicators from which the algorithm could learn. These problems triggered reflections on their key claim and their activities for producing knowledge. As for the HR professionals, they were recurrently surprised by unexpected insights the algorithm produced, such as the finding that MultiCo tends to hire extraverts even though the learning algorithm suggested that introverts were performing better in the organization. These surprises made the HR team as well reflect on the proper action to take to produce knowledge.

Next, we examined each of those turning points in more detail, going back to the observation data to search for additional evidence on why these deviations occurred, what preceded them, and how actors perceived them. The purpose of our analysis was to identify the patterns and generative mechanisms that underlay the process instead of causal sequences and to present a “realist tale” (Van Maanen 2011). For example, we performed a narrative analysis to represent the developers’ assumptions, which revealed their paradigmatic beliefs about the nature of knowledge, such as their belief that knowledge was best created by learning from data on employees as opposed to relying on HR professionals judgment. This analysis drew our attention to the activities developers engaged in to produce knowledge independent of the HR professionals, and developers’ shift to collaborating with the HR professionals after reflecting on their activities in interacting with the domain.

We then proceeded to theorizing with our data by developing an explanation for how knowledge production with ML unfolded. For example, we conceptualized the activities through which developers tried to oppose the domain experts and produce knowledge independentlywith ML as “excluding domain expertise.” We conceptualized the activities through which developers incorporated the experts’ insights into the ML system to produce relevant knowledge to the domain as “including domain expertise.” We came to understand that the iterations between excluding and including domain expertise arose from actors’ mutually reflecting on and adapting how they came to know candidates. Our findings report on this mutual learning process, which explains how ML evolved over time into a new human–ML hybrid practice for performing knowledge work.

## Findings

We found that, despite the initial goal of producing knowledge independently, the organization arrived over time at a new practice of hiring that relied on a combination of ML and domain expertise, which we label a human–ML hybrid practice. The hybrid practice emerged as both ML developers and HR professionals engaged deeply with the technology and were confronted with problems around producing knowledge with ML. Prompted by these issues, developers and experts learned about and then adapted the opposing activities through which they produced knowledge by excluding and including domain expertise. Through repeated cycles of such mutual learning, two initially opposing practices of hiring— judging and selecting job candidates by relying on ML or by relying on domain expertise—eventually converged, creating a new hiring practice that combined both elements. Following the chronological sequence of events, we present our findings according to three mutual learning cycles between the ML developers and domain experts, as summarized in Table 4. Before elaborating on these mutual learning cycles, we first explain how the ML developers claimed to offer knowledge that was superior to those of domain experts.

## Setting the Stage

In the summer of 2012, after having experienced the downsides of flawed expert judgment in the job application process, Richard decided to found an ML company, “NeuroYou.” The company’s mission was to change the traditional ways of hiring to overcome the bias and discrimination in candidate selection processes. His idea was to develop an ML-driven tool that helps organizations make quality hiring decisions by understanding “who people really are and know how effective they are going to be in the future” (company documents). Many of NeuroYou’s developers mentioned that it was because of these values, particularly the commitment to developing a deeper understanding of people’s “true potential” that is free of human biases, that they decided to join NeuroYou. NeuroYou’s developers were young and progressive; most were recently graduated neuroscientists who felt that they could contribute to creating a better world by joining the company. As one ML developer explained:

I think it’s a really important movement at the moment to give everyone a fair opportunity, view everyone as equal, and focus more on people’s innate qualities as opposed to what school they went to, what they look like, or how they sound. That’s the reason I’m interested in [working at NeuroYou]. In AI and this progressive movement and to see people as a whole and not look at them subjectively. (ML developer, NeuroYou)

NeuroYou’s approach to judging job candidates was claimed to be superior to domain experts. Rather than relying on experts’ methods prone to bias and subjectivity, such as CVs that can boast elite university background, and job interviews where judgments about candidates are formed within the first seconds, NeuroYou relied on online games that generated raw, and unmediated data on people’s personalities and skills. The games were used as input for a learning algorithm that predicted candidates’ fit with the organization. Typically, the algorithm was trained with data on high- and low-performing employees, so it “learned” which personality traits and skills characterized a good employee for this particular organization. With this ML approach, NeuroYou’s ML developers believed they could mitigate experts’ biases, path-dependence, and inefficiency. For example, NeuroYou’s company brochure advertised:

However hard you try, fatigue and personal bias can always cloud judgement, risking talent and potential slipping through the recruitment net. NeuroYou can lend a helping hand, saving you time and increasing decision-making accuracy. (Company documents)

In the summer of 2017, NeuroYou had the opportunity to develop a ML-driven selection tool for the international company “MultiCo,” which later became one of NeuroYou’s largest and longest-standing clients. Tim, MultiCo’s HR innovation manager, approached NeuroYou because he believed hiring could be much faster and more efficient, especially when the company had to select large groups of candidates, as they did for its trainee programs:

We have a real pain point around recruiting and retaining talent across Europe, and we want to see how we can improve that using something other than traditional methods. (Tim, HR innovation manager, MultiCo)

To provide evidence of this claim, NeuroYou ran a small-scale pilot on two groups, comparing 250 candidates for the trainee programs with 40 successful trainees in the organization. To the surprise of MultiCo’s HR team, the algorithm inferred from the data that successful trainees were often introverts, while the experts had systematically rejected candidates who reflected this personality trait. Sarah, HR manager at MultiCo, was astonished and interpreted this finding as evidence of problems with HR’s expertise in selecting the right candidate for the job, saying, “This suggests bias and shows we are not always assessing candidates on what actually makes them successful!” (Field notes, team meeting). Within a month of seeing the pilot results, MultiCo signed an agreement to work with NeuroYou.

## Constructing Training Data

In the winter of 2017, the ML developers started their knowledge production journey at MultiCo. Initially, they sought to produce knowledge independent of domain experts by excluding these experts’ input, instead generating a large training dataset on current employees in the company. However, when they introduced this new activity, the developers were confronted with a “data-selection problem,” where the machine does not know what data to select and learn from. This problem led the developers, together with the HR professionals, to reflect on what actions are appropriate to produce knowledge, eventually resulting in the construction of training data that included the professional standards HR professionals used to judge employees.

## Excluding Domain Expertise

The ML developers started collecting a dataset of pairs of employee attributes and performance outcomes with which to train the algorithm. NeuroYou’s online games measured the employees’ attributes, which promised a “true blind audition” by ignoring protected characteristics like age and gender and evaluated more than 150 personality traits and skills instead. MultiCo’s HR team appreciated the online games’ ensuring objectivity in hiring, as demonstrated in the narrative of a presentation they gave to MultiCo’s managers:

By providing scientific, data-driven evidence in our assessments, we eliminate bias and we guarantee objective evaluations. NeuroYou’s “blind” assessments provide us with detailed analytics and reports that focus on the attributes that really matter when hiring a candidate. (Company document, MultiCo)

Since the developers could not tell which traits and skills were central to new hires’ ability to be successful in the company, they proposed that MultiCo’s employees played all of the online games. By datafying the complete skeleton of a person’s behavior, the algorithm could learn which attributes were most relevant to performance:

More is better for us, particularly in those early stages. Because we never know what we’ll find, we never know what correlates with other stuff that we get in the future, so it’s always good to tank more data initially, and then look at the signals in the data. (Richard, CEO, NeuroYou)

However, here the HR professionals confronted the developers with their view that such expectations were unrealistic, as employees were unwilling to create such a “datafied” mirror of themselves, as it would take too much of their time. Moreover, the experts did not see the value of many of the attributes. For instance, they considered the attribute “vocabulary skills” redundant: Their view was that since only highly educated employees were recruited, the candidates could be assumed to have vocabulary skills, so assessing that attribute would only add irrelevant information, or “noise,” to the dataset. Marvin reflected on this discussion:

We explained to MultiCo that, if you test so many people in this time period, you are generating billions of data points. That was then a discussion point about the model and analytics because MultiCo quite rightly asked, “Okay, three billion data points. Now what? What is good? How does that help us? Because we don’t need more data if it’s more noise.” So that turned very quickly into the learning we did with them.

The HR team’s pushback confronted the developers with the first manifestation of the independence-relevance tension around a data-selection problem: while turning employees characteristics into data allowed the algorithm to learn which attributes were important on its own, developers could end up measuring irrelevant attributes or none at all. Similarly, selecting an outcome variable suggested that the developers had to know what defines a “good” employee, for which there is no universal definition. As developers “had no insight into which key performance indicators (KPIs) were available or meaningful for MultiCo” (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou), they realized the need to include domain experts because of their understanding of the attributes and indicators that are relevant to success as an employee.

## Including Domain Expertise

To overcome the data-selection problem, where the machine lacks insight into what data is useful to select and learn from, the developers engaged with the HR team to learn about their professional standards for judging good employees. For example, to decide which personality traits and skills were important to assess, the developers asked the experts to pro vide them with job descriptions that included trainees’ desirable qualities and statements about MultiCo’s company culture. As a developer explained,

What we actually did was we went to MultiCo and asked about their cultural ideals, as they have a very specific brand culture, and what they expect from their employees and the work at the organization as a whole. They sent us a list of statements and a short explanatory paragraph about each one. We took that and thought, “Well, what kind of attributes can we link those to?” (ML developer, NeuroYou)

These descriptions helped developers to infer which attributes were relevant to training the algorithm. For instance, the trait “leadership” was frequently emphasized, so the developers incorporated this trait into the training dataset. In this way, the developers narrowed the initial set of 150 attributes to 49 attributes to measure with their online games. Similarly, to select performance indicators, the developers held meetings with the HR professionals to find their shared norms regarding what constitutes a good employee. Constructing this outcome variable forced the HR team to reflect concretely on the indicators that determine whether someone will perform well in the organization:

Marvin: One thing that would be good to know is what the KPIs are in terms of what we are actually benchmarking against—how you actually view top performers, so we can take various parameters and measure relevant attributes.

Tim: Yeah, we can do that via our annual performance reviews.

Sarah: We have two things: target achievement and annual reviews.

Tim: I don’t like target achievement; I would do annual reviews.

(Field notes, client meeting)

This exercise made the team aware that many of MultiCo’s performance indicators were inconsistent and subjective, which triggered broader reflection on how they judged employees’ performance. For example, during an internal discussion with colleagues, one HR manager reflected:

What is performance in this functional area? Line managers say themselves that their annual reviews of employees are biased because they’re not connected to the targets they achieve or other variables they measure. So, now the question is: how to measure performance? (Field notes, internal meeting MultiCo)

As no better indicators were found, the HR team decided to give the developers the annual performance reviews, which they thought represented the most holistic assessment of employees’ performance. When the developers saw that the data was rating-based, as managers rated employees based on their judgment of how well they performed, they were disappointed. However, they realized that, without using such subjective reviews, they would be unable to supply the algorithm with any definition of a good employee at all, leading to a much worse outcome.

We will often compromise on less objective KPIs because the worst option would be that the client says, “Okay, never mind. This is too complicated. We are not doing the pilot, just tell us what to do.” That puts us in a very “interesting” position because then it’s just us guessing what “best” looks like, which would be the worst of all options! (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

The developers constructed the training dataset by including HR’s professional standards on useful attributes and performance indicators. This deviation from their initial pursuit of independence was triggered by their confrontation with a data-selection problem, when the developers needed the experts to help them decide what employee data was useful to select for the algorithm. Eventually, this effort led the developers to adapt the ML approach to bring it closer to the domain experts’ world.

## Building a Model

By the summer of 2018, the algorithm was learning on its own how to recognize a good employee from the training data. While their goal was to exclude subjective domain expertise from the algorithm, the developers stumbled on the “frame problem,” where the produced insights of the machine are limited to what developers supply in one form or another. This problem prompted a new cycle of mutual learning between developers and HR professionals, resulting in augmenting the ML model by including HR’s vision of the workforce.

## Excluding Domain Expertise

The ML developers ran a learning algorithm on the training dataset to generate a predictive model. They experimented with several kinds of algorithms, such as random forests and neural networks, but eventually decided on linear techniques because of the importance that the algorithm be explainable and legally accountable in the HR domain. They split the dataset into a training set and a test dataset, training the learning algorithm on the former and validating the accuracy of the patterns it discovered on the latter. They considered this ML activity to be superior to relying on the HR professionals’ selection criteria since it allowed the developers to focus on what actually works instead of being constrained by unvalidated assumptions and hypotheses. As a developer explained,

I tend to stay away from psychology theory. I tend to be concerned just about empirical results, which is the machine learning approach. A lot of progress is being made in ignoring all this theory that’s been built up over decades. By just using these technologies and plugging in the data, a lot of the time these learning algorithms perform better than complicated theories. It is more results-driven; it’s focused on what exactly you’re trying to achieve. (ML developer, NeuroYou)

The algorithm then arrived at a model that captured the learned relationships between attributes and employee performance at MultiCo. When the developers presented the model to the HR team, the experts were surprised that the algorithm had discovered several relationships that were at odds with their usual views. For example, while the HR professionals had considered cognitive intelligence to be critical for their employees to do well in the organization, the algorithm revealed that cognitive skills did not matter and that social traits like energy and assertiveness were highly predictive of performance. To the HR team, this result showed the importance of moving beyond the academic results and IQ that had dominated their selection practice and assessing a broad spectrum of social traits that affect performance:

When you get into MultiCo, high cognition doesn’t differentiate you as a high-performer. Especially, if you take our trainees, you don’t select them for their thinking capabilities, technical skills, or engineering skills. They are no “Googlers.” We take leaders. (Sarah, HR manager, MultiCo)

While HR professionals appreciated the algorithm for generating new insights, they also felt that insights based on the past should not dictate what the company is going to be in the future. In several cases, the patterns the algorithm discovered triggered the experts to reflect on the limitations of insights that were based on historical data if they wanted to develop hiring strategies for the future. For example, the algorithm revealed a negative correlation between an employee who found “good working conditions” important and his or her performance at MultiCo. Apparently, successful employees did not put much value on working conditions like job security and work–life balance. Incorporating such a variable in the model would reject a candidate who cared about the working environment and work–life balance. The HR professionals felt that such insight, even though reflective of the present state of affairs, should not inform their hiring practice, as it misaligned with their shared vision of creating a sustainable workforce in the future. Marvin reflected on the discussion:

They said that, from their HR perspective: “That’s not right. That’s actually not what we want because we actually want people to say it’s about working together and feeling cared for by the company.” On one hand, you could argue that people can compromise easily on working conditions because they are good already, but they were highly concerned about that. They said, “We don’t want to select for that, and we don’t want to take that into consideration because that’s not the kind of workforce we want to build.” (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

Similarly, the HR team voiced its own interpretation regarding the cognitive skills that the algorithm judged to be irrelevant to performing well at MultiCo. The experts argued that, although cognitive skills might not be important for their current employees, this requirement was likely to change in the future:

The whole profile of people in sales is going to change eventually. People won’t have their boots on the ground forever. It’s not efficient; technology is coming, so the cognitive skills needed for what a salesperson will be doing in the future are completely different from the skills of a current salesperson who is negotiating with a small store owner on the ground. So how much effort do we spend on trying to design the optimal profile for today’s salesperson when we’re also thinking about the future? (HR manager, MultiCo)

The HR team’s questioning whether the backward-looking nature of the algorithm produced insights that could inform future action prompted another tension between independence and relevance. This time the tension centered on the frame problem, in which the algorithm is limited to what developers supply in one form or another. The developers realized that, if they wanted to come to a model that could be meaningfully applied, they would have to overcome the algorithm’s nearsightedness.

## Including Domain Expertise

The developers coped with the frame problem by engaging with experts to understand their broader vision of how the workforce should evolve over time. Developers held several intensive three-hour meetings with the HR team to evaluate the variables of the predictive model systematically, leaving room for the HR professionals to reflect on the insights that the algorithm had produced. Marvin reflected on these discussions:

Those were then later very interesting discussions because we could only model what we see as predictive and what would make sense from our understanding of what they were looking for. They knew, of course, their own internal understanding better than we ever could, plus they have the benefit of being able to “future-proof” it. We can only work with the data that we have now, and say, “This is your status quo.” (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

The developers began to consider HR’s insights as valuable additions to the model, so they made several changes to the variables in the model. For example, Sarah asked to add two cognitive variables to the model that the algorithm had deemed irrelevant to incorporate MultiCo’s aspiration of hiring more people with cognitive skills in the future:

The cognitive variable wasn’t predictive, but Sarah still wanted it included at a certain point to say, “We think our organization is this at the moment, but we know the organization needs to evolve and change into that, and the best way to do that is not only to run the change management based on the employees we have, but also to change who comes in and then naturally drives the change.” (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

As a result of the developers’ augmenting the predictive model with the experts’ vision about the future workforce, the model increasingly converged with how experts judged candidates, leading to a newly shaped model that combined the algorithm’s insights with those of the experts. Although this was another departure from the pursuit of independence, the developers realized that, without including experts’ vision, they would be unable to overcome the frame problem and bring novelty and improvement to MultiCo:

It’s not replicating what is there; now, that would be a problem! I think the one thing that it can help us to avoid is the point where we get to the end of, let’s say, a year of working with the algorithm, and the business feels it’s stagnating because we were brought in to revolutionize and change things. If we are just bringing the same people in as before, just a bit more efficiently, we have failed on that, and that wouldn’t be good for the client either. But if we can say we are bringing in people they wanted anyway, and they are reliably different, that’s better for us as well. (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

## Using the Model in Practice

By the fall of 2018, the model was formally launched to judge the fit of more than 10,000 job candidates who applied for MultiCo’s yearly trainee programs and select them based on the algorithmic prediction. Along the way of using the model to exclude the manual screening by HR professionals, the ML developers were confronted with a “mismatch problem,” where the machine fails to match its operations to human reasoning. This problem made developers realize that the algorithmic predictions would not be acted on without including senior managers’ situated hiring practices.

## Excluding Domain Expertise

With the deployment of the ML system, the learning algorithm performed the initial screening stage that the HR professionals had performed, which filtered out 6,000 of the 10,000 candidates deemed inappropriate for human attention (company documents). For the organization, then, the ML system seemed to deliver on its promise of increasing efficiency. The candidates played a series of 15 online games that generated data for the algorithm to use to predict their fit with the organization. Candidates who were predicted to be a good fit with MultiCo sat for a final group interview in which senior managers judged their performance. Senior managers had the algorithmic predictions on candidates during the interviews and made the final hiring decisions.

When the algorithm was up and running, the developers were eager to see how the organization at large took up its insights so the algorithm could be fed with new data and revise its learned patterns over time. The developers were surprised that the managers rejected most of the candidates that the algorithm had selected, particularly candidates that the algorithm predicted to be exceptional fits. The HR professionals also perceived this finding to be highly problematic, as it suggested that the algorithm was unable to provide useful insights into candidates and was wasting the managers’ time. Marvin reflected on the discussion to which this issue gave rise:

The perfect model would be that we just say, “Look, these people scored higher on everything; hire them. I don’t care whether you like them or not, you’re going to hire them.” But we’re not the ones paying their salaries, so we can’t actually say that’s the best way of doing it....I think that decision comes down to the organization. Do they want to trust the numbers and say, “Okay, we might have been assessing people wrong. If someone gets 85% we are just going to hire them because we trust the data.” Or do they want someone who fits better in terms of what they like? (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

This question confronted developers with another manifestation of the independence–relevance tension, which now centered on a mismatch problem: Although the algorithm produced predictions about candidates, these predictions were worthless if they failed to match managers’ reasoning and were not acted on. The developers again reflected on whether their ML activities were appropriate for judging candidates, realizing that, if they wanted the predictions to be acted on, they needed to include not only the experts’ standards and vision but also the experts’ reasoning about candidates in situated practice.

## Including Domain Expertise

To overcome the mismatch problem, developers were interested in learning how senior managers were judging candidates on the ground. Although they initially aimed to stay far away from how hiring was actually performed in practice, the developers realized that, if they wanted the managers to take up the algorithmic predictions, they would have to dig deeper into managers’ reasoning for selecting candidates during the group interview. In this way, they hoped to solve the puzzle of managers discarding what the algorithm saw as ideal candidates. As Marvin explained,

Based on the candidates that we’ve presented you, it’s a tighter spectrum, so you should find them generally more appealing. The thing is—and that’s where it turns a bit into a detective case—that the only information we have from the final stage is the decision. We have no parameters. We don’t know why they said yes or no....Now we have to have this little “black box” model itself that is the final interview. We just need to find a way of cracking that. (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

To do so, the developers requested access to any kind of data that would help them develop categories for why managers hired or rejected candidates. However, the HR team informed them that managers did not produce such data because they traditionally discussed candidates in a private and informal setting right after the final interviews. Subsequently, the developers asked whether managers could write their feedback individually as electronic text, thereby changing a core aspect of the managers’ hiring practice, in which decisions were made through social consensus. The HR team agreed to introduce the change, reasoning that turning managers opinions into data would facilitate feedback to candidates and uncover relevant selection criteria. Sarah debriefed the managers about the new change:

There is a big change I have introduced here, which is to write comments in the [NeuroYou] tool. Previously, we only voted yes or no. This time I also ask you to put comments in the tool for two reasons.

First, those comments are the most valuable feedback to candidates. Thinking about the candidates’ experience, this is the best they can take out of the experience, that our highest leadership has taken the time to give them comments. Another thing that’s important is to challenge ourselves about what we need and want from the candidates. The more you put in the comments, the more help you provide me in knowing what you expect from the candidates. We want to search for candidates that fit your criteria. (Field notes, briefing group interview, MultiCo)

Consequently, a key expert hiring practice that involved a lot of social interaction was transformed so the developers could find the categories that played important roles in managers judgment of candidates. The developers considered that, by including these expert perceptions, they could bring the model closer to the reasoning and interpretation of decision-makers, making the algorithmic insights more relevant to the organization. As Marvin explained,

I would like to think of it as a benefit because it actually means that we can model something that humans can make sense of. If I were just to say that this person has a faster reaction time, better taskswitching time, and is more extroverted, so hire her, then it doesn’t translate into human perception. But if I can model something to say that this person is going to behave in a way that aligns with how you like people to behave around you, that makes it inherently more meaningful. (Marvin, CSO, NeuroYou)

The insight into managers’ reasoning about candidates allowed the developers to demonstrate the value of their ML approach by revealing new evidence of flawed human judgment in hiring. For example, after mining the text data with managers’ comments on candidates, Marvin reported to the HR team that managers seemed to suffer from an “anchoring bias,” that is, a tendency to use an outstanding candidate as a parameter by which to evaluate all others. Sarah appreciated this insight and reflected on other ways in which MultiCo’s hiring practice could be transformed:

For sure, what’s great is awareness because as soon as we see that bias like this influences the results, then we can decide what we do about it. Do we use that information for “bias-breaking”? What do we do in terms of the final interview? Do we want to eliminate it? I am happy that you did this analysis. It’s too early to say what we will do with this knowledge, but it allows us to look differently at the process and go back to the exercise of finding out, if we are looking to find the best talent, how do we do it? (Field notes, client meeting)

In sum, the ways of how candidates were judged and selected by the machine and by domain experts increasingly converged in the organization, as ML developers and HR professionals mutually reflected on and adapted their activities. This mutual learning between the actors resulted in the emergence of a new practice through which hiring was performed that relied on a combination of ML with experts’ practice-based knowledge: a hybrid hiring practice.

## Discussion

This longitudinal study of developing ML for hiring provides a revelatory case of how knowledge is produced in organizations using ML. We found that the grand goal of developing knowledge independent of domain experts does not hold when ML is used to transcend complex knowledge work, such as in the case of candidate selection. Instead, this study highlights how machine knowledge production involves managing a dialectic tension between independence and relevance in which ML developers iterate between excluding domain expertise from the tool and including it. As a result, a new human–ML hybrid practice emerges with which candidates are judged and selected by a learning algorithm that is trained, augmented, and aligned based on the situated knowledge of domain experts.

Building on the findings of the case, we offer a general explanation for how ML developers manage the tension between producing knowledge independentlyand maintaining relevance to the domain. Figure 4 shows that developers do so by engaging in a mutual learning process with domain experts, which over time results in the emergence of a new human–ML hybrid practice in which ML and domain experts perform knowledge work together. Our theoretical model explains that the emergence of a hybrid practice is an outcome of long-term engagement between developers and experts, which we conceptualize as mutual learning, and which is prompted when actors deeply engage with the machine and are confronted with problems around how ML performs in action. These problems include a data selection problem, where the machine does not know what data to select and learn from (e.g., Barocas and Selbst 2016); a frame problem, where the machine is constrained by the goal functions and available data (e.g., Salovaraa et al. 2019); and a mismatch problem, where the machine fails to match its operations to human reasoning (e.g., Burrell 2016).

The model highlights that, through continuous engagement with the technology and repeated confrontation with its requirements and outputs, both groups of actors start to reflect on the appropriateness of the ML system’s and the experts activities for producing knowledge. In the hiring case, both the ML developers and the HR professionals appreciated the ML system’s activities for their ability to produce insights with a degree of objectivity, novelty, and efficiency, but they also realized ML’s lack of relevance to the domain when it neglected valuable practice-based knowledge. The actors also became aware of the subjectivity of many of the expert’s activities, such as managers’ tendency to judge candidates relative to one another, but also realized that their standards, vision, and practices guided them in developing a useful system. These reflections prompt the developers to adapt the ML system’s activities by iterating between excluding domain expertise from the system and including it. For instance, the developers initially excluded HR’s assumptions and hypotheses by training a model on data but eventually shifted to including HR’s vision after reflecting on the backwardlooking nature of the model. Similarly, including and analyzing managers’ perceptions of candidates triggered reflection on the presence of unconscious bias in selection, encouraging new efforts to exclude domain expertise.

Over time, recurring cycles of mutual learning lead to the ML practice and traditional expert practice converging, forming a new human–ML hybrid practice through which knowledge work is performed. The theoretical model developed in this study suggests that, rather than a purposeful trajectory to reach a desired human–ML configuration, mutual learning is a dialectical process (Vaast and Pinsonneault 2021; Van de Ven and Poole 1995) in which an ongoing tension between independence and relevance triggers opposing practices to produce a new hybrid construction. We elaborate below on the contributions of our model.

## Theoretical Implications

Our main finding that actors manage the tension between independence and relevance by engaging in a mutual learning process that evolves into a human–ML hybrid practice offers several contributions to the literature.

First, the study generates new insights for research that has understood system development as a process of mutual learning between developers and users (domain experts) (e.g., Alavi and Leidner 2001; Boland 1978; Gallagher and Mason 2009). This literature field has generally focused on facilitating the engagement of experts in the design process through various participatory techniques, such as prototyping and agile methods, so the developers can understand the experts’ interests and the experts learn how the technology functions (Bygstad et al. 2010; Hirschheim et al. 1995; Rames et al. 2010). While this field has recognized the need for developers and experts to engage in a process of learning from each other, our study departs from it by putting forward the role of the knowledge system itself in triggering this process. For example, the hiring case we highlight shows that involvement with the ML system led the experts to reflect critically on their hidden assumptions and hiring practice, such as their preference for extraverted candidates. Thus, much as developers and experts with different backgrounds and cognitive styles learn from each other, the properties of ML—training data, learning algorithms, predictive models— can trigger reflection on the deeper assumptions that guide how knowledge is produced. Therefore, this offers a new view on mutual learning by moving away from the emphasis on learning through participatory techniques and toward learning through confrontation with a ML alternative.

The theoretical model developed in this study also contributes to research that has taken a practice perspective on knowledge (e.g., Levina and Vaast 2005; Orlikowski 2002; Schulze 2000; Schultze and Boland 2000). This work has emphasized the constructed, negotiated, and situated nature of knowledge, and argued that, as a result, knowledge work cannot be replaced by ML technologies nor captured in computer systems (e.g., Dreyfus 2007; Hadjimichael and Tsoukas 2019; Pettersen 2019). We found that developers are confronted with discrepancies between the envisioned ML approach and the complexity of knowledge work, such as the inability of the system to know what defines a good employee; however, contrary to the assumed incompatibility between ML and practice-based knowledge (e.g., Dreyfus 2007; Hadjimichael and Tsoukas 2019; Pettersen 2019), we found that situated knowledge can converge with knowledge generated by machines through a process of mutual learning. In this process, the technology pushes actors to reflect on the value and limitations of opposing ways of generating knowledge, and to adapt the ML activities to reconcile both approaches. Such mutual learning triggered by ML technology suggests that we should reconsider the traditional view of the difficulty, if not impossibility, of the marriage between ML and situated knowledge.

Moreover, this study adds to practice-based research that has found that power struggles and conflict accompany the arrival of new algorithmic technologies. This literature stream has recognized that algorithmic technologies like data analytics, ML, and robotics are associated with a radical change in what counts as valuable knowledge and how it is produced (e.g., Elish and Boyd 2018; Kitchin 2014; Pachidi et al. 2020). While scholars have highlighted how such technologies trigger struggles and power conflicts between developers and domain experts (e.g., Anthony 2018; Pachidi et al. 2020), our study shows that an alternative trajectory that leads to actors reaching a new practice that combines ML with domain expertise is also possible. The case we documented indicates that such collaboration is likely to happen when developers and domain experts deeply engage in the course of ML development, triggering reflection on their opposing ways of producing knowledge and adaptation to realize the potential of both. Thus, our model suggests that literatures’ emphasis on resistance and power struggles should be updated to include a more collaborative view in which actors mutually shape a new practice for performing knowledge work.

This study also adds to research on human–ML hybrids that has studied various forms of interdependence between humans and machines (Balasubramanian et al. 2021; Lindebaum et al. 2020; Rai et al. 2019; Raisch and Krakowski 2020; Seidel et al. 2019). These scholars emphasized that humans and machines excel in different cognitive capabilities, such as humans in rich and diverse learning, and machines in formal rationality (Balasubramanian et al. 2021; Lindebaum et al. 2020), and have to be brought together to leverage their distinct benefits. In line with the work of Seidel et al. (2019), our study similarly recognizes the importance of mutual learning to reconcile the different types of reasoning and cognitive rules that humans and machines apply. Our practice perspective shows that in addition to aligning the mental models of humans and machines, it is valuable to recognize how these models may not be apparent a priori to either experts or the algorithm, but emerge in practice when actors engage in development together. For example, in the hiring case, the HR professionals were unable to articulate their own model of a good employee until they were triggered by the algorithm to reflect on this question. Even more so, our practice perspective highlights that knowledge on what defines a good employee did not reside in the mental model of any individual HR professional, but was collectively and implicitly shared in the organization and needed to be “discovered” by both developers and HR professionals in the process of building a new algorithmic hiring practice.

Finally, we add to research on the augmentation–automation debate in ML. In line with Raisch and Krakowski (2020), we highlight how ML systems call for managing a tension between augmentation and automation, in which insights are produced with expert involvement (augmentation) or without it (automation). This study analyses how these approaches emerge from everyday design, implementation, and use activities (Bailey and Barley 2020; Leonardi 2009; Orlikowski 1992) and suggests that ML for automation or augmentation purposes is not a conscious managerial choice but an emergent process that evolves from iterating between excluding domain expertise and including it. In the hiring case we documented, automation was the initial goal, as ML was understood as a solution to the inefficiency and biases that come with experts’ work. However, as the actors engaged with the technology and learned about their opposing ways of producing knowledge, the idea of using domain expertise to augment the ML system became increasingly common. Thus, this study shows how automation can slide into increased augmentation through a process of mutual learning between developers and domain experts.

## Practical Implications

Understanding machine knowledge production as a process of mutual learning offers practical implications for managers, developers, and domain experts who are engaged in managing and developing ML in organizations. Our study shows that developers and domain experts should prepare to enter an interdependency relationship with each other in which developers rely on experts to define, evaluate, and complement machine inputs and outputs, and developers help experts to discover previously unknown insights from data. This work requires both groups to learn new skills and engage in training to participate fully in this new relationship. For instance, experts need statistical education, knowledge about data legislation (e.g., General Data Protection Regulation), and understanding of the moral concerns around the use of ML. In turn, developers must look beyond technical conditions and learn about the practical domain’s local standards, values, and routines. In the hiring case we documented, this effort involved the developers’ learning about the local definition of what a good employee entails because there is no universal definition.

This study also highlights that developers benefit in many ways from the interactive and hybrid approaches that allow for rapid, focused, and incremental updates to the ML system (e.g., Amershi et al. 2014; Holzinger 2016; Rai et al. 2019). While prior work has suggested that a combination of human and ML competencies is the optimal solution for achieving high levels of performance and knowledge work (Davenport and Kirby 2016; Holzinger 2016; Rai et al. 2019), our ethnography cautions about the laborious nature of the engagement between developers and experts in terms of time investment and commitment to reflect on and adapt corresponding practices. Therefore, managers and leaders of ML projects should start distilling principles and guidelines for designing domain experts’ involvement in the ML process so developers and experts can prepare for the deep engagement and reflection that ML systems are likely to trigger when such systems are developed for real-world domains.

Finally, our findings indicate that a human–ML hybrid practice that combines ML with practice-based knowledge is difficult to reach—or at least develops more slowly—when developers are disconnected from the domains in which their tools operate. In particular, we expect that theoretical ML practitioners (e.g., data scientists at universities and research centers) and ML practitioners who have limited experience with the knowledge work in the domain (e.g., outsourced vendors, remote data science departments) to face significant discrepancies between the ML paradigm they initially advocate and the organizational reality. Our theoretical model is helpful for these practitioners to plan ahead for the obstacles they can encounter and to prepare for a process of mutual learning to ensure that useful knowledge is produced for the organization.

## Future Research Directions

Several boundary conditions are worth noting and open opportunities for further research. First, the case we documented was peopled by ML developers and domain experts who believed in technology’s potential for benefitting knowledge work. Such a shared motivation can influence domain experts’ engagement in and commitment to the development process, as experts who resist the technology are less likely to make the required time investments. Second, the type of ML we considered, supervised learning, can influence the extent to which domain involvement is required and when this is likely to surface. However, as unsupervised systems still require human input at various stages, such as defining a priori “meaningful” clusters and manually interpreting them (Bechmann and Bowker 2019), many of the lessons drawn from this study may also be revelatory for this type of ML. Finally, our study focused on HR professionals, a group that has historically suffered from a low professional status and so has framed AI as an opportunity to reach professional respectability (e.g., Ulrich and Dulebohn 2015). However, highly professionalized groups, such as physicians and academics, can interpret AI as a threat to their expertise, provoking negative professional responses to the technology (Anthony 2018). Given these three boundary conditions, we call for future studies to analyze various types of ML for a variety of professional groups and with diverse levels of commitment between developers and experts.

In conclusion, we offered one of the first ethnographic accounts of the development of a ML system for knowledge production. By reporting on the process of machine knowledge production with qualitative detail, we provided a useful foundation for understanding how the independence-relevance tension is managed in ML. We found that developers can produce knowledge with ML with some degree of independence and relevance by engaging in a mutual learning process with domain experts, eventually resulting in the emergence of a new human–ML hybrid practice for performing knowledge work. ML applications are increasingly entering the world of organizations and producing knowledge for many consequential decisions, such as who is hired for a job, who is considered creditworthy, and who is convicted of a crime. Despite visions of ML’s discovering impactful insights on its own, the process of machine knowledge production, as explained in this paper, can help to account more realistically for the intense and transformative marriage with the domain achieving this goal takes.
