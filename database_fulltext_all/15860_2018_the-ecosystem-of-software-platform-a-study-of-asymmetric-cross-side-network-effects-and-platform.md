# THE ECOSYSTEM OF SOFTWARE PLATFORM: A STUDY OF ASYMMETRIC CROSS-SIDE NETWORK EFFECTS AND PLATFORM GOVERNANCE<sup>1</sup>

Peijian Song School of Business, Nanjing University, Nanjing CHINA {songpeijian@nju.edu.cn}

Ling Xue J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 U.S.A. {lxue5@gsu.edu}

Arun Rai J. Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, Atlanta, GA 30303 U.S.A. {arun.rai@eci.gsu.edu}

Cheng Zhang School of Management, Fudan University, Shanghai CHINA {zhangche@fudan.edu.cn}

In the context of software platforms, we examine how cross-side network effects (CNEs) on different platform sides (app-side and user-side) are temporally asymmetric, and how these CNEs are influenced by the platform’s governance policies. Informed by a perspective of value creation and capture, we theorize how the app-side and the user-side react to each other with distinct value creation/capture processes, and how these processes are influenced by the platform’s governance policies on app review and platform updates. We use a time-series analysis to empirically investigate the platform ecosystem of a leading web browser. Our findings suggest that while the growth in platform usage results in long-term growth in both the number and variety of apps, the growth in the number of apps and the variety of apps only leads to short-term growth in platform usage. We also find that long app review time weakens the long-term CNE of the user-side on the app-side, but not the short-term CNE of the app-side on the user-side. Moreover, we find that frequent platform updates weaken the CNEs of both the user-side and the app-side on each other. These findings generate important implications regarding how a software platform may better govern its ecosystem with different participants.

Keywords: Software platform, two-sided markets, network effects, platform governance, value creation and capture

## Introduction

Extant literature has categorized four types of platform businesses: exchanges, advertising-supported media, transaction devices, and software platforms (Evans 2011; Sriram et al. 2015). Software platforms, such as operating systems and web browsers, are extensible codebases of software systems that provide core functionalities for the applications that run on them (Baldwin and Woodard 2009). They are emerging as dominant models for software-based services and innovation (Evans et al. 2006; Tiwana et al. 2010). Based on their technological architectures, software platforms operate as twosided markets to attract both end users and third-party developers that supply complementary apps (Boudreau 2012;

Ceccagnoli et al. 2012). Similar to other platforms, software platforms are characterized by cross-side network effects (CNEs).<sup>2</sup>

In the context of software platforms, CNE refers to the phenomenon that the size and growth of one side, either the user-side or app-side, reinforce the size and growth of the other side (Anderson et al. 2014; Boudreau 2012). CNEs differ from the traditional notion of network effects that focus primarily on the mutual influences among network participants on a single side of a market and have strategic implications for the governance of a platform ecosystem with both users and developers. First, CNEs influence a platform’s strategies to build the installed base on either side. The installed base is typically assessed using measures such as the numbers of apps and users, the variety of apps, or the usage behavior of users. For instance, a platform may subsidize one side (e.g., free usage for users) and make the ecosystem appealing to the other side (e.g., attract more third-party app developers) (Hagiu 2006; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Weyl 2010). Second, CNEs can help incumbents by allowing them to leverage their market advantages on one side by building dominant positions on the other side and creating substantial entry barriers. A software platform that starts with minor leads on both sides is likely to win the entire market over time, even if it is of inferior quality (Zhu and Iansiti 2012). Therefore, understanding how the two sides mutually influence each other through CNEs is critical to the software platform establishing competitive advantages by leveraging CNEs.

In the extant empirical literature on CNEs, the focus is primarily on investigating their presence and magnitude (Sriram et al. 2015). The asymmetry among CNEs on different sides of the platform has received less empirical attention. Some recent studies in other non-software platform contexts have shown that CNEs on different sides can be asymmetric in both signs and magnitude. For example, Wilbur (2008) shows that, in television networks, increases in viewers make networks more attractive to advertisers, but increases in advertisements make networks less attractive to viewers.<sup>3</sup> In a setting of customer-to-customer (C2C) exchange platform, Chu and Manchanda (2016) show that the growth of the seller-side has a larger CNE on the growth of the buyer-side than vice versa. We examine the asymmetry among CNEs but differ from previous studies in two respects. First, we focus on the setting of software platforms, in which platform participants (i.e., users and app developers) may have distinct objectives, behaviors, and capabilities from those in other platform contexts. Second, and more importantly, we consider CNE asymmetry in the temporal dimension. Specifically, we address the following research question: How do the influences of the user-side on the app-side (which we refer to as user-to-app CNEs) and the influences of the app-side on the user-side (which we refer to as app-touser CNEs) in software platforms differ in terms of their longterm and short-term effects? The examination of temporal asymmetry is mainly motivated by the consideration that CNEs evolve over time (see Clements and Ohashi 2005). From a complex ecosystem perspective (Tanriverdi et al. 2010), uncovering the temporal dynamics of mutual adaptation between two sides should help the platform owner adjust the platform’s strategies on both sides and better manage feedback loops.

In addition to CNEs, strategic actions and decisions about platforms are examined in the extant empirical literature. Prior studies, however, have focused primarily on issues of pricing (e.g., Kaiser and Wright 2006; Liu 2010) and platform competition (e.g., Dubé et al. 2010; Zhu and Iansiti 2012). Less effort has been devoted to revealing how the governance actions of a platform affect CNEs. Since platform governance influences the ecosystem in which the two sides interact with each other, its impact on CNEs is nontrivial and therefore deserves research attention. We bridge this gap in the literature by addressing a second research question: How does software platform governance affect the mutual influences between the app-side and the user-side? In particular, we differentiate from the existing literature on platforms by focusing on two non-pricing governance policies of a software platform: the duration of app reviews and the frequency of platform updates.

Drawing on the theoretical lens of value creation and value capture (Bowman and Ambrosini 2000; Lepak et al. 2007), we consider how user-to-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs differ in value creation/capture processes and how these value processes are influenced by software platform governance. We carry out a time-series analysis using a unique dataset about a leading web browser: Mozilla Firefox. We construct a longitudinal weekly-level dataset spanning a period from 2008 to 2013. Using a vector auto-regressive (VAR) analysis, we examine the dynamics of mutual influences between browser usage and third-party app releases, and how these mutual influences are affected by Firefox’s policies on app review and platform updates. Our findings reveal temporal asymmetry between user-to-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs: specifically, growth in the quantity and variety of apps and variety of apps drives end-user platform usage in the shortterm rather than in the long-term. In contrast, growth in enduser platform usage drives growth in the quantity and variety of apps in the long-term rather than in the short-term. These results advance our understanding of the temporal dynamics underlying the interdependence between the two sides of a software platform.

We also find that the app review time influences the app-side and the user-side differently. In particular, long app review time significantly weakens the long-term user-to-app CNEs. However, we do not find a significant impact from long app review time on the short-term app-to-user CNEs. In addition, frequent platform updates significantly weaken the long-term user-to-app CNEs. However, regarding the short-term app-touser CNEs, frequency platform updates significantly weaken the effect of app quantity, but not that of app variety, on end users’ platform usage. These findings generate important implications as to how the management of a software platform can be improved.

## Theory and Hypothesis Development

## CNEs in Software Platform and Value in Network Effect

In the platform literature, CNEs have long been conceptualized as the way in which the attractiveness of the platform on one side is dependent on the size of the other side (Rysman 2009; Weyl 2010). In the context of software platforms, positive CNEs manifest when growth in the size of the user base helps the platform motivate more third-party app development, and when the growth in size of the app base helps the platform attract more users. However, recent research on network effects suggest that network effects are not all about size, but are also determined by the nature of network value and the value creation/capture processes of network participants (Afuah 2013). Therefore, to better conceptualize CNEs based on value, we adopt the theoretical lens of value creation/capture from the strategy literature (Bowman and Ambrosini 2000; Lepak et al. 2007), which has been employed in recent studies on network effects (Afuah 2013), and contextualize this perspective to software platforms.

The perspective of value creation/capture distinguishes between two types of value to various stakeholders (e.g., individuals, organizations, and society): use value and exchange value (Bowman and Ambrosini 2000; Lepak et al. 2007). Use value refers to the quality of a product or service as perceived by users in relation to their needs. Exchange value refers to the tangible or intangible monetary value that can be realized by the seller when the exchange of a product or service takes place, or the payment by the user to the seller for the use value of the product or service. When the exchange does not entail monetary payment (e.g., in the case of free software), exchange value may be reflected in intangible benefits (such as reputation gains) to the seller. The distinction between use value and exchange value can be illustrated using the example of a typical security protection app in Firefox, Permit Cookies. As one of the earliest apps in Firefox, the app allows end users to explicitly permit (or deny) the use of cookies by the sites they visit. When Permit Cookies was available in Firefox, Firefox users could directly use the app to derive value from its functionalities, and this value capture process did not require much additional effort by users (except an easy procedure for installation). Likewise, when other competing security protection apps were made available in Firefox, directly using these apps allowed users to derive value from their additional security protection functionalities.

From the standpoint of the developers of Permit Cookies, the emergence of a large number of Firefox users with security protection needs provided them with a potential market. They could not derive value directly by simply accessing or “using” these Firefox users through the platform. Instead, they needed to undertake various time/effort-consuming processes to identify app functionalities that serve user needs and eventually realize them in Permit Cookies. For example, detecting a potential vulnerability in cookie use and implementing functionalities to disable cookies from designated sites were among the processes that developers of Permit Cookies undertook for their product to be eventually favored by Firefox users. Only when Firefox users adopted and liked the app could developers derive value (e.g., in reputation) as an exchange for the app they provided. Likewise, for developers of competing security protection apps, they could only derive value (tangible payments or intangible reputation) from Firefox users after the developers invested effort to identify and eventually realize other security protection functionalities in their apps provided to users for exchange.

Based on the theoretical distinction between use value and exchange value, we distinguish between the CNEs that the user-side has on the app-side (i.e., user-to-app CNEs) and the CNEs that the app-side has on the user-side (i.e., app-to-user CNEs). In user-to-app CNEs, app developers find the software platform a more valuable place to participate when there are more platform users into which they can tap. Therefore, user-to-app CNEs are essentially based on exchange value, as app developers do not derive value from directly “using” platform users but derive value from serving users in exchange for their tangible or intangible payments. In contrast, in app-to-user CNEs, users find the platform a more valuable option to use when there are more complementary apps for them to use on the platform. Therefore, app-to-user CNEs are essentially based on the use value, as users can derive value (e.g., perceive aesthetic or functional quality) from directly using these apps.

The distinct value bases of user-to-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs also lead to distinct processes of realizing and capturing value by the two sides. For the app-side, since it cannot directly “use” the platform’s users, it needs a process of converting user interest in the platform to interest in apps. Such a process should entail identifying user demand (for certain apps), developing apps, and making apps satisfactory to users so that exchange value can be generated. For the user-side, as apps available on the platform are ready to be used, end users need minimum additional processes other than using these apps to derive use value. Such a distinction in value creation/capture processes on the two sides can eventually lead to temporal asymmetric features of user-to-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs.

## Temporal Asymmetry in CNEs

Our characterization of the temporal features of CNEs is based on the concepts of short-term effect and long-term effect on market participants as discussed in the marketing literature (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1995; Pauwels and Weiss 2008). Conceptually, a short-term effect refers to the case in which market participants exhibit immediate reactions to certain marketing events or actions. A long-term effect, in contrast, refers to the case in which market participants’ reactions to marketing events or actions are persistent over time. Extending these concepts to our context of software platforms, we consider a short-term effect as the immediate response in growth of one side (either the app-side or the user-side) to the growth of the other side. Likewise, we consider a long-term effect as the persistent response in growth of one side to the growth of the other side. Conceptually, shortterm effect and long-term effect are not mutually exclusive. For example, if the growth-fostering effect of one side on the other side takes effect immediately and is also persistent over time, such a CNE is characterized by both a short-term effect and a long-term effect.

Considering the process of realizing exchange value by the app-side, we expect that the response of the app-side to the user-side takes effect in the long-term rather than in the shortterm. This assertion is supported by the theoretical perspective of commitment in the strategy literature (Ghemawat 1991). On the one hand, using app development to realize exchange value is a process of resource commitment, which is not a simple process that can be done instantaneously. Developers need to figure out platform users’ underlying demand for specific types of apps, organize required resources based on their own expertise and constraints, engage in actual development, and make their products accessible to users. Many processes make it difficult for developers to respond immediately to the growth of a platform’s user base by providing ready-to-use apps. For example, it may not always be a straightforward process to identify the user demand for specific apps (Eisenmann et al. 2011). Developers may be heterogeneous in terms of their resource constraints and development capabilities, making it less likely for the appside as a whole to quickly respond with a large number of apps (Boudreau 2012). Upon completion, developers also have to go through the app review processes imposed by the platform, which further delays the release of apps (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013). As a result, the responses of the app-side to the user-side are less likely to take effect immediately, and therefore user-to-app CNEs are less likely to manifest as a short-term effect.

On the other hand, the resource commitment perspective suggests a tendency of persistent app development as a response to the user-side growth. When developers make development decisions, they are less likely to give up their committed development plans for several reasons. Irreversible investments in specialized resources (e.g., development teams) can lock developers into development processes (Harrigan and Porter 1983). Difficulty in redeploying allocated resources (e.g., specialized tools) can lock out developers from alternative opportunities. Lags in adjusting development strategies often constrain developers from deviating from current development plans. Organizational inertia built over time can also lead to developers to having built-in biases that maintain the status quo of development plans (Harter and Slaughter 2003). As a consequence, although app development and app releases are less likely to be completed instantly, they will likely occur eventually, but with a time lag. The growth in the app base will therefore be realized with a delay in time and in the long-term. ## H1a: User-to-app CNEs in a software platform are characterized by a significant long-term effect, but with no significant short-term effect.

In contrast, the realization of use value by the user-side is a relatively simple process and requires many fewer resource commitments by end users. When apps are available on the platform, users just need to respond by using the platform to try the apps that interest them. Users do not need any extra value-creation process to capture the use value and therefore their responses do not have to be delayed. Some other factors may further expedite user-side responses. For example, app centers and search functions offered by software platforms get newly released apps quickly exposed to users (Haigu and Spulber 2013). Word-of mouth generated on the user-side also helps draw new users to the platform to quickly try new apps (Zhang et al. 2012). Therefore, the response of the userside to app-side growth is likely to take effect immediately. Such a response should result in a quick increase in platform usage, based on which users can use apps. Consequently, app-to-user CNEs are likely to manifest a significant shortterm effect.

App-to-user CNEs, however, are less likely to be characterized by a significant long-term effect. The rationality of this assertion can be theoretically justified by the wear-out effect, which the marketing literature conceptualizes as the quick decay in the effectiveness of marketing-related actions over time (Bass et al. 2007). Prior research has found that the effectiveness of various marketing actions, such as advertising and product-line extension, can die out quickly (e.g., after one or two weeks) (Leone 1995; Pauwels 2004). The growth of the app-side is a product-line extension process on the platform. In this case, the wear-out effect can be explained by various factors. First, changes in user knowledge may reduce user interest in new apps (Calantone and Sawyer 1978), and the easy conditions of trial apps and available online information can expedite this process. Second, multihoming allows competing platforms to offer similar apps (Evans 2011), which can generate a so-called “copy wear-out effect” (Naik et al. 1998). Third, rapid app development and expansion on a platform may also generate cognitive challenges to users. The user tendency of forgetting (Bass et al. 2007) can contribute to the decreasing attractiveness of new apps over time. Proliferation of available options may also pose challenges for users to identify apps that truly fit their needs and therefore affect the long-term commitment of users (Grime et al. 2002). Consequently, even if the increase in new apps draws users to the platform, short-term user interests are not enough to guarantee users’ long-term commitment to the platform. Fourth, many apps have short lifespans and may only be appealing to users in the short-term. For example, from tracking over 830,000 iOS and Android apps, Flurry Insights found that only 4 of 19 categories of iOS and Android apps (e.g., weather, health and fitness, news and magazines) have a median user-retention rate above 25% after 30 days (Klotzback 2016). ## Software Platform Governance

Platform governance in software platforms refers to all policies and mechanisms through which a software platform operator exerts influence over participants on both sides and coordinates operations in the ecosystem (Tiwana et al. 2010). In general, platform governance includes three broad categories of policies and mechanisms. First, although pricing is often linked with profitability, it is also an inherent governance mechanism. Decisions regarding which side to subsidize and the use of pricing as an incentive mechanism all influence the interaction between the two sides (Rochet and Tirole 2006; Tiwana 2014). Second, mechanisms used to coordinate and control platform participants are the central elements of platform governance. Third, some policies of a platform’s self-development play an important role in governance (Cusumano and Gawer 2002). The design and improvements in a platform’s own architecture and features shape the general environment in which cross-side interactions take place. Therefore, certain self-development of the platform (e.g., APIs) cannot be completely separate from platform governance (Tiwana 2014).

We differ from prior empirical studies on platform governance (which have primarily considered pricing and platform competition issues) by focusing on two non-pricing mechanisms in software platform governance and considering how they influence CNEs. The first mechanism is app review, which reflects the way in which the platform addresses governance through regulation and control of third-party developers’ development behavior (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013; Maurer and Tiwana 2012). We specifically examine the duration of app review processes. Platforms often impose standards on the content and technical specifications of third party apps. The review processes ensure that apps meet performance standards, reliability, and compatibility. For example, Apple’s App Store imposes a wide range of rules that cover a range of requirements from user interface design, functionality, and content to the use of specific technologies. App review by the App Store can take up to two weeks. Submitted apps can be rejected for various reasons, such as lack of information, functional errors, and poor interface design (Starr 2014). Developers who fail app review are required to the fix identified problems before resubmitting their apps. Reviews on the revised versions further delay final app releases.

The second mechanism we consider is updates to the platform itself, and we specifically examine the frequency of platform updates. Since the platform serves as the basis for cross-side interactions, platform updates are an important way for the platform managers to address governance by improving the platform’s design and architecture (Tiwana 2014). Platform updates often need both the app-side and the user-side to make adaptive actions, such as conforming to changing APIs and addressing compatibility issues. In this regard, platform updates should also influence how the two sides respond to each other through CNEs.

## Software Platform Governance and CNEs

In user-to-app CNEs, the app-side responds to user-side growth mainly by creating and capturing the exchange value of apps. Given the growth of the user-side, however, long app review time can generate three effects that weaken the appside response in growth. First, long app review time directly delays the release of new apps (Ghazawneh and Henfridsson 2013), making app developers less able to respond in a timely fashion to user demands for certain types of apps. For example, in a recent survey, iOS app developers revealed that they often have to wait more than a week for Apple to approve the fixing of bugs in their original app versions. Developers expressed concern that these time-consuming review processes undermine their ability to respond to the expanding market and satisfy the needs of a growing user base (Graham 2016).

Second, long review times can escalate the burden on developers and result in more uncertainties in developers’ resource commitments. Long review times are often associated with more thorough reviews, which require developers to carry out more demanding revisions of their original app versions. Developers need to allocate more resources and adjust their other development plans to complete revisions. For example, iOS developers reported that they often plan for up to a month of slack time in their app release plans due to the long and demanding review processes (Graham 2016). Consequently, developers who have constrained resources or are uncertain about whether they are able to make satisfactory revisions are also more likely to forgo their app development. The app-side can therefore be constrained from developing apps in response to user-side growth because of long app review times.

Third, long app review processes may negatively affect developers’ resource commitment and incentives for future development (Hilkert et al. 2010). With long app review times, the platform generates high opportunity costs for developers in their future development. When developers anticipate these opportunity costs that are generally out of their control, they will be more reluctant to commit future resources to app development for the platform. Given competing development alternatives, the length of a focal platform’s review time can drive developers to switch to alternatives. As a result, the app-side will be less able to respond to user-side growth with a steady increase in app development over time. Considering these effects, we expect that long app review times weaken user-to-app CNEs.

## H2a: User-to-app CNEs in a software platform are weakened by long app review time.

In app-to-user CNEs, the user-side responds to app-side growth mainly by capturing the use value of apps. Long app review times can negatively affect user interest in released apps. First, by delaying the release of apps, time-consuming review processes can make apps obsolete and reduce their use value. Given the volatility of users’ online tastes (Sun 2012), delayed apps can become less attractive to users. For example, a developer team previously submitted an app for Apple TV app about a week before the release of Apple TV. However, a two-week app review time eventually resulted in an app being barely noticed by users because the excitement for Apple TV apps had already died down by the time some apps were released (Graham 2016). Second, long app review times can slow down developers’ processes for releasing patches for bugs reported by users (Dave 2015). As a result, users get hurt due to the reduced reliability of apps. ## H2b: App-to-user CNEs in a software platform are weakened by long app review time.

In juxtaposition to H2b, we take into consideration some mechanisms in app review that make long app review times strengthen app-to-user CNE. Long app review times are likely to be associated with more thorough and diligent reviews, which should help improve the quality of apps and their use value (Fisher and Oberholzer-Gee 2013). Thorough app reviews can help correct more errors or potential weaknesses in submitted apps, and also ensure that apps better conform to platform standards and generate complementary value. For example, when a formal app review process was absent, users on Google Play were more likely to encounter apps that did not live up to their expectations and violated Play Store policies. This motivated Google to formalize its processes for app review and approval (Arce 2015). App quality improvement driven by thorough app reviews can also make apps more valuable to users. With more valuable apps, the platform may also become more attractive to users and app-to-user CNEs can therefore be reinforced. We therefore develop a competing hypothesis to H2b:

## H3a: User-to-app CNEs in software platform are strengthened by frequent platform updates.

In juxtaposition to H3a, we take into consideration potential tradeoffs in the frequent releases of platform updates. Despite the benefits of platform updates, the frequent releases of updates may also generate counterproductive effects. Updates on the platform inevitably change the basis on which users and developers interact with each other, and the frequent introduction of these changes may constrain user-to-app CNEs for several reasons. First, developers have to adjust their app development to the new platform functionalities (Boudreau 2010). If the updates are released over-frequently, some developers that are less capable may lack a sufficient time buffer to adapt (Boudreau 2012). Recent research also suggests that generational transitions initiated by platforms often make it difficult for complementors to sustain their performance (Kapoor and Agarwal 2016). Consequently, the app-side may not be able to respond effectively to the user-side growth in a frequently changing platform environment, weakening the user-to-app CNE.

Second, frequent platform updates can introduce uncertainties for developers about constantly changing platform configurations (Mullins and Sutherland 1998) and the extra resources they should commit to adapt. Developers need a time buffer to resolve these uncertainties. The frequent releases of updates can escalate developers’ concern about uncertainties about the platform’s configurations and disincentivize their commitment to the platform. Such disincentives can also undermine the app-side response to the user-side growth and weaken user-to-app CNEs. Third, platform updates can affect the accessibility of older apps and force developers to commit resources to app updates (Kingsley-Hughes 2012). Frequent platform updates can therefore create more technical debt (Ramasubbu and Kemerer 2015), that is, a buildup of software maintenance obligations over time. Such technical debt for developers may constrain their resource commitment for new app development (Bavota et al. 2015), and weaken the appside expansion to serve the growing demand from the userside.

Moreover, releasing updates frequently can put the focal platform at competitive disadvantage, especially when developers have multihoming options (Evans et al. 2006; Hyrynsalmi et al. 2016). The frequent changes of the focal platform can make it appear to be less stable than competing platform environments. Developers who prefer more manageable adaptation burdens and limited uncertainties about the platform configuration may find the platform’s competitors more attractive. The availability of multihoming options further makes it more difficult for the focal platform to use its user base to keep and expand its developer base (Zhu and Iansiti 2012). Therefore, frequent platform updates are likely to negatively affect user-to-app CNE in a competitive environment. ## H3b: User-to-app CNEs in a software platform are weakened by frequent platform updates.

Frequent platform updates can also generate countervailing effects on how the user-side reacts to the app-side growth (i.e., app-to-user CNEs). As Table 1 indicates, platform updates may benefit users by providing them additional platform features and an enhanced environment to use apps. In this regard, platform updates may reinforce app-to-user CNEs over time. However, the frequent releases of platform updates can also be detrimental to the user-side. First, frequent updates of the platform may create usage interruptions for individual users. For instance, Firefox updates often require restarting the browser, introduce backward incompatibility with old add-on apps (Mandel 2012), and even change the user interface (Kingsley-Hughes 2012). When these issues occur too frequently, they can disrupt end users’ habitual usage. Users are also forced to commit additional cognitive resources to adapting to the changes. Users with lower capabilities to overcome the usage interruption are likely to be discouraged from continuing to use the apps on the platform, leading to weakened app-to-user CNEs. This logic is in line with insights from research on software updates in the working environment (such as the enterprise software environment). For example, update reminders, alerts, notifications, and installations can cause interruptions in workflow (Hodgetts and Jones 2007), and these system-initiated interruptions are a major impediment to the productivity of IT users in the workplace (Ortiz de Guinea and Webster 2013). Also, system updates that do not introduce new technical features are generally not favored by users and may even affect their continued system usage (Fleischmann et al. 2016).

Second, the usage interruptions can also lead to reduced externality among users themselves. Updates inevitably make some users lag in adaption and gradually lose momentum of using the platform (Venkatesh et al. 2008). This causes temporary shrinking of the user base for word-of-mouth (WOM) of apps. When platform updates occur too frequently, this temporary shrinking can accumulate and eventually hurt how apps attract users through WOM. As a result, app-to-user CNEs can become weaker. Considering the tradeoffs, we expect that frequent platform updates generate an overall weakening effect on app-to-user CNEs. The main reason is that we expect app-to-user CNEs to take place primarily in the short-term (as in H1b). The benefits of platform updates to users may not manifest immediately in the short-term as they also require user adaption processes. In contrast, usage disruption and the shrinkage of user WOM base are likely to be caused immediately by usage barriers. Thus, we expect that the downsides of the frequent releases of platform updates dominate the benefits of updates in the short-term. We therefore hypothesize

H3c: App-to-user CNEs in software platform are weakened by frequent platform updates.

## Methodology

## Research Context

Our empirical setting is a major web browser: Mozilla’s Firefox. Since its debut in late 2004, Firefox remained the second-most used web browser until late 2011 (when Google Chrome surpassed it). Currently, Firefox is the third most widely used web browser, with approximately 16% in market share. As an open-source system, Firefox relies more on a platform ecosystem to sustain its operations than do other browsers. It extends its product boundaries by encouraging a large number of third-party developers to supply complementary apps. These apps provide add-on functionalities, such as videoconferencing and privacy protection, within Firefox’s core framework.

To ensure app quality and user experience, Firefox mandates most hosted apps to be reviewed by a team of editors. Developers are required to upload their apps to the developer hub and provide related information, such as descriptions and preview images. Uploaded apps are then scanned by editors using a variety of tools, which can warn editors about potential flaws in the source code. Developers may be asked (usually through emails) to provide additional information or improve certain aspects of the apps before they are further evaluated. The editors’ original review notes may be used in the evaluation of resubmitted apps that have been previously rejected. Firefox also updates its own browser platform to add features and improve quality. These updates may also require third party apps to be updated for compatibility. For end users, Firefox can automatically check for available updates and inform users to manually upgrade their installed browsers to the latest version. The upgrading, however, may require time-consuming processes of downloading updates and rebooting computers.

The platform ecosystem of Firefox is an appropriate setting for our research for several reasons. First, the free-of-charge feature of Firefox makes it less likely that our examination of CNEs would be confounded by any pricing effect. Second, the editors of the Firefox platform offer weekly app review data through the app forum. The longitudinal data collected from the app forum can be used to assess the frequency and duration of app reviews and to quantify their effects on CNEs. Third, Firefox’s platform update policies are largely exogenous to CNEs, as Firefox’s update decisions are generally not based on interactions between the app-side and the user-side. This feature should minimize endogeneity concerns about platform update decisions and enable us to better assess the impact of platform updates on CNEs.

## Data and Measurement

We tested our hypotheses using a longitudinal week-level dataset about the Firefox ecosystem from July 2009 to October 2013 (223 weeks). We use data aggregated at the weekly level mainly because daily data on CNEs and platform governance does not entail sufficient variations, and monthly data does not provide the granularity to reveal ongoing patterns of CNEs and platform governance. Our data also indicates that the status of third-party apps often changes on a weekly basis.

We collected information about end users and third-party apps from the official website for Firefox add-on apps (https:// addons.mozilla.org/en-us/firefox). The site records all thirdparty add-on apps that have run on Firefox since it started in 2004. For each app, the site provides detailed information, including the initial launch time, update history (e.g., when and how many times), category, and the developer’s name. As complementary components of the Firefox browser platform, these apps are free to end users. As of October 2013, there were more than 10,000 apps available on this platform, spanning 14 categories.

In examining CNEs, we focus on two key aspects of the appside: the quantity of apps and the variety of apps. The examination of app quantity reflects the direct CNEs through which the increased user base drives the growth of the overall app base. The examination of app diversity better reflects the indirect CNEs through which the increased user base drives the development of complementary apps. To assess app quantity, we use a measure app number (AN), which represents the total number of apps available on Firefox on a weekly basis. We log-transformed AN values to address the skew in distributions. To assess app variety, we use a measure app diversity (AD), which is operationalized using a Herfindahl-like index measure. Specifically, we define $H I = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { N } ( x _ { i } / x ) ^ { 2 }$ , where $x _ { i }$ is the weekly number of apps

in the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ category and x is the weekly total number of apps. Therefore, x<sub>i</sub>/x is the weekly share of the $i ^ { \mathrm { { t h } } }$ category of apps. A lower level of HI suggests a higher degree of weekly app diversity. To make the resulting interpretation more intuitive, we let AD = 1 – HI (as 1 is the maximum HI value). Therefore, a higher value of AD means a higher level of weekly app diversity.

Regarding the user-side, we use a measure user usage (UU) defined as the weekly total number of times Firefox is used by all Firefox users. Firefox tracks end users’ usage behavior. One use is defined as each time a user launches the Firefox web browser. User usage provides more objective and accurate information about user demand than do other alternative measures, such as downloads. Because of the skew in distributions, we use the natural logarithm of UU values.

To examine app review time, we collected data from the official Firefox app forum (https://forums.mozilla.org/ addons/), where the editors of Firefox post the weekly statuses of the app review queues. We use this data to calculate the average waiting time of newly released apps each week, and construct a measure app review time (AR), which is defined as the natural logarithm of the average number of days it takes for a new app to pass review. Finally, to examine platform updates, we leverage an important policy change event by Firefox. On March 21, 2011, Firefox changed its update policy to significantly reduce the intervals between browser updates. Before this date, Firefox updated its browser platform approximately once a year. After this date, the average time interval between updates was reduced sharply to about six weeks. We use a binary indicator as the measure of platform update frequency (PU), with a value of 0 for all weeks before March 21, 2011 (i.e., the period of less frequent updates), and a value of 1 for all weeks after March 21, 2011 (i.e., the period of more frequent updates). Our entire sample covers 99 pre-change weeks and 123 post-change weeks.

We also include some control variables. The first one is entry of a key competitor (CE), which may affect the platform’s performance on both sides. It is measured using a dummy variable that indicates whether or not the key competitor, Google Chrome, had entered the browser market. The second factor is platform market share (PMS), which controls for important marketing condition changes (e.g., entry into a new foreign market) that may influence CNEs. PMS is measured using the proportion of all web browser users who use Firefox, and this data was obtained from StatCounter, a leading web traffic and usage analysis tool. We also control for seasonal effects by using a set of three seasonal dummy variables SEA = [Sea1, Sea2, Sea3] in the model. Table 2 shows the definitions and summary statistics of key variables. The average values for user usage, app number, and app diversity are 291 million (unlogged value), 5,831 (unlogged value), and 0.846 respectively.

## Model Specification and Estimation

In our empirical analysis, we adopt a time-series analysis technique, vector autoregression with exogenous variable (VARX) (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1995; Luo 2009; Pauwels 2004). This approach has been employed in recent IS and marketing literature (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2012; Luo et al. 2013; Trusov et al. 2009) and it allows us to capture dynamic interactions and feedback effects between the two sides of the platform. In our research context, VARX has several advantages over alternative modeling techniques. First, it can assess both the short-term (i.e., immediate) effects and the long-term (i.e., persistent) effects of endogenous variables on each other. It therefore allows us to characterize app-to-user CNEs and user-to-app CNEs based on their temporal features (Chang and Gurbaxani 2012). Second, VARX models can simultaneously capture the dynamic and intricate mutual influences among different variables, helping better illustrate CNEs between the two sides of the platform.

We adopt a standard VARX procedure as in the literature (e.g., Dekimpe and Hanssens 1999), which includes the following steps. First, we determine the appropriateness of using VARX based on Granger causality tests (Granger 1969). Second, we determine the model specification (VARX in levels, VARX in differences, or error-correction forms) based on the unit-root and cointegration test results. Third, using information criteria, we determine the number of lags in model specification. Finally, we derive generalized impulse response functions (GIRFs) to assess both short-term and long-term effects and test hypotheses.

In line with prior studies (e.g., Adomavicius et al. 2012; Trusov et al. 2009), we conduct a series of Granger causality tests to explore whether explanatory variables explain the variation of dependent variables, in addition to the lagged values of dependent variables. Granger causality tests offer valid arguments on causality in time-series data. By using lags of up to 20 periods (Trusov et al. 2009), we find Granger causal relationships among endogenous variables (e.g., app diversity Granger causes user usage (p = .00)). These results suggest the need to use a full dynamic model, such as a VARX model.

Model Specification. We use unit root tests to determine whether endogenous variables (i.e., AN, AD, and UU) are evolving or stationary. Stationarity of endogenous variables implies that the fluctuation of these variables caused by any unexpected changes will eventually dissipate and these variables will revert back to their deterministic patterns without a permanent regime shift. The variances of stationary variables are finite and time invariant (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1999). We use an augmented Dickey-Fuller test (ADF) to check stationarity. With first-difference values, the ADF tests of all endogenous variables reject the null hypothesis of a unit root (see Table 3) and suggest stationarity. We thus estimate the VARX model using the first-difference values of endogenous variables. Also, in model specification, we need to determine the appropriate number of lags used for endogenous variables. The Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Schwarz-Bayesian information criterion (SBIC) suggest that the lag of two periods is the optimal choice (AIC = -21.93, SBIC = -19.33). Our VARX models therefore include lags of up to two weeks.

We first propose a VARX system to capture dynamic interactions between the user-side and the app-side. We include a vector of the exogenous variables, an intercept C, and a deterministic-trend variable T that captures the impact of the omitted, gradually changing trend of the dependent variables. To test hypotheses on the short-term and long-term effects of CNEs, we employ the impulse response functions (IRFs) derived from the estimated parameters and variance-covariance matrices from the full VARX model. The main reason is that the VARX estimated coefficients are not usually directly interpretable due to general multicollinearity issues with including lagged terms (Sims 1980). IRFs are used to simulate the over-time impact of a change (or shock) to one endogenous variable (over its baseline) on the dynamics of the full multi-equation system (Enders 2010). The IRF results depict the net effects of all the modeled actions and reactions of endogenous variables over time. We adopt the generalized impulse response functions (GIRFs) because in this case, the sequences of moves of endogenous variables in the system become less an issue of concern (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1999; Luo et al. 2013). Using GIRFs, we simulate the fitted VARX system using a Monte Carlo approach with 1,000 runs. The resulting estimated means and standard errors of endogenous variables are used to test the significance of the changes of these variables over time. The short-term effects are assessed by examining whether the one-week lagged changes of other endogenous variables following a shock to a specific endogenous variable are significantly different from zero. To assess the long-term effects, we trace out a time period of 20 weeks (Dekimpe and Hanssens 1999), as GIRF values usually stabilize after such a long period. We examine whether the lagged changes of other endogenous variables after following a shock to a specific endogenous variable are still significantly different from zero after 20 weeks.

## Results

## Main Results

All VARX models have reasonably good model fit and explanatory power. For example, F-statistics for models (1) – (3) are 54.03, 29.54, and 30.63, respectively (p < 0.01). The adjusted R-squares for user usage in models (1) – (3) are 0.37, 0.38, and 0.38, respectively. The adjusted R-squares for app number in models (1) – (3) are 0.57, 0.63, and 0.58, respectively. The adjusted R-squares for app diversity in models (1) – (3) are 0.26, 0.25, and 0.26, respectively.

Table 4 reports the hypothesis test results on the short- and long-term effects of CNEs. Regarding app-to-user CNEs,

Table 4 shows that user usage has significant long-term effects on both app number and app diversity. The short-term effects of user usage on the app-side, however, are not significant. These results suggest that when the installed base of end users expands, the app-side does not respond with quick growth in either app number or app diversity. Instead, in the long-run, the app-side exhibits slow but persistent growth in both the number and variety of apps. Such patterns are illustrated in Figure 1, which shows the accumulated impulse responses of endogenous variables and also illustrates the short-term and long-term effects. In Panel A, the solid curves represent the over-time mean changes of app number and app diversity following a one-unit shock to user usage. The dotted curves represent the change levels that are onestandard-deviation above or below the corresponding mean levels. Panel A shows that in the short-term (after 1 week following the growth shock to user usage), the increases in app number and app diversity do not significantly deviate from zero, meaning that the user-side growth does not immediately trigger a significant growth of the app-side, possibly because only a few new apps can be developed and released so quickly. However, the app-side changes increase over time and in the long-term (e.g., after 20 weeks following the growth shock to user usage), the changes are significantly different from zero. This means that new apps keep emerging over time as more developers complete their time-consuming app development and eventually the app-side response in growth manifests in the long-term.

In contrast, Table 4 shows that both app number and app diversity have a significant short-term effect on user usage. However, their long-term effects on user usage are not significant. In Panel B of Figure 1, the solid curves represent the over-time mean changes of user usage following a oneunit shock to app number and app diversity. The dotted curves represent the change levels that are one-standarddeviation above or below the corresponding mean levels. Panel B shows that in the short-term (after 1 week following the growth shocks to app number and app diversity), the increase in user usage significantly deviates from zero, meaning that the more available apps can immediately attract more users to use the platform possibly because users do not need extra steps to use the platform. However, such increase declines over time and in the long term (after 20 week following the growth shocks to app number and app diversity), it becomes nonsignificant. It means that user momentum gradually disappears and does not sustain in the long term. Therefore, H1 is supported.

With regard to the effect of app review time on CNEs, Table 5 reveals that the interaction between app review time and user usage has a negative long-term effect on both app number and app diversity. These results suggest that the longer the average time of app review, the weaker the growth in both number and diversity of apps as a response to the user-side in the long-term (i.e., less sustainable). Given that the user-toapp effects are primarily long-term effects, H2a is supported. In addition, Table 5 also shows that longer app review time even weakens the short-term effects of user usage on app number, as indicated by the negative short-term effect of the interaction between app review time and user usage. This suggests that longer app review time makes the response of app number (to the user-side) even less immediate in the short-run. However, app review time does not influence the effect of user usage on app diversity in the short-term.

Table 6 shows how frequent platform updates influence userto-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs. Specifically, the interaction between platform update frequency and user usage has a negative long-term effect on both app number and app diversity. The implication is that when the platform updates itself more frequently, the growth of both app number and app diversity as a response to the user-side becomes weaker in the long-term (i.e., less persistent and sustainable). In other words, frequent platform updates prevent the app-side from committing to persistent growth in both the number and diversity of apps. In contrast, frequent platform updates have no significant influence on the short-term effects of user-toapp CNEs. Given that the user-to-app CNEs are primarily long-term effects, H3a is not supported, and H3b is supported. To help understand the interaction effect, we use Figure 2 to visually illustrate how app number and app diversity change over time after a one-unit shock on user usage, in both the case of infrequent platform updates and the case of frequent platform updates. The left portion (right portion) of Figure 2 shows that as a response to the one-unit shock on user usage, app number (app diversity) increases more significantly in the case of infrequent platform updates than in the case of frequent platform updates. These results illustrate that frequent platform updates significantly reduce the impact of user usage on the app-side (i.e., the user-to-app CNEs).

Table 6 also shows that the interaction between platform update frequency and app number has a negative effect on user usage in the short-term. In other words, when the platform updates itself more frequently, the short-term response of the user-side to the increased number of apps becomes weaker. However, frequent platform updates do not significantly weaken the short-term response of user usage to app diversity. Therefore, H3c is partially supported. A potential explanation is that the growth in app diversity provides more app complementarity that generates additional value to end users. Such complementarity-based value creation may also counteract the hassles of platform updates to end users and help attract users to respond to the app-side in the short-term.

To provide additional insights into the moderating effect of frequent platform updates, we split the sample and estimate the model for both the time period before the change in platform update policy and the time period after the change in policy. We examine the difference between these two periods in terms of the short-term and long-term effects of both userto-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs. The results, summarized in Appendix A, are consistent with those of the main analysis. In the pre-change period (with infrequent platform updates), user usage has a significant long-term effect on both app number and app diversity (see Table A1 of Appendix A). In the post-change period (with frequent platform updates), neither the short-term effect nor the long-term effect of user usage is significant (see Table A2 of Appendix A). Similarly, in the pre-change period, number of apps has a significant short-term effect on user usage (see Table A1 of Appendix A). In the post-change period, this effect is nonsignificant (see Table A2 of Appendix A). App diversity, however, has a short-term effect on user usage in both the pre-change period and the post-change period. In this regard, frequent platform updates weaken user-to-app CNEs and partially weaken app-to-user CNEs.

From the original dataset, we draw a sample with replacement 100 times and rerun the VARX models on each sample. The comparison of the estimates in the main analysis with their corresponding distributions of bootstrap estimates suggests that all significant results hold. Thus, there is no concern regarding the stability of the parameters of interest.

Second, we also collect data on user download (UD) for the platform and use it as an alternative measure to characterize the user-side. We consider the total number of times the platform is downloaded and log-transform this variable to address the distribution skew. We rerun the VARX models with the user usage measure replaced with UD. The results based on UD, as summarized in Appendix B, are qualitatively consistent with the main results. Specifically, the increase in UD leads to long-term growth of both number and diversity of apps (in Table B1), and this long-term effect is weakened both by long app review times (in Table B2) and by frequent platform updates (in Table B3). The increase in number of apps leads to the short-term growth of UD (in Table B1), and this short-term effect is weakened both by long app review times (in Table B2) and frequent platform updates (in Table B3). However, app diversity has no significant effect on UD. A potential explanation is that new users who have relatively less knowledge about different types of apps are more likely to download than are experienced users. Therefore, the aggregate information such as total number of apps is cognitively more appealing to new users than detailed information such as the diversity of apps.

Third, we try alternative measures that characterize the appside. We collect additional data on app developers and calculate the measures of developer number (DN) and developer diversity (DD) using the same approaches as for app number and app diversity, respectively.<sup>4</sup> We rerun the VARX models with the measures app number and app diversity replaced by DN and DD, respectively. The results, as summarized in Appendix C, are qualitatively consistent with the main results. The growth of user usage leads to long-term growth in both DN and DD, and the growth of DN and DD lead to a short-term growth of user usage. The long-term effects of user usage on DN and DD are weakened by both app review time and frequent platform updates. The shortterm effects of DN and DD on user usage are weakened by frequent platform updates, but not by app review time.

Fourth, in the main analysis, we use a binary variable to distinguish between the period with frequent platform updates and the period with infrequent updates. To verify the robustness of our results, we also try a continuous variable. Specifically, we derive the time intervals between the releases of different Firefox versions. We reverse-code platform update time intervals by subtracting the interval values from the maximum interval value, so that a high measure value means frequent platform updates. Using this measure, we rerun the VARX analysis. As shown in Table D1 of Appendix D, the results are qualitatively consistent with the main results (i.e., frequent platform updates weaken both user-to app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs).

Fifth, we verify our findings on frequent platform updates to the software platform by taking into consideration the frequency of updates by competitors to their software platform. We collect additional data on the historical updates of Firefox’s key competitor, Google Chrome. We derive the time intervals between the releases of different Chrome versions, and reverse-code the time intervals by subtracting the interval values from the maximum interval value. Therefore, a high (low) value of reverse-coded interval means Chrome engages in frequent (infrequent) releases of its updates. We then include the measure of reverse-coded intervals as an additional control variable in the model of Appendix D and rerun the VARX analysis. Compared to that in Appendix D, this analysis further takes into account the competitive environment and controls for the update frequency of the key competing platform. The results, as summarized in Appendix E, show that our findings remain robust—that is, frequent updates to a software platform weaken both user-to app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs for it.

## Additional Insights

We conduct additional analyses to develop insights that complement our main findings. First, our main findings reveal that long app review times neither weaken nor strengthen app-to-user CNEs. A potential explanation is that although long app review times delay app releases, more thorough app reviews may improve the quality of apps and counteract the negative impact of delayed app releases. To further investigate the impact of app review time on app quality, we collect additional weekly data on the quality of new apps. On the official website for Firefox apps, end users can rate apps after they use them. Ratings are based on a fivestar scale, with one star indicating extremely low quality and five stars indicating extremely high quality. For each new app, we use the average user rating as a measure of quality. We then create a separate VARX model including app review time and app quality as endogenous variables, and other exogenous variables. The results, as summarized in Table F1 of Appendix F, show that long app review times significantly increase the quality of new apps in both the short-term and the long-term. This finding provides a key insight about why app review times may not necessarily weaken app-to-user CNEs. That is, the app quality improvement associated with long app review processes is likely to offset the negative impact of delayed app releases. It is also worth noting, however, that such app quality improvements are still not strong enough to dominate the wear-out effects and lead to significant longterm increases in user usage.

Second, to gain more insights on the impact of app quality, we conduct an additional analysis by dividing the sample into two subsamples: a high-rating sample with app ratings above the mean and a low-rating sample with app ratings below the mean. Then, we examine the app-to-user effect separately for the high-rating sample and the low-rating sample. Table F2 in Appendix F summarizes the results. The results show that app-to-user CNEs are relatively stronger in the high-rating subsample than in the low-rating subsample. These findings provide additional support for the explanation that improved app quality (as a result of long app review times) can make apps and the platform more attractive to users and counteract the negative effect of app review time on app-to-user CNEs.

Third, as mentioned, a potential reason for the negative moderating effect of frequent platform updates on user-to-app CNEs is that app developers may be forced to devote more resources to updating their existing applications, making it more challenging for them to respond to user-side growth with new apps. To further verify this insight, we specify a new VARX model that incorporates frequent platform updates, the number of updated apps, and the number of new apps as endogenous variables, along with other exogenous variables. The results, as summarized in Table G1 of Appendix G, show that frequent platform updates significantly increase the number of updated apps in both the short-term and the long-term. The number of updated apps significantly decreases the number of new apps in both the short and long terms. Therefore, these findings support the explanation that frequent platform updates weaken user-to-app CNEs by constraining developers’ efforts in new app development.

We also rerun the full VARX model (3) with aggregate measures for the number and diversity of apps replaced with separate measures for the number and diversity of new apps and updated apps. The results in Table G2 of Appendix G show that the interaction between frequent platform updates and user usage has a negative effect on both the number and diversity of new apps in the long-term. This suggests that frequent platform updates weaken the long-term effect of user-to-app CNEs for new apps. In contrast, the results also indicate that the interaction between frequent platform updates and user usage has a positive effect on the number of updated apps in the long-term. The effect of this interaction on the diversity of updated apps, however, is not significant. These findings suggest that frequent platform updates essentially drive developers to commit more resources to update their existing apps, and such resource commitments are widely spread across diverse categories of apps. As a result, developers have limited efforts to develop more new apps as a response to the user-side.

## Discussion

## Theoretical Implications

Our work builds on the platform literature that has shown the existence of CNEs and contributes to it by surfacing the nature of the asymmetry among different types of CNEs. Our theorization of asymmetric CNEs based on a value creation/ capture perspective and the supporting empirical findings generate two important theoretical implications for characterizing the two-sided platform. First, our findings shed light on the differential temporal effects that the app-side and the user-side of a software platform have on each other due to their underlying different value creation/capture processes. The pursuit of exchange value requires the app-side to engage in resource commitment processes, which make the app-side less likely to grow immediately as a response to the user-side expansion. However, the commitment of resources is likely to drive developers to realize their development over time, making the CNEs from the user-side to the app-side eventually manifest in the long-term. In contrast, the pursuit of direct use value makes the resource commitment processes of the user-side relatively simple. The user-side is therefore more likely to grow immediately as a response to the app-side expansion. But over time, the user-side momentum is less likely to be persistent in the long-term due to the lack of commitment. In this regard, the differential value pursuit and resource commitment drive the distinct immediacy and duration of CNEs on the two sides. Our reliance on a value perspective to understand network effects essentially echoes the call in recent network studies to go beyond network size explanations of network effects (Afuah 2013).

Second, our revelation of the differential temporal effects provides insights on how feedback, specifically delays in response to feedback and instability in the generated feedback (Tiwana 2014), works differently on the two sides of a platform. Our findings suggest that delays are more of an issue for the supply-side (the app-side in our case) responding to demand-side (the user-side in our case) feedback, possibly due to the relatively more complex resource commitment processes on the supply-side. However, instability in supplyside feedback to the demand-side is less of an issue as supplyside resource commitments foster a persistent supply-side feedback to the demand-side. In contrast, instability in demand-side feedback to the supply-side can be an issue due to volatility in platform users’ taste for apps.

The evidence on the influence of platform governance policies on CNEs extends the understanding of platform models, as the extant research has largely taken CNEs as exogenously given (e.g. Eisenmann et al. 2006; Parker and Van Alstyne 2008; Seamans and Zhu 2014). Our findings suggest that both user-to-app CNEs and app-to-user CNEs can be weakened by frequent updates of a software platform, and that user-to-app CNEs can also be weakened by a long app review process. An important implication is that there are potential trade-offs in platform governance policies that may not be revealed by adopting a narrow frame of reference, say the platform to the exclusion of CNEs, in the evaluation. For instance, quality control and improvement through platform updates may be viewed, from the platform owner’s local perspective, as benefits. However, these policies may also have nuanced implications on the interdependencies with and among platform participants, such as app developers and users. Indeed, governance policies that are considered to be advantageous for the platform owner may impose mutual adaptation challenges for other ecosystem participants. For example, despite the benefits of platform updates, the process of releasing updates too frequently may inevitably disrupt the smooth interactions across sides. Therefore, the impacts that these governance policies can have on the evolution of the platform itself require careful consideration alongside the impacts that these governance policies can have on cross-side evolution and interdependencies in the platform ecosystem.

The insights from our study also contribute to the theoretical understanding of software development strategies. First, past literature has suggested the sequential provision of multiple versions of software as an optimal way to signal quality and gradually build installed bases in the consumer market with network externality (e.g., Gallaugher and Wang 2002; Padmanabhan et al. 1997). Our study suggests that the new development environment (e.g., the hyperspeed of the web/ mobile world), in which platforms are vital in bridging apps and users, may challenge traditional sequential strategies to develop versions of software. The CNEs in the platform ecosystem intensify app competition as users face a variety of choices (Boudreau 2012). The short-term effect of app-touser CNEs also suggests the difficulty in drawing long-term user interest. In this case, the quality of introductory versions becomes more important. App developers may have to reconsider how they balance resource commitments between seeding initial market share with high-quality introductory versions of an app and refining quality of the app in subsequent sequential versions to maintain and grow the installed bases.

Second, while past research has largely assumed that sequential development is under the control of developers (e.g.,

Padmanabhan et al. 1997), we delineate that platform governance policies can potentially interfere with developers’ sequential development and app releases. Long app review times may impede the new app releases that developers use to seed initial installed bases. Platform updates may force app updates and even make app updates a burden for developers. In this regard, developers should take into account the potential influences from platform governance actions, and plan their sequential development strategies accordingly to make their app upgrades and releases more adaptive to the context of the platform ecosystem.

The consideration of platform governance policies also extends the lens of IS governance to an ecosystem context that is characterized by complex adaptiveness. The extant IS governance perspectives have been primarily concerned with internal organizational arrangements (e.g., Sambamurthy and Zmud 1999) or with interorganizational arrangements of dyadic relationships (Klein and Rai 2009; Rai and Tang 2010). In contrast, the platform governance perspective is concerned with the platform ecosystem and diverse stakeholders (not only the platform owner), and needs to uncover characteristics of complex adaptive systems (Tanriverdi et al. 2010), such as interdependencies and coevolution in the ecosystem. Our findings reveal that the trajectory and direction of coevolution of the app and user-sides of the platform are affected by the governance actions of the platform owner. Our study’s revelation of the temporal features of CNEs suggests that platform owners may be able to dynamically adapt platform governance to accelerate (or decelerate) CNEs and realize temporary advantages over time—an idea that is especially important in complex adaptive systems where concatenating temporary advantages is more likely to yield competitive advantages than simply seeking a sustainable source of competitive advantage (e.g., Tanriverdi et al. 2010).

## Practical Implications

The characterization of asymmetric CNEs has implications for platform managers who often face a coordination challenge when CNEs enable participants on each side to form expectations about the other side (Spulber 2010; Tucker and Zhang 2010). To address this issue, platforms usually need to decide which side to prioritize in resource commitment (Hagiu and Spulber 2013). Our findings suggest that given the long-term effects of user-to-app CNEs, platform managers need to be patient and persistent in supporting the app-side growth. They may also subsidize the app-side in a way that expedites the app-side’s response to user-side growth. Given the shortterm effect of app-to-user CNEs, platform managers may devote resources to sustain the user-side response to the appside and mitigate feedback decays on the user-side. This implication can also be generalized to non-software platform contexts. For example, in platforms such as Airbnb, hosts (suppliers) often engage in more complex processes of resource commitment (e.g., property maintenance, safety assurance, picture preparation, etc.) than renters. Therefore, akin to our findings in the software platform context, the responses of the host-side and the renter-side to each other may differ in immediacy and duration. Airbnb can provide services that assist hosts to prepare their properties and more swiftly capture the fast-growing demand from renters. On the renter side, Airbnb can place more weight on approaches (e.g., community building, feedback management) to improve renter engagement in the long term. Providing greater transparency about future host-side development to the renter-side (e.g., more development in amenities, house rules, and professional displays) may maintain or increase momentum of renters and sustain long-term renter-side growth.

Another key practical implication for platform managers is to build governance capabilities in order to reduce the conflict between governance actions and the growth of the platform ecosystem through network effects. Platforms should take action to improve the efficiency of their governance processes, such as diligently reducing app review times. For example, recently Apple announced its promise to significantly reduce the average time for app review and approval to less than two days (Webb 2016). The intention was to bring Apple more in line with its key competitor, Alphabet Inc.’s Google. In contrast to Apple, which checks apps before including them in the App Store, Google Play often reviews apps after making them available to users for download so as to avoid delays caused by app review. Platforms may also use more transparent information on review status to better manage the expectations of app developers and users and alleviate their frustration. Differentiated review processes for heterogeneous apps can be employed to expedite reviews of apps that are heavily requested by users.

Platform managers should also build their capability in managing frequent platform updates as it is critical for platform growth and competition. When comparing Firefox and Chrome in terms of update management, Jono DiCarlo, a former Firefox developer, concluded that “doing rapid releases poorly just made Firefox look like an inferior version of Chrome” (Kingsley-Hughes 2012). Therefore, platforms should make serious efforts to enhance their software architectures (e.g., compatibility, standardization of APIs, and modulation), expand their support to both app developers and users, and even rely on assistance from certified support professionals and open communities to streamline their platform update processes. Doing so can help minimize the negative impacts of frequent platform updates on both developers and users, and sustain the growth of the platform ecosystem.

Regarding practical implications to app developers, our study suggests that app developers need to consider strategies and techniques to overcome the potential adverse effects of platform governance. For example, the dynamic software update technique (e.g., Hayden et al. 2012; Seifzadeh et al. 2013) can facilitate and simplify app update processes. Developers may consider using more of these techniques in adapting to frequent platform updates. Second, developers may also consider more flexible strategies in the development stage to be better prepared for subsequent platform governance actions. For example, emerging hybrid development approaches (Beckman 2012) allow developers to use more generic tools (e.g., Javacript) to fuse their apps with the native features of platforms. Adopting these app development approaches in the first place provides developers the flexibility and convenience in subsequent revisions and improvements driven by platform governance.

## Limitations and Future Research

This study has some limitations and creates opportunities for future research. First, our field data is about a specific software platform. Future research may consider generalizing the insights on asymmetric CNEs by examining other software platforms (e.g., operating systems and mobile platforms) and non-software platforms (e.g., B2C and C2C transactional platforms). The CNEs in emerging platform business models (e.g., sharing economy) and the influences of many technological and non-technological platform governance factors are also worth exploring.

Second, this study focuses on a free software platform and therefore the role of pricing is not relevant in our context. However, pricing can be an important governance mechanism of a platform (Parker and Van Alstyne 2008), and is critical to other types of software platforms (e.g., enterprise software). The examination of pricing issues may help shed light on how CNEs influence the competition between a platform and third party developers for profit. Therefore, future research may focus on for-profit platforms and specifically consider the pricing effect.

Third, our study focuses primarily on the interactions between a platform and its own two-sided markets. The data availability keeps us from fully endogenizing the inter-platform competition in our research model. Future research may explore how the evolving competition among platforms is influenced by asymmetric CNEs and the interactions among CNEs and other platform strategies. Future research can evaluate how platform strategies may strengthen, rather than weaken, CNEs and enable platforms to accrue competitive advantages over others. Situations in which platforms may intentionally weaken CNEs so as to gain more control over the two sides also deserve attention.

Finally, future research may adopt different theoretical perspectives, such as ecology theory, to examine the evolution of the ecosystems of software and non-software platforms. These perspectives may help generate additional insights on the ecology processes on the supply-side and the demandside, and the temporal dynamics of cross-side network effects. The roles of many other sides (in addition to the supply-side and the demand-side) that generate valuable products/services and influence cross-side network effects can be taken into consideration.

## Conculsion

Our study surfaces the asymmetric features of CNEs in the software platform ecosystem and the impacts of platform governance mechanisms on CNEs. Our findings reveal that user-to-app CNEs primarily take effect in the long-term, while app-to-user CNEs primarily take effect in the short-term. We find that long app review times weaken user-to-app CNEs, but not app-to-user CNEs. Our results also suggest that long app review times result in enhanced app quality that may counteract the negative impact of delayed app releases (caused by long app review times). In addition, we find that frequent platform updates weaken user-to-app CNEs, in part because frequent platform updates force developers to commit more effort to updating existing apps rather than developing new ones. Frequent platform updates partially weaken app-to-user CNEs by mitigating the attractiveness of increased app numbers, but not that of increased app diversity, to end users. Overall, our study elaborates our understanding about the dynamics of network effects in two-sided platforms and how governance practices of platforms can be leveraged to establish a virtuous cycle of CNEs for the vibrant growth of the platform ecosystem.
