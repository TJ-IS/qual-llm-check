# CIO: concept is over

John Leslie King

School of Information, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, USA

Correspondence:

## Abstract

In the last 20 years the 1990 joke that CIO is starting to mean ‘Career Is Over’ has been buried under the proliferation of CIOs, but the concept behind the CIO – the idea of the C-level executive – probably is over. The C-level fad is waning as the title ‘chief’ is debased, but more important the very sensibility of the idea of the chief is embodied and entombed in a managerial orthodoxy of 20th century industrial administration that simply cannot hold together much longer. It is time for management educators, starting with people in the information systems field, to look beyond orthodoxy to a risky but engaging new world in which the M-Form organization and all that went with it is seen as a way station en route to a new kind of human enterprise.

## Introduction

wo decades ago, BusinessWeek startled the information systems world with an article titled ‘CIO is Starting to Stand for Career is Over’ (Rothfeder and Driscoll, 1990). The information systems world was no stranger to criticism, but the BusinessWeek article challenged the one bankable thing the IS field had going for it: educating people to be IS leaders in business. The 1990 article was wrong: there are now more CIOs than ever, and trade publications aimed at CIOs are more abundant than in 1990. Corrections have come from many sources (e.g., Alter, 2008), at least one in BusinessWeek itself (Bloomberg-BusinessWeek, 2005) and many from more partisan sources.<sup>1</sup> Indeed, CIOs are gaining influence in organizations (Weill and Ross, 2004; Ross et al., 2006). Maybe CIO means ‘Career is Ongoing.’ <sup>2</sup>

Since 1990, the IS field has experienced the double whammy of the dot.com collapse and a more general economic collapse. IS enrollments declined but are now rebounding. The IS field may be a living example of The Undead, with the ‘Career is Over’ crisis offering some lessons for the next incarnation.

Something big is afoot. One might ask whether CIO means ‘Concept is Over,’ where the concept is the ‘C-level’ executive itself. The C-level fad has been around for a while, but it is waning, and in its decline one can see that the information systems field is about transformation and transgression. This is not a new theme: the argument has been debated over whether the IS field is marginalized by other fields within contemporary management schools (King and Lyytinen, 2004; Lyytinen and King, 2004, 2006; Weber, 2006; Teo and Srivastava, 2007).

Rather than revisiting that line, this essay is a wake-up call to IS academics, especially those in management schools. In three parts, it tells the story of the changing fortunes of C-level titles, a shift in the orthodoxy of American management practice and education in the 20th century, and the rise of a new way of looking at enterprise. The first part (‘All that is Solid Melts Into Air’) establishes the C-level executive as a fad grounded in a mid-20th century management orthodoxy focused mainly on the alignment of strategy and structure. The second (‘The Stickiness of Managerial Orthodoxy’) explores changes underway in that orthodoxy. The third (‘Breaking Away’) describes a phase-shift in thinking about human enterprise, with particular implications for the IS field.

## All that is solid melts into air

The CIO title is an upclassing of the earlier titles Data Processing Manager and Information Systems Manager. <sup>3</sup> CIO triggered a wholesale debasement of the title ‘chief, which was formerly reserved for the top person (CEO), the head of operations or administration (COO/CAO) and the person who worried about money (CFO). CIO broke the dam, and ‘C’ has since been applied to all kinds of jobs: General Counsel (Chief Legal Officer), the head of human resources (Chief Talent Officer), the director of training (Chief Learning Officer), and so on. A business journalist captured this well in 2008: ‘There’s been such a wave of title inflation in corporate America these days, it’s no wonder corporations have had to build such huge headquarters towers: They need office space to house all those ‘C level’ executives’ (Virgin, 2008). Wikipedia lists nearly 50 corporate titles that begin with the word ‘chief.’ <sup>4</sup> Everyone knows the idiom about too many chiefs.<sup>5</sup> The C-level executive is dying from overexposure; its demise signals a deeper tale of change in the paradigm of industrial administration arising from the middle of the 20th century. That paradigm is being upended by new capabilities enabled by digital information technologies.

By standards of human history, this change has been rapid; however, given the fast pace and perspective of contemporary management research, it has been almost too slow to notice. Normative management research and education can smooth over this change for a while, but in time the change will overwhelm all in its path. The idea is not new. In their 1848 polemic The Communist Manifesto, Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels explained human history as a long period of stable dialectical conflict between classes, oppressor and oppressed (patricians and plebeians, landowners and tenants, masters and slaves). They said capitalism was different; it created an unstable class struggle. The bourgeoisie, who owned the means of industrial production, oppressed the proletariat, the workers who ran the machines, and this conflict could not settle down. Capitalism was dependent on technology, and required constant change to accommodate ever-improving means of production.

Chapter 1 of The Communist Manifesto contains the famous line, ‘All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses, his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind.’ <sup>6</sup> Technology-driven capitalism represented a fundamental departure. Marx and Engels were right about this, but wrong in the particulars. They pitted thesis against antithesis to forge the synthesis of global communism, arrived at by stages beginning with socialism. The revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat would arise as the proletariat overthrew the bourgeoisie; Marx thought the Paris Commune of March–May 1871 was an instance of this (Marx, 1871). The ideal ‘classless’ society would subsequently emerge, and true communism would be achieved.

This prediction faltered; a century and a half after The Communist Manifesto, communism is all but gone. It seemed pretty solid to those in its thrall, including the anti-communist opposition, but it melted into air during a few short years near the end of the 20th century. This was not the only error in prediction made by Marx and Engels. They thought the revolution would begin in industrialized Europe (England, France, Germany), but it took hold first and deepest in Russia, a feudal society in early stages of industrialization, and subsequently in another slowly industrializing country, China. The most bitter early enemy of communism, National Socialism, arose in Marx’ highly industrialized birthplace, Germany. One thing Marx and Engels got right was that technological progress embodied in capitalism changed the game for humanity.<sup>7</sup>

Management researchers seldom cite Marx, but they like Joseph Schumpeter (cf. Nolan and Croson, 1995). Writing in the early 20th century, Schumpeter acknowledged the insights of Marx, agreeing that capitalism would eventually decline and be replaced by some form of socialism. Schumpeter saw unbridled capitalism as highly disruptive, and he was wary of disruption, given his early work on the business cycle in economic development. He predicted that capitalism would be subordinated to corporatism, abetted by government, to create regulated change that society could tolerate. Capitalism’s virtues, according to Schumpeter, were mainly in what he called the money market, which made capital available to the entrepreneur – a social leader who used new ways of doing things, and usually new technology, to disrupt the existing order. Schumpeter did not mention anything melting into air, but he is remembered for his ‘gales of creative destruction.

Clay Christenson builds on Schumpeter’s insight in his 1997 best-seller, The Innovator’s Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail. He argues that some technologies are ‘sustaining,’ while others are ‘disruptive.’ Sustaining technologies reinforce the status quo, while disruptive technologies disturb the status quo. The innovator faces a dilemma: either sustain the industry with incremental contributions or upend the industry with disruptive contributions. Christenson’s focus is primarily on within-industry innovation. He starts with the hard disk drive industry, branches from there to other industries, and ends up in services, especially education (Christensen et al., 2008, 2011). The logic of his argument can be taken beyond the borders of any given industry to the broad realm of human enterprise: any technology that is sufficiently ubiquitous and important can disrupt not merely a given industry, but all of enterprise.

Information technology and the information systems that are formed from IT meet those criteria. They pose a threat to established patterns of doing enterprise, and threaten established understanding of enterprise. They have already started altering apprentice structures for management training, reward structures and regulations established to affect management behaviors, and the canons of schools of management and administration. Information systems researchers seldom create the innovations that disrupt enterprise but they do (or should) report on disruption, despite the risks. This has not been an easy transition. Information systems researchers have found their colleagues less than enthusiastic about stories of IT-enabled ‘transformation,’ especially during the dot.com boom. The IS field suffered payback when the bubble burst, and has been contrite since. However, that contrition should be limited to mistaking the glimmer for the gold itself. The dot.com boom was not a false alarm; it was the harbinger of much bigger changes, including the end of 20th century managerial orthodoxy.

## The stickiness of managerial orthodoxy

Technology has always been part of human enterprise. This is true both with respect to the way enterprise is conducted, and the way we understand it to be conducted (Schroeder, 2007). How enterprise happens and how we understand it are increasingly disconnected. Technological enablement leads to fundamental changes in the practice of enterprise, but the orthodox understanding of management fails to notice and lags behind. The first half of this section describes the role of socio-technical change in building the human enterprise of the 20th century; the second describes the creation of managerial orthodoxy built upon that enterprise and that is incapable of recognizing the changes now underway. The changing reality of human enterprise will surely sweep away the current orthodoxy; the question is whether people in management schools will influence how that happens.

Agricultural enterprise, anchored in ancient knowledge and tradition, changed relatively little over many centuries. Work was done by humans and animals, and the lore of agrarian life was bound up in the limitations of such work. Social organization was stable: landowners ruled and subjects submitted to that rule. The power of the ‘landed’ classes gave way to merchants and bankers following the opening of sea trade routes to the orient and the New World between the 16th and 18th centuries. New forms of organization emerged to exploit and expand these changes, including the limited liability corporation that added the concept of ‘ownership in’ to the longestablished ‘ownership of.’ Technology played a major role in the rise of sea trade (cf. Law, 1987), and an even larger role in industrialization.<sup>8</sup>

Early industrialization started with wind and water power, but it was the mastery of the heat gradient and the power of combustion that really changed the world. By 1712, Thomas Newcomen’s ‘atmospheric engine’ was using steam to pump water out of mines, and less than a century later steam was powering ships and trains, as well as factories. The industrial revolution brought rapidly growing technical capability together with rapidly growing demand for new ways of doing things. The merger of know-how and technology, what this paper refers to as socio-technical systems, emerged as the key to progress. In particular, new communications technologies enabled the rise of ‘system’ in American industrial enterprise, and system soon spread around the world (Yates, 1989). This was not a simple story of evolving technologies, political ideologies, managerial know-how, or similar components; rather, it combined all of these in an ecology of change that was highly interactive and much less deterministic or functional than it sometimes appears. In the process, enterprise became increasingly complicated, and a subtle but essential shift occurred in the late industrial revolution.

The complication of enterprise challenged the inherent right of the owner to run the business. The Bolshevik revolution stripped property rights from individuals, necessitating the rise of a technocratic cadre to run things – the nomenklatura. But even in the anti-Bolshevik West, the game was changing. The monopolistic behavior of US tycoons of the late 19th century triggered an array of anti-trust measures between 1880 and 1920. The first American professional schools of management started during this time: Wharton at the University of Pennsylvania in 1881 and the Harvard Business School in 1908. ‘Scientific management’ became popular through the work of Frederick Taylor and others, and their efforts were widely seen as part of a systems perspective. The world’s first great industrial sector emerged: motor transport (automobiles and trucks powered by internal-combustion engines operating on good roads). Ransom Olds adopted the assembly line for automobile production in 1901, Henry Ford created production engineering between 1905 and 1910, and the modern industrial era began.

The advances from motor transport permanently altered industrialization. Industrial labor unions arose, smallinvestor capital markets became prominent, regulatory apparatus grew, the concept of ‘public’ accounting displaced the earlier ‘cost’ accounting. Companies were increasingly ‘public’ entities that interacted with and answered to an order of authority quite different from that of the tycoon age (Johnson and Kaplan, 1987). This change was mirrored in the public sector with the rise of ‘reformed government’ and what Waldo (1948) called the Administrative State. The era of professional management and the professional manager was born.

No case better illustrates this than General Motors (GM). GM was created by the entrepreneur William Durant, the successful owner-operator of Michigan’s Flint Road Cart Company. His company became the largest of its kind in the world, and his success drew the attention of the fledgling Buick Motor Company. In 1904, Buick hired Durant as its General Manager (what we would today call a combination of CEO and COO). In 1908, Durant leveraged Buick on the growing money market to create a holding company, General Motors. He sold stock in GM, and with the funds raised acquired Oldsmobile and Cadillac, as well as auto parts suppliers. Durant’s main competitor, owneroperator Henry Ford, focused on standardizing production and driving down the cost of his products. In contrast, Durant focused on providing customers with an array of purchase choices, a car for every niche in the market. In contrast to Ford’s vertical integration of production, Durant saw auto parts as a market where companies could buy and sell, providing parts at the lowest cost.

Durant was an entrepreneur, but he was not a professional manager. By 1910, he was overextended, and his creditors forced him out of GM. He co-founded the Chevrolet Motor Company with race driver Louis Chevrolet in 1911, regaining sufficient financial success to create United Motors (UM) Company in early 1916. Under the direction of a smart electrical engineering graduate from MIT, Alfred Sloan, UM became a major parts supplier. Durant bought enough GM shares to resume control of the company in 1916, and soon brought Chevrolet and UM into GM. But he faltered again, and in 1920 the DuPont interests had sufficient ownership of GM to force Durant out for good.<sup>9</sup> Sloan rose to GM’s leadership, built on Durant’s insights, and married them to extraordinary administrative acumen that eventually displaced Henry Ford’s ‘unitary form’ or U-Form organization with the GM ‘multidivisional form’ or M-Form organization.

Sloan was a paradigmatic professional manager. GM’s market share was 12% when he took over; when he retired a quarter-century later, GM’s market share was 56%. He described how he accomplished this in his memoir (Sloan, 1957).<sup>10</sup> Business historian Alfred Chandler (1962) characterized American enterprise in four phases: the Civil War to 1880 (tycoons, business empires, and vertical integration of the U-Form); 1880–1900 (the rise of the professional manager); 1900 to WWI (product line development and diversification); and post-WWI (the M-Form organization). In GM, the professional manager Sloan replaced the entrepreneur Durant, creating the M-Form organization that separated strategic (i.e., non-market-related) action from operational (i.e., market-related) decision making. The heart of the M-Form was that structure followed strategy.<sup>11</sup> At GM, strategy was made centrally, while operational decision making was decentralized. ‘Corporate’ would generate capital and exploit economies of scale and scope for cost reduction, while ‘Division’ would design, manufacture, and market in competition with both GM and non-GM products. Oliver Williamson declared the M-Form organization to be consistent with transaction cost economics (Williamson, 1975) and later called it ‘y the most significant organizational innovation of the twentieth century’ (1985: 279). The M-Form organization became enshrined in management education during the 1960s. This was about the same time that the early effects of modern digital information technology could be seen, but those effects were largely occluded by the emerging orthodoxy.

Human enterprise became increasingly global during the 1960s, encouraged by the experiences of WWII and the Cold War that altered the sense of what was possible with large-scale socio-technical systems. A lot of this had to do with the technical success of the USSR, which had made tremendous investments in science and industrialization during the Stalin era and had attained prominence in the military sphere (Bernal, 1939; Rosenberg, 1982). The USSR detonated its first nuclear weapon in 1949, just four years after the United States. The same year, it introduced the TU-4 long-range bomber, capable of carrying nuclear weapons to North America. Both the bomb and its delivery system were ‘knock-offs’ of American originals, but they appeared to work. The capitalist West responded by creating the North Atlantic Treaty Organization, NATO, in 1949. Soviet scientific and technical prowess grew, culminating in the launch of Sputnik, the world’s first artificial satellite, in 1957. Over the next 20 years, NATO countries, led by the United States, met the Soviet challenge in science and technology and surpassed it in virtually every area of socio-technical systems.

This is seen clearly in the little-known story of Project SAGE (King, 2010). Project SAGE, the Semi-Automatic Ground Environment, was created between 1949 and 1959 as the world’s first distributed, networked, humancomputer system. It arguably launched the US computer industry, and demonstrated that computers could be applied to managerial problems far beyond the calculating and printing tasks accomplished by the precursors of digital computers. For example, it produced the Semi-Automatic Business Research Environment, or SABRE, which provided the base for the world’s first computerized airline passenger reservation systems. ‘System’ had become key to American management in the latter part of the 19th century and the early part of the 20th century (Yates, 1989), and by the mid-20th century the idea itself had become an integral part of American intellectual development.<sup>12</sup> Management orthodoxy was a merger of the concept of ‘system’ and the practical success of the M-Form organization.

Given the importance of socio-technical systems to the practice of enterprise during the middle of the 20th century, it seems odd that new technologies, and especially information technology, were ignored by the growing orthodoxy in management education. But this becomes more understandable when one considers the profound shifts in management education during the 1960s, and the sustained growth of management education after this time. In the mid-1950s, most formally educated managers were graduates of Bachelor’s degree programs, many of which had grown out of professional schools of accounting and similar ‘business’ training. This was challenged by two influential studies, one funded by the Carnegie Foundation (Pierson, 1959), the other funded by the Ford Foundation (Gordon and Howell, 1959). These studies argued that managers in an increasingly complicated socio-technical world needed grounding in the liberal arts rather than technical skills taught by the management education programs of the time. Moreover, they suggested that management education be concentrated at the postbaccalaureate level. Finally, these studies suggested that students not be trained in ‘sectors’ such as banking, transportation, agricultural business, and industrial administration, but that instead graduate management education should take a ‘functional’ perspective around finance, marketing, accounting, operations, etc. These recommendations shaped management education profoundly.

American management education also grew rapidly. AACSB-accredited programs grew from fewer than 100 in 1960 to about 400 by 2000 (Figure 1).<sup>13</sup> Most of this growth was in graduate education. Total BBAs grew, but normalized growth (BBA degrees divided by the number of accredited programs granting them) declined after the mid-1980s (Figure 2). Growth in total and normalized MBAs has been steady (Figure 3). New management education programs had to adopt legitimate pedagogies. The simplest way to do this was through memetic mimicry, a form of voluntary institutional isomorphism (Meyer and Rowan, 1977; DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Scott, 1995; Frumkin and Galaskiewicz, 2004). This was reinforced by coercive isomorphism, particularly accreditation by the AACSB.<sup>14</sup> The intellectual bases of American management education continued to evolve during this period, but the strengthening of orthodoxy arising from the interests served by establishing and maintaining an orthodox ‘canon’ was overpowering. Established programs benefited from the proliferation of imitators, and new programs benefited from the stability of the existing order.

The seeds of the information age were germinating at this time; and with them the embryonic Information Systems field. Thomas J. Watson, CEO of IBM Corporation, reportedly said in 1943 that there might be a world market for five computers – a prediction that proved wildly wrong.<sup>15</sup> The aforementioned SAGE system, begun in 1949, laid the groundwork for many future innovations in IT. The first UNIVAC I computer went to work for the US Census in 1951, six decades after that pioneering organization adopted Herman Hollerith’s punched card tabulator. General Electric and Metropolitan Life Insurance acquired computers in 1954, and it was the US insurance industry that subsequently taught the world how to use digital computers for administrative data processing (Yates, 2005). Integrated circuits enabled the minicomputer and microcomputer, and by the 1980s networked computers were evolving toward the Internet (King et al., 1997; Abbate, 1999). The ever-improving price performance of computers embodied in Moore’s Law helped the digital revolution along.

By the 1990s, it was pretty clear that management practice was changing; the late 1990s, with the dot.com boom, nearly turned management education upside down. Why was the orthodoxy of management education slow to change? It takes time to comprehend the import of the socio-technical transformation. Chandler and Williamson wrote about the M-Form organization in the 1960s and 1970s; but few recognized it for what it was in the early part of the century when it began. Similarly, it has taken time for the information revolution to find its place in management education. This is not because management scholars have ignored the effects of the digital revolution: in 1958, Harold Leavitt and Thomas Whisler challenged the management field to think about the long-term implications of IT use in organizations with a paper titled ‘Management in the 1980s.’ But by the 1980s their vision proved no more prescient than did George Orwell’s 1949 novel 1984. Were they wrong, or merely ahead of their time? Similarly, Michael Hammer’s 1990 Harvard Business Review article, ‘Reengineering Work: Don’t Automate, Obliterate,’ now seems rather quaint. It launched the business process reengineering (BPR) movement, and few talk seriously about BPR now because it was a fad that came and went. Yet, BPR grew directly from a well-known information systems fact: sometimes you gain as much or more from doing ‘systems analysis’ as you do from building new systems because you discover that a lot of what you are doing does not make much sense.

BPR was a step in the evolution of methods for looking at enterprise ‘end-to-end,’ and helped to legitimate the concept of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP). ERP systems have been implemented by many organizations, with consequences that are still not well understood. One can look at the ERP movement as an effort to capture the elusive goal of successful central planning that bedeviled communist societies, as Hayek (1944) noted. It is startling to imagine that contemporary information technology might make possible an objective sought by enemies of capitalism throughout the 20th century. It could happen; large and complicated global supply chains suggest this. The information age will not necessarily be much different for enterprise than the industrial age, but the magnitude of the change that might happen is significant, and with it the potential to transform dramatically the practice of enterprise in ways different from those implied by the managerial orthodoxy of 20th century industrialization.

Managerial orthodoxy is ‘sticky’ because of the incumbent power of management education and its compliments in the employment structures of contemporary business organizations and consulting firms. These are all in it together, and their interests are not served by major change. Yet it is hard to imagine that they can avoid significant change given that industrial production that underlies the management orthodoxy on which they depend now accounts for less than one-eighth of GDP and even fewer jobs. Indeed, more than 80% of all jobs are in the service sector (US Census, 2007). Yet the old industrial paradigm is imposed on a reality that is rapidly changing away from its industrial origins.<sup>16</sup>

This takes the discussion back to the C-level executive. The word ‘chief ’ implies leadership, and management schools claim to prepare people for leadership. But what kind of leaders are they preparing? As discussed below, the link between ‘leadership’ and ‘chief ’ is increasingly misleading as the information age progresses. The US alone is producing more than 300,000 BBA graduates and more than 150,000 MBA graduates, for nearly half a million professional business graduates entering the market per year. It seems unreasonable to expect that all of them will be ‘chiefs’ at some point, yet it is possible that all of them can be leaders. Perhaps it is time to sunder the tight link between ‘leader’ and ‘chief.’ To do this, we must confront the dominance of structure, and in particular a certain notion of structure, in our thinking about human enterprise.

Robert Eccles (1990) of Harvard Business School once observed that managers pay attention to structure – decision rights and resource allocation – because it is one of the few things they can control. Managerial orthodoxy makes structure one of the most important factors in human enterprise. To be fair, structure is ancient; the Biblical book of Exodus, which is at least 4500 years old, provides a detailed account of Moses implementing a hierarchical structure for decision making (Exodus 18). An extraordinary amount of human capital has been invested in designing and refining structure, and there is little question of its historical importance in building the world we now have. But will organizational structure remain as central to human enterprise as it has been in a new era of ubiquitous information and communication? Even modest change on the margin in the role of organizational structure in human enterprise could precipitate a dramatic upheaval in the way human enterprise evolves. And there are tantalizing indications that such a change is underway.

One of the most interesting puzzles of the late 20th century was the so-called productivity paradox, summarized well in economist Robert Solow’s famous quip, ‘You can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics’ (Solow, 1987). This referred to the fact that, despite extraordinary investments in information technology during the period 1970–1985, the traditional measures of productivity did not register significant gains. A variety of responses to Solow’s observation were provided, ranging from critiques of measurement and methods (cf., Brynjolfsson, 1993) to admonitions that such changes take longer than people imagine (cf., David, 1990).<sup>17</sup> Both observations proved correct. The measures were problematic; many had been developed in mid-20th century to explain the Great Depression, well before the era of computerization began. And, as noted above, the effects of change take a while to become clear. The hoped-for productivity effects of information technology arrived in the late 1990s and early 2000s, but they came with some surprises (Stiroh, 2002; Jorgenson et al., 2005, 2010).

Initial and short-term productivity effects from IT were due mainly to the rapidly decreasing cost of IT products and services (i.e., decreasing factor prices), but long-term productivity effects appear tied closely to changes in organizational structure, especially the ability to decentralize. Organizational structure remains important, but a trend toward organizational decentralization is ontologically at odds with the notion of the ‘chief ’ as usually articulated in the C-level discussion. The chief concept is inherently centralizing, putting much authority for an organization’s functional activity in the hands of one individual. It is possible to use the term ‘chief ’ to refer to intermediate-level leaders in organizations, consonant with decentralization, but this is not the pattern one sees when examining the growth of the C-level executive.

In addition, the nature of productivity improvement from information technology is different than that seen in historical capital-labor substitution, as first seen in the automation of the public-switched telephone network in the 20th century (King, 2011). Agricultural mechanization and the industrial revolution resulted in a much smaller number of people producing goods and services enjoyed by many, but automation of communications increased the ‘workforce’ by making the end-users the operators of the infrastructure. Direct dialing of telephones replaced telephone company operators with customer operators, and the era of networked computers made many end-users responsible for their own configuration management – an idea that was unthinkable in the mainframe era. The most striking aspect of such changes is that the distinction between ‘producer’ and ‘consumer’ might be blurring.

The IT revolution in retailing illustrates this. Both the ‘big-box’ store with its IT-enabled supply chains and so-called e-tailing that takes place online are dependent on disintermediation, tightening the connection between the original supplier and the ultimate customer. Advertising is being revolutionized by Internet search that ties ads directly to what individuals are seeking. The rise of online news is causally related to a decline in traditional news sources, again dis-intermediating the value chain and, in many cases, making the consumer into a producer via comments on blogs, Twitter feeds and so on. Entertainment has moved steadily from bricks to clicks, and the business models of entire industries such as recorded music, television and motion pictures are changing. The online game industry, which did not even exist before the 1990s, might now generate global revenues in excess of motion pictures (Maxim, 2004). These changes are over and above the revolutions already seen in manufacturing, finance, organizational administration, and so on underway since the middle of the 20th century. The full effect of social networking technologies such as Facebook remains to be seen, but their rate of growth is phenomenal with respect to both uses and users. Managerial orthodoxy tells us very little about these remarkable changes; in many cases, it is difficult to find any connection between that orthodoxy and the most interesting aspects of these new phenomena.

As organizations like General Motors went bankrupt in the early years of the 21st century, young IT-industry entrepreneurs were amassing vast fortunes for coming up with ‘outside the box’ services. Many of the most visible among these entrepreneurs lack professional management education, and some dropped out of college altogether. When they hold a ‘chief ’ title, the title seems stuck on them like an out-of-fashion suit. These new leaders of enterprise are not ‘role-holders’ in the model of mid-20th century organizational sociology; they are closer to Schumpeter’s social leaders whose most salient acts are to overthrow rather than reinforce the existing order. Yet they are not early 20th century entrepreneurs, either. They stand in opposition to the managerial orthodoxy of the 20th century, and sooner or later, management education must acknowledge this. Information technology is changing management practice, and the academic information systems community should be moving away from the concept of the CIO.

## Breaking away

CIO does not stand for ‘Career Is Over.’ Many people will hold the CIO title in the years ahead, and the IS field will continue to prepare some people to take this path. But the first signs are appearing that the ‘Concept Is Over,’ where the concept is the very idea of the C-level executive. In that case, a different future for the IS field presents itself. The C-level executive is a management fad, and such fads come and go. The more important issue is how human enterprise works, how it is changing, how it will change in the information age, and what professional managers should be taught as a result of this new reality.

The affinity between transformative and transgressive is worth pondering. If information technology is transformative, good IS research will be transgressive. Much research uses the strategy of constraint optimization, finding the answer within a set of clearly articulated constraints. (The ‘control’ in an experiment is an imposed constraint) The ‘long now’ relaxes the important constraint of time. Leading IS journals address this by narrowly constructing constraints, typically with regard to rigor and relevance. Most leading journals (IS and otherwise) favor rigor, further narrowing the aperture for relevance. The ‘long now’ releases constraints, making good research difficult, but IS research must move away from a focus on the immediate, and include the perspective of the ‘long now (Brand, 1999).<sup>18</sup>

Research that fails to fit managerial orthodoxy is likely to be deemed weak. This is partly due to the relaxing of the constraints of disciplinarity, meaning the established conventions of given academic disciplines (Klein, 1990). There is considerable criticism of disciplinarity, but in its defense it often carries effective and relatively low-cost mechanisms for maintaining intellectual discipline. Fields that have cultivated deep disciplinarity do work of good intellectual quality; the risk they run is having their work reduced to irrelevance if they fail to account for the changes taking place in the world. Throwing off disciplinarity usually requires investing more in intellectual discipline to make up for what is lost. This is hard enough for individual researchers to do; it is much more difficult for individual researchers to persuade their colleagues to do the same in order to judge new work fairly.

The rigor/relevance distinction sounds like a reasonable balancing act, but it only works when things are stable.

Researchers can then take comfort in what is considered rigorous and relevant right now, which almost always means back then. Understanding is mostly backward looking for the simple reason that there are no data about the future. Learning how things did happen is often a good beginning to figuring out how things will happen, and academics are wise to avoid the slippery slope of ‘big change.’ The academy’s inherent conservatism is intended, in part, to guard against sliding down that slope. Unfortunately, for the IS field it is not possible to be safe. The choice is how best to live dangerously.<sup>19</sup>

The IS field ought to be driven, not by how things are, but how they might be. This is a speculative venture, and there is endless speculation in the research literature and the trade literature of management. Much of that speculation is undisciplined, and thus the focus of IS researchers should be on explicating mechanisms such as the specific causal steps by which speculative notions can become real, as seen in Yates’ explication of the way in which communication technologies such as the telephone became central to the operation of the Dupont Company when a progressive generation of leaders took over (Yates, 1989). With such mechanisms in place, it becomes possible to track the changes to determine whether those mechanisms are working as predicted. In addition, it is wise to avoid speculation about dramatic changes that simply ‘will materialize’ over the long run, and concentrate instead on slow, accretionary changes that yield significant effects over time. Such effects might be gradual and linear, but they often are sudden and nonlinear. This is well illustrated by a metaphor.

Liquid water ‘builds up’ to a boil or ‘builds down’ to freezing, but when it boils or freezes the effects are immediate and obvious. The psychologist Kurt Lewin provided a phase change metaphor to describe organizational change: unfreezing, transition, refreezing (Lewin, 1947). The IS field is caught in a phase change. Things are unfreezing and heading into transition. The field is pulled in multiple directions. On the one hand, research cannot be in constant flux: scholarly endeavor requires methodology, discipline, valuation, and conservation. Research requires time to develop, to test, to establish as legitimate, and to impart to emerging generations of scholars. On the other hand, times change, and research must change with the times or it becomes irrelevant. The IS field lives in an awkward straddle between the pursuit of the new and reconciliation with the old. This is not an easy matter to resolve. Most scholars prefer stability because it makes things easy. Orthodoxy helps to ensure stability. It is reinforced by older scholars who hold some kind of tenure. Max Planck had it right when he said science advances funeral by funeral.<sup>20</sup>

The IS field should separate its long-run interests from the fortunes of the CIO, partly because CIO is a fad that cannot last, and more importantly because the managerial orthodoxy underlying the notion of ‘chief ’ is faltering. The idea that the information systems field can find its future in the managerial orthodoxy of 20th century industrial administration is folly because that orthodoxy is fracturing. Over the next decade, the transformative and transgressive effects of the information revolution will become more visible as human enterprise changes. In the short run,

136

there might be benefit in research focused on modest changes (e.g., making enterprise more efficient), but the long run will be governed by a larger and more disruptive reality.

Information systems are already changing human enterprise. The IS field should be grounded in such change, and consider how best to prepare leaders for the emerging enterprise of the 21st century. It might be comforting to know that CIO does not stand for ‘career is over,’ but it is disconcerting to think that the orthodox understanding of human enterprise and management education is changing. The IS field itself is risky business, but that is nothing new. As Marx (1852) said, people make their history, but they do not make it exactly as they please.
