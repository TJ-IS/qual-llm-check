# A DAILY FIELD INVESTIGATION OF TECHNOLOGY-DRIVEN SPILLOVERS FROM WORK TO HOME<sup>1</sup>

Alexander Benlian

Department of Business, Law and Economics, Technische Universität Darmstadt, Hochschulstr. 1, 64289 Darmstadt GERMANY {benlian@ise.tu-darmstadt.de}

Although recent theoretical developments and empirical studies indicate that technology-related stress may have negative and positive consequences for employees across life domains, the majority of previous IS research on technostress has focused on its downsides at work and has neglected to study how and why technology-related stress may spill over from work to home. Furthermore, while much of our knowledge of technology-related stress and its effects derives from cross-sectional studies examining between-person differences, there is a need for longitudinal, daily investigations that take a within-person view. Integrating the challenge–hindrance stressor framework with affective events theory and work–home spillover literature, we propose a broader conceptualization of technology-related stressors, referred to as technology-driven (TD) stressors, which comprise technology-driven challenge (TCS) and hindrance (THS) stressors, and examine how and why daily TCS and THS experienced at work affect the relationship between employees and their partners at home. In an experience sampling study of 115 employees who responded to daily surveys both at work and at home over a two-week period, we found that while THS are negatively related to partnership satisfaction via negative affect, TCS are positively related to partnership satisfaction via positive affect. We also investigated the moderating effect of work–home role integration (WHI) and perceived organizational support in work–home boundary management (POS) on the strength of the within-individual spillover processes. Our results show that WHI acts as a double-edged sword for letting TCS- and THS-triggered positive and negative affect spill over to partnership satisfaction, whereas POS serves as a facilitator of positive affect and as a buffer against negative affect. Broadly, our study shows that understanding daily TD work stressors is important because their negative and positive downstream effects often do not stop at employees’ workplace boundaries but actually penetrate and shape their everyday lives at home.

Keywords: Technology-driven stress, challenge–hindrance stressors, technostress, spillover, work–home boundary, affective events theory, experience sampling, multilevel analysis

## Introduction

It is 9:30 a.m. in the office. Hannah waits for an email from a colleague with updated financial data to be included in a report she has to prepare by 10:00 a.m. for an important status update meeting. Unfortunately, her email program erroneously filters the email as spam, producing severe time delays. She almost messes up the meeting and feels annoyed and stressed out. Over the course of the day, Hannah cannot let go of these negative feelings that linger on. As she returns home at 6:45 p.m., she is grouchy and takes her anger out on her husband Marc, who becomes frustrated.

The next morning at work, Hannah feels thrilled as she discovers, in a weekly tech newsletter, a new Excel add-in that helps her save time in completing an urgent project task that is due at 2:00 p.m. that afternoon. Exhilarated by this learning experience and filled with positive energy, Hannah returns home in the evening and quickly infects Marc with her good mood. Both are feeling happy.<sup>2</sup>

Modern information and communications technologies (ICT)<sup>3</sup> permeate almost every aspect of our daily work and often put us in challenging situations that create stress and mixed feelings through system errors, steep learning curves, and fluctuating workloads. Work stress induced or significantly shaped by ICT is a highly prevalent phenomenon in our workplaces, with often problematic effects not only for employees’ productivity but also for their relationships at home (Butts et al. 2015; Tams et al. 2018). Stressed ICT workers tend to be fatigued, prone to mistakes and injuries, and more likely to be absent, resulting in healthcare costs that are twice as high as those of other employees (Ahuja et al. 2007; APA 2018; Stich et al. 2019). Compounding this problem, technology-related work stress often bleeds over into employees’ home domains with the potential to drive a wedge into romantic relationships (Falconier et al. 2015). Collectively, the consequences of work stress, from depression and divorce to heart disease, cost the U.S. economy an estimated \$200 to \$300 billion a year in lost productivity (Alicea 2016). Clearly, if organizations do not intervene with preventive measures and by giving employees adequate coping strategies, the penalty for rampant technology-related work stress could be tremendous.

As our opening vignette illustrates, however, treating technology-related stress as uniformly bad may be an oversimplification because this type of stress often comes in many shapes and fluctuates from one day to the next. On one day, technology-related stress may be experienced as a hindrance to goal achievement; on another day, it may be generated by a challenge that has been tackled or overcome. Furthermore, as alluded to above, the feelings created through such stress often do not stop at the boundaries of the workplace but instead linger on in employees and are carried over to their homes, where they affect relationships with partners. The seemingly small technology-driven spillovers of such feelings may, therefore, have serious and consequential impacts on employees’ partnership satisfaction.

Despite the pervasiveness and complex nature of technologyrelated stress, previous IS research has mainly focused its investigations on technostress, characterized as a modern disease caused by individuals’ inability to deal with ICT in a healthy manner (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008), by examining how and why technostressors affect work outcomes and how their adverse effects can be mitigated in organizations (e.g., Tarafdar, Pullins, and Ragu-Nathan 2015; Tarafdar et al. 2007). Although these insights are valuable because they shed light on how technostress affects our working lives and how we can cope with the dark side of ICT, our understanding of technology-related work stress and its cross-domain effects is still far from being complete (D’Arcy, Gupta et al. 2014; Tarafdar et al. 2019). From our review of the technostress literature (see Table A1 in Appendix A), we highlight three salient issues that have remained largely unexplored.

First, the vast majority of prior technostress research has painted a rather dire picture of technology-related stressors by suggesting that they are overwhelmingly harmful, even though IS scholars have pointed to the need to examine the largely overlooked positive facet of technostress (Tarafdar et al. 2019). Recent empirical and meta-analytic studies on work stress (e.g., Cavanaugh et al. 2000; Crawford et al. 2010) have also consistently supported a two-dimensional framework positing that work stressors that people tend to appraise as potentially hindering their personal development and accomplishments (i.e., hindrance stressors) should be distinguished from stressors that people tend to appraise as challenges and opportunities that can promote their personal growth and achievements (i.e., challenge stressors). This framework is called for mainly because these two types of stressors have divergent effects on various affective and behavioral responses (LePine et al. 2005). Given that ICT are often an essential ingredient of and critical contributor to such negatively and positively appraised work stressors in today’s increasingly digitized work environments, the question arises whether a significant piece of a broader conceptualization of technology-related stress has so far been omitted.

Second, previous organizational research consistently shows that stress experienced in the workplace can spill over to the home domain and may even cross over to employees’ partners. For example, recent studies have reported that stress at work can reduce time spent with the spouse at home (Ilies et al. 2007). Although IS scholars have started to look at technological antecedents of work–life conflict (i.e., the perceived conflict between the demands of work and other life domains) and its implications for individual work and nonwork outcomes (e.g., Ayyagari et al. 2011; Yun et al. 2012), little to no research has examined precisely whether and how technologyrelated stress spills over from work to affect relationships with partners at home and which factors may influence these spillovers. An examination of the far-reaching and pervasive effects of technology-related stress may, however, be important to help firms support work–life balance programs that address stress management more holistically, thus allowing firms to retain top talent in the long term (Sarker et al. 2018).

Third, despite the advances of recent research, much of our knowledge of technology-related stress derives from crosssectional, retrospective studies examining between-person differences. However, technology-related stress is manifested day-to-day and is often the product of discrete events encountered in the work environment that shape employees’ momentary affect in situ. Although IS scholars have called for research into the potential episodic nature of ICT-based work stressors (Ayyagari et al. 2011), few studies have responded to this call (e.g., Galluch et al. 2015; Tams et al. 2018), and they have left important questions unanswered. Thus, there is a need for longitudinal, daily investigations of technologyrelated stress that take a dynamic, within-person view of how such stress and its short-term affective effects unfold over time.

Motivated by these research gaps and integrating the challenge–hindrance stressor model with affective events theory and work–home spillover literature, our study proposes a broader conceptualization of technology-related stressors, referred to as technology-driven (TD) stressors, which in turn combine technology-driven challenge (TCS) and hindrance stressors (THS). While TCS are TD stressors that present opportunities for individual learning and personal growth, THS refer to TD stressors that hinder progress toward personal goal attainment and accomplishments. Based on this broader conceptualization, we investigate the daily affective effects of TCS and THS that potentially spill over from work to home to affect employees’ partnership satisfaction. As a global subjective evaluation of the quality of one’s romantic relationship and a reflection of partnership happiness, partnership satisfaction represents a crucial outcome variable in the home context that is particularly suited to capturing daily affective experiences transcending the work–home boundary (Ilies et al. 2009). Moreover, it is one of the most important determinants of emotional instability and relationship dissolutions of couples, all of which may have serious downstream ramifications for employees’ work productivity and long-term well-being (Margelisch et al. 2017). Because the spillover of TD stressors critically depends on individual and organizational factors, our research model also accounts for two essential moderators that are not only intricately linked to work–home boundary permeability but also provide complementary insights. Specifically, we examine the roles of work– home role integration (i.e., employees’ trait-like preferences for separating or blending work and home life) and perceived organizational support in work–home boundary management (i.e., employees’ perceptions of organizational climates and practices that support employees’ individual boundary management) in shaping the cross-domain spillover process.

We tested our research model with a total of 115 employees participating in an experience-sampling study in which they were asked to respond to daily surveys both at work and at home over a period of two consecutive work weeks. The results supported our model advancing technostress research in three important ways. First, while previous technostress research has portrayed technology-based stressors as unequivocally detrimental, we draw a more complete and balanced picture by showing that such stressors are not all created equal but can have detrimental (THS) and beneficial (TCS) effects for employees. Second, we shift the literature’s predominant focus on the effects of technology-related stress from a withindomain to a cross-domain perspective. Whereas prior technostress research has largely examined the effects of technostressors on attitudinal and behavioral outcomes confined to single, isolated domains, we examine more far-reaching consequences of TD stress that trigger affect-based spillovers from one domain (i.e., work) to affect relational outcomes in another domain (i.e., home). Finally, the within-person approach that we take departs from previous technostress research that is rooted in a relatively static conceptualization of technostress. Our approach not only allows us to zoom into the dynamic and fluctuating nature and effects of TD stress unfolding within persons over time but also responds to previous calls for increased attention to the short-term nature of technology-related stress (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Galluch et al. 2015). Overall, our model answers both why and under what conditions the beneficial or detrimental effects of TD stress created at work spill over to affect employees’ partnership satisfaction experienced at home.

## Theoretical Background

In this section, we first develop a broader conceptualization of TD stressors—contextualized from the challenge– hindrance stressor framework of the work stress literature— and delineate them from the classical technostress creators investigated in previous IS literature. We continue by laying the conceptual foundation of work–home spillover and drawing on affective events theory as the theoretical glue binding TD stressors and work–home spillover together via an affect-based mechanism. These three theoretical perspectives form the backdrop for an overarching conceptual model that sets the stage for theorizing the research model of this study.

## Technology-Driven Challenge and Hindrance Work Stressors

Early IS research investigating the phenomenon of stress has focused on the effects of general workplace stressors (e.g., role conflict, work overload) on ICT professionals and how they deal with these stressors in organizational settings (Ahuja et al. 2007; Moore 2000). With the proliferation and intensification of ICT use in our daily work tasks, IS research has more recently shifted its focus to technostress, a concept defined as stress experienced by end users in firms as a result of their use of and greater dependence on ICT (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2007). The IS literature has widely recognized that technostress is an increasingly ubiquitous phenomenon at our workplaces with often problematic and unintended consequences for employees and their organizations (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Tarafdar, D’Arcy et al. 2015). For example, previous studies have found that employees experiencing technostress have lower productivity and job satisfaction and decreased commitment to their organizations, leading to a litany of health and quality-of-life problems (Tarafdar, Pullins, and Ragu-Nathan 2015). Drawing from the transaction-based model of stress (Lazarus and Folkman 1987), Ragu-Nathan et al. (2008) have provided a useful and widely adopted conceptualization for understanding technostress creators (or simply technostressors), which refer to conditions or stimuli that directly induce stress in organizations. This conceptualization of stressors distinguishes among different factors ranging from techno-overload to techno-uncertainty. Collectively, based on literature that builds on a rather pessimistic perspective on technologyrelated stress (e.g., Brod 1984; Weil and Rosen 1997), these classical technostressors overwhelmingly focus on the harmful effects of technology-related stress, neglecting the possibility that work stress induced or significantly shaped by ICT may also have beneficial effects on employees and their organizations.

Indeed, prior research in the broader work stress literature has early recognized that demand conditions that are perceived to exceed employees’ abilities can have positive consequences if handled properly (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). According to Selye (1978), people often express enjoyment and even euphoria when engaging in challenging tasks. For example, being assigned to a demanding new project may create positive forms of stress and tension (so-called eustress) within employees by encouraging them to reach higher than they previously assumed they could and to accomplish more daunting tasks. Organizational research has repeatedly supported that challenging work stressors are positively related to motivation (e.g., effort), attitudes (e.g., job satisfaction) and work performance, suggesting that employees are willing to exert extra effort in the presence of such stressors to meet challenges and attain the associated benefits (Podsakoff et al. 2007). As a result, previous literature has made it clear that work stress cannot be fully understood within purely negative parameters of human experience (Nelson and Cooper 2007). Accounting for the dual nature of work stressors and their differential relationships with outcomes in organizations, Cavanaugh et al. (2000) developed the challenge–hindrance stressor model, which draws on Lazarus’s (1991b) appraisal theory of emotion. According to this theory, work stressors are prime examples of affective stimuli that provide information about progress or hindrance with regard to some valued outcomes. In this regard, people appraise stressful situations as either potentially threatening or potentially promoting mastery, personal growth, or future gains (Lazarus and Folkman 1984). In their model, Cavanaugh et al. accordingly distinguish between two broad categories of workplace stressors. Challenge stressors are work demands that create opportunities for personal growth and development. The main work demands that have been identified include workload, time urgency, learning opportunities, and job or task complexity (LePine et al. 2004; Prem et al. 2017). Employees tend to perceive these demands as opportunities to learn, achieve, and demonstrate the type of competence that tends to get rewarded. In contrast, hindrance stressors refer to work demands that do not present the potential for personal growth and rewards and may actually be perceived as barriers that thwart progress toward goal attainment. The prime work demands that have been identified include hassles or obstacles, resource constraints or inadequacies, role or task ambiguity, and role or task conflict (LePine et al. 2016; Webster et al. 2011).

Although the challenge–hindrance stressor framework has found broad support in meta-analyses and recent empirical studies across scholarly disciplines (e.g., Crawford et al. 2010; LePine et al. 2016), previous IS research has been largely silent about the idea that technology-related stressors may also be distinguished between challenge and hindrance stressors (Tarafdar et al. 2019). This silence is surprising given that previous research pointed to the need to translate general work stressors into more specific, technology-based stressors (e.g., Addas and Pinsonneault 2018a; Chong et al. 2011; LePine et al. 2004), as ICT have unique characteristics, such as pace of change, connectivity, synchronicity, or anonymity (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Matook and Brown 2017), that set technology-related work stressors largely apart from the non-ICT work stressors typically examined in organizational research. A simple extrapolation from non-ICT work stressors would thus neglect these distinct ICT features in the stress process and, in this way, the importance of studying ICT-specific organizational interventions. In addition, while previous research has largely focused on generic work stressors based on pure human agency (e.g., supervisors, coworkers), we believe that technology-based work stressors deserve special treatment and closer investigation because of the sociotechnical nature of ICT that essentially and distinctly shapes the frequency, valence, and intensity of the stress experienced at work. Overall, this underscores that TD stressors are likely not reducible to generic non-ICT stressors.

Responding to the above and broader calls for contextualizing general theories in IS research (Hong et al. 2014), we apply the challenge–hindrance stressor framework to identify context-specific TD work stressors and use this lens to distinguish between two broad types of stressors, which we refer to as technology-driven challenge stressors (TCS) and hindrance stressors (THS), to emphasize our focus on the dual nature of technology-related stress. Along the lines of this broader conceptualization, we define TCS as TD work stressors that present the potential for individual learning opportunities and personal growth, while THS refer to TD work stressors that are inhibitory to individual learning and personal growth. Our conceptualization of TD stressors adapts the general types of work demands being grouped into the broader categories of challenge and hindrance stressors by relating them to the essential and unique roles that ICT play in TD stressors. Based on this conceptualization, THS refer to work demands in which ICT create “bad” stress by posing an obstacle to task fulfillment, being a resource constraint, eliciting role or task ambiguity, and producing role or task conflicts that all may thwart individual learning or personal growth. In contrast, TCS capture work demands in which ICT promote “good” stress by helping employees handle high workloads, meet challenging deadlines, improve their ICT knowledge and digital competencies, or solve complex problems, which all present opportunities for individual learning and personal growth. As mentioned earlier, previous IS research has primarily looked at how work stressors in general, and technostress creators in particular, affect employees in the work domain, but it has largely neglected the fact that those stressors can have more far-reaching, pervasive effects that reach beyond employees’ workplaces to permeate their home life outside of work hours—a phenomenon called work–home spillover, to which we turn next.

## Work–Home Spillover of Technology-Driven Stress

According to the work–home spillover literature (Lambert 1990), spillover is an intraindividual contagion process that occurs when salient experiences at work affect emotions, attitudes, or behaviors within the same individual at home. Spillover can be either positive or negative. When spillover refers to negative processes, the literature commonly refers to it as negative work–home spillover, while positive processes are commonly described as positive work–home spillover (Greenhaus and Powell 2006). Edwards and Rothbard (2000) distinguish among affect, values, skills, and behavior as spillover mechanisms. The basic logic underlying these mechanisms is a similarity relationship between these two life domains, and spillover occurs when there is a positive association between a work and a home construct. That is, skills, values and behavior acquired through work induce similar skills, values and behavior at home by affecting an individual’s knowledge structure, value system, and habitual behavioral styles. However, the process for affective spillover is more complicated. As per Greenhaus and Powell (2006), positive affect created at work enhances social functioning and performance within the home domain, which then translates into more positive affect, attitudes, and relationships with family members. This process occurs in the opposite way with negative affect (Ilies et al. 2009).

Previous work–home spillover research has shown that spillover processes can be triggered by a range of stressful events at the workplace, including job demands and supervisor- or coworker-induced stressors such as abusive supervision, workplace incivility, ostracism, or bullying (e.g., Bolger et al. 1989; Rosen et al. 2016). Because spillover theory suggests that stressful demands experienced at work may be carried over to the home domain and thus cross physical and temporal boundaries, a key focus in spillover research has also been to identify individual and organizational moderators (e.g., personality traits or organizational support) that affect boundary permeability to better understand when and why the emotions and behaviors of one sphere transcend the boundary into the other (ten Brummelhuis and Bakker 2012). While the broader work stress literature has made substantial progress in examining the effects of non-TD stress spillovers and how they may be shaped, it comes as a surprise that TD stress spillover has received only scant research attention so far. Only a few studies in organizational research have examined the effects of ICT use at the work– home interface (e.g., Butts et al. 2015; Harris et al. 2011), and even fewer studies have explicitly looked at TD stress spillover from work to home.

To the best of our knowledge, and based on our review of technostress research in the IS literature (see Appendix A), we identified only three studies that investigated technologyrelated stress at the work–life boundary. While Ayyagari et al. (2011) examined the effects of intrusive technology characteristics on work–home conflict and strain, Yun et al. (2012) focused on office-home smartphone use to examine its effects on work-to-life conflict and job stress. Moreover, Chen and Karahanna (2018) specifically examined how afterhours work-related technology interruptions affect exhaustion and performance in work and nonwork domains. Although all three studies have provided important insights into the role of

ICT in blurring the line between work and other life domains, our theoretical understanding is still limited in that prior research has not yet clearly delineated the cross-domain spillover mechanisms that may explain why technology-related work stressors affect home outcomes and how this spillover is potentially shaped by work–home boundary moderators. In addition, most prior technostress research has used crosssectional studies to examine between-person differences in chronic technostress perceptions far removed from immediate stress reactions. However, as illustrated in our opening vignette and evidenced in previous research, technology-related stress is a transient and erratic phenomenon that affects employees’ work experiences on a daily basis and thus may also trigger short-term work–home spillovers that have more immediate effects on employees’ emotions, attitudes, and even relationships at home (Ayyagari et al. 2011). As such, it is essential for a more fine-grained portrayal of TD stress to examine stressors at a level close to the manner in which the immediate experiences unfold naturally—that is, at a withinperson level that allows capturing the daily fluctuations of TD stressors and their dynamic nature (Hamaker 2013).

Integrating the challenge–hindrance stressor model that helps identify context-specific TD stressors with work–home spillover literature that sheds light on the cross-domain spillover process, Figure 1 presents an overarching framework that serves as a conceptual background to our research study. This framework generally makes sense of how TD stressors may affect home outcomes (e.g., attitudes, behaviors, or relationships) via different spillover mechanisms (e.g., affect, values, skills, or behavior) and how the spillover process may be influenced by work–home boundary moderators (e.g., individual differences or organizational factors) and additional influencing factors at work and at home (e.g., non-TD work stressors, family member effects).

## Affective Events Theory as Foundation for Work–Home Affective Stress Spillover

In this study, we draw on affective events theory (AET; Weiss and Cropanzano 1996) to theorize the effects of daily TD stressors on partnership satisfaction via an affective spillover mechanism (Edwards and Rothbard 2000). AET explains the role of affect in the relationship between individuals’ work experiences and their attitudes and behaviors. At the heart of AET is the premise that various events at work, including work stressors, have immediate affective consequences; in other words, they generate emotional reactions in employees affective states. In turn, affective states are linked to the formation of attitudes and behaviors. AET clearly defines workplace events as significant occurrences that precipitate change in one’s circumstances. Individuals usually experience generalized emotional states, yet daily uplifts (positive events) and hassles (negative events) punctuate and are proximal causes of their emotional states over time. According to AET, positive events are viewed as goal congruent to one’s personal growth and are differentiated from negative events, which are viewed as incongruent with one’s goals and personal strivings. Goal congruence, which refers to the degree of benefit or harm an individual perceives in an event, pertains to whether the encounter brings one closer to one’s goals or further away from them. Experiences appraised as bringing one closer to one’s goals invoke positive emotions or pleasant feelings, while experiences that pull one further from one’s goals invoke negative emotions or unpleasant feelings (Oreg et al. 2018).

AET provides us with an appropriate theoretical lens to comprehend the affective effects of daily TD work stressors at the work–home interface for three main reasons. First, because workplace stressors are one of the most significant incidents at work (Ohly and Schmitt 2015) and are “prime examples of affective events that may generate emotional responses” (Rodell and Judge 2009, p. 1439), AET has been repeatedly invoked in previous research to theorize the affective effects of work stressors in general and of challenge and hindrance stressors in particular (e.g., Zhang et al. 2018), and it has also been extended across the work-nonwork interface (e.g., Butts et al. 2015). Second, by explicitly distinguishing between positive and negative workplace events, AET captures the nuances of employees’ affective reactions and thus closely maps onto the TCS-THS distinction advanced in this study. Finally, the AET perspective provides a conceptual focus that not only explicitly recognizes the short-term, within-person fluctuations in employees’ affect as a function of daily workplace demands (Weiss and Beal 2005) but also accounts for subsequent affective work–home spillover effects (Carlson et al. 2011). Overall, AET serves as useful theoretical glue that ties together different types of TD stressors and their work– home spillover effects via an affective spillover mechanism.

## Model Development

Building upon the overarching framework in Figure 1 and drawing on an affective spillover mechanism, we develop a research model that offers logic and evidence to suggest that positive and negative affect carry over the effects of daily TCS and THS experienced at work to partnership satisfaction at home (see Figure 2 for a preview).

Because the spillover process from work to home does not occur in a vacuum, moderating factors may affect the permeability of the work–home boundary and thus amplify or attenuate the spillover process. According to role boundary theory (Ashforth et al. 2000), employees create and maintain boundaries around the work and home domains as a way of simplifying and ordering their environment. Kossek et al. (2005) found that the way employees manage work–home roles and the boundary between the two domains is primarily influenced by personal characteristics and by workplace climates that are more or less attuned to employees’ preferences. In light of these findings, we suggest that work–home role integration (WHI) and perceived organizational support in individual work–home boundarymanagement (POS) (Hammer et al. 2011; Ilies et al. 2009) may deserve closer examination as important moderating factors for two main reasons. First, given that boundary permeability is a core theoretical link between stress-induced affect at work and partnership satisfaction at home (Clark 2000), we argue that WHI and POS, by speaking directly to boundary permeability, strike at the heart of and considerably shape the work–home spillover process. Second, WHI and POS also provide distinct yet complementary insights: While WHI refers to employees’ own, trait-like preferences for separating or blending work and home life, POS refers to employees’ perceptions of organizational climates and practices that may vary in how they support employees in (pro-)actively shaping their individual work–home boundary. ## Technology-Driven Challenge and Hindrance Stressors as Affective Events

Following previous IS research (Galluch et al. 2015; Tams et al. 2018), we propose that TD stressors are significant instances of work events that trigger positive or negative emotions. Delineating between TCS and THS, we first argue that TCS are experienced as positive work events that may spur positive affect. Our rationale is that TCS provide opportunities for personal growth and achievements because the characteristics of ICT inherent in TCS present the options for enhancing individual skills and tasks that are likely to promote the experience of personal accomplishment. For example, referring back to our opening vignette, when Hannah masters a new Excel add-in that helps her save time in completing an urgent project task, she accomplishes her work more effectively and improves her personal productivity because of the unique capabilities of ICT. In a similar vein, when employees push themselves to learn new ICT skills or increase their virtual competences (e.g., via online courses or a knowledge management system) that help them progress toward their goals and fulfill career ambitions (e.g., a certificate, salary raise, or promotion), the acquired knowledge and skills are likely to provide them with a sense of personal achievement and thriving at work (Wan et al. 2012; Wang and Haggerty 2011). All of these accomplishments experienced in challenging work events, uniquely enabled or supported through ICT characteristics such as flexibility, automation capacity, or (self-) adaptation (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Matook and Brown 2017; Tarafdar et al. 2019), should elicit positive affect in employees.

Prior work stress research suggests that challenge stressors, such as taking on additional work or completing work under time pressure, can foster the experience of pleasurable emotions because employees tend to view opportunities for growth or personal mastery as meaningful and desirable (Zhang et al. 2014). Experience-sampling studies in particular could support the link between daily positive work events and positive mood (Miner et al. 2005). Extending this logic to TD stressors and in line with AET, we expect TCS to evoke positive affect in a distinct manner as well. When employees use the often-indispensable features of ICT as crucial workplace tools to handle heavy workloads and demanding time schedules, they may not only appraise such experiences as challenging and thrilling but also appreciate the ICT-driven progress made in their goal pursuit. In a similar vein, ICT use can help employees solve complex (e.g., multitasking, computationally intensive) problems in unique ways or help them make connections between seemingly unrelated pieces of information to form new means–ends relationships that result in the generation of novel ideas and solutions (Aral et al. 2012; Müller-Wienbergen et al. 2011). In this way, TCS create positive affect because ICT use can enthuse and encourage employees by increasing their self-efficacy and their ability to tackle and master the challenging work tasks they encounter on a daily basis (Burton-Jones and Grange 2013). The constructive and affirmative experience of using ICT capabilities in high-stakes work events, when deadlines or performance evaluations are on the line, and where the leverage of ICT is highest, is particularly likely to generate positive affect for the upside potential and recognition employees might receive. Consistent with the above arguments and previous empirical findings, we thus suggest that employees experience higher positive affect at work on days when they encounter more TCS because these experiences are appraised as opportunities for growth, learning, and goal attainment. More specifically, we hypothesize the following:

## H1: On days when employees encounter more TCS at work, they experience higher positive affect compared to days when they experience less TCS.

By contrast, we propose that THS should trigger negative affect because they are appraised as negative events that inhibit personal growth and goal attainment. Indeed, AET suggests that negative affect should result from harms and threats to valued outcomes. Because they are viewed as deterrents to obtaining valued outcomes, hindrance stressors represent the very type of threats that Lazarus (1991a) suggested would invoke negative affect. Concurrent with these arguments, previous work stress research has consistently found a positive relationship between hindrance stressors, such as role ambiguity, role conflict, and resource constraints, and negative affect (e.g., LePine et al. 2016). Moreover, research using within-person designs could show that negative affect not only varies considerably from day to day but is also positively affected by hindrance stressors across days (e.g., Potter et al. 2002).

From the perspective of negative daily events at work, THS represent major impediments that can prevent employees from achieving their goals and valued outcomes. For example, in our opening vignette, when Hannah encounters a situation in which her email program erroneously filters a much-awaited email from a colleague as spam, she feels annoyed and stressed out because this email filtering issue hinders the flow of her work. Other ICT-induced work blockers such as system glitches, breakdowns or upgrades that lead to unavailability or even loss of work resources may also stop employees in their tracks, preventing them from accomplishing their tasks (Riedl et al. 2012). Similarly, when employees realize that ICT systems lack important features that would be useful for faster task fulfillment or when such systems force them to change their work practices, they often feel angry and upset because these systems stand in the way of reaching higher-level goals (Stein et al. 2015). Moreover, when employees must grapple with unsolicited interruptions mediated through emails, instant messages, or social media (Addas and Pinsonneault 2018a) and recognize that such incidents slow down work processes and endanger timely task completion, they should be more likely to feel anxious about potential setbacks or performance failures (Galluch et al. 2015). Compounding such problems are employees’ heightened concerns when dealing with role ambiguities and task conflicts that arise from ICT use and require sustained efforts (e.g., when employees are expected to be reachable anytime and anywhere via their smartphones). These concerns may greatly undermine employees’ feelings of self-efficacy and even completely derail them in their goal pursuit, leading to their experience of negative affect (Ortiz de Guinea and Webster 2013).

Taken together, and in line with AET, we propose that employees—through an appraisal that their progress toward goal achievement is hampered—experience higher negative affect at work on days when they encounter more THS. Accordingly, we hypothesize the following:

H2: On days when employees encounter more THS at work, they experience higher negative affect compared to days when they experience less THS.

## Spillover of Technology-Driven Stress on Partnership Satisfaction via Affect

To this point, we integrated the challenge–hindrance stressor model with AET to hypothesize the proximal affective effects of TCS and THS at work. Synthesizing AET with work– home spillover literature, we suggest that TCS and THS also have far-reaching effects beyond the workplace and affect employees’ partnership satisfaction at home via an affective spillover mechanism. Partnership satisfaction is a global subjective evaluation about the quality of one’s partnership and is particularly relevant to capturing daily affective experiences that transcend the work–home boundary (Heller and Watson 2005; Ilies et al. 2009). Furthermore, a healthy partnership is an important protective factor against emotional instability and relationship dissolution, all of which may not only have far-reaching downstream consequences for employees’ wellbeing but also feed back into their work productivity (Margelisch et al. 2017).

AET suggests that people engage in emotion-focused coping that is intended to deal with their particular emotional reactions (Weiss and Cropanzano 1996). From this perspective, the emotions triggered by affective events invoke attitudes and behaviors that help employees cope with the experienced stressors. Taking this idea a step further, organizational research has extended AET from the work to the home domain by highlighting the possibility that positive and negative affect can spill over to affect attitudes and behaviors at home (Butts et al. 2015; Ilies et al. 2007). As discussed earlier, Edwards and Rothbard (2000) identified mood or affective spillover as an important causal mechanism linking the work and family domains, conceptualizing spillover as the “effects of work and family on one another that generate similarities between the two domains” (p. 180). Consistent with this view, Judge and Ilies (2004) suggested that a dynamic affect reinforcement process supported by affectcongruency may explain affective spillover from work to home. In this process, employees bring with them the positive emotions from work as they enter home, where the positive interpretations of the day’s work events further generate pleasant feelings and evaluations. By contrast, negative emotions lead to unpleasant feelings and evaluations via affectcongruent associations. Therefore, the valence of the retrospective evaluations regarding work will influence the affective states experienced by employees at home and subsequently influence attitudes and behaviors.

Such a spillover mechanism from affect in one role to attitudes and behaviors in another role is supported by Greenhaus and Powell (2006), who suggested that positive and negative affect evoked in one role can influence satisfaction in another role via their influence on role engagement and performance. According to this perspective, negative affect not only narrows one’s attention and promotes a self-focused orientation but also depletes an individual’s energy because the affect-control processes that are needed to terminate an unpleasant state are effortful (Green et al. 2003). By contrast, positive affect broadens an individual’s focus of attention and enhances people’s mental capabilities and vigor, increasing or replenishing psychological resources (Rothbard 2001). With more (or fewer) psychological resources being available, positive (negative) affect in one role can enhance (impair) interpersonal functioning and task fulfillment in another role, thereby increasing (decreasing) role engagement and promoting positive (negative) attitudes in the second role. Based on these notions, previous research has found positive affect created at work to facilitate perspective-taking and interpersonal helping in marital relationships because it increases one’s psychological availability and emotional support to the partner (ten Brummelhuis and Greenhaus 2018). By contrast, negative affect experienced at work has been shown to be associated with employees’ absent-mindedness and behavioral withdrawal at home, which implies that time and attention are siphoned away from social bonding and partnershipenhancing behaviors (Madrid et al. 2015).

Based on these arguments and previous empirical findings, we suggest that positive affect transferred from work to home enhances employees’ available psychological resources and creates more externally focused attention that should be related to higher emotional support provided to the partner and hence to increased partnership satisfaction. Conversely, we suggest that negative affect carried over from work to home costs psychological resources and creates an inward focus of attention, all of which should hinder employees from sensitively focusing on the partner and hence undermine partnership satisfaction. Thus, we hypothesize the following:

H3a: On days when employees experience high positive affect at work, they report higher partnership satisfaction at home for that day compared to days when they experience low daily positive affect.

H4a: On days when employees experience high negative affect at work, they report lower partnership satisfaction at home for that day compared to days when they experience low daily negative affect.

Above and beyond the direct impact of affect on partnership satisfaction, we also advance a mediation argument to suggest that affect created through TD stressors at work can be carried over to influence employees’ partnership satisfaction at home. On days when employees grapple with more disturbing IT incidents at work, it is more likely that experiences of THS negatively spill over onto partnership satisfaction by coloring employees’ assessments of their partnership via an affective pathway (Edwards and Rothbard 2000). Specific characteristics of ICT implicated in THS, such as always-on connectivity, rapid pace of change, or synchronicity (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Matook and Brown 2017), are often chiefly responsible for system failures or interruptions such that they likely generate strong negative feelings when they impede employees’ work progress and personal striving, often leaving a bad taste in employees’ mouths. In other words, THS are likely to be experienced as annoying and frustrating, producing negative feelings that may continue to tug at employees over the course of the day, hindering them from investing sufficient resources into their partnership at home. Furthermore, THS that elicit negative affect at work can give rise to elevated levels of resource depletion, manifested in increased fatigue, and make employees emotionally unable to unwind from work at home, which is linked to impaired role engagement and interpersonal functioning (Benlian et al. 2019). Employees fraught with negative affect may have fewer resources available to devote to their partners at home. As a result, they might be withdrawing from home responsibilities and partnershipenhancing behaviors on days when they are disturbed by the occurrence of THS at work. Previous research has shown that individuals have particular difficulties in psychologically detaching from the effects of technological work stressors, given their strong effects on resource depletion and ICT’s growing availability across life domains (Derks et al. 2014; Ohly and Latour 2014). In sum, we thus expect that the experience of partnership satisfaction at home can become contaminated, so to speak, with the spillover of negative affect triggered by THS at work.

By contrast, on days when employees have more thrilling experiences with ICT that create uplifts and opportunities for personal success at work, it is more likely that TCS spill over onto partnership satisfaction via a positive affective path. As mentioned earlier, using and mastering ICT, with their defining features for a growing share of today’s work tasks, often makes the difference in successful task fulfillment (Tarafdar et al. 2019). When employees experience instances in which ICT use can significantly increase task performance or even help solve critical work problems (Benlian 2015), occurrences of TCS are likely to trigger enthusiasm and positive affect because they add to employees’ personal growth. As positive affect generated through TCS can linger on and nurture subsequent positive evaluations at home (Powell and Greenhaus 2010), it is reasonable to expect that partnership satisfaction increases with more experiences of TCS at work. Positive affect also expands individuals’ repertoire of pro-relationship behaviors and emotional support, such that employees should be more likely to be attentive and responsive to the daily challenges of a partnership. Taken together, we thus hypothesize the following:

H3b: Daily positive affect mediates the relationship between TCS and daily partnership satisfaction, such that TCS are positively and indirectly related to daily partnership satisfaction through daily positive affect.

H4b: Daily negative affect mediates the relationship between THS and daily partnership satisfaction, such that THS are negatively and indirectly related to daily partnership satisfaction through daily negative affect.

## Work–Home Role Integration and Perceived Organizational Support as Moderators

Individuals vary in their preferences for integrating or segmenting aspects of work and home (Kreiner 2006). Preferences for role segmentation versus role integration are regarded as opposite ends of the same continuum; we use the term work–home role integration to refer to this continuum. Overall, “segmenters” prefer to keep work and family separate from each other, creating and maintaining a boundary or “mental fence.” Individuals who segment the work from the home domain psychologically compartmentalize their identities in these two domains such that “work is cordoned off from domestic life” (Nippert-Eng 1996, p. 89). “Integrators,” on the other hand, prefer to combine elements of both domains, essentially removing boundaries between the two and blending facets of each (Reyt and Wiesenfeld 2015). For example, segmenters prefer using separate mobile phones or notebooks for work and private issues and having two Outlook calendars, one for each domain. By contrast, integrators would use the same smartphone and notebook for work and private issues and have one Outlook calendar for business and personal activities. Of course, most individuals are not “pure types”—rather, their position on the continuum depends on individual preferences.

Work–home role segmentation generally limits the psychological influence of work on the home domain. Individuals who segment their work and home roles are less likely to think about work while they are at home because they erect relatively impermeable (“thick”) boundaries between domains that help them focus primarily on the role relevant to the life domain in which they are operating at that time. Segmenters are also less likely to allow work demands to interrupt the time they devote to partners and family (Greenhaus and Beutell 1985). Because they psychologically compartmentalize their work and family role identities, they are more likely to be able to turn off work stressors when in their home role and leave behind work issues that would be dysfunctional at home, whereas for integrators, work-related stress would continue to tug at them and linger at home. By psychologically increasing boundary permeability between work and home roles, integrators eliminate the walls between the two roles, thereby permitting work experiences to affect not only their roles as employees but also their roles as home members. The resulting overlaps and blurring of roles increase the likelihood of confusion, interruptions, role contamination, and competing demands (Nippert-Eng 1996).

As such, the home affect and attitude of employees with low WHI (i.e., employees who, for example, prefer turning off their work cell phone at home) should be less strongly influenced by the quality of their work experiences than the home affect and attitude of employees with high WHI. In other words, employees with highly integrated work–home roles, who, for example, take a cell phone call from their supervisor while enjoying dinner at home with their spouse, are more likely to carry over stressful work experiences to their partners after work, thus showing a stronger link between their daily affect and partnership satisfaction than those employees with segmented work and home roles will show. In sum, we argue that WHI positively moderates the strength of the (within-individual) effects of daily positive and negative affect created through TD stressors at work on daily partnership satisfaction at home. We thus predict the following:

H5a: WHI strengthens the effect of daily positive affect on daily partnership satisfaction such that the effect of daily positive affect on daily partnership satisfaction is stronger for employees who are more rather than less work–home role-integrated.

H5b: WHI strengthens the effect of daily negative affect on daily partnership satisfaction such that the effect of daily negative affect on daily partnership satisfaction is stronger for employees who are more rather than less work–home role-integrated.

Employees differ not only in their WHI preferences but also in how they perceive their organizations to be supporting them in managing their individual work–home boundaries, hereafter also referred to as perceived organizational support in work– home boundary management (POS) (Kossek and Lautsch 2012). Examples of such support include providing flexible work arrangements (e.g., flextime, unpaid leave), child care assistance (e.g., on-site childcare facilities), and financial assistance for dependent care (Hammer et al. 2013). Given that POS is intended to lessen work–home boundary conflicts interfering with work responsibilities, we propose that employees who perceive stronger POS can react more sensitively to stressful work demands, allowing them to discriminate about what will or will not pass through the work–home boundary to affect relationships at home. In other words, consistent with the notion of differential permeability (Kreiner et al. 2009), we argue that employees experiencing high POS can make finer-grained distinctions about what punctures the work–home boundary, so that negative affect spillover can be buffered and positive affect spillover can be enhanced.

Higher POS can strengthen employees’ psychological and physical resources and free up capacities such as time and energy (e.g., by mitigating concerns about work-family conflicts) so that they can cope more considerately with emotions in the wake of stressful work demands (Allen 2001). Moreover, higher POS can also increase employees’ flexibility and discretion to determine how, when and where they deal with those emotions and, as a result, whether (positive or negative) feelings that arise may or may not transcend the work–home boundary (Bulger et al. 2007). Specifically, under high POS, employees should have the resources and discretion to alleviate anger or frustrations stemming from THS right at the workplace, thus making work–home spillover less likely to occur. For example, employees who perceive and recognize that their organizations help them secure flexible work arrangements and advice when sudden family issues pop up at work can allocate more attention to regulating negative feelings right at the point of origin. On the other hand, feelings of excitement and positive mood created through TCS at work are more likely to be enhanced and transferred to the home domain under high POS because such support provides employees with extra resources (e.g., energy) and flexibility (e.g., options to let positive work experiences inspire private life) to facilitate work–home enrichment (Greenhaus and Powell 2006), thus allowing work and home roles to be purposefully enacted in ways that enrich each other. Based on the above logic, we therefore propose the following:

H6a: POS strengthens the effect of daily positive affect on daily partnership satisfaction such that the effect of daily positive affect on daily partnership satisfaction is stronger under high POS than under low POS.

H6b: POS weakens the effect of daily negative affect on daily partnership satisfaction such that the effect of daily negative affect on daily partnership satisfaction is weaker under high POS than under low POS.

## Methods

## Participants

Participants in the current study were recruited from different organizations randomly drawn from the Hoppenstedt database of German companies. A total of 23 of the 300 firms we invited agreed to participate; the firms represented more than 10 different industries, including finance, construction, software, healthcare, and transportation. As a key part of the study involved employees’ ratings of their interpersonal relationships with their partners, only employees who were married or were living together in a committed relationship were eligible to participate. The human resources (HR) departments sent coded invitation packets to 500 employees, inviting their voluntary participation and assuring confidentiality. A total of 184 participants and their spouses signed up to take part in the study, of which 115 couples provided complete data on the initial entry survey and at least five days (out of a maximum of 10 days) of matched daily surveys, resulting in a final response rate of 23%, which is comparable to other studies targeting couples of employees and their partners (Harrison and Wagner 2016).

All HR departments indicated that their organizations had no formalized policies about work–life balance but would rather offer contemporary work arrangements (e.g., flextime, unpaid leaves). Employees had an average age of 45.3 years, 55.7% were male, 45.2% held a university degree, the average organizational tenure was 12.4 years, and the vast majority of them (89.6%) had a full-time job with Monday to Friday work schedules. Employees worked in a variety of positions (e.g., general managers, product/project managers, developers, sales staff) and held jobs in research and development (18%), operations (21%), IT (28%), marketing (19%), and sales (14%). We performed t-tests to compare the demographics from the employees included in the data analysis with the demographics of the invited employees who did not participate in the study. We found no significant differences on any demographic variables captured in this study.

## Experience-Sampling Procedures and Measures

Participation in the study consisted of two major parts. In the first part of the study, employees completed an entry survey that assessed demographics and between-person variables (i.e., WHI, POS, controls). In the second part of the study, which began one week after the completion of part 1, data were collected using experience sampling methodology (ESM), which features repeated measurements of the same participants with a focus on assessing variables that exhibit substantial within-person variation over the short term that would usually go unnoticed with cross-sectional surveys (Fisher and To 2012). ESM also allowed us to access employees and their partners in their everyday environments and to measure constructs where they occur and are experienced, thus minimizing recall biases (Bolger et al. 2003). Thus, this multiple measurement design helped to paint a more accurate picture of daily TD work-to-home stress spillovers in the lives of participants than would be obtained using a single retrospective survey. In our study, employees were asked to complete four short surveys each workday over a two-week period, while employees’ partners—in order to conduct supplementary analyses based on partner-rated partnership satisfaction—were asked to respond to one daily survey every evening over the 10 weekdays (see Figure B1 in Appendix B for an overview). We chose a 10-day period for the daily surveys based on the recommendation of Reis and Wheeler (1991, p. 287), who contended that a “2-week record-keeping period is assumed to represent a stable and generalizable estimate of social life.” In line with previous ESM studies and based on conversations with the firms’ HR departments about their employees’ typical work rhythm, we chose a fixed-time schedule for daily data collection (e.g., Culbertson et al. 2012).

Specifically, on each morning of the study, participants were sent the link to the first daily survey at 9:00 a.m. They were asked to report their positive and negative affect in the morning as a control for possible serial effects. The link to the second daily survey was sent to participants at 2:30 p.m. to ask them to report about the frequency of TCS and THS they experienced. At 5:00 p.m., in a third daily survey, participants were asked to report their positive and negative affect again. Then, each evening at 8:30 p.m., participants received the link to the last daily survey that assessed their daily partnership satisfaction. At about the same time every evening, employees’ partners also received the link to a daily survey assessing their positive and negative affect as well as their partnership satisfaction. The time lag between morning, afternoon, and evening surveys allowed us to examine changes in affect levels and to establish temporal separation and causal precedence between the predictor and outcome variables. All items in the daily surveys referred strictly to daily responses following the convention of daily-level ESM studies. All surveys involved were implemented using an online survey platform, and the links to surveys were sent through emails (see Appendix B for more details on procedures and study incentives).

We adopted measurement items from validated instruments for almost all constructs in our study except for daily TCS and THS for which we developed new constructs and corresponding scales following MacKenzie et al. (2011) and Moore and Benbasat (1991). Appendix B presents the construct development procedures for daily TCS and THS and the measurement items of all constructs used in our ESM study. Based on the sample collected in the main study, we found that a two-factor measurement model fit the data significantly better than alternative models, which is also consistent with previous meta-analytic tests (e.g., LePine et al. 2016) examining the factorial validity of the challenge– hindrance stressor model (see Appendix B for the results of the confirmatory factor analysis). Although the appraisal process is implicit in the challenge–hindrance stressor framework and validation studies have generally supported the assumption that challenge stressors are appraised as challenging and hindrance stressors are appraised as hindering (LePine et al. 2016), we conducted a pilot study (see Appendix B) that confirmed that TCS were appraised positively as offering the potential for mastery, personal growth or accomplishment, whereas THS were appraised negatively as having the potential for harms and threats to valued outcomes. We included age, gender, number of children, organizational tenure, and job autonomy as between-person controls in the entry survey, and we controlled for employee morning positive and negative affect and for partner evening positive and negative affect as within-person controls (e.g., Butts et al. 2015; Ilies et al. 2009; Miner et al. 2005).

## Analytic Strategy

Given the nested nature of our data, we used multilevel analysis in Mplus 8.0 (Muthén and Muthén 2008-2017) and R 3.4.2 (Bliese 2016) to test our hypotheses. As a three-level model (i.e., days, nested in employees, nested in organizations) revealed that the percentage of variance between organizations in our focal study constructs was nonsignificant and trivial, we proceeded with a more parsimonious two-level model, with days nested in employees. Specifically, all direct effects in our research model (i.e., Hypotheses 1, 2, 3a, and 4a) were tested by using random coefficient models (Raudenbush and Bryk 2002); the indirect effects of TCS and THS on partnership satisfaction (i.e., Hypotheses 3b and 4b) were tested by using multilevel mediation models (i.e., a 1–1–1 mediation model; Bauer et al. 2006); and finally, the crosslevel moderating effects of WHI and POS (i.e., Hypotheses 5 and 6) were tested in path-analytical models involving Level 2 moderators. Multilevel analysis and random coefficient models remedy many of the threats for type I and type II errors by explicitly accounting for and modeling the nonindependence of observations in the data (Bliese and Hanges 2004). Before conducting these analyses, we centered Level 1 predictors around individuals’ means (i.e., group-mean centering) to remove any between-person variance among the variables, meaning that the relationships among the Level 1 variables are not confounded by individual differences, such as response tendencies, personality traits, and demographics (Scott et al. 2014). In testing the cross-level moderation, the moderators at Level 2 were centered around their grand means to alleviate potential multicollinearity problems (Hofmann and Gavin 1998).

## Discussion

The goal of this research was to advance understanding of how daily TD stressors experienced at work unfold in differential effects over time and potentially spill over to influence important partnership-related outcomes at home. Integrating the challenge–hindrance stressor model with AET and work– home spillover literature, we proposed a broader conceptualization of TD stressors and examined how they affect employees’ partnership satisfaction via an affective spillover mechanism. In an ESM study using daily surveys of 115 employees from various organizations, we found that while THS exhibited negative spillover effects via negative affect, TCS had distinct and substantial positive effects on partnership satisfaction via positive affect. These affective spillover effects remained even when taking into account autoregressive affective patterns and partners’ evening affect. Moreover, our study revealed that WHI and POS played important moderating roles at the work–home boundary by differentially promoting or inhibiting these technology-driven affective spillover processes.

## Implications for Theory and Research

We believe that this study contributes to technostress research in particular and to IS research on technology-related stress in general in three specific areas. First, whereas past technostress research has painted an overly bleak picture of technology-related stress, what has been missing is theory and research that moves beyond viewing technology-related stressors as unequivocally negative and uniformly detrimental incidents and instead treats them as unique events with their own properties and interpretations. Extending and contextualizing the general challenge–hindrance stressor model to the IS domain, our study complements previous technostress research by introducing and validating an integrative, more balanced conceptualization of technology-driven stressors. Specifically, we highlight the importance of making a theoretical distinction between THS and TCS that co-occur at work yet produce differential (i.e., detrimental or beneficial) affective responses in employees. The finding for TCS is particularly important, as it shines a positive light on the effects of TD stressors, thus expanding past technostress research that has provided a rich portrait of the adverse effects of technostress while neglecting to examine its positive facet (Tarafdar et al. 2019). This expanded approach addresses a longstanding asymmetry in technostress research and shifts the prevalent conversation from technology-related stress as being purely harmful to having both dark and bright sides.

Second, although previous technostress research has a great deal of utility for guiding research on the consequences of technostress in work settings, the more distal effects on nonwork domains such as employees’ homes have received only scant attention so far. In other words, technostress, and technology-related stress more generally, has so far largely been examined as a within-domain phenomenon. Those few studies that have looked at the work–life interface have treated cross-domain influences of technology-related stress predominantly as a unitary and static concept (e.g., as work– home or work–life conflict) rather than as a dynamic process that captures the flow from one domain to the other. Our study departs from previous technostress research by placing a spotlight on boundary-transcending spillover mechanisms that reveal how TD stressors originating in one domain may significantly impact individuals and their partnerships in another domain. Accounting for the extended reach of TD stressor consequences beyond the workplace thus allows IS scholars to achieve a more holistic understanding of TD stressors as a cross-domain phenomenon that are consequential not only to work outcomes but also to significant nonwork outcomes (Chen and Karahanna 2018). We also advance research on technostress effects by drawing on work–home boundary management literature to integrate perspectives on employees’ individual differences and perceptions of organi zational support that shape the cross-domain spillover process. Most studies in technostress research have tended to focus on one set of moderating factors (e.g., personality traits) or the other (e.g., organizational practices) at a time (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Srivastava et al. 2015), but rarely have they been considered together, even though they allow nuanced insights into the boundary conditions of TD stress spillover. While WHI acts as a double-edged sword for letting TCS- and THS-triggered positive and negative affect spill over to home outcomes, POS selectively allows positive affect to spill over into the home but blocks negative affect.

Third, previous technostress research has assumed a relatively static and chronic model of technostress and has looked at how aggregated, retrospective stress experiences relate to outcomes. Unfortunately, static between-person approaches cannot account for dynamic within-person processes that explain when and why technology-related stress impacts individuals on a daily basis. Our findings extend previous research, suggesting that TD stressors are an inherently within-person phenomenon in the sense that on a daily basis, individuals must navigate through varied experiences of TD stressors that exhibit considerable fluctuations. By using a within-person approach based on ESM, we took a “magnifying glass” to zoom into the microlevel processes of TD stressors and looked at how their effects unfold on a day-today basis. Our results show that TD stressors may cast a positive glow or a dark shadow over our perceptions, which fluctuate within employees during the day. In echoing previous calls for increased attention to the short-term nature of technostress (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Galluch et al. 2015), our study advances previous research by drawing attention to the microlevel, which provides a novel lens for portraying the dynamics of our everyday TD stress experiences. Broadly, our study contributes to IS literature on technology-related stress by showing that between-person studies prevalent in technostress research should be complemented with withinperson research designs to gain a more complete understanding of TD stress within and between individuals.

## Practical Implications

Given the increasing number of employees who are affected by TD stressors at work, the results of this study have important implications for organizations and their employees in their pursuit to (pro-)actively cope with the intricate and pervasive effects of these daily stressors.

First and foremost, a critical take-home point of this research for organizations and their managers is to recognize that daily TD work stressors are more nuanced and enduring than previously thought. Our study shows that TD stressors are not all created equal and that they should not be universally treated as bad. In trying to prevent their employees’ TD work stress experiences from spilling over to the home domain, however, managers often run the risk that they inadvertently lump all types of TD stressors together, which deprives them of the opportunity to leverage positive work–home spillovers. Organizations are thus well advised to take stock of all kinds of TD stressors at work and to distinguish good from bad ones, as TCS can be helpful not only in creating positive affect at work but also in increasing partnership satisfaction at home, which may in turn feed back into work productivity. Moreover, our findings indicate that TD stressor effects are not confined to the work domain but likely reach into employees’ home lives through affective processes. When one considers the contagious effect of emotions on others (e.g., Neumann and Strack 2000), as we have noted in our opening vignette, this spillover effect may spread even farther at home. Such pervasive consequences of TD stressors point to the importance of establishing work–life balance programs that address TD stressors more holistically to retain top talent in the long term. The benefits of TCS in particular could be enhanced by encouraging employees to see them as developmental challenges. Coaching or mentoring from supervisors and peers (e.g., using positive reflection interventions; Bono et al. 2013) may, for example, facilitate employees’ sensemaking of TD stressors.

Second, organizations must walk a fine line when choosing what type of WHI preferences they want to emphasize in what positions when hiring and training employees. Our findings suggest that work–home role segmentation may serve as a valuable intervention point to prevent, or at least dampen, negative spillover effects across contexts because it is effective in containing TDS and negative affect in the domain where they are generated. In light of this, employers may, for example, train employees to meaningfully segment their work and family roles (e.g., through psychological detachment strategies; Park et al. 2011) and supply more extensive opportunities to enhance segmentation by revising a culture that emphasizes “always online” and “24/7” accessibility. Because role segmentation also inhibits positive work–home spillover, employers may simultaneously provide trainings in selectively integrating work and family roles in areas in which the transfer of positive emotions generated through TCS creates maximum value.

Finally, according to our findings related to POS, organizations are well advised to help employees manage their work–home boundary to both decrease negative and increase positive affective spillover from TD stressors at work. To this end, organizations could increase their efforts at implementing and promoting family-supportive practices that would make employees feel that their employer cares about their individual work–home challenges. For example, organizations could nurture a compassionate workplace climate in which individual work–home boundary management is respected and granted adequate priority vis-à-vis job-related work demands. Alternatively, organizations could pay more attention to investing time and effort in developing managers who are able to provide proactive family support, or they could formally acknowledge employees with effective work–home boundary management styles as role models.

## Strengths, Limitations, and Future Research Directions

The current research has a number of strengths, including the collection of longitudinal and multisource data in an ESM study that allowed us to assess TD stress spillover at the work–home interface much more precisely than retrospective, memory-based reports ever could; separation in time and space among our focal constructs; and autoregressive affect patterns. Like any study, however, we draw attention to some limitations that can be leveraged to inform future research.

The first limitation relates to potential reverse causality. Our study examined TD stressors and spillover only in one direction, from work to home. Thus, it focused on the work-tohome interface to the exclusion of the home-to-work interface. Although we can empirically rule out some reverse-causal effects via lagged measurement of variables and further supplementary analyses (see Appendix D), it is still possible that partnership satisfaction in the morning at home affects employees’ experiences of TD stressors at work. Future research may attempt to overcome this limitation by using experimental designs (e.g., long-term intervention studies) that allow stronger causal inferences. In a similar vein, additional research is warranted to provide a fuller perspective of two-way spillover effects at the work–home interface.

Second, because our data were collected at the daily level, it was difficult for us to investigate whether the strength or intensity of TD stressors might impact employees’ affective reactions. Such an investigation would require data that measure TD stressors at the event level because the same stressor may be perceived to have different strengths by different employees. For example, it is possible that one major system breakdown may take a heavier toll and thus have a stronger negative effect than numerous minor incidents. While our theoretical interest was focused on an aggregate-level perspective of TCS and THS, and their categorization and measurement were theoretically driven and closely aligned with the challenge–hindrance stressor model, we invite future research to employ richer measures of TD stressors and focus on discrete ICT-induced or ICT-mediated TCS (e.g., ICT as developmental job experience) or THS (e.g., ICT as straightjacket) that will enable their examination (i.e., of frequency, valence, and intensity) at a more granular level. In doing so, future research might also consider conducting further construct development studies to create and validate measures of these disaggregated TCS and THS. On a similar note, future research may also want to incorporate ICT-related capabilities and characteristics (e.g., complexity, connectivity, or synchronicity) more explicitly into the nomological network of TD stress spillover (e.g., as stress creators or mitigators) to further advance an IS-focused understanding of this phenomenon.

Third, regarding our data collection utilizing a four-point daily measurement approach, the existence of a link between TD stressors and affect measured in the early and late afternoon and ensuing spillover effects on partnership satisfaction measured in the evening—despite a range of experiences that can occur in the time gaps between the measurement points— attests to the robustness of our findings, given that such a design provides a conservative test of our predictions. Even so, we did not consider any factors other than employees’ morning affect and partners’ evening affect that may have allowed us to examine additional dynamics and rule out alternative explanations for employees’ affective experiences and partnership satisfaction. For example, how would another incident (e.g., dealing with an abusive supervisor behavior) between early and late afternoon alter the affect induced by TD stressors at noon? How would employees’ experiences during their trip back home (e.g., a traffic jam) influence the spillover process we observe in our study? Future research might address these questions. Finally, our study context (e.g., all participants of the focal study were sampled from organizations in Germany) and sample characteristics (e.g., we focused on people married or living together in a committed relationship) may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future research may cross-validate our research model using different contexts, cultures, and sociodemographics.

Despite these limitations, our study provides several directions for future research that can build upon the overarching framework in Figure 1. First, our research focused on affective spillover mechanisms. This focus was driven by theoretical considerations regarding the transient and fluctuating nature of daily TD stressors. Future research may examine other spillover mechanisms, such as values, skills, or behavior, that provide alternative or complementary accounts. Second, we chose to focus on boundary management moderators that were particularly relevant within the work–home spillover process. By extension, other moderators—especially those that foster an IS-centered understanding of technostress spillovers—may be of interest to future researchers. For example, it would be worthwhile to investigate how the design, use and implementation of boundary spanning IS (e.g., cloud and mobile services) affect the crossdomain effects of TCS and THS (Benlian et al. 2018). Influences from non-TD stressors at work (e.g., supervisor- or coworker-induced stressors) or from family members at home (e.g., children) other than those incorporated in our study may also be considered to gain a better understanding of the condi tions shaping work–home spillover. Finally, our work echoes and extends Tarafdar et al.’s (2019) agenda for future inquiry into technostress in several important ways. On the one hand, our study answers their calls for research by putting forth a novel conceptualization of techno-eustress in the form of TCS and by depicting affect-based coping responses as affective work–home spillovers, while leveraging a longitudinal ESM design to capture the dynamic aspects of the technostress process. On the other hand, we also extend their research agenda by showing that there is considerable room for increasing the theoretical heterogeneity in conceptualizing technostressors and for expanding the scope of inquiry into domains beyond the workplace and the focal individual. We thus encourage future research to examine TCS and THS as well as complementary notions of TD stressors to open up spaces for prospective thinking about the multifaceted nature and effects of the technostress phenomenon. More studies are also needed to examine technostress spillover at the work–life boundary and crossover of technostress that may transpire within social relationships to affect significant others and family members beyond the focal individual, as alluded to in our opening vignette.

## Conclusion

This study represents an important step toward better understanding the dual nature and differential effects of TD stressors at the interface between work and home. Specifically, we found that TCS and THS experienced at work evoke positive and negative affect that spill over to shape partnership satisfaction at home. We also showed that this process is moderated by WHI and POS, such that WHI acts as a double-edged sword for letting positive and negative affect spill over to home, while POS serves as a facilitator of positive affect and as a buffer against negative affect. Together, our study draws a more balanced picture of technology-related stress, extends research on ICT-based stressors to the intersection of different life domains, and suggests a number of promising research areas that are highly relevant in an era in which work–home boundaries are becoming ever more blurred through the pervasiveness of ICT.

## Appendix B

## Construct/Scale Development, Measurement Items, Confirmatory Factor Analysis, and Data Collection Procedures

## Construct/Scale Development for Technology-Driven Challenge and Hindrance Stressors

Because prior conceptualizations and operationalizations of technostress focused on the adverse effects of technology-related stress and the between-person level, we developed new measurement items for TCS and THS tailored to the daily level of analysis. We drew on previous empirical and meta-analytic papers on the (two-dimensional) challenge–hindrance work stressor framework in the organizational work stress literature and on literature focusing on the frequency of stressful demands experienced by employees in their daily work (e.g., LePine et al. 2005; LePine et al. 2016; Rodell and Judge 2009) to contextualize the general challenge–hindrance work stressor framework (Cavanaugh et al. 2000; LePine et al. 2004)<sup>8</sup> to the IS context. In addition, we used the contents of Ragu-Nathan et al.’s (2008) measurement items of technostress creators (i.e., techno-overload, techno-invasion, techno-complexity, techno-insecurity, techno-uncertainty) (see also Tarafdar et al. 2007) as well as broader technostress and IS literature (in particular about the role of ICTs in stressful work situations) as conceptual anchors to further specify the content domain of the two TD stressor constructs.<sup>9</sup> Translating general work stressors into context-specific constructs (i.e., technology-driven challenge and hindrance work stressors in our study) by enriching them with previous related conceptualizations and literature is also consistent with the general direction of formulating contextualized constructs (e.g., Hong et al. 2014; Whetten et al. 2009)

Based on the four main challenge (i.e., workload, time urgency, learning opportunities, and job/task complexity) and hindrance work stressor facets (i.e., hassles/obstacles, resource constraints/ inadequacies, role/task ambiguity, and role/task conflict) that are commonly combined into aggregate-level challenge and hindrance stressor constructs (e.g., Cavanaugh et al. 2000; LePine et al. 2016), and informed by the abovementioned technostress and broader IS/work stress literatures (e.g., Addas and Pinsonneault 2015; Galluch et al. 2015), we developed a conceptual mapping from these general work stressors to corresponding technology-driven challenge and hindrance stressors (see Table 1 in the main text) with the goal to combine these facets into aggregate-level TD challenge stressor (TCS) and hindrance stressor (THS) constructs, generally reflecting the potential for individual learning opportunities and personal growth/gains (TCS) or capturing hindrances or impediments to individual learning and personal growth (THS). Consistent with previous methodological literature (Nickerson et al. 2013), this deductive, top-down approach in deriving TD challenge and hindrance stressor facets was complemented (and iteratively revised) by inductive, bottom-up procedures that we employed during an extensive scale development process for the TD stressors.

We started the scale development process by deriving and adapting a set of preliminary items for daily TCS and THS drawn from previous work stress and technostress literature. We then followed the steps taken by LePine et al. (2004) and Cavanaugh et al. (2000) to ensure content validity of the scales. We obtained critical incidents<sup>10</sup> on typical daily technology-induced or -mediated stressors at work from a sample of 84 employees and 5 academic experts (Flanagan 1954; Gremler 2004; Tan et al. 2016). Based on this input, the preliminary item pool was iteratively extended and validated using participants’ responses to open-ended questions to ensure that the scale items fully and clearly captured the conceptual domain of each construct and were meaningful across employees with diverse backgrounds (MacKenzie et al. 2011). In general, participants’ responses corresponded with the global TD challenge and hindrance stressor categories and only minor revisions had to be made. In this iterative process, problematic items were also reworded, combined with other items, or dropped (DeVellis 2003).

To support construct validity and to evaluate how well the candidate items tapped the two aggregate TD challenge–hindrance stressor factors and were distinct from strain (Ayyagari et al. 2011; Moore 2000), we conducted a Q-sort procedure (Hinkin 1998; Moore and Benbasat 1991; Nahm et al. 2002). For this purpose, a different group of 15 employees was invited and given a thorough description of TD challenge and hindrance stressors as well as strain, along with a pile of 3 × 5-inch index cards that each included one item and a corresponding brief description. The employees were then instructed to assign each of the cards either to one of the three factors or to an “ambiguous” category. During the task, the employees were asked to think aloud and explain each of the card assignments. We noticed that all employees felt comfortable with assigning each of the items to one of the three factors. Hit ratios ranging between 88-100% supported the construct validity of the two types of TD stressors (Moore and Benbasat 1991).

The entire (employee-rated) survey was then pre-tested in two phases. First, we administered a pen-and-paper version at a focus group with 15 employees invited to a major public university in Germany,<sup>11</sup> where all of the 15 employees completed the survey and provided feedback. Based on their feedback, we shortened the survey and made minor modifications to the order and wording of some items. Second, we conducted a pilot survey with another convenience sample of 94 employees and specifically requested suggestions concerning both the survey design (i.e., length and order of questions) and item wording based on open-ended input fields. Most importantly, we included two three-item measures for TD challenge and hindrance appraisals, adapted from previous research (LePine et al. 2005; LePine et al. 2016; Ohly and Fritz 2010) to our study context in order to check whether TCS were truly perceived positively (as potential for mastery, personal growth or achievement), whereas THS were truly perceived negatively (as potential for harms and threats to valued outcomes). The three items for TD challenge appraisals (“Fulfilling the work demands induced or mediated by ICT today helps to improve my personal growth and well-being,” “I feel the work demands induced or mediated by ICT today challenge me to achieve personal goals and accomplishment,” and “The work demands induced or mediated by ICT today bring me closer to the accomplishment of personal goals”) and TD hindrance appraisals (“Fulfilling the work demands induced or mediated by ICT today thwarts my personal growth and well-being,” “I feel the work demands induced or mediated by ICT today constrain my achievement of personal goals and development,” and “The work demands induced or mediated by ICT today stand in my way to accomplish personal goals”) closely parallel previous appraisal measures (LePine et al. 2016; Ohly and Fritz 2010). As a first step, we conducted confirmatory factor analyses for all the TD challenge–hindrance stressor and appraisal items in the pilot survey. Results indicated that a four-factor measurement model (÷<sup>2</sup>/df = 1.39, RMSEA = .04, SRMR = .07, CFI = .94, TLI = .94) fits the data significantly better than an alternative model with all TD challenge stressor and appraisal items loading on one factor and all TD hindrance stressor and appraisa items loading on another (÷<sup>2</sup>/df = 3.91, RMSEA = .11, SRMR = .13, CFI = .88, TLI = .89; ÷<sup>2</sup>(2) = 333.61, p < .05) and another alternative model with all TD stressor items (TCS and THS) loading on one factor and all TD appraisal items loading on another (÷<sup>2</sup>/df = 5.78, RMSEA = .15, SRMR = .17, CFI = .73, TLI = .75; ÷<sup>2</sup>(2) = 542.91, p < .01). In a second step, we conducted multilevel regression analyses and found that TCS were appraised positively as potential for mastery, personal growth or achievement $( \gamma = . 2 3 ; p < . 0 5 )$ , whereas THS were appraised negatively as potential for harms and threats to valued outcomes $( \gamma = . 2 9 ; p < . 0 1 )$ . As a result of the review of participants’ open-ended comments, we also clarified survey instructions and modified the wording for some items. Overall, the scale development procedures and pilo studies resulted in a validated set of eight items for TCS and eight items for THS.<sup>12</sup>

## Measurement Items

The measurement items for the principal study constructs and their anchors are shown in Table B1 and described below. It is important to reiterate here that in the instructions of all our daily surveys, we particularly emphasized that participants should provide answers that are reflective of their daily (and not more general) experiences. This emphasis should also reduce recall bias.

Technology-Driven Challenge and Hindrance Work Stressors. As a result of the scale development procedures described above, TCS and THS were measured with eight reflective items respectively. As identified in our contextualization of the challenge–hindrance stressor framework to the IS domain (see Table 1 in the main text), the TCS items tapped into the four TCS stressor facets, while the THS items captured the six THS stressor facets. Participants evaluated the frequency of TCS and THS in their daily work using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (extremely often).

Positive and Negative Affect. We used the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson and Clark 1994) to measure participants daily affect. From the PANAS scale, we chose five adjectives for measuring positive and negative affect, respectively. Sample adjective descriptors for positive affect are “interested” and “enthusiastic.” Sample adjective descriptors for negative affect are “upset” and “irritable.” Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they have felt today as indicated with each adjective descriptor using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (slightly or not at all) to 7 (very much)

Partnership Satisfaction. The daily partnership satisfaction of employees was assessed with 3 items using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). This scale has been developed in the family and marriage literature and successfully applied in previous ESM studies (Ilies et al. 2009; Norton 1983). To mirror employee perceptions from their partners’ perspective, we also collected partner ratings of partnership satisfaction in daily evening surveys using the same three-item scale as in the employee survey. We used this measure in a supplementary analysis (see Appendix D).

Work–Home Role Integration. We measured employees’ WHI using a slightly modified version of the three-item Work–Family Role Integration-Blurring Scale (Desrochers et al. 2005; Ilies et al. 2009). The anchor points for the scale ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); thus, higher scores are associated with more integrated work–home roles.

Perceived Organizational Support in Individual Work–Home Boundary Management. POS was assessed with 3 items using a 7-point Likert scale adapted from Kossek et (al. 2001) and Kossek and Lautsch (2012). Higher scores reflected employee perceptions of stronger organizational support in individuals’ work–home boundary management. The items were introduced with contextual information in the entry survey to clarify what is meant by an organization helping employees manage their work–home boundary (e.g., help with scheduling conflicts, advice when unanticipated family issues come up at work).

Between-Person and Within-Person Controls. We included age, gender, number of children, organizational tenure, and job autonomy as between-person controls in the entry survey because studies have shown that these variables may impact spillovers to home outcomes (e.g., Butts et al. 2015; Ilies et al. 2009). Age (AGE), gender (GD, number of children (CH), organizational tenure (OT), and job autonomy (JA) were measured as follow: Age and organizational tenure were measured in (number of) years, and gender was dummy coded as “0” female and “1” male. Number of children was assessed as the count of children living in the focal employee’s household. Finally, job autonomy, defined as the degree to which individuals perceive that they can determine the method, pace, and effort to accomplish tasks in the workplace (Spector 1986), was measured with four items adopted from Ahuja and Thatcher (2005) on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 7 = “strongly agree.” The four items were as follow: “I have a lot of freedom to decide how I perform assigned tasks,” “I control the content of my job,” “I have the authority to initiate projects at my job,” and “I set my own schedule for completing assigned tasks.”

Consistent with previous studies (e.g., Ilies et al. 2017), we also controlled for within-person variables. To account for possible auto-correlation in the affect scores and diminish concerns of reverse causality (e.g., Miner et al. 2005; Spieler et al. 2017), we controlled for the positive and negative affect scores employees had reported in the morning from work (PAM and NAM). This strategy also allowed us to partial out the affect arising from employees’ work during the day from the affect created at home and carried over to work in the morning. Furthermore, we controlled for partners’ evening positive and negative affect (PAE and NAE) because previous studies have shown that employee-rated partnership satisfaction may also be influenced by partners’ mood and affective states that captures, at least partially, partners’ own experiences during the day (Ilies et al. 2009). Finally, we controlled for potential cross-affective effects. Although previous work stress research has consistently shown that challenge (hindrance) stressors predominantly evoke positive (negative) affect, some studies have found that challenge (hindrance) stressors may also be negatively related to negative (positive) affect (e.g., Lin et al. 2015). The within-person control variable were assessed using the same scales used to assess afternoon affect at work (see Table B1).

## Data Collection Procedures for Experience-Sampling Study

As mentioned in the main paper, participation in the study consisted of two major parts. At the start of the study (part 1), employees and their partners completed an initial survey that assessed demographics and individual-level variables (i.e., WHI, POS, and between-person control variables). In the second part, which began one week after the completion of part 1, data were collected using experience sampling methodolog (ESM) in which focal participants (i.e., employees) were asked to complete several short surveys each workday over two consecutive weeks that included 10 weekdays (see Figure B1). Employees’ partners were also asked to respond to one daily Internet-based survey every evening over the 10 weekdays.

We chose a 10-day period for the daily surveys based on the recommendation of Reis and Wheeler (1991, p. 287) who contended that a “2-week record-keeping period is assumed to represent a stable and generalizable estimate of social life.” Similarly, Trougakos et al. (2014) have recommended a period of two weeks for organizational ESM studies because it is considered a representative snapshot of an employee’s work life. Further, a 10-day period has also been used in many recent ESM studies in organizational contexts, including studies with a focus on shortterm, episodic stress, with the basic reasoning that it provides a meaningful window into employees’ everyday work experiences (e.g., Ilies et al. 2017; LePine et al. 2016).

All surveys involved in the current study were implemented using a Web-based survey platform and the links of surveys were sent to participants through e-mails. Data collected in this manner are generally of comparable quality to data collected via paper-based methods, and provide a heterogeneous sample (Smith et al. 1997). To ensure that employees and their partners did not align their perceptions, we explicitly noted in our survey instructions that they should express their opinions independently without interacting with each other. Based on log-file analysis, we also checked participants’ response patterns (e.g., synchronicity of responses, usage of same or different devices for survey completion) but did not detect any anomaly. All participants were given a 1 hour window to respond to the survey. To increase participation, a reminder email was sent to participants who did not finish the survey within half an hour. Participation in the study was voluntary, and employees and their partners were compensated for their participation. Employees received up to €40, and partners up to €10, depending on how many surveys they completed. In addition, each participating couple was entered into a drawing for a household product valued at €200 as an incentive for participation.

Because all participants had Monday to Friday work schedules, the two-week duration of the study gave each focal employee the opportunity to respond to 10 surveys of each type (morning, early afternoon, late afternoon, and evening), with each employee’s partner also having the opportunity to respond to 10 surveys (in the evening). In total, focal employees completed 3,865 out of total possible 4,600 daily surveys (115 employees × 40 daily surveys) resulting in an effective level 1-N of 1.001. Spouses completed 915 out of total possible 1,150 daily surveys (115 partners × 10 daily surveys).

## Confirmatory Factor Analysis Based on the Experience-Sampling Study Sample

To confirm the validity and robustness of the two (reflective) measurement models of TCS and THS, we conducted a multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) with TCS, THS and employee-rated partnership satisfaction based on the sample collected in the experience-sampling study using Mplus 8.0 (Muthén and Muthén 2008-2017). Previous methodological literature (e.g., Heck and Thomas 2015; Hu and Bentle 1999) suggested that values of greater than .90 for the comparative fit index (CFI) and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) to indicate acceptable mode fit, while it suggested values of less than .08 for the root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA) and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) to imply adequate model fit. The CFA revealed that a three-factor solution showed acceptable goodness-of-fit $\textstyle ( \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 . 5 7 ;$ RMSEA = .06; SRMR = .04; CFI = .94; TLI = .93). Then, we tested a two-factor model described by employee-rated partnership satisfaction and a general factor of TD stressors where items of both TCS and THS were loaded together. This strategy allowed us to examine discriminan validity and whether TCS and THS represent distinct constructs or the same construct. Results showed poor goodness-of-fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 5 . 3 2$ RMSEA = .13; SRMR =.08; CFI =.76; TLI =.71). Furthermore, a ÷<sup>2</sup>-difference test between a two-factor model of TCS and THS and a one factor model was significant ( ÷<sup>2</sup> = 510.89, p < .01). These results not only supported the two-factor structure proposed in this study but also suggested that TCS and THS were empirically distinct from each other and other related constructs.

In additional CFAs, we compared the proposed first-order two-factor model to various second-order factor models (with TCS and THS having varying reflective and formative specifications on the first- and second-order levels; Jarvis et al. 2003) and found in all ÷<sup>2</sup>-difference tests that the (first-order) two-factor model fit the data much better than any other second-order factor model (all p < .01). Finally, we compared the proposed first-order reflective two-factor model to a first-order formative two-factor model and the results indicated that the reflective specification fit the data significantly better than a formative specification (p < .05). Accordingly, and consistent with previous empirical studies and meta-analyses (e.g., Chong et al. 2011; LePine et al. 2016; Podsakoff et al. 2007) validating a reflective two-factor structure, we measured TCS and THS as two reflective first-order constructs.

## Supplementary Analyses

## Linearity between Stressors and Affect

Although previous ESM studies have shown that the frequency of daily work stressors is quite low because they are low base-rate phenomena with a natural range restriction such that one is unlikely to find curvilinear effects (Rosen et al. 2010), we conducted supplementary analyses to examine potential nonlinearities. In particular, we tested whether TCS had an inverted U-shaped relationship with positive affect, where TCS promote positive affect up to a tipping point, beyond which the relationship would turn negative when the “challenge” is taken too far (e.g., Busse et al. 2016; Haans et al. 2016). Consistent with previous ESM studies, we did not find evidence for a curvilinear effect (i.e., the quadratic coefficient of TCS was not significant), suggesting that the relationship between TCS and positive affect is linear. We also did not find curvilinear relationships between TCS and negative affect or between THS and negative (positive) affect. Collectively, these findings are in line with previous research that could find little or no support for the curvilinear relationships between work stressors and performance outcomes (e.g., LePine et al. 2005; Podsakoff 2007).

## Partner-Rated Partnership Satisfaction as Home Outcome

We also investigated whether the TD stressor effects were persistent enough to cross over to employees’ partners at home, which would not only provide a robustness check to our findings but also reveal how far TD work stressors would reach into employees’ home domains. To do so, we used partner-rated partnership satisfaction (PSP) as home outcome variable. The results in Table D1 show patterns of results that are largely consistent with our results based on employee-rated partnership satisfaction. Besides generally weaker relationships between the focal variables in our model in absolute terms (and, as expected, stronger relationships between partner-rated affect in the evening and partnership satisfaction), the only substantial difference we found was that the cross-level interaction effect between WHI and positive affect on PSP was not significant (ã = .01, p > .05), which means that daily positive affect does not cross over to partners of employees with high WHI (only daily negative affect crosses over to partners). All other cross-level interaction effects were however significant and in line with the patterns of findings with employee-rated partnership satisfaction as home outcome. Daily positive and negative affect also mediated the relationships between daily TCS and THS and daily PSP. The estimate for the indirect effect of daily TCS on daily PSP through daily positive affect was .06, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [.01, .12]. Similarly, the estimate for the indirect effect of daily THS on daily partnership satis faction via daily negative affect was –.07, with a 95% CI of [–.14; –.01].

In sum, the affective effects of TCS and THS did not only spill over to employees’ partnership satisfaction via positive and negative affect but also crossed over to their partners’ partnership satisfaction, demonstrating the persistence and cross-domain relevance of TD work stressors.

## Reverse Causality

Even though our theory specifies that TD stressors are likely to influence partnership satisfaction at home through affect, our data structure enabled us to test a model wherein we reversed the causality of our measures (i.e., positive and negative affect in the morning as the independen variables influencing TCS and THS experienced at work that in turn affect partnership satisfaction at home). The results of this analysis were not as compelling as our hypothesized model. Specifically, positive and negative affect in the morning did not have main effect relationships with TCS and THS (all ps > .05). Furthermore, we examined lagged effects of partnership satisfaction on next-day TCS and THS, controlling for morning positive and negative affect. These analyses should assess whether employees’ partnership satisfaction potentially affec experiences of TCS and THS at work. However, the analyses revealed that partnership satisfaction did not have main effects on next-day TCS and THS (all ps > .05). Given these results, our data appears to provide stronger support for our theoretically derived model.
