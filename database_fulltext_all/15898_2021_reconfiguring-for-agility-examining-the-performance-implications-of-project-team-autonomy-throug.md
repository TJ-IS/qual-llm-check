# RECONFIGURING FOR AGILITY: EXAMINING THE PERFORMANCE IMPLICATIONS OF PROJECT TEAM AUTONOMY THROUGH AN ORGANIZATIONAL POLICY EXPERIMENT <sup>1</sup>

Narayan Ramasubbu Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh, Pittsburgh, PA, U.S.A {narayanr@pitt.edu}

Indranil R. Bardhan McCombs School of Business, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX, U.S.A. {indranil.bardhan@mccombs.utexas.edu}

Agile software development, a paradigm that emphasizes project team autonomy and the value of responding to changes over following standardized processes, has gained prominence in the software industry. Prior investigations on the adoptions of agile paradigms for software operations and their performance implications have typically focused on isolated aspects of software development processes. In this study, we adopt a configurational perspective of software operations and assess the causal impacts of adopting an organizational policy that grants higher levels of autonomy to project teams. Building on the equifinality framework proposed in organizational studies, we posit that an organizational policy that provides higher levels of autonomy for software teams engenders performance-enhancing adaptations through agile reconfigurations of project operations. To test our hypothesis, we collaborated with a commercial software firm and collected data from a policy experiment at the firm. We examined project level data spanning a four-year observation period during which the firm implemented a new policy that significantly reduced the hurdles for project teams to autonomously reconfigure their operations. The results support our postulation and shed light on how an organizational policy that provides greater autonomy to software teams for designing their context-specific project configurations can improve project performance.

Keywords: Agility, distributed software teams, software processes, project configurations, equifinality, policy experiment, information systems development, software engineering

## Introduction

Firms are increasingly adopting an agile paradigm for their software operations, departing from the erstwhile best practice of organization-wide process homogenization, and the use of standardized methodologies (Ågerfalk et al. 2009; Lee and Xia 2010; Dingsøyr et al. 2012; Ramasubbu et al. 2015). In contrast to structured and plan-oriented work practices that emphasize stringent process controls, the agile paradigm favors providing autonomy to teams for embracing processes “that rapidly or inherently create change, proactively or reactively embrace change, and learn from change while contributing to perceived customer value (economy, quality, and simplicity), through its collective components and relationships with its environment” (Conboy 2009, p. 340). Although process standardization and controls enacted under the planoriented paradigm have been associated with significant improvements in project productivity and software quality in the past (Krishnan et al. 2000; Jiang et al. 2004; Agrawal and Chari 2007; Harter et al. 2012), there is increasing recognition in the information systems development (ISD) literature that recent advancements in flexible approaches prescribed under an agile paradigm of software operations are beneficial to firms (Maruping et al. 2009; Lee and Xia 2010; Ramesh et al. 2012).

To investigate the performance implications of adopting an agile paradigm for software operations, scholars have often prescribed a contingency-based approach that focuses on the alignments between specific business scenarios and software development process characteristics (Shenhar 2001; Slaughter et al. 2006; Austin and Devin 2009). Prior investigations on implications of the agile paradigm for software operations, however, have focused on isolated themes with specific concepts such as pair programming (Balijepally et al. 2009), customer participation (Maruping et al. 2020), trade-offs between flexibility and efficiency (Lee and Xia 2010; Subramanyam et al. 2012), efficacy of control modes (Maruping et al. 2009), process diversity (Ramasubbu et al. 2015), and ambidextrous capabilities (Tiwana 2010; Ramesh et al. 2012). As a result, while much progress has been made on specifying and understanding the unique processes and operational aspects of an agile software development paradigm, there continues to be a dearth of rigorous integrative analysis on performance implications that are caused by conversion to an agile software development paradigm. This study aims to fill this gap in the literature on agile software operations by relying on a real-world policy experiment at a major software firm.

We adopt a configurational view of software operations, which conforms to the theoretical perspective that project (or organizational) performance is influenced by clusters of interconnected work structures and practices (e.g., Gresov 1989; Gresov and Drazin 1997; Payne 2006; O’Leary and Cummings 2007; Kudaravalli et al. 2017). Accordingly, we propose that the level of work dispersion, process diversity, and project governance investments are key constituent choices made by project teams for designing their operational configurations. We posit that when an organization embraces an agile paradigm, project teams are bestowed with higher levels of autonomy, which enables them to reconfigure their operational configurations. Such autonomous reconfigurations can help project teams adapt to their specific environments, which we expect contributes to better performance. In other words, our postulation is that when project teams have the latitude to create and choose context-specific configurational options, they can shift their project operations from a scenario with suboptimal functional trade-offs to a scenario of configurational equifinality that improves overall performance.

To empirically test our hypothesis, we collected a unique, longitudinal dataset from a policy experiment at a global software firm that spanned a 50-month observation period (2013-2018). During this period, the firm experimented with a policy change that targeted the adoption of an agile paradigm for its software operations. The policy experiment marked the beginning of a significant shift for the firm from a centralized, control-oriented governance approach, under which the firm regulated project teams for strict compliance with organizational-level prescriptions, toward granting more autonomy to project teams. Using longitudinal data from 461 commercial projects executed by the firm, we examined the performance implications of the organizational policy change. Our analysis shows strong support for our prediction that an organizational policy granting higher levels of project autonomy would lead to better performance.

Our study makes three primary contributions to the ISD literature. First, drawing on prior studies on organization design (e.g., Gresov 1989; Gresov and Drazin 1997; Payne 2006), we introduce the theoretical idea of equifinality or the potential equivalence of different operational structures to the ISD literature. By utilizing the equifinality framework, we extend the application of contingency thinking to study software project performance by relaxing the long-held implicit assumption regarding one-to-one mapping between specific operational structures and project functions. Second, using a unique dataset from a policy change experiment at a global firm, we test our theoretical prediction and document evidence for the causal impacts of an organizational policy granting higher levels of project autonomy to software teams. Finally, to explain the causal impacts of project autonomy, we demonstrate the application of the equifinality framework by developing a configurational approach to examine software operations. By integrating previously isolated considerations in the ISD literature, we propose work dispersion, process diversity, and project governance, as three key constituent design elements of a project configuration in global software operations. We believe that the configurational approach, coupled with the recognition that operational structures do not equate to project functions, can serve as a robust foundation for empirical ISD studies which aim to shed new light on managing persistent tradeoffs in the software industry. Finally, we leverage our findings to launch a discussion on issues that arise when granting greater autonomy to project teams and how those issues can be managed in software operations.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section, we present our conceptual framework by drawing on configurational and contingency theories in the organization design literature and develop our hypothesis. Then, we describe our data collection and analysis procedures, following which we present the results of our analysis. We conclude by highlighting opportunities for theory development and empirical ISD research on managing projects when software teams enjoy a high level of autonomy.

## Theory and Hypothesis

Applications of contingency theory dominate the literature on software project performance, and prevailing explanations of structured versus agile paradigm choices for firms’ software operations typically focus on the fit between specific environmental variables and the structural characteristics of the firms (Shenhar 2001; Slaughter et al. 2006; Austin and Devin 2009). An implicit assumption in the extant contingency analysis of software project performance is the presence of a one-to-one correspondence between project structures and project functions.<sup>2</sup> As a result, a typical contingency theory application includes hypothesizing pair-wise linkages between specific contingencies and project structures (e.g., requirements uncertainty and use of pair programming), conducting empirical assessments of fit of observed linkages, and then drawing comparisons with theorized ideal profiles of fit. Such an approach, however, fails to consider the possibility that multiple project structures and functions may be combined and/or substitute for each other to influence project performance (Gresov 1989; Doty et al. 1993; Nidumolu and Subramani 2003; Ramasubbu et al. 2015). For example, effective application testing and higher code coverage in software operations may be achieved via multiple structural alternatives, either using pair programming, a conventional task allocation scheme, or a combination of these approaches. To overcome this drawback, organizational theorists recommend relaxing the one-to-one conceptual mapping between operational structures and functions, and recognizing the possibility that structures that are drastically different in their designs may yield equivalent functions and equifinal performance (Gresov and Drazin 1997). We follow this recommendation to develop the conceptual framework for our study.

Figure 1 presents a conceptual framework, which is an instantiation of Gresov and Drazin’s (1997) classification scheme, for describing the functional equivalence scenarios that unfold in software operations. The vertical dimension represents the degree of conflicting demands imposed on the software operations of a project. Some potentially conflicting demands documented in the software literature are client expectations regarding high productivity and lower numbers of conformance errors (Krishnan et al. 2000; MacCormack et al. 2003), high efficiency and responsiveness to change (Lee and Xia 2010; Subramanyam et al. 2012), and usage of custom features and easy upgradeability (Ramasubbu and Kemerer 2016). The horizontal dimension in Figure 1 is the range of structural options available to project teams for enacting their operational functions. For instance, a restrictive organizational policy that requires all project teams to utilize standardized methodologies and process templates provides a lower number of structural options. In contrast, project teams that enjoy higher levels of autonomy to use a diverse set of process templates or to reconfigure themselves during a project’s lifecycle possess a larger set of structural options to enact their operational functions.

Quadrant 1 in Figure 1 represents a scenario where specific operational structures are standardized across a firm and prescribed as ideal for project operations. In such scenarios, firms have been known to adopt a centralized governance framework for software operations and exert stringent control over project-level processes (Nidumolu and Subramani 2003; Ramasubbu et al. 2008). For example, such firms are known to design standardized process templates for their projects and monitor project teams for compliance with prescribed organizational standards (Ramasubbu et al. 2015). Such centralized governance and control schemes ensure that project teams make use of the ideal structural option that is shown to improve project performance when conflicting demands on project operations are low (Harter et al. 2000; Jiang et al. 2004; Agrawal and Chari 2007).

When the degree of conflicting demands on project operations increases, however, teams with fewer structural options (quadrant 2 in Figure 1) are subject to trade-offs between competing functions. For example, project teams facing volatile customer requirements and guidelines regarding strict adherence to pre-established resources or schedules would need to accept a trade-off between their planned functionality deployment and lower customer satisfaction. The presence of such trade-offs has been reported to lead to lower performance (Gresov and Drazin 1997; Payne 2006). To counter this, software organizations can respond by adopting an agile paradigm and endowing more autonomy to individual project teams. Teams that leverage greater autonomy to create additional structural options would be able to reconfigure their operations and adapt to the environmental changes that they face. Thus, higher levels of project autonomy can help teams shift their operations to scenarios represented in Quadrants 3 and 4 in Figure 1.

In the scenario represented in Quadrant 3 of Figure 1, project teams face a lower degree of conflicting demands in their operations, but they possess multiple structural options. Given these multiple options, project teams are likely to choose between structural alternatives based on their specific skills, prior experience, and preferences, leading to a natural fit between team-level capabilities and operational structures and thus resulting in overall higher project performance. Project teams facing the scenario in Quadrant 4 also possess multiple structural alternatives, but they face conflicting operational demands that give rise to functional trade-offs. Thus, project teams may choose among several structural options to emphasize certain functions that they believe can lead to higher performance. We refer to these operational structure-function choices as project configurations. Unlike the ideal profile scenario (Quadrant 1 in Figure 1), multiple project configurations may lead to equifinal performance and the specific configuration choice of a team reflects just one way that the team may adapt to its environment. Moreover, in contrast to the suboptimal scenario (Quadrant 2 in Figure 1), project teams with multiple configurational options can reconfigure their project operations in suitable ways to adapt to environmental changes and address demands that may arise from potential shifts in functional trade-offs. Thus, project teams in the equifinal configuration scenario can reap higher performance even when their configurational choices deviate from the ideal profile recommended by their organization.

In summary, when organizations shift from prescribing a centrally controlled ideal profile to an agile paradigm, they endow software teams with higher levels of autonomy to create multiple options for their project configurations. Leveraging higher levels of autonomy, project teams would adapt to specific environmental changes by reconfiguring and choosing among the multiple configurational options that they deem fit to create value. Hence, compared to the structured and centrally controlled development paradigm (i.e., regulated use of an ideal profile), we expect the use of an agile paradigm that endows greater autonomy to project teams to result in higher levels of project performance. We present this theoretical expectation as a formal hypothesis for empirical examination:

Hypothesis: All else being equal, providing higher autonomy to individual project teams to design their operational configurations will improve their project performance.

## Policy Experiment and Data Collection

We collaborated with a leading global software services vendor firm and collected data from a policy experiment that the firm undertook. Work dispersion occurred in the firm’s projects because of the worldwide operations for deployment of the firm’s software services at various client locations and its employment of more than 125,000 software developers at different development centers around the globe. The firm is widely considered to be a leader in software process innovations and all development centers of the firm were assessed at the Level-5 of the CMMI development model.<sup>3</sup>

During the observation period of our study, the firm was aggressively expanding its business and undertook an internally focused initiative to revamp its software operations. As part of this business growth initiative, the firm enacted an important policy change in one of its business divisions in the second quarter of 2015, which enabled software teams in the division to more easily reconfigure their project operations. Prior to the policy change, the firm had prescribed an ideal profile for all project operations, which was based on the CMMI framework and a centralized governance approach. Project-level compliance to the prescribed profile was monitored closely through a centralized unit, the Software Engineering Process Group (SEPG), whose director reported to the chief operating officer of the firm. As part of the policy change, the requirement to stringently comply with the prescribed ideal profile was relaxed, and individual project teams were granted autonomy to design and use operational configurations that they perceived to be more suitable for their environments. Thus, the firm experimented with a move toward an agile paradigm for its software operations.

This policy experiment provided an ideal opportunity for examining how functional equivalence occurs in project configurations and for collecting data to test our hypothesis on the performance consequences caused by project autonomy. We note that the policy change decision was made by the firm’s executive management team and we did not have the opportunity to randomize the policy “treatment” to individual projects. We were, however, granted permission under the purview of a nondisclosure agreement to observe data on 461 projects across a 50-month period between 2013 and 2018. Of the 461 projects, 146 projects were in the treatment group or had operated with greater levels of project autonomy under the new policy of the firm. The remaining projects (N=315) constituted a matched control sample that we requested from the firm. Matching was performed using a nearest-neighbor approach with pre-treatment covariate variables, after ensuring that all projects belonged to the same industry and had similar lifecycles (starting dates and contractual duration). The firm shared quarterly, project-level data for 16 quarters in the form of archival logs in XML format, downloaded from the firm’s internal business intelligence system. We parsed the XML logs to derive variables and collate the dataset for our analysis.

To parsimoniously operationalize a project’s configuration in the firm’s context, we employed three important dimensions that managers at the firm considered for designing their project operations: (1) work dispersion, (2) software process diversity, and (3) project governance. Work dispersion arising from the spatial and temporal separation of project team members was an important consideration for project managers because it influenced the way that project tasks were structured and coordination and division of labor were achieved. These factors have been recognized as important drivers of performance in the ISD literature (Sarker and Sahay 2004; Espinosa et al. 2007; O’Leary and Cummings 2007; Langer et al. 2014). To accommodate the spectrum of options that an autonomous project team would have for its software process designs (e.g., from structured to lean software processes), we used software process diversity as the second configurational dimension (cf. Ramasubbu et al. 2015). Finally, as recommended by prior studies on project controls (e.g., Kirsch 1997; Choudhury and Sabherwal 2003), we considered investments in project governance activities that facilitate managerial control as the third key design dimension of a project’s operational configuration.

We utilized value added and customer satisfaction as our performance measures and included a host of environmental project characteristics as control variables in our models. Value added is an appropriate measure of performance in our context, as it measures the extent to which an existing system is functionally enhanced by the efforts of a project team, after controlling for other relevant factors, including the size and quality of the original software code base, team size, and other project configuration parameters. To ensure the robustness of our findings, we utilized customer satisfaction as an alternate measure of project performance and replicated our analysis. In ISD studies, a log-linear formulation of the software production function is typically used to predict project outcomes (e.g., Banker et al. 1991; Kemerer 1995; Krishnan et al. 2000; Ramasubbu et al. 2008). Accordingly, we logtransformed the variables in our regression analysis.

To recall, we did not have permission at the research site to randomize the treatment assignment. Hence, to estimate the causal performance implications of the new policy that granted higher levels of project autonomy, we used three different approaches that are relevant for examining such quasi-experimental data: (1) difference-in-differences (DID) estimation, (2) generalized synthetic control (GSC) approach, and (3) causal impact analysis using Bayesian structural time series (BST) approach.

A key identification assumption for DID analysis is that the performance outcomes of treated and control projects, on average, would have followed parallel trends in the absence of the policy change treatment at the firm. It is possible that there are unobserved time-varying confounders in the empirical context that lead to a violation of the underlying identification assumption of the DID approach. We approach this problem in the following ways. First, as recommended by Abadie (2005), we conditioned on pretreatment observable variables using matching in order to balance the influence of potential time-varying confounders between the treated and control project groups. Second, as proposed by Xu (2017), we employed the GSC approach to derive artificial or synthetic counterfactuals for all treated projects by considering the pretreatment performance outcomes of the treated projects as a benchmark to reweight the actual projects available in the control group. In the GSC approach, we did not discard any observations from the control group and were able to compare multiple treated projects with synthetic controls in a single run, which is more efficient than synthetic control methods that consider unitary treated units (Abadie et al. 2010). Finally, to accommodate the time-varying influence of contemporaneous covariates throughout the study’s observation time period (16 quarters) and to infer the temporal evolution of the causal impact of the policy change at the research site, we used the modeling approach proposed by Brodersen et al (2015)<sup>5</sup>. In this approach, a Bayesian structural time-series (BST) model is first constructed using the time series of performance of a focal treated project and a set of control time series (i.e., performance of projects in the control group). The BST model is used to derive a counterfactual, and posterior inference is then performed on the counterfactual.

## Why the Increase in Project Performance?

Given the strong evidence of performance-enhancing benefits of the policy change granting higher levels of project autonomy, a natural question arises with respect to the underlying mechanisms that can explain these performance improvements. Our conjecture is that project teams in the treated group utilized their newly granted autonomy to adapt and efficiently restructure their project operations. Since we did not randomize treatment assignments during the policy experiment, the potential to uncover causal evidence for the posited mediation mechanism is limited. To motivate further research, we present exploratory evidence for the underlying mechanisms using an analysis of the intermediate outcomes and observed equifinality shifts. We present evidence for our conjecture by first showing how the policy change treatment affected the three dimensions of a project configuration: (1) work dispersion, (2) process diversity, and (3) project governance, and then demonstrating how these adaptations collectively shifted the projects to a state of configurational equifinality.

In Table A1 of the Appendix, we present the results of three models where we estimate the effects of policy change on the three selected dimensions of project configurations. Our results indicate that projects in the treatment group increased their work dispersion levels by about 5% and process diversity levels by about 8%, on average. These increases indicate a significant deviation from the prescribed levels of the ideal profile configuration that was recommended by the firm in the pretreatment epoch. Even as work dispersion and process diversity levels increased during the project reconfigurations, the treated project teams decreased their project governance investments by about 5%, on average, and were still able to comply with the organizational standards for project delivery. These results collectively indicate that reconfigurations undertaken by the autonomous project teams were efficient. Thus, we highlight that the configurational adaptations undertaken by the project teams formed a key link between the adoption of an agile paradigm for software operations and eventual performance outcomes.

To examine the shift in equifinality across policy regimes, we used multivariate cluster analysis to identify different clusters of operational configurations among the treated projects, and then used the cluster membership information to predict project performance in the two policy regimes. The details of cluster analysis and related results are presented in the Equifinality among Project Clusters section of the Appendix. The results show that in the old policy regime, the ideal profile approach suited just one cluster of projects and the performance of three other clusters of projects was significantly lower relative to the ideal-profile cluster of projects. In contrast, under the new policy regime, differences in performance between projects in the ideal profile cluster and each of the three other clusters were statistically insignificant (p-value > 0.10). This indicates that the adoption of the new organizational policy, which facilitated operational reconfigurations with greater autonomy, helped project teams shift away from suboptimal operational configurations (Figure 1, Quadrant 2) and toward configurational equifinality (Figure 1, Quadrant 4).

## Discussion

While organizational studies have long acknowledged the importance of functional equivalence in different organization designs (Gresov 1989; Doty et al. 1993; Gresov and Drazin 1997; Payne 2006), the concept of equifinality has not taken root in the ISD literature. An important contribution of this research is the application of the equifinality framework to assess the performance effects of an organizational decision to grant greater autonomy to software project teams. Empirical evidence on the effects of such policy decisions by organizations is rare (Bloom et al. 2013), and our contribution is grounded in utilizing a policy experiment at a large, global software firm. In contrast to a one-size-fits-all ideal profile approach, we find that a policy granting higher levels of autonomy to project teams improves performance and engenders configurational equifinality in software operations. Therefore, we recommend that software firms take a holistic view of their projects’ environmental context when making project configuration choices, rather than deploy rigid, standardized rules for organizing their project work. Such an approach moves beyond enforcing compliance with an organizationally prescribed, ideal profile and grants greater autonomy to individual teams for creating structural and functional options, and for reconfiguring those options in an agile way when new project situations arise.

Our results also provide an empirical basis to identify the underlying sources of performance improvements when project teams enjoy a higher level of autonomy. In the words of a client account manager at our research site, granting higher autonomy and the recognition of equifinality in operational configurations engenders a beneficial problemsolving culture in project teams:

[After we transitioned to the new policy,] it was a relief to see the number of escalations go down. We resolve issues in production without having to worry about [software engineering process group] audits. Our team has certainly become more energized to focus on improving systems and client relationships (Client Account Manager, interview transcript)

It is possible that higher levels of project autonomy may not necessarily guarantee the elimination of inefficiencies in all situations. We note that the performance-enhancing trends we have reported happened in a high maturity process environment; the firm pursued process standardization and cultivated a culture of continuous improvement for over two decades. It is unclear if project teams that are embedded in other low process maturity environments would be able to leverage higher levels of autonomy for choosing beneficial functional and/or structural options. Thus, further research is needed to examine the performance implications of policies granting greater autonomy to project teams in a variety of organizational settings before we can broadly generalize our findings.

Overall, our study raises an important consideration for future theory development in the context of configurational equifinality: When granting higher levels of autonomy to software teams, how can performance-enhancing triggers be preserved and performance-degrading triggers be mitigated? To shed light on this question, we believe there is a need to apply the proposed equifinality framework to the study of project controls. Adopting a configurational view of project controls would help to unearth the trade-offs and functional equivalences of various project control configurations.

In addition, we highlight two risk mitigation mechanisms that were mentioned by practitioners at our research site: (1) improving transparency of project information, and (2) facilitating social learning. In a centralized governance and ideal profile approach (Quadrant 1 in Figure 1), it is customary to institute role-based access to important information, such as client contractual terms and long-term operational goals. Executives with access to this information can then design routines and formal controls to ensure the alignment of project operations with desired outcomes. However, as teams transition from the ideal profile toward developing more structural options for their operations, there is a need to democratize access to strategic information to avoid suboptimal choices. Improving transparency of project information would help autonomous project teams to manage their trade-offs in a way that aligns with organizational goals. A project manager at our site shared her experience, which reflects the need to improve greater transparency:

In the initial months [after adopting the new policy that granted higher autonomy], we made several incoherent decisions in prioritizing and scheduling the release of user-requested features. Individual team members had the autonomy to develop and deliver features, but the delivered packages to end-users didn’t reflect an overall integrated improvement to the business functions due to a lack of coherence between the delivered features. We convened a joint workshop to identify the root causes of client dissatisfaction and subsequently developed a “balanced scorecard” approach that showed the overall goals of the project and the contractually mandated system improvements to all teams. Like a Gantt chart, the scorecards also displayed the overall progress of all “story points” pursued by developers and their dependencies with each other. When teams work with clients and make decisions, the ability to have a global view of the engagement is essential. (Project Manager, interview transcript)

Another practice we noticed at our research site involved the enhancement of social learning among autonomously functioning teams. For instance, metrics reported by the project teams had a “lessons learned” section, which was used to develop cases and was archived in the firm’s knowledge management system. The contact information of the contributing team members and their contextual notes, in addition to the quantitative project metrics, were preserved in the organization’s memory. Individual developers could access and search the knowledge management system to identify similar cases, reach out to contributing authors, and learn from the prior experience of other project teams. This approach contributed to enhanced clan control among the distributed sites and the development of shared experiences and socially enforced norms (Kirsch 1997; Maruping 2009). The following reflection by a programmer at the research site illustrates how the social learning approach can help prevent suboptimal choices:

We were always a metrics-based company. Now, we have the ability to add our “voice” to the metrics that we report. Apart from the usual project management metrics, we are now able to access stories and personal experiences, which I find to be an invaluable source of guidance when I am uncertain about how to proceed with a client request (Programmer, interview transcript)

The practitioner insights that we gathered through field interviews complement our quantitative findings and support our call for further research on designing project control configurations and governance processes that are suitable for agile software teams.

In this study, we established the performance-enhancing effects of adopting an agile paradigm for software operations and granting greater autonomy to project teams. In doing so, we broaden the theoretical domain of ISD research to consider equifinality in project configurations. We expect this to spur future ISD research studies that embrace a configurational perspective and recognize the presence of equifinality in project configurations while examining the interrelationships between the design of software processes, team autonomy, and control portfolios.

## Equifinality among Project Clusters

To examine the shift in equifinality across policy regimes, we used multivariate cluster analysis to identify different clusters of operational configurations among the treated projects. Drawing on prior research, we deployed a combination of hierarchical clustering and K-means clustering (Payne 2006). Our stopping rule logic emphasized large Harabasz pseudo-F values, large Duda–Hart Je(2)/Je(1) values, and small Duda–Hart pseudo-T-squared values, as prescribed in the literature (Milligan and Cooper 1985). This approach to cluster projects using pretreatment information resulted in four distinct clusters of operational configurations. To examine the association between these clusters and project performance, we performed a regression analysis with value added as the dependent variable and included the cluster membership information as predictors for the two policy regime periods.

The results of our analysis are presented in Table A2. Panels A and B in Table A2 pertain to the old and new policy regimes, respectively. Panel A shows that membership in Project Clusters 2, 3, 4 have significant negative coefficients, whereas Cluster 1 has a significant and positive coefficient. Tests of average differences in value added between projects in Cluster 1 and each of the Clusters 2, 3, and 4 are statistically significant at p-value < 0.01. This result shows that while the ideal profile approach suits Cluster 1 projects well, the organizationally prescribed configuration for projects in clusters 2, 3, and 4 is sub-optimal, and their performance is underwhelming relative to Cluster 1 projects. Given the limited structural options available during the pre-treatment period, teams in Project Clusters 2, 3, and 4 fac constraints, relative to project teams in Cluster 1, in managing trade-offs in their respective environments.

In contrast, the results in Panel B of Table A2 show that the performance of projects in Clusters 2, 3, 4 are not significantly different from those in Cluster 1 in the new policy regime. Tests of average differences in value added between projects in Cluster 1 and each of Clusters 2, 3, and 4 are statistically insignificant (p-value > 0.10). This indicates that membership in pre-treatment project Clusters 2, 3, and 4, which faced suboptimal trade-off scenarios, is no longer a significant predictor of performance differences relative to Cluster 1 projects. These results reveal that the policy change induced a shift from an organizational environment of suboptimal operational configurations towards configurational equifinality.
