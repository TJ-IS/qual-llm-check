# PRODUCING THE “WE” IN HIGH-RISK ONLINE ACTIVISM: IDENTITY CONFIGURATIONS IN MY STEALTHY FREEDOM<sup>1</sup>

Mahya Ostovar

Lero—The Science Foundation Ireland Research Centre for Software, J.E. Cairnes School of Business & Economics University of Galway, Galway, IRELAND {mahya.ostovar@universityofgalway.ie}

Ulrike Schultze

University of Groningen,

Groningen, THE NETHERLANDS {u.schultze@rug.nl}

The research on online social movements generally concludes that collective identity, i.e., the sense of weness that individual protesters in a movement share, is not only unattainable but also dispensable, even though it is considered a defining feature of traditional movements. In this paper, we explore one of the boundary conditions of these findings, namely the riskiness of protest practices. Analysing the high-risk social movement, My Stealthy Freedom (MySF), which contests compulsory hijab in Iran in a way that hybridizes online and offline protest practices, we show that a sense of collectiveness can be instantiated in online social movements, why it is critical to the success of high-risk activism, and how it is (re)produced. Comparing and contrasting three instantiations of MySF, each of which was enacted on a different social media platform, we develop a theoretical model of how feelings of collectiveness are enacted in high-risk online activism. In addition to providing guidance for online movements where collective identity is desirable, our study challenges prior research on online activism by theorizing the role of embodiment, affect, and the dialectic between activists’ personal and the movement’s collective identity.

Keywords: Collective identity, personal identity, social media, hybridity, social movement, practice, identity configuration

## Introduction

Social movements, that is “networks of informal interactions between a plurality of individuals, groups and/or organizations, engaged in political or cultural conflicts, on the basis of shared collective identities” (Diani, 1992, p. 1), increasingly rely on social media. They have therefore garnered considerable attention in IS research (e.g., Vaast et al., 2017; Venkatesan et al., 2021). Despite the diversity of online activism under investigation in IS, extant studies have yet to theorize the role of risk. High-risk activism, e.g., Arab Spring (Charrad & Reith, 2019), entails the performance of protest such that activists expose themselves to “anticipated dangers—whether legal, social, physical, financial” (McAdam, 1986, p. 67). In addition to the immediate cost that protesters generally incur when they perform acts of resistance (e.g., opportunity cost of time spent in a march or sit-in), highrisk activists expose themselves to uncertain future costs, i.e., risks (Wiltfang & McAdam, 1991).

With few exceptions, e.g., Berish movement in Malaysia (Leong et al., 2020), most of the movements examined in IS research thus far can be classified as low-risk. For example, by (re)tweeting digital content, the activists in the Gulf of Mexico oil spill movement incurred minimal cost/risk (Vaast et al., 2017). However, in the protests after a fatal rape in India (Tarafdar & Kajal Ray, 2021), as well as those for LGBTQ rights (McKenna, 2020), participants incurred physical (e.g., injury), socioemotional (e.g., public humiliation), financial (e.g., threatening work environment), and other harms. While none of these studies theorize risk explicitly, risk implicitly manifests in the type of identity made salient. In the high-risk movement explored by Tarafdar and Kajal Ray (2021), feelings of solidarity, which are indicative of collective identity (Stewart & Schultze, 2019), emerged as a key success factor. In contrast, Vaast et al.’s (2017) research on the lowrisk Gulf of Mexico oil spill movement emphasized personal identity, i.e., personalized expressions of individual protesters’ perspectives and sense of self, as central to online activism (Bennett & Segerberg, 2011).

Theorizing risk in online activism is important because collective identity, i.e., “a shared and interactive sense of weness” (Snow & Corrigall-Brown, 2015, p. 174), plays a critical role in the enactment of high-risk movements (McAdam, 2022). It motivates individuals to join a risky movement (McAdam & Paulsen, 1993), to construct activist identities (Gamson, 1991), and to escalate the riskiness of their activism (Loveman, 1998). However, collective identity is deemed to be both unattainable and unnecessary in online activism (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012), as social media use mobilizes activists through their personal beliefs and meanings, rather than collectively negotiated, shared ones (Gamson, 1992).

The objective of our study is to unpack the relationships between technology mediation, risk, and collective identity in online activism. We seek to gain insight into the boundary conditions of the extant theorizing of collective identity’s role in online movements by addressing the question: How is a sense of collectiveness produced in the online performance of highrisk activism? To do so, we examined My Stealthy Freedom (MySF), a high-risk movement that voices women’s opposition to compulsory hijab in Iran. Hijab is the practice of veiling by which Muslim women cover their bodies and hair, except for their faces, in the presence of men outside their immediate family. Enacted in physical space (e.g., women taking photos of themselves in public) and on three social media platforms, i.e., Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter, MySF exemplifies the hybridization of embodied and virtual protest practices.

We theorize this empirical context by adopting a performative view of identity (Schultze, 2010) and a configurational approach (Mazmanian et al., 2014) to the I/we dialectic that characterizes the relationship between personal and collective identity. Comparing and contrasting the practices performed in the three MySF instantiations, we identify identity configurations capable of (re)producing a sense of we-ness in high-risk, online activism and develop a conceptual model of how these configurations are (re)created.

Our study offers a number of contributions. First, by theorizing what role collective identity plays in high-risk online social movements and how it is generated, we suggest that conclusions about collective identity’s irrelevance to online activism require further scrutiny. Second, we abductively develop a conceptual model for theorizing how individual and collective identities are intertwined into configurations that exhibit a sense of we-ness. Third, we identify three movementlevel practices that support the development of collectiveness. These practices are particularly relevant to social movement organizations operating in repressive regimes but also to movements in liberal political systems where activists may be subject to risk, e.g., reputational risks faced by participants in the #MeToo movement.

The paper proceeds as follows: We introduce high-risk activism and the role of collective identity therein and review the literature on collective identity in online activism. We then provide an overview of our ontological stance on individual and collective identity. After describing our empirical case, as well as our data collection and analysis strategies, we present our theoretically informed rendition of the three instantiations of the MySF movement. Comparing and contrasting the protest practices and identity configurations of the three instantiations, we then answer our research question and present our theoretical model of how identity configurations capable of (re)creating a sense of collectiveness are produced. We conclude by contemplating the paper’s contributions to the literatures on high-risk online activism and collective identity in online activism as well as to practice.

## Risk and Identity in Social Movements

## High-Risk Social Movement

The distinction between high- and low-risk activism is defined in terms of costs and risks. Costs are “the expenditures of time, money, and energy” (McAdam, 1986, p. 67) or any negative effects (e.g., fatigue) experienced during participation in a movement. In contrast, risks are “anticipated dangers—whether legal, social, physical, financial, etc” (McAdam, 1986, p. 67) resulting from activism. As “the activists’ subjective anticipation or expectation of a cost that they may incur as a result of their movement participation (e.g., being arrested, paying a fine, being beaten, tortured, or killed)” (Wiltfang & McAdam, 1991, p. 989), risks are perceived future costs. The riskiness of activism can be conceptualized as a multidimensional continuum on which the costs and risks vary not only by degree (from low to high), but also by the type of harms, specifically physical (e.g., arrest, death), socioemotional (e.g., stigma, harassment), or financial (e.g., job or property loss). In high-risk movements, participants expect to incur high immediate and/or high future costs.

As risks “depend not only on the activist’s own actions, but on others’ responses to the activist’s actions” (Wiltfang & McAdam, 1991, p. 989), the context in which activism occurs plays an important role. For example, for women identifying as victims of sexual abuse in the #MeToo movement, the socioemotional costs (e.g., embarrassment) and risks (e.g., bullying, job loss) varied considerably across cultures and countries (Starkey et al., 2019). Similarly, signing an Amnesty International petition online (Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016) is generally low risk in Western countries, but the same act can result in significant negative consequences in repressive regimes (Opp & Gern, 1993). Indeed, state repression is one of the main determinants of a movement’s riskiness (Almeida, 2018), as even peaceful forms of protest (e.g., church mobilization in El Salvador— Gamson, 1991) are likely to provoke violent responses from the regime and possibly fellow citizens.

## Collective Identity in High-Risk Social Movement

Adopting McAdam’s (2022, p. 1) definition of high-risk activism as “a cyclical process by which increasing integration into the activist community leads to both a greater identification with the aims and collective identity of the movement and ever riskier and/or costlier forms of activism,” we note that collective identity plays a key role in (1) joining the movement, (2) constructing an activist identity, and (3) escalating the riskiness of protest.

Joining: Since the perceived riskiness of activism impedes recruitment and participation (Shriver, 2000), collective identity presents the movement as a cohesive body that affords some degree of protection to activists. The collective becomes a safe space (Gamson, 1991), that is, a physical and/or figurative site relatively free from threatening others, such that marginalized individuals can engage in identity development, as well as garner emotional connections and support from the people with whom they share a common fate and identity (Wallin-Ruschman & Patka, 2016). Such spaces are continuously enacted through a set of ritualized practices that challenge actual and imagined fear (The Roestone Collective, 2014).

In threatening environments, there is not only a greater need for safe spaces (Fetner et al., 2012), but also elevated levels of distrust among the strangers that make up a movement. Safety practices, e.g., preserving individuals’ anonymity (Ho, 2024), allow movements to create trust networks in which activists not only share an interest in minimizing their respective risk but also have their emotions and values validated by like-minded others. This generates positive emotions, promotes trust, and reduces the perceived risk of protesting (Drury, 2020). Moreover, seeing the large number of fellow activists willing to sacrifice their safety and well-being for a cause unleashes a process of “collective empowerment” (Hirsch, 1990) that emboldens protesters; when they feel that their actions are imbued with the power to effect the sought-after social change, the costs they incur become easier to justify.

Constructing activist identity: Risk strengthens the view of the movement as a moral community that is unjustly threatened (Jasper, 2008) and generates collective emotions such as solidarity (Honari, 2018), i.e., feelings of devotion and commitment to others in a collective (Stewart & Schultze, 2019). This reciprocal commitment allows activists to respond to threats as “an angry group rather than as isolated, frightened individuals” (Hirsch, 1990, p. 245). This sense of one-ness is indicative of activists’ identities being shaped through “deepening ideological socialization” and “integration into the activist network” (McAdam, 1986, p. 69).

As individuals become activists, they increasingly see their fate tied to that of the collective. Believing that the cost of protest should be collectively shared, they feel a moral obligation to enact protest despite their risks (Hirsch, 1990). When such commitment to the group is individuated and personalized through bonding, i.e., layering a personal, affective dimension over the relationships among activists (Ho, 2024), the collective is more likely to be regarded as “worthy” of the cost of enacting high-risk protest practices (Hirsch, 1990).

Escalating risk: For high-risk activism to be effective over time, the protest practices must become increasingly risky (McAdam, 1986, 2022). Risk escalation occurs both at the individual and group levels. At the individual level, newcomers should be able to perform relatively safe protest first and graduate to increasingly risky practices (Wiltfang & McAdam, 1991) as they come to identify with the collective and their role as activists (Loveman, 1998).

Hirsch (1990) shows that activists’ dramatic performances of self-sacrifice (e.g., hunger strike) can evoke feelings of shame in bystanders. Comparing their own protest with the much higher costs incurred by the self-sacrificing activists leads individuals to confront the cost of their inaction. They regard leaving their self-sacrificing peers “to face the wrath of authorities alone” (Hirsch, 1990, p. 250) as morally reprehensible. Feelings of shame then compel them not only to engage in risky protest practices, but also to escalate the riskiness of their protest practices, thus narrowing the gap between them and their self-sacrificing peers.

## Collective Identity in Online Activism

Online activism is frequently associated with low cost/risk (Van Laer & Van Aelst, 2010). Derogatory labels such as clicktivism (Halupka, 2018) and slacktivism (Kristofferson et al., 2014) capture the “low-risk, low-cost activity via social media, whose purpose is to raise awareness, produce change, or grant satisfaction to the person engaged in the activity” (Rotman et al., 2011, p. 3). As contemporary social movements increasingly rely on social media, globally distributed activists experience neither a common “now” (Kavada, 2010) nor a shared space in which they can move with and become attuned to one another (Lee & Ingold, 2006). Instead, online activists perform individualized protest in isolation and share their personal experiences on their social media pages. This privileges personal identity (i.e., their sense of self and individuality—Suh et al., 2011) over collective identity (Bennett, 2012).

Technology-mediated collective action is theorized as giving way to connective action where “the identity reference is more derived through inclusive and diverse large-scale personal expression rather than through common group or ideological identification” (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012, p. 744). Amnesty International (Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016) and Occupy Wall Street (Kavada, 2015) exemplify connective action; people join easily in the coordinated performance of contentious action despite holding beliefs and meanings at odds with those of their fellow activists. With online movements becoming less “singular, unified, homogeneous, coherent, and mass” (Kavada, 2016, p. 8), membership prototypes are ambiguated, thus challenging a sense of collectiveness (Jarvenpaa & Selander, 2023).

Collective and personal identities are reconfigured in online activism as users can increase their self-expression and relax their affiliation with the group (Milan, 2015). Thus, “the ‘we’ is experienced through the ‘I,’ and not vice versa [as in the past]” (Milan, 2013, p. 72): “What changed [with online activism] is the role played by the group, as visibility results in a spiral process that originates and ends within the individual, rather than dissolving into the group… visibility creates individuals-in-thegroup rather than groups” (Milan, 2015, p. 7, italics added).

Most research on social movements takes for granted that sense of we-ness is not only unlikely to arise online but is also dispensable (e.g., McDonald, 2015). However, this conclusion is based on viewing online activism as diametrically opposed to traditional movements, which rely on physically embodied protesters willing to incur costs. Activism that hybridizes onand offline protest practices, where protesters’ bodies are possibly at risk, is rarely studied (exceptions include Mercea, 2012) and remains untheorized.

## I/We Dialectic

People have two competing urges: individuation vs. attachment and self-preservation vs. self-alienation (Zahavi, 2018). These form a dialectic, that is, a relationship where the opposing poles (e.g., I vs. we) cannot exist independently but rather complete one another (Buckham, 1936). For example, individuals rely on others to validate their identity performances such that the focal actor’s self is brought into being (Schultze & Brooks, 2019). Similarly, for a collective to exist, it needs individuals that share something in common. The temporarily stabilized intertwining of personal and collective identities that represents a momentary synthesis of the I/we dialectic forms different configurations, e.g., individuals-in-the-group (Milan, 2015). Prior to developing a conceptualization of how such configurations emerge, we outline the ontological assumptions of identity adopted in this paper.

## Performative Personal Identity

There are two dominant ontological perspectives on identity: the representational and performative view. The representational view of personal identity, which dominates IS, sees individuals as agential, goal-oriented actors, i.e., doers behind the deed (Hickey-Moody & Wood, 2008) who have the capacity to direct their behaviour to produce an intended, coherent self-image. In their study of users’ identification with their avatar, Suh et al. (2011) adopted a representational view, defining personal identity as “the totality of the individual’s thoughts and feelings having reference to himself as an object” (p. 713). Similarly, IS research that adopts Goffman’s (1959) impression management theory (e.g., Leonardi & Treem, 2012; Vaast, 2007) assumes that identity performances are directed by a stable self.

However, individuals’ sayings and doings, as well as identity claims and statements, are not simply representational; they are constitutive of selves and situations. Performativity (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2014; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014) is the theoretical lens adopted here. It acknowledges that knowledgeproducing practices are not merely re-presenting reality but also producing the reality they purport to reflect. For example, a performative utterance (e.g., “I now pronounce you man and wife”) made under specific circumstances (e.g., in a church, by a religious official), does not merely describe an action, but also enacts a certain reality (e.g., a legally binding marriage) with material consequences (Austin, 1975).

The performative view sees an individual’s sense of self as an effortful accomplishment produced in and through sociomaterial doings and saying (Butler, 1993; Ibarra & Barbulescu, 2010). Identity is thus the result—rather than the source—of the practices one performs. For example, Mahmood (2001) argues that the everyday practice of veiling in Islamic society produces feelings of shyness and piety, which results in a sense of self as a “good Muslim woman,” the idealized female identity promoted by the Islamic Republic in Iran, for instance.

Others play a key role in performatively producing an individual’s identity (Schultze & Brooks, 2019), as an individual’s self is only brought into existence when others validate it by responding appropriately (e.g., laughing at a joke) to the focal actor’s identity performance. When an identity performance is ignored, the focal actor’s self fails to come into existence. Such performative failures or misfires (Callon, 2010) do not allow “the individual to fully realize the concept [of self] and lay claim to ontological status” (Green, 2007, p. 33).

## Performative Collective Identity

These ontological perspectives of personal identity have a corollary in collective identity; Fominaya (2018) distinguishes between a representational “product” and a performative “process” view. Most social movement research adopts the “product” view (Fominaya, 2018), defining collective identity as “a sort of public good produced by movements and available to everyone” (p. 434). This product view of collective identity is inadequate when theorizing contemporary movements, especially in light of their diverse ideological and organizational bases and the heterogeneous identities of their activists. More appropriate is a “process” view of collective identity, where the “reciprocal identification of shared belonging come[s] about through a whole way of doing politics, located in the movement subcultural milieu, rather than common interests, ideologies, or given identities” (Fominaya, 2018, p. 473).

From the process-oriented, performative perspective, social movements are “action systems … not entities that move with the unity of goals attributed to them by their ideologues or opponents” (Melucci, 1995, p. 53). Collective identity is thus “this process of ‘constructing’ an action system” (Melucci, 1995, p. 44), i.e., “a learning process that leads to the formation and maintenance of a unified empirical actor that we can call a social movement” (p. 49). The sense of we-ness performatively produced is “fluid and relational, emerging out of interactions with a number of different audiences (bystanders, allies, opponents, news media, state authorities)” (Polletta & Jasper, 2001, p. 298).

To study performative identity in the context of activism, a focus on protest practices is required. Protest practices are “open, spatially-temporally dispersed sets of doings and sayings organized by common understandings, teleo-activities (ends, tasks, emotions), and rules” (Schatzki, 2016, p. 32) through which activists express their opposition to the status quo. Protest practices are sociomaterial (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008), implying that typical divisions between human vs. material agency, on- vs. offline protest, or individual vs. collective actors are eschewed in favor of relationality and entanglement (Nyberg, 2009; Scott & Orlikowski, 2014). This conceptual infrastructure is well suited for theorizing hybrid protest practices that integrate physically embodied and virtual protest practices.

As the enactment of protest practices implies joint tasks that rely on repeated social interactions between at least two people, emotions (e.g., enthusiasm [Lawler, 2003] are inevitably produced. Emotions bridge the gap between personal (me-ness) and collective identity (we-ness) (Thoits & Virshup, 1997) as individuals can tune into the mood of the collective, thereby syncing their own sense of self to the collective’s identity.

## Affective Attunement

Melucci (1995) argues that “emotional investment” is crucial to creating and sustaining collective identity: “passions and feelings, love and hate, fate and fear are all part of a body acting collectively” (p. 45). The term collective emotion captures macrolevel affective phenomena “that emerge from emotional dynamics among individuals who are responding to the same situation” (Goldenberg et al., 2020, p. 155). When individuals become aware of each other’s emotions, their own emotional state is affected. Group identification is thus the driver and the outcome of collective emotions (Goldenberg et al., 2020).

Online movements are particularly susceptible to strong emotions as social media blend opinions, facts, and emotions (Papacharissi & de Fatima Oliveira, 2012). Activists are thus likely to be “swept up in” and moved by the online movement’s affective forces (Bille & Simonsen, 2021), mood, or atmosphere that float around and beyond the members of a group (Ahmed, 2014). Even though participants in online movements may not be or think like each other (e.g., share interests or experiences), they align themselves through feelings (Papacharissi, 2015).

Enacting (affective) attunement (Kanyeredzi et al., 2019; Papacharissi, 2015), i.e., calibrating one’s body to the emotional expression of others such that one is moved to act (Ahmed, 2014), activists synchronize their affective state to the group’s. This synchronizing to others’ rhythm, affective state, or quality of being can lead to the development of collective identity and agency (Forlè, 2024). Through affect, a sense of we-ness can be produced online despite protesters’ inability to draw on physical co-presence and joint action.

## Case: My Stealthy Freedom

MySF social movement began in May 2014, when Masih Alinejad, a well-known, exiled Iranian journalist, created a Facebook (FB) fan page by that name. MySF’s purpose was to oppose compulsory hijab in Iran. Before the 1979 Islamic revolution, under the Shah’s Westernization efforts, many women chose not to veil. In 1984, however, hijab, the practice of veiling, became compulsory for women in public spaces. Enforcement of the law fell to societal monitoring and the Morality Police, a police division with the authority to arrest women without proper hijab in public.

MySF emerged in response to the 14,000 “likes” and the flood of comments on a photograph that Masih had posted on her personal FB page. The photo showed her running down a street lined by pink-blossoming trees in London, where she lived in exile at the time. Her arms were outstretched, and her unruly mop of dark curly hair was bouncing behind her in the wind. The following caption accompanied the image:

Every time that I run in a street of a free country and feel the wind in my hair, I remember that my hair was like a hostage in the hands of the Iranian government.

In their comments, Iranian women expressed their deep desire to experience what they imagined the freedom of a body unconstrained by a manteau, of hair liberated from a headscarf, and of movement unconfined by cultural norms would feel like. This effusive and emotional reaction to a personal photo reminded Masih that compulsory veiling was an issue many Iranians—especially women—cared about deeply. To build on the collective emotions her post had generated, Masih then posted a photo that had been taken when she was still in Iran, driving a car with the headscarf dropped around her shoulders. She captioned it:

This is also my freedom, but it is stealthy. And I’m sure lots of Iranian women exist who don’t believe in hijab and if one looks at their photo albums one can find similar hijabless photos … What do you think of publishing these photos?

Spurring her FB friends to look through their collection of images to identify those where they were hijabless in public, Masih encouraged them to send these images to her so that she might post them anonymously on her personal FB page. The overwhelming response to her idea then moved her to channel the apparent groundswell of resistance into a movement:

When women themselves sent photos, I realized they want to have their own platform to talk about their rights. So, I didn’t decide to launch a campaign, rather it was the Iranian women who were talking, commenting, and posting their photos. (emphasis added)

Masih created the MySF FB page as the site for the women’s protest. Here she published photos of hijablessness in public spaces in Iran on behalf of the women in the photographs. Remaining unidentified and unidentifiable signified the stealthiness of the protest.

May 2017 marked the beginning of a new campaign entitled White Wednesdays (WWeds). It entailed MySF supporters wearing white on Wednesdays and recording videos of themselves talking about hijab as they went about their everyday lives. These videos were then posted on activists’ own Instagram (IG) accounts or sent to Masih to be published on her page.

On Wednesday, December 27, 2017, when a 32-year-old woman, Vida Movahed, enacted a form of protest that became known as “Girls of Enghelab Street” (GES), the third instantiation of the MySF movement emerged. Vida stood hijabless on a utility box on Enghelab (“revolution” in Persian) Street, one of the busiest streets in the center of Tehran, with her white headscarf tied to a stick. Silently holding the scarf like a peace flag, she was eventually arrested. The photos and videos of her act that passersby had recorded were first shared on Masih’s IG page but then went viral on Twitter.

Throughout the life of the movement, the protest practices became increasingly risky (Figure 1). Arrests and prison sentences became more and more commonplace. Moreover, Masih, despite living in exile, was threatened. In September 2019, Masih’s brother was arrested and sentenced to 8 years in jail. In July 2021, the FBI revealed<sup>2</sup> that the Iranian authorities had plotted to kidnap Masih in the U.S. In July 2022, a man was arrested with a rifle in front of Masih’s home in Brooklyn.<sup>3</sup> Masih has been living under protection ever since.

We consider MySF to be particularly well-suited for our investigation into how collective identity is produced in highrisk online activism. First, its enactment relies on different social media platforms, specifically FB, IG, and Twitter. Furthermore, the evolution of MySF’s protest practices and its longevity provide us with not only rich, longitudinal data but also opportunities for comparative analysis across the three social media platforms. Second, MySF has been consequential; the growth in protester arrests suggests that the movement is perceived as increasingly threatening to Iranian norms and institutions. The considerable infrastructure of activists and best practices that supported the “Woman, Life, Freedom” movement in 2022, where the headscarf featured strongly as a symbol of oppression, is also attributed to MySF. Lastly, MySF hybridizes on- and offline protest, which provides the opportunity to gain a more nuanced understanding of risks to the body in online activism.

## Data Collection

Our data collection is three-pronged: (1) observing the movement’s online presence, (2) copying content from the movement’s various online instantiations, and (3) interviewing (both synchronously and asynchronously) MySF activists.

## Observations

The first author, who grew up in Iran, followed the MySF FB page from its inception in 2014. She employed methods associated with online ethnography (Hine, 2008), specifically engaging with the online content in real time. She kept track of the evolution of the MySF-FB page, documenting in her field notes significant events such as the first time a photo of a man or a religious woman protesting hijab appeared. These observations sensitized her to the meanings that emerged around certain events and the feelings that enveloped the community.

## Online Content

With Masih’s knowledge, we collected online content (see Table 1 for summary), copying it onto a hard drive. In our sampling, we focused on dimensions such as identifiability (e.g., face shown, close-up), location (e.g., deserted or busy, symbolic), group or individual photos, and the approximate age of the activist. This data collection practice, which provided us with immediate insights into the evolution of the protest practices, especially in terms of escalating riskiness, helped us identify important themes and adapt our interview questions accordingly.

## Interviews

Between June 2016 and March 2020, we interviewed 25 MySF participants, as well as Masih and a MySF FB page’s admin/translator. We followed 11 of these interviewees online over time and conducted follow-up interviews with them. These inquiries (23 in total) took the form of asynchronous text- or voice-based Q&A exchanges. This provided us with insight into their journey as activists. Also, with their consent, social media content posted by eight of our interviewees on their personal FB or IG accounts was copied.

Due to the anonymity of the MySF photos, we depended on Masih to identify potential interviewees. She connected us with women who had exhibited considerable commitment to the movement through their contributions to its content and the recruitment of new members. In line with the logic of theoretical sampling (Patton 1990), which advocates choosing empirical cases (i.e., interviewees) that exhibit the phenomenon of interest (i.e., identification with the movement) naturally and intensively, we believe that the data generated from talking to these activists offers us the requisite richness for our abductive theorizing.

The first author conducted the interviews in Persian. To build rapport with the interviewees, who were making themselves vulnerable by speaking openly to a stranger about behavior that constituted a punishable offence, she recorded a 3-minute video message introducing herself. In the video, she talked about her experience with compulsory hijab, her feelings about the law, and her motivation for studying MySF. This video was then sent to the candidate interviewees as an introduction. The interviews lasted 53 minutes on average (min: 30; max: 115). With one exception, all interviewees agreed to recording the interaction. The interviews were then transcribed and, for the most part, translated into English.

Relying on a mix of unstructured (Qu & Dumay, 2011) and narrative (Czarniawska, 2000) approaches to interviewing, the interviewees were encouraged to recount, in detail, incidents related to hijab and particularly meaningful acts of resistance against it. Throughout the interviewees’ narrativization, the interviewer probed their experiences as a MySF activist, their sense of MySF as a collective, as well as their affective responses to performing acts of resistance and seeing protest enacted by others. She also asked interviewees to elaborate on their experiences before, during, and after their protest enactments. Finally, she explored interviewees’ perceptions of others’ comments and photos. Follow-up interviews were completed at the time of the GES protests. These interviews focused on the evolution of the movement and its sense of collectiveness, the interviewee’s reflections on her protest performances, and her own sense of her activist identity.

## Data Analysis

Given the three instantiations of MySF, i.e., MySF-FB, WWeds, and GES, comparison and contrast (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) formed the foundation of our analytical strategy. The initial step in our data analysis was open coding (Corbin & Strauss, 1994). Consistent with the best practices of digital ethnography (Pink et al., 2015), we started our analysis with a focus on what people did to enact activism. We thus identified the practices that enacted protest and produced each of the three MySF instantiations. We worked our way from merely describing these practices (i.e., using photos on FB vs. videos on IG vs. (re)tweeting on Twitter) to exploring what these practices produced with regard to feelings/mood and I/we dialectic in each of MySF’s instantiations. Our open codes thus included practices of protest (e.g., taking off the headscarf, wearing white), emotions (e.g., fear, excitement), and feelings of me-ness (e.g., brave activist, fighter, spectator) as well as weness (e.g., feeling less alone, commitment).

After zooming in on “doings and sayings carried out using a variety of tools and resources” (Nicolini, 2009, p. 1400), we continued with zooming out, focusing on interconnectedness of practices and the effects produced by different practice arrangements. For instance, publishing a photo on MySF was connected to metavoicing (e.g., others commenting on it), which, in turn, was associated with other photos and the escalation of risk over time. We also analyzed how these practice arrangements produced emotions and identities. Finally, focusing on the common patterns in the practices identified across the three instantiations, we divided our protest practices into four main categories of performing the embodied act of resistance, recording, publishing, and metavoicing.

Our analysis can best be described as abductive (Locke et al., 2008; Peirce et al., 1973). Relying on theories of individual and collective identity in (online) social movements, as well as performativity as our sensitizing device (Walsham, 2006), we moved back and forth between theory and data. Our process largely reflected the three steps of abduction: (1) applying a theory and re-reading the data in light of it, (2) observing an element of doubt and surprise in the empirical phenomena when applying the theory, and (3) articulating a new theory explaining the phenomena and resolving the surprise (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007).

The element of surprise that emerged from our reading of the data through the prior research lay in the incongruence between the literature’s claims about collective identity being unattainable and irrelevant in online activism and our data, in which feelings of collectiveness were evident. Our questions around the why of this poor theory-data fit led us to the literature on high-risk social movements, which highlighted the importance of collective identity. However, this literature did not theorize the role of technology in risky activism. The puzzle around the production of collective identity in high-risk activism enacted online thus became our focus of inquiry. To resolve this puzzle, we iterated between theories (especially the practice perspective, collective emotions, attunement, and the interaction between individual and collective identities) and the data (particularly the differences across the sociomaterial enactments of the three MySF instantiations) to develop a conceptual scaffold that ultimately stabilized into our theoretical model (Figure 12) through repeated (re)reading, (re)theorizing and (re)presenting of our empirical material.

## MySF Enactments

Comparing and contrasting the protest practices performed in the three instantiations of the MySF movement, we categorized them under the four umbrella practices of (1) performing the embodied act of resistance, (2) recording, (3) publishing, and (4) metavoicing (see Table 2 for summary). Metavoicing is “engaging in the ongoing online knowledge conversation by reacting online to others’ presence, profiles, content and activities” (Majchrzak et al., 2013, p. 41). It takes the form of commenting, liking, and sharing. We then describe the sense of we-ness produced in each instantiation and its relation to the risks taken by individual activists.

We also leveraged the conceptual scaffold derived from the prior literature to offer a theoretically informed description of “what is going on” in high-risk social movements that are enacted online. In the theory development section, we then identify recurring patterns in the production of MySF to derive movement-level practices and identity configurations that address how a sense of collectiveness was produced (Bertilsson, 2015).

## My Stealthy Freedom Facebook Page (MySF-FB)

The MySF movement originated as an FB page bearing its name. It was created in May 2014 and generated 3276 posts by the end of 2019 (Figure 2 for distribution over time). Engagement during the first month was high and grew at an increasing rate (Figure 3).

Initially, performing MySF-FB protest entailed a woman submitting a photo of herself in public without a headscarf to Masih. Many had such images, typically taken in a secluded place, in their photo albums. By publishing such a photo, a private moment of public hijablessness was transformed into a globally visible act of resistance and, as such, political action. By making their opposition to compulsory veiling public, which most had never done before, ordinary Iranian women started down a path of becoming activists:

I always wanted to do something, but didn’t know what. I always wanted that the hijab law gets abolished … I was looking at the [MySF] photos and I was scared … Then, once, I told myself “until when do we want to be scared? Someone should do something, otherwise nothing is going to change” … That’s when I decided to send a photo too.

## Performing the Embodied Act of Resistance

Taking hijabless photos entailed coordination among coactivists (e.g., person taking the photo), choosing a location (e.g., sites with religious significance), deciding on the placement of the headscarf (e.g., holding it like a sail overhead, dropping it like a tissue), and performing embodied gestures (e.g., smiling, jumping). While taking off one’s headscarf in public was illegal in Iran, the activists generally performed this practice only momentarily. Nevertheless, this represented a very risky act that elicited a mix of emotions, as recounted by Nina:

I was scared but I enjoyed it too. Because, even if you do it for a short time, you might encounter a [prohijab] person that ruins your day. But it was interesting to see the people passing by. Some women were looking at me with admiration, probably they knew what I was doing. Some were smiling at me, and some were ignoring me as if they didn’t see me.

The assembly of a photo’s visual features culminated in a statement directed primarily at Iranians, for whom the symbolic meaning of the performance was expected to be unequivocal. As indicated by Mehrnaz, enacting protests at sites with religious or historical significance increased the provocativeness of the act, as well as its risk:

I wanted to focus on the things that our opposers are sensitive about, such as the mosque, morality police, or billboards with verses from the Quran or phrases promoting hijab. People like to show their courage through the places where they take their photos.

As the places where photos were taken became riskier and protesters became increasingly identifiable (see Figures 4, 5, 6, and 7), stealth gave way to boldness. Merhnaz recalled:

Some of the comments [under my first photo] were saying, “you took this photo in a deserted place stealthily.” So, many people like me repeat these acts [in ways] that weren’t stealthy to say, “look, we can do this everywhere! We want this freedom everywhere, not only in our backyard or our alley or our neighborhood.”

## Recording

In MySF, most of the photos were taken by someone other than the protester. Shirin saw this as an indication of support as well as part of MySF’s network effects:

What is interesting for me [when I look at the MySF photos] is that somebody has taken this photo. So, there is at least one other person who supports her. So, they [MySF activists] have become at least two people, and then when the photo is published on the page, the number of these people [protesting against hijab] increases.

Since the photo created a record of a crime being committed, this practice significantly increased the riskiness of the protest. As Sepide recounted, even unpublished and unstaged images of hijablessness could be used as evidence to convict a woman in Iran:

When I was arrested, I was charged with the crime of unveiling. I had a hijabless photo [that they found when searching my phone]. The crazy thing was that in that photo, my scarf had slipped unintentionally. I had three or four photos taken at the same time and in one of them my scarf had just slipped … and they used that photo as evidence against me.

Some activists carefully staged their protest and even made a professional photoshoot of it. For example, at considerable cost and risk, Mehrnaz hired a professional photographer to capture her third MySF protest in the main square of Isfahan. This protest was anything but stealthy, which was also evident in the MySF community’s reaction to her photo: “When I sent this photo, many people told me ‘how brave you are that you went to Shah Square and took hijabless photos like this!’”

## Publishing

The photos that the activists produced were then sent to Masih directly or sent to the MySF page. Since the MySF site was set up as a FB fan page configured so that only people with administrative rights (i.e., Masih and two assistants) could post original content on it, the images were then uploaded anonymously to the page. However, this occurred only after Masih had completed a vetting process, through which she sought to ensure that MySF could not be discredited by detractors (e.g., the government) for publishing protest performances that later turned out to be fake (e.g., photoshopped) or published against an individual’s will.

Once Masih had received the image along with an accompanying message written by the activist, she validated the sender’s identity by looking at the submitter’s FB profile, e.g., checking out the number and types of friends the person had. If a protester’s identity checked out, Masih then reached out to the submitter to ensure that the activist was aware of the risks associated with publishing the image. Only after Masih had assured herself that the protester was comfortable with the potential consequences of this political act (e.g., family conflict, job loss) did she pass the contribution on to one of the admins, who then translated the text accompanying the photograph from Persian into English and then posted the new content.

In securing the activists’ consent for publication, Masih sought to impress on them the riskiness of their action and the emotional toll it might take. Nooshin expressed the initial terror she felt after the publication of her first protest performance: “When [my photo] was published on MySF… I couldn’t sleep that night. I was expecting that any moment someone would knock on our door and arrest me.”

As a gatekeeper, Masih was strategic in releasing content to the FB page. Even though she did not follow a fixed publication schedule, she released new material steadily at a rate of \~600 posts per year. This gave the movement a continuous presence. Additionally, she exercised her editorial power over the photos released for publication, thereby legitimizing some protest performances while discarding others. For example, when a girl sent a photo in which she had turned her face away from the camera, Masih opted not to publish it, as the performance was out of sync with the level of risk-taking exhibited by activists at the time:

… because everybody was sending such brave photos [in which they could be identified] and she had sent a photo of the back of her head! … Then she wrote to me in the comment section again saying that “I sent a photo to Masih but she didn’t publish it and she broke my heart. What does she think? … That photo was a brave act for me!”

This incident suggests not only that the protest practices were becoming costlier and riskier over time, but also that Masih had a significant hand in shaping MySF-FB’s sense of collectiveness. Since all the protest performances were aggregated in a single location, the level of risk-taking was readily apparent. Performances that were not attuned to the movement’s atmosphere of bravery thus stood out and were unlikely to pass Masih’s gatekeeping. Figures 4 and 5 show the progression of photos riskiness along two dimensions: activist identifiability and riskiness of location. Figures 6 and 7 show the trend numerically. Riskiness of locations refer to both their busyness (e.g., the photo in the middle in

## Metavoicing

While the photos on the FB page were subject to gatekeeping and posted anonymously, viewing, commenting on, liking or resharing the posts was not only available to all FB users, but also identified. Most comments celebrated the activists strength and courage: “Bravo!,” “I am jealous of your bravery,” and “Great image of a beautiful rebel. Don’t ever give up.” The instantaneity and emotionality of metavoicing played an important role in creating a sense of we-ness; it highlighted the affective attunement between the activist and the MySF-FB community. For Elnaz, this produced a sense of belonging to the movement: “The feeling that you are among people who think like you and who are protesting like you by sending photos, is an amazing feeling.”

Commenting on, liking, and resharing also algorithmically pushed content to the News Feed of a MySF supporter’s FB friends, thus increasing MySF’s visibility. Since those enacting the MySF protest were anonymous, thus severely limiting the network effects the activists themselves could mobilize, metavoicing practices were particularly important.

## Sense of We-ness and Its Effect on Perceived Risk

Despite the escalation of risk apparent in MySF-FB, the anonymity and relative homogeneity of the protest performances, their aggregation in a single space and the celebratory metavoicing responses they elicited, the geographically dispersed and unidentified activists coalesced into a unified body that prioritized the “we” over the “I”: “When you enter this page and you follow it, you don’t see yourself as an individual anymore; you see yourself as part of a movement” (Laleh).

The collective was characterized by boldness and courage:

[The images on the MySF page] are put together like pieces of a puzzle and shows me a new map of Iran …

Putting these images next to each other paints a real and bold image of “us,” the “us” that has been lost for so long.

Swept up in the excitement of protesting against compulsory hijab publicly without the activists—even the identifiable ones—being arrested, empowered the (would-be) activists. The value of the movement thus relativized the perceived risk of protest sufficiently for (would-be) activists to overcome their fear and act more boldly than they thought possible:

This page has given us the bravery and courage. I, myself, wasn’t brave enough before to even express myself, but this page has made me so brave that now I can express myself easily even among strangers and not be afraid of anything. (Azade)

The sociomaterial arrangements of the MySF-FB moved individuals to enact increasingly risky protest practices, thereby exhibiting its performative capacity to empower Iranian women who had never before protested into brave activists in a high-risk movement.

## White Wednesdays (WWeds)

In April 2017, concerned that the FB audience was shrinking, and that the engaging content was increasingly moving to IG, Masih initially struggled to see this platform’s potential. However, once she received and was moved by a video from a woman singing in the street, the idea for WWeds evolved. As Mana, one of the first activists who sent a video to the page, noted, video was not only a richer medium, but also a more credible one:

I think you can’t rely on photos that much. I mean I know and I believe that someone has gone and taken off her headscarf in a public space to take a photo, but many people might not believe it. But videos seem more natural, more believable. (emphasis added)

Figure 8 shows the decline in MySF-FB’s engagement (average monthly “likes”) versus its increase on IG. Despite MySF’s move to IG, the FB page remained active and relatively up to date, as curated IG content was posted there.

On May 22, 2017, 3 years after the launch of the MySF FB page and in response to pro-hijab rallies enacted by women wearing the traditional black chador (i.e., a large cloth that covers all body except for the face), Masih announced the WWeds campaign in a one-minute video. Dressed in white, Masih outlined the campaign:

Men with white bracelets and women with white scarves, please send us your videos. The emblem of our protest is white, the color of peace… We will show our opposition every Wednesday, wherever we are, by wearing the sign of our campaign: the white color.

## Performing the Embodied Act of Resistance

The practice of wearing a white headscarf every Wednesday offered Iranian women an opportunity to declare their anti-hijab sentiments publicly in their everyday social settings (e.g., family, work, university), without violating Iran’s hijab laws. While legal, such overt identification with the MySF movement was risky, however, as it made activists targets for abuse and recrimination in their social, family, and work environments:

I was working in [name of a famous company in Iran], but because of my activities in WWeds and because of my photos, they were threatening me all the time; from threats of firing me to threats of not extending my contract anymore. So, I had to resign. (Negar)

By bringing the MySF agenda into everyday life (i.e., no longer stealthy), embodied encounters among activists and supporters became possible. As Laleh described, physically encountering like-minded people generated an immediate sense of connection and we-ness:

I do this every Wednesday. I go walking with my cousins every Wednesday wearing white. The other time, in a mall, we saw a girl and a boy. The boy was wearing a white shirt and the girl had a white headscarf. When we saw each other, we smiled at each other, and at the same time, we said “White Wednesdays.” It was a great feeling; we couldn’t stop smiling!

After a while, a riskier adaptation of wearing white on Wednesdays was developed; it entailed dropping the white headscarf to the shoulders while in public. This created continuity between the MySF-FB and WWeds instantiations of the movement. By refusing to continue to “protest stealthily and stay underground” (Masih), the WWeds protest produced an atmosphere of confrontation with the out-group. Wearing white, women—and some men—provoked the people with whom they interacted daily (e.g., neighbors, colleagues), demanding that they take a position on hijab every Wednesday. As Tarane experienced, such confrontational behavior could invoke physically violent reactions:

I wasn’t wearing my headscarf; I think a woman with a chador saw me from afar. As she was approaching, she was telling me something. I responded “Yes?” She asked me to wear my headscarf. I told her that it was none of her business. As soon as I responded like that, she slapped my hand and my phone was thrown. Then she beat me up.

## Recording

To share their protest performances, activists recorded selfie videos, turning their phone cameras on themselves as they walked or stood in a public place. They then talked about their opposition to compulsory hijab and their experience of defying the law. They also invited others to join the protest. As Aida’s first experience with WWeds shows, walking in the street without a headscarf took tremendous courage:

It was really difficult for me. … In the alley, I took off my headscarf to take the video, but… I couldn’t talk. Can you believe it!?? No voice was coming out of my mouth when I was trying to talk… I got scared for a few moments.

Compared to the staged, still images of MySF-FB, WWeds recordings provided insight into the activist’s emotional state and situated sociophysical environment. The videos also captured the reactions of passersby, interactions with fellow activists, and altercations with or arrests by the morality police. Such videos (re)produced the sense of we-ness among MySF supporters, delineating the activist in-group from the out-group of hijab proponents.

## Publishing

Although some activists still preferred to send their videos to Masih for anonymous publication, others posted them to their own social media accounts, especially on IG. The mundaneand everydayness of the material typically shared on IG belied the extraordinary courage the WWeds performance demanded. Individually posted WWeds performances provided insights into the activist’s beliefs, as well as information about her offline identity, e.g., name, social relationships, interests. Parvane, who had previously used IG to advocate for animal rights, noted that the selfie format made her advocacy of MySF more personal:

I didn’t use to post selfies except on special occasions. But now I post several selfies every Wednesday to show I’m against compulsory hijab, hoping others would learn about it.

As activists leveraged their personal social media networks, Masih’s vision of a distributed model of activism where “everyone [would] be able to own the campaign, to feel as a campaigner of her own community or network” materialized. For example, Rana, an avid mountain climber, led a group of mountaineering activists to enact a community-specific WWeds protest. This entailed climbing significant peaks in Iran and raising a white headscarf at their respective summits. They recorded short videos of their actions and posted them on different sites. By building ties between MySF and established communities of interest, the repertoire of protest performances that enacted WWeds expanded, as did its reach.

Awareness of and access to activists’ WWeds videos were enabled through two IG practices: hashtagging and following. By tagging the content with “#WWeds,” individual protest performances could be linked and searched. Such tagging was a risky practice, as it unequivocally classified a given performance as an intentional protest and identified the performer as a MySF activist. This made her vulnerable to arrest and online harassment:

Someone sent me a message on IG and started a religious discussion. He then called me “Kaffir” [Meaning “non-believer,” a slur that equates an individual to a criminal] publicly and many people attacked my page, insulting and threatening me. (Rana)

Furthermore, hashtagging served the same function in the virtual setting as wearing white did in the physical world: it made it possible for WWeds activists to find, follow, and build relationships with each other, and thus to create geographically situated or interest-based (sub)communities. Masih’s IG page on which she reposted curated WWeds performances and followed some of the activists, aggregated WWeds content, and made MySF present.

## Metavoicing

Relationships among IG users were enacted through “follow” links, which were unidirectional rather than mutually acknowledged, as in FB. Nevertheless, the ties between an activist and her followers tended to be strong as the activist was likely to target her weekly videos at her followers, addressing them directly and sharing her feelings and beliefs with them. By layering affective sentiments over the existing ties with followers, strong bonds of commitment were produced. These bonds of commitment between activists and their followers were also apparent in metavoicing. Comments tended to be highly affective and personalized, e.g., referring to past posts or shared experiences as is apparent in Parvane’s response to a fellow activist’s video: “I’m proud of you, my brave friend. I’ll never forget that day [the video was recorded].” In other responses, an activist noted the history of their relationship: “The first time I saw you on FB, I thought ‘she is amazing!’ Throughout these years, as I have known you more, I realized what a brave and powerful woman you are.”

The activists were lauded for being not only brave for enacting their protest despite the risks but also for being powerful, evidenced by the regime’s concerted efforts to arrest WWeds activists. The bonding enacted via individuated expressions of personal thoughts and feelings within an activist-follower network meant that an activist, when arrested, could rely on her trusted network of committed supporters. Neda’s WWeds video illustrates this:

They arrested Yasaman, Mojgan became her voice. They arrested Mojgan, Fereshte became her voice. Now, they’ve arrested Fereshte, [and] … I’m Fereshte’s voice. Arrest me too! Arrest all the people who act to find freedom and equality!

## Sense of We-ness and its Effect on Perceived Risk

Despite its loosely coupled aggregation of distributed activistfollower networks, which consisted of trusted supporters, WWeds generated intercommunity we-ness. The cadence synchronized the protest enactments and (re)constituted the collective on a weekly basis: “I was feeling I have to send at least a video or a photo per week. I was thinking, ‘if I feel I belong to a community, I need to do this’” (Tarane).

The display of white symbols on Wednesdays, which drew a clear distinction between members of the in- and out-group, created a sense of cohesion even among strangers. Roya recounted, she felt compelled to act in solidarity with fellow protesters without knowing them: “Once, in a metro … I saw that a mother and her daughter had taken off their headscarves … As soon as I saw them, I took off my scarf too.”

The women enacting the WWeds protest confronted those around them, forcing these bystanders to face a post-hijab future in which veiling would be optional in Iran. As narrated in a WWeds video, by “normalizing” women’s right to choose, activists compelled everyone they encountered to take a position on compulsory hijab:

We want the freedom of choice. To do this, we need to normalize an abnormal practice [being hijabless]. We can transform something abnormal to normal with repetition. Let’s, wherever we are, make a small protest against compulsory hijab every Wednesday.

However, the supposed “smallness” of the WWeds protest practice belied the risks that the activists were taking. A comment on Sepide’s IG page, which reads: “with only a white headscarf, you have entered a fight with a loaded gun [metaphor for the regime and pro-hijab groups’ violent response],” highlights that the WWed’s activists had become fighters, whose weekly enactment of a post-hijab vision of Iran in all walks of everyday life, threatened the regime so much that it stepped up its repression of the movement. Between December 2017 and February 2018, 29 WWeds activists were arrested; 10 more were imprisoned between April and June 2019. Their prison sentences ranged from seven to 23 years but were reduced to a maximum of five and a half years upon appeal.

By following other WWeds activists on IG, those that enacted the WWeds protest were aware of the risks they were taking and the likelihood that they would “pay the price for freedom [they sought], … [and their] insistence and power.” Ironically, these arrests also served to increase the perceived value of the protests vis-à-vis its risks, enabling activists to justify their self-sacrifice to themselves and others. Furthermore, the bonds of commitment, trust, and support established in the activist-follower networks provided assurances that an individual’s arrest would mobilize others to fight for her release and “take her place.”

## Girls of Enghelab Street (GES)

On Wednesday, December 27, 2017, Masih published on her IG a photo of a woman standing hijabless on a utility box on Enghelab Street, a busy road in Tehran, holding a stick with a white scarf tied to it (Figure 9). Some of the onlookers immediately associated her performance with WWeds; the man who shot one of the videos narrated on it: “Today is Wednesday. This woman climbed [on the utility box] and is doing a WWeds protest.” However, Vida Movahed, a 31- year-old with a 19-month-old daughter, was unknown to Masih and other MySF activists. It would take four weeks before Vida, who had escalated the protest to this new level of risk, was identified. In the meantime, she became the “Girl of Enghelab Street” (GES).

On January 16, 2018, as Vida’s identity was still unknown, Masih published posts on her IG and Twitter pages, inviting everyone to participate in a twitterstorm, i.e., a coordinated spike of activity that “trended” a given hashtag, to focus global media attention on the GES activist’s safety and well-being: “Tomorrow on the white Wed at 10 am: Twitterstorm asking #Where\_is\_GES; and from 5 to 6 pm with white symbols in the streets.” A graph of the GES hashtag (Figure 10) shows two spikes: the first represents the twitterstorm orchestrated by Masih; the second occurred organically when, on January 24, Vida’s identity was revealed.

## Performing the Embodied Act of Resistance

The physical enactment of the GES protest was extremely costly and risky in that the activist performed it on her own and with the certainty of physical assault during and after her inevitable arrest. One of the GES protesters, of whom there were more than 10, had her leg broken when being kicked down from the box. Azam, a GES activist who fled Iran after she had been released from prison, explained how she worked up the courage to perform the protest:

I took my white scarf and went to Enghelab St. I wrote a text [explaining my decision] … But I was scared. I told myself that if I post this text on my IG, there is no way back; then, I have to do it! So, I posted the text and climbed the utility box.

In an interview with Reuters, Azam later described the mix of fear and power that she felt:

From the moment I climbed up, I was a bit scared; [my daughter] Viana was in my head all the time: what will happen to her? … But at the same time, I was feeling strong. I’ll never forget that moment. I was feeling that I’m the most powerful woman on earth!

Four weeks after the inaugural GES performance, Nargess replicated Vida’s protest, standing on the same utility box as Vida. Even though Nargess was technically the third woman to enact the GES protest, she was deemed the second and lauded for transforming Vida’s original performance into a protest practice. The pluralization of the hashtag to “#Girls\_of\_Enghelab\_Street” [emphasis added] captured this shift. In a post on her FB and Twitter pages, Nagress articulated her wish that Vida’s protest not be a one-off:

After seeing the image of the women with free hair and her headscarf on a stick standing on the highest point of history, you wait. You wait for someone else to stand up and continue, … Isn’t the second person the continuance of what happens next?

## Recording

Unlike MySF and WWeds, recording in GES was not guaranteed, as it was enacted by a passerby rather than the activist herself or an accompanying co-activist. Masih explained:

I didn’t know Vida. In WWeds, everybody would record a video themselves and send it to me. But Vida didn’t send me any video. Somebody else did, and the man in the video said “she is doing WWeds,” so I felt responsible for her fate.

Nevertheless, given the audaciousness of GES protests, they were recognized as extraordinary and newsworthy performances, making their capture highly likely.

## Publishing

Similar to recording, publishing GES content relied on unknown others. To the extent that they either knew to send the image to Masih or had social media accounts with sufficient followers, the recorded material eventually reached people who recognized and tagged it as a GES performance. Because publishing was controlled by neither Masih nor the activist, GES content was distributed across various platforms, especially IG and Twitter.

In contrast to this GES publishing practice, Azam, who had announced her plans to perform a GES protest to her IG followers ahead of time, managed to post a photo of her performance to her Twitter page after her arrest. In a published interview, she explained:

I asked the police agents to give back my phone so that I can call my family and tell them about my arrest. … They gave it to me. I took a screenshot of my photo [that I found online] and wrote: “I might not get released soon, but I will continue fighting.

## Metavoicing

GES introduced a new metavoicing practice, namely hashtag trending. Referred to as twitterstorming on Twitter, this practice combined the practices of liking, tagging, and commenting with time-boxing to activate the platform algorithm that elevated a hashtag’s relative rank on social media. This practice was particularly effective in the production of news, which has a short half-life. Even though content trending can occur spontaneously, on Iranian Twitter, this practice was often strategically organized. It involved users with high network centrality (e.g., Masih) announcing the exact day and time and when their network of followers should (re)tweet messages using a specific hashtag. The tag needed to be unique and new, as concentrating its use in a short time frame increased its potential for virality.

Successful content trending also required detailed knowledge of how the algorithm worked, as the following instructions sent to GES activists indicate: “To friends who have just joined the twitterstorm: Twitter identifies the tweets containing only the hashtag as spam. Write at least a sentence accompanying the hashtag.”

Twitterstorming represented the collective, digital protest performance that completed the individually enacted, embodied GES protest, giving the activist a voice to speak on behalf of the collective: “As we have a voice of our own here through our collaboration, that heroic girl [Vida] will also have a voice with our help! Be her voice as she stood up for our rights!”

By monitoring the rank of the hashtag, the GES activists got immediate feedback on the effectiveness of their online protest. A tweet observing this real-time monitoring read: “Apparently, some of the women of my home country are becoming the number one trend of Twitter tonight! #GES.” Reaching the #1 rank on Iranian Twitter meant that the protest had achieved its goal of drawing global attention to the martyr-activist’s plight: “#Where\_is\_GES has reached international media. Now, it’s time to ask the UN, Amnesty, and human rights organizations to ask the regime about where is the GES?”

## Sense of We-ness and Its Effect on Perceived Risk

Many Iranians were enthralled by the enormity of the GES performance and became fixated on learning more about Vida, who Masih referred to as a “warrior,” and what had happened to her:

I remember the day when #Vida\_Movahed put the dominant ideology on a stick very well. I was constantly checking news channels and social media, and my heartbeat was becoming faster seeing her photos. I was inspired and excited by that much rebelliousness, bravery, and freedom. #Girls\_of\_Enghelab\_Street

Even though few—if any—of the online GES activists had experienced an embodied GES protest or were planning to enact it, they were swept up in a collective emotional charge that was characterized by anticipation and apprehension, i.e., suspense:

I felt a mixture of excitement and pride, and anxiety and worry. Something that we had been waiting for years had finally happened: … without saying anything, a woman has raised our voice of opposition, and our voice has reached everyone. And at the same time, we were also worried about the situation and conditions of this woman.

Compared to the GES performers’ self-sacrifice (i.e., their willingness to act in ways that ran counter to their own interests, e.g., leaving small children behind) to dramatically express Iranian women’s opposition to compulsory veiling, the risks of microblogging were negligible. This made nonparticipation, which was visible to one’s Twitter followers, difficult to justify. Furthermore, as future beneficiaries of the martyr-activists’ heroism, they owed it to the GES protesters to advocate for her release, which relied in large part on news of her dramatic protest going viral and reaching a global audience: “We have to ask for the immediate release of the #GES … Join me! Tweet and retweet as much as you can, so that the demand for her release trends” (emphasis added).

The comparably low-risk nature of twitterstorming, the moral obligation of the martyr-activists to complete their selfsacrificing protest by making it globally impactful, the sense of safety that comes from being one of many microbloggers, as well as the cost of non-participation, reduced the perceived risk of participating in the twitterstorm. Once part of the GES microbloggers, the online activist was so swept up in the collective emotion of suspense around the twitterstorm’s ability to trend the #GES hashtag to a top rank that concerns about risk were likely to recede into the background.

## Theory Development

Having identified what protest practices produced a sense of we-ness in each MySF instantiation and how collective identity made risky activism possible, we now explain how this sense of collectiveness was generated. Seeking to derive the movement-level practices by which the I/we dialectic in each MySF instantiation were synthesized into relatively stable configurations, we relied on comparison and contrast. In this way, we sought to “recover the practices through which [a configuration] comes into being and sustains its effects” (Suchman, 2012, p. 49). Unlike the protest practices that were visible, these movement-level practices needed to be inferred from their effects. Similarly, Drazin and Sandelands’s (1992) autogenesis perspective explains social organizations in terms of the interplay of different levels of structure; protest practices that are observable correspond to “observed structure,” while the movement-level practices described in this section correspond to “deep structure.”

A configuration is the constitutive entanglement of heterogeneous components (Orlikowski, 2007) that is enacted through the ongoing configuring of opposites (Mazmanian et al., 2014). We leveraged this concept to theorize the ongoing accomplishment of me-ness and we-ness (Thoits & Virshup, 1997) in online activism. Drawing on Milan’s (2013, 2015) insight that the relationship between collective and personal identities is reconfigured when activism goes online, such that the “I” is privileged over the “we,” we conceptualize the ongoing (re)synthesis of the I/we dialectic at the heart of social groups as an identity configuration.

An identity configuration refers to the relational inseparability of individual and collective identities that are both (re)produced and (re)configured through the ongoing enactment of doings and sayings (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008). In our study, the performance of the protest practices in each of the three MySF instantiations distinctly configured collective and individual identities to produce three identity configurations: imagined community, connected communities, and flash crowd (Table 3).

## Identity Configuration

Figure 11 depicts the three identity configurations enacted in each instantiation. It also shows the privileging of the dialectic poles (“I” vs. “we”); the darker poles are prioritized.

Imagined community: In MySF-FB, individual activists performed relatively undifferentiated personal identities. Not only did they remain anonymous, but their embodied protest was quite homogeneous. In this way, the “I” dissolved into the “we,” contributing to the sense of collectiveness despite the lack of identified, interpersonal communication among the activists. The strong sense of collective identity that emerged is consistent with Anderson’s (1986) work on nations as imagined communities, i.e., socially constructed groupings that are imagined by the people who perceive themselves as members.

The imagined community provided individual activists with sufficient freedom to elaborate on the fairly standardized protest enactments, which led to an escalation of protest riskiness. While activists who innovated on the embodied protest stood out (e.g., the first to post a photo in which she was identifiable) and received effusive applause, such an elevation of status was temporary as others imitated the performance and developed it further. Thus, the collective as actor was privileged over individual activists.

Connected communities: In WWeds, the synthesis of the I/we dialectic was achieved through individuals’ commitment to the weekly rhythm of protest, and personal interaction among identified activists and their IG followers. Because WWeds activists felt beholden to their trusted networks of followers, they not only performed a protest every week but also personalized it to make it interesting and meaningful to their followers. With access to activists’ regular, self-revelatory protest performances, followers responded with personalized messages that validated activists’ status as fighters speaking on their behalf. In conjunction with the WWeds hashtag, which aggregated the protest performances into the collective, activists’ identities were prioritized over the collective’s. This synthesizing of the I/we dialectic into an individuals-incollective configuration was evident in the celebrity status of some WWeds activists on IG (e.g., Sepide with 82k and Parvane with 26k followers).

Flash crowd: In GES, there was little connection among the many individuals who were moved by the martyr-activist’s performance to engage in twitterstorming. While each relied on their own Twitter followers and personal skills to influence the hashtag trending algorithm, such that the embodied GES protest became global news, the homogeneity of the practice and the focus on the collective accomplishment of the trending hashtag prioritized collectiveness, synthesizing the I/we dialectic into an individuals-in-temporary-collective configuration. The collective’s existence was limited to the duration of the twitterstorm.

Having elucidated the three identity configurations, we now describe three movement-level practices that were enacted, albeit differently, in the three MySF instantiations (Table 3). Presencing the collective, orchestrating collective action, and moving activists to enact risky protests provides the foundation for the performance of the collectively defined yet individually enacted protest practices in a high-risk movement.

## Presencing the Collective

For individuals to feel that they are part of a collective, especially an online one, they need to have a sense that it is “real.” This implies that the others that make up the collective need to be socially present, i.e., emotionally and psychologically “there” (Gunawardena, 1995). This is accomplished by appropriately responding to others (Schultze & Brooks, 2019), which generally takes the form of immediate and engaged metavoicing in reaction to new content.

Each of the three MySF instantiations accomplished presencing differently. In MySF-FB, the aggregation of all actions and interactions of activists in a single place represents a spatial form of presencing. A visit to the MySF page, possibly prompted by the News Feed of a given FB user’s friends, makes the movement readily apparent through the volume and recency of its members’ engagement, e.g., the publication of and responses to new photos. However, spatial presencing required considerable gatekeeping from Masih and the FB-page admins who curated the content and controlled the steady release of protest performances.

Only by filtering the IG content via the #WWeds hashtag could the collective be brought into existence. This dynamically connected the otherwise distributed communities of WWeds. Following a given activist, however, offered a more visceral experience of presence, due to the activistfollower bonds. Given that the movement is “there” only when individuals follow either a hashtag or an activist, we regard this as an interactive form of presencing.

In GES, presencing was accomplished through simultaneity. Completing the martyr-activist’s protest by raising global awareness to it, the collective was brought into existence for the window during which the activists trended the hashtag. By virtue of the collective, constituted through the concurrent metavoicing of many influential tweeters, the #GES hashtag rose temporarily to first place on Iranian Twitter, thereby completing the GES protest.

## Orchestrating Collective Action

Orchestrating collective action entails the production of a shared mood to which individual members can then affectively attune themselves. When individuals have reciprocal other-awareness (León et al., 2019) and are able to interact, the emotional dynamics between them generate collective emotions (Goldenberg et al., 2020). When swept up in collective emotions, individuals are moved to act in sync. Both rhythmic attunement (i.e., synchronization of behavior, e.g., Nessler & Gilliland, 2009) and emotional attunement (i.e., synchronization of affective states, e.g., Thonhauser, 2018) boost collective identity and agency (Forlè, 2024).

In MySF-FB, orchestrating collective action was accomplished through the homogeneity of the protest practice. Notwithstanding activists’ increasingly provocative elaborations of the core protest performance, the repeated publication of photos featuring hijabless women in public places produced a shared mood. Drawing on their own embodied experience of being a woman in Iran, MySF members tuned into an activist’s emotion (e.g., fear) and imagined what enacting the protest would feel like. This vicariously stepping into the shoes of a woman just like themselves, being awed by her accomplishment and seeing the celebratory response of the collective, produced collective emotions of bravery and empowerment.

The rhythm of protest produced a shared mood of confrontation in WWeds. By wearing white in everyday life every Wednesday, activists formed a visible collective body despite their geographic distribution. Confronting their families, neighbors, and anyone they met on a Wednesday with a post-hijab vision of Iran, the activists (re)produced an us-versus-them struggle and a fighting spirit that they tuned into on a weekly basis. With their commitment to their followers and the movement as a whole, activists faced the risk of arrest every week.

In GES, by generating momentum, the protest practices orchestrated collective action. The time-boxed nature of twitterstorming generated a sense of urgency, which contributed to the mood of suspense, in which the anticipation of trending the chosen hashtag was intertwined with concern about the martyr-activist’s welfare. Attuned to this collective emotion, the online GES activists persevered with metavoicing through the twitterstorm window. Monitoring the hashtag’s rising rank in real time also buoyed the collective mood, spurring the activists on to maintain momentum.

## Moving Activists to Enact Risky Protest

High-risk movements depend on developing resilient activists capable of performing daring protest. Key moments in activist development include recruiting and socializing as well as escalating the risk profile of protest practices (McAdam, 2022). The movement’s sense of collectiveness supports activists’ progression along their “career path” by decreasing the perceived risk and increasing the perceived value of protest (e.g., effecting social change). Higher value/risk ratios increase the likelihood of activists’ risk escalation.

In MySF-FB, the sense of we-ness created a safe space that reduced the perceived risk of protest practices. As an entry point into high-risk activism, the risks of enacting the MySF-FB protest were considerable; many experienced tremendous fear. However, the sense of unity that the homogeneity of the protest performances and the metavoicing produced afforded activists a sense of safety. Being one of many and relatively indistinguishable from fellow activists gave individuals a sense of anonymity that reduced the perceived risk of the protest. The collective emotions of bravery and empowerment, in turn, increased its perceived value.

WWeds’ connected communities created individuated, affective relationships between activists and their followers. The videos, in which they expressed their opposition to hijab in self-revelatory, emotional terms, built solidarity and (re)constructed the division between the in- and the outgroup. That WWeds activists were accosted, arrested, and sentenced to long prison terms contributed to a collective identity of a threatened, moral community that individuals felt an obligation to. Committed to shouldering a share of the costs, followers validated an activist’s sense of self, thereby increasing the perceived value of protesting.

In GES, individuals were shamed into participating in the twitterstorm. The reputational costs of non-action are particularly high when the protest practice is low-risk/cost compared to martyr-activists’ self-sacrifice (e.g., a hunger strike). In GES, non-participation was visible not only to a large number of activists but also to individuals’ social networks. While the perceived cost of metavoicing diminished relative to the reputational cost of inaction, the value of twitterstorming increased with network size.

## Producing a Sense of Collectiveness in Online Social Movement

Having identified what protest practices enacted the identity configurations that characterized the three instantiations of MySF, as well as how the dialectic between individual and collective identities was temporarily stabilized (i.e., the three movement-level practices), we now abstract our theorizing further and visualize (Figure 12) how a sense of collectiveness is produced. The numbers represent the movement-level practices: (1) presencing the collective, (2) orchestrating collective action, and (3) moving activists to enact risky protest.

Given our performative view of identity, protest practices (i.e., sociomaterial doings and sayings that challenge the status quo) form the core of our model. Performing an embodied act of resistance, recording it, publishing it, and metavoicing over these acts, drive the (re)production of a sense of we-ness (i.e., the peripheral layer of the model).

Just as the collective enactment of physically embodied protest practices (e.g., marching together) can align activists physically, emotionally, and spiritually, individuals in an online movement can feel “in sync” with or “get the vibes” of the collective (Hopenwasser, 2008). As they “tune into” or are “swept up in” feelings that are not their own, they are moved by the movement’s “collective affects” (Anderson, 2009). Attuning to an online movement’s atmosphere is an embodied accomplishment (Ahmed, 2014).

However, attunement is not limited to individuals tuning into the collective’s emotions; instead, it is a bidirectional practice of synchronization (Hopenwasser, 2008), i.e., a “sociality of becoming responsive to others” (Ahmed, 2014, p. 9). For example, “if someone looks a little down you may try to lighten the mood with a joke, whereas if someone is very upset you may try to comfort them with a hug” (Ash & Gallacher, 2015, p. 72). Attunement is thus central to performative identity (Schultze & Brooks, 2019), as the selves that individuals perform are only brought into existence when others validate them.

As specific assemblages of how individuals see themselves (personal identity) and the collective as an actor (collective identity), identity configurations (e.g., imagined community) are temporary syntheses of I/we dialectics (e.g., “I” dissolved into the “we”). This synthesizing (re)produces the requisite feelings of we-ness in high-risk movements.

Movement-level practices (i.e., numbers 1, 2, and 3 in the model) shape the practices and conditions of their respective layer. These practices also align the three layers in a way that identity configurations that exhibit a sense of we-ness are produced. In the case of MySF, these movement-level practices were performed by Masih in her capacity as the organizer, a role typically played by social movement organizations (SMO). Since most social movements, even online ones, have an (informal) SMO (Tsatsou, 2018), we theorize that these movement-level practices are enacted by an entity other than the collective.

The first movement-level practice, presencing the collective, shapes the synthesis of the I-we dialectic. For individuals to join and identify with a movement, the collective needs to be “there.” This practice shapes how presence is enacted, e.g., through spatiality, interactivity, or simultaneity. The second movement-level practice, orchestrating collective action, (re)shapes attunement to optimize the production of a shared mood on a given social media platform. This practice is particularly important in online movements, where the embodied experience of shared affect builds the cohesion necessary for collective action. The third movement-level practice, moving activists to enact risky protests, (re)shapes the way that protest practices with different levels of risk are performed such that coherent identity configurations with their requisite sense of collectiveness are (re)produced on a given social media platform.

## Discussion

Having answered our research question by developing a model of how identity configurations that exhibit a sense of we-ness are produced, we now reflect on our contributions to the research on high-risk online activism and identity in online activism.

## High-Risk Online Activism

Online activism is frequently described as low cost/risk (Van Laer & Van Aelst, 2010), enacted through fairly homogeneous practices, specifically metavoicing (e.g., Treré, 2015). For example, the IS literature has mostly focused on practices that demand little effort and risk-taking from participants (e.g., Miranda et al., 2016; Selander & Jarvenpaa, 2016). Furthermore, the impact of online activism is viewed with skepticism, as creating an illusion of protest and being incapable of achieving real change (Lewis et al., 2014).

Our research challenges this characterization of online activism by theorizing the effortful enactment of high-risk protest practices that involved activists’ bodies and contributed to social change in a highly repressive context. While prior research has acknowledged hybrid activism (e.g., Caren et al., 2020; Syed & Silva, 2023), it has nevertheless privileged online practices and discounted activists’ embodiment (exceptions include Maher & Earl, 2019).

In our study, the role of activists’ physical bodies is central because the body is at risk when protesters are accosted physically or incarcerated; they are key in evaluating the value/risk ratio of high-risk activism. Additionally, as the seat of emotions, bodies are central to attunement, that is, tuning into collective emotions (Goldenberg et al., 2020) and being moved into synchronized action (Papacharissi, 2015). Although some research has explored the role of affect in online activism (Ahmed et al., 2017), we highlight its mediating role between the enactment of protest practices and the production of a sense of we-ness.

We also identify a movement-level practice, “orchestrating collective action,” aimed at producing a shared mood. In our comparative analysis, we note three distinct modes of synchronizing individuals’ and the collective’s emotions. These modes, i.e., homogeneity, rhythm, and momentum, draw on individuals’ embodied experience vis-à-vis others, thus emphasizing the importance of embodiment and affect in online activism.

Although the literature on high-risk movements highlights the importance of collective identity (McAdam, 2022), it nevertheless fails to articulate how a sense of we-ness affects activists’ willingness to take risks. We synthesize the prior literature on high-risk activism to propose ways in which collective identity might contribute to motivating individuals to join the movement, socializing them to construct an activist identity, and moving them to escalate their risktaking. We highlight the role that a sense of we-ness plays as individuals weigh perceived risk against perceived value in these three stages of the activist career path.

In addition, we identify three ways in which a sense of we-ness affects an individual’s risk/value assessment. How feelings of collectiveness move activists to expand their risk-taking depends on the movement’s identity configuration. For example, creating a safe space for activists to join and develop a sense of collectiveness was apparent in the imagined community configuration, while solidarity and shaming shaped the risk/value calculus in WWeds and GES, respectively.

## Revisiting Identity in Online Activism

Prior literature maintains that collective identity is neither feasible in nor a requisite of online activism (Bennett & Segerberg, 2012). Our research challenges this argument that the shared sense of we-ness that creates a collective actor loses its relevance when activism moves online (McDonald, 2015). We demonstrate that high-risk activism is a boundary condition for these claims and highlight not only the role that collective identity plays in processes critical to the success of high risk social movements (e.g., individuals joining the movement) but also how a sense of we-ness affects the risk/value trade-off that individuals use to justify putting their bodies at risk for a cause.

Inspired by Milan’s (2015) arguments that collective identity is not replaced by individual identity in online activism but instead reconfigured, we develop a new conceptualization of collective identity in online movements. Conceiving of individual (“I”) and collective (“we”) identity as paradoxically intertwined in an I/we dialectic, we develop the notion of identity configuration to capture the momentary yet repeatedly enacted syntheses of me-ness and we-ness. Through our abductive theorizing, we identify three different identity configurations (i.e., imagined community, connected communities, and flash crowd), which are produced through a different set of sociomaterial protest practices enacted on different social media platforms.

We also develop a theoretical model (Figure 12) of how identity configurations are (re)produced such that online social movements generate a sense of we-ness. Adopting a performative view of identity (Butler, 1995), which argues that identities are ongoing accomplishments rather than more or less stable representations of an entity, we regard the performance of protest practices as the engine of identity generation. For example, individuals became brave activists by performing MySF protest practices, and fighters by confronting all those they encountered with the possibility of a post-hijab Iran in WWeds.

The aggregation of these protest performances on FB, IG, and Twitter, in turn, produced a sense of we-ness characterized by feelings of bravery, confrontation, and suspense. Individuals who are attuned to these collective emotions are more likely to respond appropriately to other activists’ protest performances (e.g., congratulatory metavoicing), thereby validating their self-presentations (Schultze & Brooks, 2019). Emotions, at both the collective and individual levels, thus play a significant role in producing identity configurations characterized by a strong sense of we-ness.

In sum, our model of how identity configurations that exhibit sense of we-ness are produced in online activism offers a corrective to the representational view of identity that dominates the IS literature (e.g., Suh et al., 2011). It also counters the cognitivist view of collective action privileged in IS research; rather than relying on shared “we-intentions” (Li et al., 2022) to create a collective actor, shared affect is accorded this coordinating role.

We also identify three movement-level practices that are significant in the production of identity configurations that exhibit a sense of collectiveness. Each practice deals with a key moment in activists’ development. “Presencing the collective” is critical in getting would-be activists to join the movement; “orchestrating collective action” synchronizes participants’ protest enactments such that shared emotions, to which individuals can attune, are produced; and “moving activists to enact risky protest” leverages the sense of collectiveness to impact activists’ perceived risk and value of high-risk protest. Social movement organizations that enact these three movement-level practices also need to align the performance of these practices in order to support the production of coherent identity configurations that are capable of generating a sense of we-ness.

## Limitations and Future Research

Our contributions need to be considered in light of our study’s limitations. In particular, our study of a single high-risk movement, namely MySF, that operated in a repressive totalitarian regime, limits the cross-case generalizability to other empirical contexts. However, through our abductive theorizing, our study achieves within-case generalizability (Lee & Baskerville, 2012). By reading our data through the literatures on high-risk and online activism, as well as reading these literatures through our data, we generalize from empirical description to theory. Our theoretical insights (e.g., the concept of identity configurations, our theoretical model) thus render our results transferable to other empirical contexts.

For example, our model of how identity configurations characterized by a sense of collectiveness are produced promises to be valuable in research on online communities (e.g., Faraj et al., 2015), which are similar to low-risk movements. While this research on online communities explores how individuals’ identification with the group is accomplished and with what implications for the success of a community (e.g., Ren et al., 2012; Wade et al., 2020), this is predicated on the assumption that a group identity exists. What collective identity online communities are enacting and how remains largely unexamined. Our model offers a theoretical lens for questioning the givenness and stability of both individual and group identities. Future research might apply our model to investigate the kinds of identity configurations that are produced in online communities and low-risk social movements.

While there is little IS research that adopts a performative view of identity (exceptions include Bødker, 2017; Prasopoulou, 2017), this ontological stance challenges the either-or framing of personal vs. collective identity, online vs. offline activism, and cognition vs. affect. By conceptualizing these oppositions as dialectically intertwined, e.g., the “I” completing the “we” (and vice versa), we note that phenomena are ongoing accomplishments that are in a continuous state of becoming. Change can only be effected by shifting the practices that produce the phenomena. Future research could fruitfully investigate the corrective changes that are made in the protest practices that enact online social movements to create and/or preserve their sense of we-ness.

Our study explores the riskiness of protest practices as one of the boundary conditions under which the generation of collective identity in online activism is both feasible and necessary. Future research could focus on exploring other conditions under which a sense of we-ness is required for the success of online activism as well as how it is produced.

## Practical Implications

Our study has important practical implications for social movement organizations (SMOs) or organizers in repressive contexts. We identified three movement-level practices that are necessary to develop a sense of we-ness, which is central to maintain high-risk activism. By performing these movement-level practices, SMOs create the conditions for identity configurations that exhibit a sense of we-ness even in the context of online activism. This is achieved by supporting individuals in the different stages of their path as activists and by aligning the three practices to accomplish relatively stable identity configurations.

Moreover, our findings shed light on the necessity of maintaining entry points for newcomers to social movements, especially in light of the inevitable risk escalation that is necessary in the evolution of high-risk activism. As experienced activists are moved to continuously ratchet up the riskiness of their protest practices, would-be activists need to be socialized into the high-risk movement by providing lowrisk protest options (e.g., metavoicing).

The MySF case offers an example of how to maintain access to both high- and low-risk activism in parallel. This was accomplished by moving progressive instantiation of MySF on a new social media platform while maintaining the original instantiations. This cumulative approach to extending MySF’s risk repertoire meant that protest practices with varying levels of risk were available to individuals at different stages of their activist career path.

Our findings also highlight the role of bodies in online activism, be it high or low risk. An individual’s ability to tune into the collective mood of a group is a condition for (re)producing a sense of collectiveness. Dramatic public displays of opposition (e.g., the physically embodied GES protest practice) are likely to elicit a strong affective response capable of mobilizing a large number of people to complete the moving protest by engaging in low-risk metavoicing. Thus, in designing the practices for online activism, SMOs should attempt to leverage the emotional charge a given practice can generate.

Finally, we highlight how the features of different social media platforms were leveraged in the production of identity configurations characterized by a sense of we-ness. We demonstrate and theorize how such sociomatrial practices as aggregating photos in one virtual place (spatiality in MySF-FB), hashtagging in distributed virtual places (interactivity in WWeds), and trending a hashtag on a single digital platform (simultaneity in GES) brought the various instantiations of the online movement into existence (i.e., made them present).

## Conclusion

This research contextualizes prior studies of online social movements that find collective identity to be dispensable. We examined a high-risk social movement and found not only that a sense of we-ness was produced across its three instantiations but also that feelings of collectiveness played a critical role in the movement’s ability to recruit and socialize activists and move them to escalate the riskiness of their protest practices. In addition to offering activist risk exposure as a boundary condition to the extant conceptualization of online activism in IS research, we also develop a theoretical model of how a sense of collectiveness is produced through an enactment of sociomaterial protest practices, affective attunement, and the synthesis of the dialectical relationship between “I” and “we.” We advance the concept of identity configuration to capture the performative intertwining of activists’ individual and the movement’s collective identities in online activism.
