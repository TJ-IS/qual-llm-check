# A continental philosophy perspective on knowledge management

Lewis Hassell

Drexel University, 3141 Chestnut Street, Philadelphia, PA 19104, USA, email: lew.hassell@cis.drexel.edu

Abstract. Knowledge management is the computer’s contribution to management ‘science’ and claims to be the successor of various trends in the business world, including, but not necessarily limited to information resources management, business process reengineering, management information systems and organizational memory. A number of definitions have been proposed for it. The very concept of knowledge used by knowledge management writers, however, is based on a dubious epistemology. This paper looks at the concept of knowledge from a continental perspective. With this perspective, we question whether what is being managed is, in fact, knowledge, and whether management will get business what it wants anyway.

Keywords: continental philosophy, phenomenology, critical theory, knowledge management

## INTRODUCTION

The idea of ‘managing’ knowledge might have been considered somewhat amusing to philosophers in earlier times. Many of those ‘throwbacks’ would probably have conceived managing knowledge to mean managing truth.<sup>1</sup> This clearly is not what advocates of knowledge management mean by knowledge. What, then, are we attempting to manage, and what activities are involved in managing this which is called ‘knowledge’?

Reading the literature of knowledge management advocates, one might almost think that the goals of knowledge management seem akin to those of education or culture – or perhaps both. The Greek concept of paideia comes to mind (Jaeger, 1970). One may think of paideia as culture, for a start. It encompasses a consciousness of (shared) values that govern human life and an approach to educating the society in those values. But can this be what the purveyors of knowledge management systems mean? One would think not. Paideia carries with it a broader time frame, befitting the passage of multiple generations. What the business community seems to mean by knowledge management might span the passage of multiple department heads, but probably not even that. Paideia entails a mutual commitment of the members of the community to some broader, higher, mutually advantageous purpose. The purpose of knowledge management seems to be more on the order of supplying a short-term competitive advantage to a business or other organizational entity. The commitment to the top level individuals comprising those organizational units is somewhat dubious, to which the many recent news stories involving corporate greed, in numerous forms, will attest.

So as to provide a proper background for what I have to say, let me state my conclusions up front. There is no knowledge outside of experience.

1 Knowledge is therefore always embodied – i.e. it is always in a physical human being. (If god(s), angels, etc. have experiences, their experiences would be qualitatively different from that of human experience, assuming they have no bodies. If they do have bodies, a great many theologians will be sadly disappointed.)

2 Experience is always the experience of some (rational) individuals in a society. The operative phrase here is ‘in a society’.

3 Knowledge therefore is associated with a social group.

4 Computerized knowledge management therefore can only ever be global information management. By global information management I mean the widespread distribution of structured, semi-structured and unstructured organizational data.

A number of implications for knowledge management may thus be derived from my conclusions:

1 Attempts to capture real knowledge are doomed to failure. Knowledge always remains ‘in the wild’ – in people in a societal context.

2 Simply providing full-text documents or semi-structured documents, like those found in Lotus Notes® or OVAL (Malone et al., 1992), can lead to less productivity (indeed, less knowledge) unless great care is given to properly filtering and categorizing the information before presenting it to employees.

But even proper management and proper organization of information and documents will probably not get you what you want anyway. What those who look to the benefits of ‘knowledge management’ are really looking for is to foster creativity, which requires going beyond management to leadership.

In what follows, I will take a look, largely founded in continental philosophy, at ‘knowledge and ‘management’ in hopes of finding out what is meant by ‘knowledge management’. If I went no further, then this would be as idle an endeavour as medieval theologians debating how many angels can fit on the head of a pin. What I hope to achieve by the descriptive process is to indicate some prescriptions about what should be considered knowledge and the domain of knowledge management. Whatever philosophy’s purpose in bygone eras, it may now be the discipline that ‘keeps everyone honest’. It realizes that words have consequences and it demands a thorough accounting of those words. The larger implications of the business community’s attempt to ‘manage their knowledge assets’ for political and social philosophy also should be considered, and we hope that this paper provides a start.

## WHAT IS KNOWLEDGE?

First, philosophy (at least the modern, Western philosophical tradition) tells us that there is no knowledge outside the possibility of experience (Kant, 1965). And experience is the experience of a sentient being. While there is still lively debate in philosophical circles concerning the exact nature of experience (one can only imagine the heavenly debate between Popper and Merleau-Ponty), for present purposes I may consider it to mean that knowledge occurs within a context of social activity, or what continental philosophers call a Life World<sup>2</sup> (Husserl, 1970b). Furthermore, according to Paul Feyerabend (1975), knowledge is essentially idiosyncratic. Knowledge lies not in discrete elements that are provably correct, but in the interplay of, sometimes conflicting, elements (Feyerabend, 1999). Thus, knowledge has a curious characteristic – much like a radioactive isotope, it has a half-life. The fact that the earth revolves around the sun is just that – a fact. It is a datum. At some time in the past, it may have qualified as knowledge (and as Copernicus discovered, that knowledge can be dangerous), but not now. Knowledge is thus something that matters.

At this point, we find ourselves moving beyond the definition of truth as agreement of knowledge with its object. A datum may be considered more fundamental: the speed of light, the sum of 1 + 1 in a based 10 system. Knowledge and the objects of knowledge management systems are far more complex. They exist in a social context.

Knowledge, as the experience of a human being, means that there is no such thing as disembodied knowledge. Intelligence is not based on deductions made from rules overlaid on some massive database. Instead, it is a way of acting within an environment or community. (Indeed, what the Turing test should have required is to see whether a computer can act undetected in a society.) Decades of experiments with databases and expert systems have failed to come even close to human intelligence (Dreyfus, 2001). Even the ‘purest’ form of knowledge, mathematics, is still (arguably) based on the embodied mind (Lakoff & Nun z, 2000).<sup>3</sup>e˜

The latest developments in neuroscience tell us that the mind cannot exist without the brain’s healthy connection with the body – the extended body (Brothers, 1997; Damasio, 2000). (N.B. This does not require a healthy body.) Given that, it is interesting that many knowledge management theorists still act as if one can disembody knowledge (the product of mind, one would assume) and put it in a computer. Indeed, recent research in the brain sciences justifies the idea, ‘The body does not . . . merely separate individuals from one another. It is, on the contrary, an indispensable condition of one person’s sharing his experience with another’ (Todes, 2001, p. 2).

One does not even have to be a philosopher to realize that knowledge is not just some elaborate data that can be shoved into a data (or knowledge) base. Even many in the business community (e.g. McDermott, 1999) recognize that knowledge belongs to communities. But what does it mean to have a community? Is this community simply a group of ‘knowledge workers’? What about the clerks, secretaries and other ‘lower level’ workers? Surely these humble workers cannot be knowledge workers – or can they? Are they part of the community? Do they support and add value to its activities? Where does knowledge begin and non-knowledge end?

A number of philosophers (among them Cairns, 2000 and de Sousa, 1987) have argued convincingly that emotions may well manifest reason, and thus knowledge, as much as belief does. If the reader is unmoved by philosophical argumentation on this matter, he or she is referred to the neurologist Antonio Damasio (2000). Damasio shows that even if the ‘logical’ centres of the brain remain intact, if the emotional centres are damaged, the afflicted individual finds himself or herself incapable of making decisions, or at least appropriate decisions. Thus, the afflicted person is ‘irrational’. Indeed, the description that Leslie Brothers (1997) gives of autistic individuals – a lack of affect, poor or no people skills, despite an often keen logical ability – could equally be used to describe even the most powerful of computers. And if emotion does manifest reason, then emotion, too, is part of knowledge.<sup>4</sup> How does this impact knowledge management? Certainly, computers would be of dubious value here, regardless of the supposed ‘great advances’ that have been made in the field of affective computing (McDermott, 1999).

There is yet another aspect to knowledge which philosophers in the continental tradition refer to as ‘authenticity’. Heidegger’s (1962) treatment of authenticity has received perhaps the most discussion. Husserl (1970a), however, dealt with this concept before Heidegger in the Sixth Logical Investigation (which Husserl entitles, ‘Elements of a Phenomenological Elucidation of Knowledge’). Furthermore, it is well known that Heidegger was influenced by Husserl’s Sixth Logical Investigation. Jordan (1991) points out that there is little difference between the two on the subject. So what is authenticity or authentic knowledge? Authentic knowledge grasps the object of knowledge itself. This grasping is not mediated by some other person or by the form of representation. It is not second-hand. Furthermore, as Merleau-Ponty (1962) points out, authenticity is not the result of ‘analytical reflection’. We believe that inauthenticity describes the knowledge management movement quite well. While the ‘knowledge’ gathered by a knowledge management system may have been authentic for the person who put it there, it is certainly not authentic for the person who finds it in the system. This ‘knowledge’ is little more than data deprived of context. The answer to this problem might seem to be to include as much of the context surrounding the knowledge as possible. This is a slippery slope as aptly demonstrated by the sociologist Garfinkel (1976). Regardless of the number of facts and related facts one deposits in any repository, it does not recreate the experience.

Authentic knowledge, then, is the ‘gold standard’ of knowledge in continental philosophy. Inauthentic experience is more akin to hearsay. Knowledge management systems, then, are akin to inauthentic knowledge. Even the knowledge management systems that attempt to form ‘communities of practice’ must be questioned. Communities of practice in the ‘natural’ (or life) world are based on common values as well as common interests and beliefs. Thus, developing a real community of practice is more than upper management mandates.

Thus, knowledge is not just organized ‘stuff’ (‘information’, ‘data’, call it what you will). Knowledge is even more than just recognizing patterns in information, which is what vendors of knowledge management products would like you to think. Conceivably, one day, we will produce computers that perform as well as humans at recognizing complex patterns in piles of data, information, pictures, etc. But today, compared with human performance, computers are still poor at pattern recognition. For knowledge to occur, computers will also have to know when not to apply the information, to make exceptions, what to value – i.e. to reason. Furthermore, they will have to collaborate with others who can help. In short, they will have to be able to do the sort of thing that any good secretary can do today.

This confusion between knowledge and information abounds in the literature. For example, Stewart et al. (2000) state, ‘It is assumed that knowledge can be managed because technologies provide the capabilities to record, organize, reorganize and recall information in response to changing company needs’ (p. 47). While they discuss the problems associated with these activities, they never seem to see any problem with the rather mystical transformation of information into knowledge through these (computer-based?) activities.

According to whatis.com, ‘Knowledge management is the name of a concept in which an enterprise consciously and comprehensively gathers, organizes, shares, and analyzes its knowledge in terms of resources, documents, and people skills’. The confusion over the difference between information and knowledge is (once again) exemplified in the next paragraph, where they say, ‘Knowledge management involves data mining and some method of operation to push information to users’. Knowledge management involves ‘pushing’ information to appropriate users? Somehow, this seems less lofty a goal than many in the knowledge management community would like. Are we then to conclude that knowledge management is information management plus email?

Making implicit or tacit knowledge is thus another goal of knowledge management systems (Kreiner, 2000; Stewart et al., 2000). But the difference between implicit and explicit knowledge is not that one is ‘in your head’ and one is recorded. Implicit knowledge is that to which one cannot give ready voice. It may be knowledge that you use without knowing that you have until ‘you find it’ – and perhaps not even then. Lewicki et al. (1992) point out that oftentimes the implicit rules by which we operate are not recognized as those by which we are operating even when they were pointed out by the researchers. This is not to say that ‘elements’ (we put this in term in quotes because we wish to stress that it is an abstraction) of implicit knowledge are explicit. They are. The process in which these elements combine and morph, however, is a creative process that does not readily lend itself to capture. In his classic study of how epic oral poets sing their poems, Lord (1970) notes that every time a singer sings his song, he recreates the song using traditional components. These traditional components comprise the elements upon which the creative process is exercised. This seems to be closer to what modern business is looking for, and it cannot be bought on the cheap.

Moreover, if Feyerabend (see above) is correct – that knowledge is the interplay of discrete elements – then you alter knowledge either by altering the elements or by altering the interplay of them. Thus, extracting the ‘knowledge’ from someone’s ‘head’ is doomed to failure. The best one can hope to do is to order these discrete elements in one or more reasonable (whatever that means) arrangements for later retrieval. But this is not what seems to be meant by knowledge management.

Given the above, we can then conclude that knowledge cannot come from simple information transmission mechanisms but must come from a culture, which implies social organization, commitment and common values. And if providing a quick and ready reference to expertise (implicit or explicit) is what knowledge management is trying to achieve, then we are in fact trying to build an electronic community. Community, however, requires more than email (or any other, even more elaborate form or groupware).

## WHAT IS MANAGEMENT?

Philosophers traditionally have not been very active in the discussion of what is meant by management. Webster’s defines ‘to manage’ as ‘to direct or conduct business affairs’. That is the definition of the word as an intransitive verb. As a transitive verb, the definition is much more to the point: ‘to direct or control the use of’; ‘to exert control over’; ‘to make submissive to one’s authority, discipline, or persuasion’. In fine, the latter definition is what is really meant by ‘knowledge management’. Certainly, organizations can exert control over documents, but knowledge management is (supposedly) much more than document management. Certainly, organizations can exert control over their patents, trademarks and trade secrets, but knowledge management is (supposedly) much more (voluminous) than that. Knowledge management is supposed to include the ‘intellectual assets’ locked up in the organization’s employees – employee expertise and knowledge. And by virtue of what authority does an organization have the right to exert control over anything that is essentially the property of a human being (i.e. essentially embodied in the human being)? Laurie & Cherry (2003) point out that for managers, even language is ‘a tool for exerting control’.

We must be circumspect in our desire to dominate. There are those who believe that if nature rails at our attempt to dominate it, this can be solved by a little more domination and manipulation. (Global warming, regardless of whether President Bush believes in it or not, is an excellent example that this sort of dominating, manipulating sort of approach to the world may not work for long.) Even if we can ‘exert control over’ our material assets – make them ‘submissive to our will’ – this just sounds a bit odd, if not unpleasant, when directed to our ‘knowledge assets’. Is there any aspect of our life – physical, spiritual, social – which modern business does not want to dominate and control? Perhaps it is time to reread Marx.

If Karl Marx is unacceptable, then perhaps Michael Useem will better suit the taste of capitalists (Useem, 1998; 2003). Useem is a Wharton professor who discusses the importance of leadership, as opposed to management. Management of a business is simply ‘running’ it. When we manage we discharge our responsibilities to the organization. Management concerns the day-to-day activities of the business. Leadership, on the other hand, is creative. Leadership adds value. Leadership exceeds goals. Leadership is ‘the effective use of power for the greater good’ (Useem, 1998). Certainly, the greater good can include the needs of stockholders and upper management, but the greater good must include the good the average worker.

And if we read what Useem says about leadership, it has two other important qualities. Leadership is difficult, and leadership is a distinctly human activity. This is not to say that leadership is only a personal activity. There is also organizational leadership. Organizational leadership is ‘the ability to meld and exploit a team of associates’ (Useem, 1998, p. 90). Organizational leadership makes people want to excel. ‘Organizational leadership includes the exercise of change and development of other people . . . Leadership, then, can be viewed as leveraging what you are given to achieve far more’ (Useem, 1998, p. 91). Thus, while Useem is certainly not a critical theorist, it would seem that his concept of leadership legitimizes the individual or the organization.

It would seem that philosophers have been silent about management simply because they felt it mundane (thus betraying a bit of philosophical snobbery). They have written no end on virtue and character. But these are elements constitutive of leadership. Leadership also inherently falls under the concept of praxis (practice). In the Nichomachean Ethics, Aristotle (1962) tells us that if we want to know how to live a good life, we need to ask the man of practical wisdom. So, too, if we want to know what it means to be a leader, we must look at the lives and conduct of leaders.

## KNOWLEDGE? MANAGEMENT?

So, we can at least safely say that we want to ‘get’ these data/information/knowledge to knowledge workers. As the reader can guess, the definition of a knowledge worker is as nebulous as the definition of knowledge in ‘knowledge management’. Programmers are often referred to as archetypal knowledge workers. Factory workers, presumably, are not knowledge workers. Nor are farmers. But what about programmers who do not keep their skills up to date, or are not imaginative in their approach to algorithm construction – are they knowledge workers? And what of the factory worker who pours over a problem on the factory floor, even dreaming about it, and one day a solution ‘dawns’ on her – is she a knowledge worker? Or the farmer who uses satellite and advanced agricultural information from the government to produce more wheat – is he a knowledge worker? And what about our ancestors who invented the wheel, or a reliable way to produce fire – were they knowledge workers? Furthermore, as pointed out above, knowledge workers do not exist in a vacuum but in a social network much broader than their own narrow field or discipline.

In any case, what one would need in order to have real knowledge is an electronic Lebenswelt. The Lebenswelt (Life World) is the world of seeing, feeling, touching – in a word, experiencing. It is not the world of science, no less one of technology. Science and technology limit our experiencing. It is one thing to know what the mechanical or chemical composition of a thing. It is another to see, feel and smell it.

Again, perhaps what we need is electronic paideia – i.e. an extended culture with a system of values and education in those values. Building communities of practice is a popular topic in management information systems (MIS) literature. But in these discussions, one gets the idea that communities of practice can be built as isolated pockets within the organization. What about the place of each of these communities of practice (one can only assume that each organization has many) within the larger organization and the social commitments that glue the organization together? However, there is precious little discussion in knowledge management literature of the development of this type of community. Where is the discussion of shared values, of common commitment within the larger organization? One suspects it left when the focus was put on the computer. Or maybe it was when employees became information ‘assets’. Or maybe when those few at the top of an organization began to make hundreds of times what those at the bottom did.

A great many knowledge management systems seem to be textual databases, or semistructured document databases (e.g. Lotus Notes® or OVAL, Malone et al., 1992). As such, they are based on some sort of text or written expression (Day, 2001). Communication between people often (usually?) involves verbal communication. Research has shown, however, that linguistic expression is considerably different in written vs. oral discussion of the same subject domain (Hassell & Christenson, 1996). The impact of these differences has yet to be adequately explored by the knowledge management community.

On a related note, an interesting debate has occurred in the linguistics community about what text even means. Specifically, does text communicate authorial intentions (what the author intended in the writing of the text), or simply transmit information (Dixon & Bortolussi, 2001)? Those who contend that text does communicate authorial intentions centre their argument on the production of fiction (Gibbs, 2001). We must wonder if this applies to non-fiction, particularly business text, where the author(s) may or may not be known to the reader. Gerrig & Horton (2001) argue that communication is not dichotomous (communicating authorial intention or not) but continuous. Text may be communication, but it certainly is not the same as spoken language. The differences can be crucial.

According to the Gartner Group, knowledge management is a ‘discipline that promotes an integrated approach to identifying, capturing, evaluating, retrieving, and sharing all of an enterprise’s information assets’ (Srikantaiah & Koenig, 2000, p. 2). This includes not just paper-based and electronic data and documents, but also ‘uncaptured expertise and experience in individual workers’ (Srikantaiah & Koenig, 2000, p. 3). This would imply that the cognitive (conscious?) life of the worker is somehow the property of the employer. (Karl Marx would be fascinated, though perhaps not shocked, at this.) While it is reasonable to believe that an employer contracts with the employee to perform certain functions, and thus owns the result (artefact) of the process or activity, the ownership of one’s expertise and experience is troubling to those of us who do not yet recognize the deification of Adam Smith.

## FINAL THOUGHTS

What I have said is not somehow peculiar to philosophers or a particularly philosophical stance. In the Harvard Business Review (hardly the bastion of wild-eyed philosophers), John Seely Brown (1991) points out that work practices must be developed as one develops products. Furthermore, as discussed previously, Useem reminds us that organizations do not move forwards simply by efficiently managing what they have. Organizations move forwards (particularly in tough and perilous times) through leadership. According to Useem (1998), leadership involves ‘improving and perhaps even transforming what we have inherited from others’ (p. 263). That is what the advocates of knowledge management are claiming for their product (or discipline). Efficiently moving data, or documents, or whatever back and forth will never result in effectively using the knowledge that is embedded in an organization. Someone with a manager’s mentality will not become a leader. They will never inspire and motivate their employees to integrate – or to borrow a term from Hegel, synthesize – past and present knowledge to create new value for the company. And the computerized ‘knowledge repositories’ are little more than electronic libraries. Mohanty (2001) notes that the creation of philosophical texts is not sufficient for philosophy. ‘All that without the internal activity of thinking is not genuine philosophy, but a body of doctrines’ (p. 38). So, too, with the more mundane fields of human endeavour, including knowledge management, information, mixed and matched however one wishes, will not produce knowledge. One of the more flamboyant scientists in Britain, Baroness Susan Greenfield, in discussing the possibility of neural implants to aid the storage and recall of facts, noted that, ‘. . . it won’t make you smarter. What you do with it, that’s the hard part’ (Duncan, 2003).

We are constantly told that the old rules do not apply any more and that we are living in a new age. It is interesting that the old rules stopped applying about the time that we stopped applying the old rules: respect for workers who possess knowledge, who do a good job, who work hard. Of course, we were also told that the old rules of the stock market – profitability, sound business models, etc. – do not apply any more. I will assume that none of the readers are planning on retiring on their dot com stock portfolio.

If we can assume, then, that knowledge is sense impressions (or data, or whatever) within a structure as interpreted within the experiential background (let us call it a Lebenswelt), then real knowledge management is not possible without true community. Polanyi (1970) correctly argues, ‘Apart from meaningless sense impressions there is no experience that abides as a “fact” without an element of valid interpretation having been imparted to it’. Interpretation occurs against a backdrop of context as it occurs within a society.

Suffice it to say that what I have argued for implies that if you spend enormous amounts of time and money creating a ‘knowledge repository’, you will be sadly disappointed. What you must spend time and money on is your workforce. Humans are not replaceable pawns in the process of knowledge production. They embody the knowledge – indeed, they are the knowledge. Useful, even provocative, bits of information can help them, but that is all. The best the knowledge management community can do is to provide information that is well categorized (Smith, 1995) – something easier said than done.

Vendors and other practitioners, who have a vested interests in providing ‘knowledge management solutions’, are perfectly entitled to continue to call what they do ‘knowledge management’. It would be more productive, however, to recognize that they are communicating data or information (which is structured data) within an organization. Real knowledge ‘management must include instilling loyalty in the employee that can only come from a sense of legitimacy (the legitimation crisis of critical theorists, as applied to corporate governance) that modern capitalism and its social structures do not necessarily possess (e.g. Habermas, 1987). The legitimation crisis is a crisis of rationality – rational humans are being made automatons, and alienated from the natural world. Thus, they are stripped of their rationality. If modern commercial organizations have any social (as opposed to financial) justification, it must be to provide a reasonable living for workers. But as more of the corporate profits continue to be funnelled to those few at the top, while wages and health care benefits are carved off from those at the bottom, post-industrial commercial organizations find it harder to socially justify – legitimize – themselves.

We are free, of course, to redefine ‘knowledge’ and then declare that you have created a knowledge management system. That, however, will not change the underlying truths. Knowledge resides in the human being – not simply in the human brain, but in the human mind, which is to say, the whole human, social being.

Recognizing that people embody knowledge, some organizations, in the name of knowledge management, try to facilitate directing people with questions to people with answers. Without the creation of a true community, this can only be ‘knowledge herding’. Community – true community – implies shared values, a shared sense of purpose, shared rewards (not thousand fold difference in pay between the average worker and top management) and motivations. Twenty years ago, those of us who were building databases discussed the problems of getting people to populate our databases. What would motivate a person to take what they know – what makes them valuable to an organization – and share it with others? If you answer that question, you will find the answer to productivity, indeed, creativity. My bet is, however, that the answer will have little, if anything to do with a computer.

In the end, whatever knowledge is, it is certainly a very messy thing. The naïve positivism of the ‘information’ disciplines (information systems, computer and information science and management information systems, etc.) notwithstanding, we must be circumspect, at the very least, of attributing knowledge, especially to inanimate objects.

## Biography

Lewis Hassell earned his PhD in Information Studies at Drexel University in 1995. Before that, he studied philosophy at UC Berkeley and the New School for Social Research. His research interests include the theoretical foundations of information systems, the use of computers to support communication and collaboration, the neurobiology of information processing and trust, and the sociotechnical aspects of information assurance. Dr Hassell currently is a consultant living in the Research Triangle Park area of North Carolina.
