Abs tract: Privacy is a significant concern of customers in the business-to-consumer online environment. Several technical, economic, and regulatory mechanisms have been proposed to address online privacy. A current market-based mechanism is the privacy seal, under which a third party assures adherence by a vendor to its posted privacy policy. In this paper, we present empirical evidence of the effect of displaying a privacy seal on the product prices of online vendors of electronic books, downloadable audiobooks, and textbooks. Using data collected on these relatively homogeneous products sold by online vendors, we find that while controlling for vendor-specific characteristics, vendors bearing privacy seals charge a premium for such products compared to vendors not bearing a seal. The paper provides empirical evidence of the economic value of privacy assurance from the customers’ perspective as measured by the price premium charged for products. The research has implications for researchers and policymakers by providing evidence that privacy is another factor that creates friction in e-commerce, and that prices on the Internet for homogeneous products need not converge.

Key words and phras es : partial least squares, price dispersion, privacy assurance, risk premium, trust.

Online b us ines -to-cons um er (B2C) sales have grown rapidly and steadily across the globe for the past several years [68]. The U.S. Department of Commerce reports that in 2002, the value of e-commerce-based B2C retail sales totaled \$44 billion in the U.S. economy. This number grew from \$56 billion in 2003 to \$134.7 billion in 2008, reflecting a large volume of B2C sales.<sup>1</sup>

While this volume of sales is expected to continue, researchers and businesses are paying considerable attention to issues that may hinder the growth of e-commerce. One issue that has been identified as a significant obstacle in the B2C context is online privacy [21, 26]. Information privacy is defined as the individual’s ability to control the collection and use of personal information [56, 65]. A commercial transaction often requires a customer to reveal private information such as identification-related information and product preferences. The collection of such information, along with its potential misuse, causes privacy concerns for a customer [37]. For example, when a firm sells a customer’s information to other firms without the customer’s permission, it constitutes a misuse [19].

Businesses use customer data in several ways that could lead to privacy concerns. First, many businesses use customer data to direct unsolicited promotions (spamming) to their customers [29]. When the customer is not given notice that he or she would receive offers in the future, then such solicitations intrude against the customer’s right to be left alone [22]. In the online environment, the cost for conducting such promotions is low, therefore making spamming much more prevalent. Second, businesses collect data on customers’ browsing and purchasing behavior to personalize products and services. By themselves, such uses may not be construed by customers as privacy violations and, to the extent they facilitate customer relationship management, may be welcomed by customers. However, advanced data-mining techniques—click-stream analysis, association mining, text mining, and statistical techniques—enable sellers to profile and price discriminate customers using the collected data [20].<sup>2</sup> Profiling and price discrimination are causes for privacy concerns among customers [13, 59]. For instance, by profiling customers using data-mining techniques, a firm could charge different prices to customers with different profiles.

Businesses often collect individuals’ purchase and personal information in one market for use by others or themselves in another market. By sharing these data, businesses are able to construct data sets that combine individuals’ browsing and purchase behavior over multiple Web sites over time. This exacerbates the occurrence of spamming and price discrimination, leading consumers to believe that they have lost control over their own data [4]. The public outcry against DoubleClick for attempting to merge online browsing data with offline transaction data is an example of customers’ reaction to threats to privacy [27]. Finally, with the proliferation of malicious software programs, it becomes difficult for firms to secure their data in absolute terms. Customers are wary that the data may fall into the hands of malcontents who will ruin them financially or embarrass them socially [52].

Businesses recognize that privacy concerns can turn customers away from conducting e-commerce transactions. It has been estimated that these concerns could lead to billions of dollars in lost revenues [39]. Although privacy concerns are not altogether a new phenomenon, they have taken on a much greater significance in recent years with the proliferation of e-commerce sites. There are several reasons that have led to this surge in privacy concerns. In the offline context, the physical nature of a firm helps build trust [69]. Further, a customer may have the option to remain anonymous during a transaction by using cash if the customer is concerned about his or her privacy. Such anonymity is usually not possible for online transactions. Customers need to set up an online account and provide various details such as contact information and financial information. These reasons underscore why privacy concerns among customers affect e-commerce.

As a reaction to the demand from customers that vendors “take privacy seriously,” vendors attempt to reassure customers through notices known as privacy policies. Such policies are posted on a firm’s site and inform customers about the types of personally identifiable information that are collected and ways such information are used or shared with other entities. However, it is difficult for customers to determine how well vendors adhere to these policies [30]. Businesses that make an effort to adhere to their posted privacy policies (“scrupulous” vendors) incur higher costs because they institute stringent data management policies and monitor data use continuously [47]. Scrupulous businesses cannot easily pass on these higher costs to customers because businesses that do not adhere to their posted privacy policies will pretend to be scrupulous as well; this is particularly so in the privacy context because it is difficult for customers to observe privacy-related practices. Customers are not able to tell which businesses do not adhere to their posted privacy policies, and hence all businesses are mistrusted [62]. Thus, efforts made by scrupulous businesses to assure privacy through a privacy policy may not result in any benefits.

To reduce the information gap between businesses and customers in the privacy context, businesses could acquire privacy seals granted by third parties. The use of trusted third parties has been found to alleviate friction in e-commerce in other contexts, such as in consumer-to-consumer markets, and in the security context in B2C markets [45]. A privacy seal provider is a third party that periodically audits, for a fee, a business’s privacy policy and its data collection and processing practices to ensure congruence between the policy and the practices [40]. If a business is found to have adhered to its stated policy, the privacy seal provider grants it a seal, which the business then displays on its Web site. Thus, by obtaining a third-party privacy seal, businesses that adhere to their posted privacy policies signal their scrupulous nature to their customers.

We observe that for many products, there currently exist some vendors that bear a seal and others that do not. For such products, customers have a choice between buying from a seal-bearing vendor that provides better privacy assurance and buying from a vendor without a seal. The latter choice is associated with an enhanced risk of privacy violation. If customers and vendors are rational, then getting additional assurance of privacy should manifest in prices that differ between vendors that bear a seal and those that do not. An interesting empirical question, therefore, is whether seal-bearing vendors are indeed able to charge a higher price for providing a higher level of privacy assurance. If seal-bearing vendors are able to charge a higher price, then it is clear that consumers are willing to trade privacy assurance for a price, which is the economic value of the assurance provided by a privacy seal.

Existing research on the effect of privacy seals has not examined whether seal-bearing vendors are able to charge a higher price than other vendors. Some prior research has examined whether the presence of a privacy seal affects customers’ willingness to disclose personal information and their intention to purchase, but the findings are inconclusive. On one hand, Belanger et al. [8] find that the presence of a privacy seal does not significantly affect customer beliefs and intentions (details are discussed in the Literature Review section). On the other hand, Larose and Rifon [34] and Miyazaki and Krishnamurthy [38] find that the presence of a privacy seal can influence customers favorably. These papers highlight the importance of studying the effect of privacy seals in online contexts. At the same time, they leave open the question regarding the economic value that could be attributed to privacy seals. In light of this, we address the following research question:

RQ: Do seal-bearing vendors in the B2C context charge a higher price than vendors that do not bear a privacy seal?

Three categories—e-books, audiobooks, and textbooks—were chosen for this study. Products in these categories are highly homogeneous, and their content is the only attribute that significantly affects pricing of most products in these categories. This helps minimize biases from product-specific factors that can affect prices. For each category, product prices were collected for seal-bearing vendors and vendors not bearing a seal. The prices were randomly sampled for a large number of products in each category. We control for various vendor-specific variables that have been theorized in extant literature to affect product pricing. Regression models were used to determine the magnitude of the price premium, if any.

The results indicate that for the product categories considered, seal-bearing vendors charge a price premium for privacy assurance through a privacy seal. The magnitude of this premium is found to be 1.5 percent compared to the prices of the vendors not bearing the privacy seal. The contribution of this paper is that it shows empirical evidence of the economic value of privacy assurance from the customers’ perspective as measured by the price premium they pay for products. An interesting implication of this research is that it provides evidence that privacy is another factor that creates friction in e-commerce. The research has policy implications in that it shows that market mechanisms for handling privacy concerns appear to be working.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide a review of related literature on privacy policies and privacy seals. Then we present the central hypothesis and develop the models used for the analyses. The data collection procedures are discussed in the fourth section, and the results of the analyses are presented in the fifth section. The findings are discussed and directions for future research close the paper.

## Literature Review

Although privacy has b een dis cus ed for over four decades (e.g., [56, 65]), the advent of the Internet has renewed discussion on this topic because of the ubiquity of information technologies and the relatively inexpensive collection and analysis of data that this has afforded [21, 35]. Various technological, regulatory, and business mechanisms have been proposed to protect customers’ private information and alleviate privacy concerns. Technology mechanisms include approaches such as privacyenhancing data mining [1, 9] that reduce the likelihood of privacy violation when data are shared among businesses. Regulatory mechanisms include legislation such as the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, which prescribes how financial services should share data [2]. Privacy policies and privacy seals are the dominant business mechanisms and are the focus of our study.

We classified the relevant literature into three groups based on the objectives of the papers. The first group examines the beliefs that customers have about privacy policies and seals [18, 39]. The second group of papers looks at how privacy policies and seals affect willingness to disclose personal information [28, 30, 34] and the intention to purchase [8]. The third objective is the comparison of government regulation of privacy and self-regulation by the industry through privacy seals [58].

In examining the diversity among privacy policies, Culnan [18] surveyed privacy policy statements of several Web sites and found a considerable diversity in terms of their comprehensiveness. This diversity, coupled with the difficulty customers have in verifying that stated policies are being faithfully implemented, are the likely causes for the emergence of privacy seals. Moores [39] studied the perceptions that customers have about privacy seals. He found, using a controlled experiment, that students were not able to distinguish between seals, and concluded that customers may not understand the role and the value of privacy seals.

There are several papers that study the effect of privacy policies and seals on customer behavior. Hann et al. [28], using an experimental approach, attempt to find out whether individuals value privacy in online transactions and, if so, how much these individuals would pay for assurance of their privacy. Asking students to rank the trade-off between three types of privacy concerns and two levels of monetary rewards, they found that respondents were willing to trade off privacy concerns for a monetary compensation of approximately \$49. Hui et al. [30], collaborating with a market research firm in Singapore, examined the effect of privacy policy statements and privacy seals on an individual’s propensity to disclose personal information. They found that although the existence of a privacy statement induced subjects to disclose personal information, the presence of a privacy seal had no significant effect. On the other hand, Larose and Rifon [34], drawing on experiments conducted on students using a dummy Web site, found that the presence of a privacy seal increased the information disclosure by subjects.

Belanger et al. [8] and Miyazaki and Krishnamurthy [38] studied how privacy policies and seals influence the customer’s intention to purchase. Belanger et al. [8], using an experimental setup with students, found that a privacy seal did not affect the intention of a subject to purchase, and concluded that subjects are likely to have already “resolved privacy concerns” before visiting a Web site to purchase a product. On the other hand, experiments conducted by Miyazaki and Krishnamurthy [38], also with students as subjects, revealed that the subjects perceived the privacy policy statement and the privacy practices of a seal-bearing vendor to be “favorable” compared to those of a vendor not bearing a privacy seal.

Finally, Tang et al. [58] analytically compared three options—government regulation of privacy, self-regulation by the industry through privacy seals, and no formal regulation of either kind (i.e., caveat emptor). They found that the privacy seal is the socially optimal solution compared to the other two options, when both the number of people sensitive about privacy and the size of the privacy loss that they face are not “high” or “low” as characterized by the authors.

## Privacy Seal, Price Premium, and Related Variables

The ab ove review of literature s ugges ts that consumers value privacy concerns, and when a vendor provides privacy assurance, the vendor is providing a service of value to the consumer. According to the theory of value-based pricing, if a vendor is fully informed about a customer’s preference and valuation, this vendor can charge a price equal to how much the customer values a product—that is, the reservation price (the willingness to pay) of the customer [46].<sup>3</sup> However, if additional costs are incurred by customers, vendors lower prices to account for the net lower reservation price. For example, search costs reduce the net reservation price [63]. Similarly, the risk of privacy violation may lower a customer’s net reservation price.

To flesh out the economic value associated with privacy concerns, the effect of potential privacy violations is framed as an expected cost calculation [61]. This expected cost (from the customer’s perspective) is the product of the following terms: the probability of misuse of the customer’s personal data by a vendor, the probability of not being able to prove that the vendor violated the posted privacy policy if a misuse did occur, and the costs from a privacy violation (such as price discrimination, identity theft, inconvenience). The cost to a customer from a privacy violation is likely to be the same, regardless of whether a vendor is seal bearing or not. But, in the event of a misuse, the probability of proving that the posted privacy policy was violated is likely to be lower when transacting with vendors not bearing a seal than with seal-bearing vendors, for the same level of effort exerted by the customer [58]. This is because the privacy seal provider acts as an intermediary between the customer and the vendor, assisting both parties in the process. In addition, because the seal-bearing vendor is evaluated periodically by the privacy seal provider, such a vendor is less likely to misuse the customer’s data, either intentionally or inadvertently.

If the risk (i.e., expected cost from a privacy violation) is significant compared to the reservation price of a product, then a vendor will need to lower the price to induce the customer to purchase the product. Consequently, a vendor that is able to assure privacy by obtaining a privacy seal will be able to charge higher prices for the same products than the price charged by a vendor that is not able to assure privacy at the same level. However, if the expected cost borne by the customer from a privacy violation is not significant, the presence of a privacy seal need not lead to a higher price for the seal-bearing vendor.<sup>4</sup> To help resolve this issue, the following hypothesis will be tested empirically:

Hypothesis: For the product categories considered, the price of a product charged by an online vendor bearing a privacy seal is higher than the price of the same product charged by an online vendor not bearing a privacy seal.

In summary, the hypothesis examines whether the adoption of a privacy seal can, by reducing information asymmetry between a customer and a vendor (in relation to the vendor’s adherence to its privacy policy statement), enable a seal-bearing vendor to charge higher prices than those without a seal. We equate this price premium with the economic value of a privacy seal to a consumer. This is analogous to how some vendors in auction markets are able to charge higher prices for their services by reducing information asymmetry, something that is made possible by the use of online feedback mechanisms [5].

## Variables Potentially Affecting Price Premium

The hypothesis can be tested using a model relating the price premium to the presence of a privacy seal. Price premium is the excess price a customer pays over the fair price and “true” value of a product [49]. To test the hypothesis, it must be ensured that the variance in prices is explained by the presence or absence of a privacy seal only, and other variables that affect prices must be controlled for. A retailer may price its products higher than another retailer due to product-related characteristics, such as product differentiation, and vendor-specific characteristics, such as reputation and service-related attributes. As mentioned earlier, we control for product-related characteristics by selecting products from three categories that are very homogeneous across different vendors. In order to account for vendor-specific characteristics, we identify from the literature a fairly comprehensive list of variables that could affect product prices [3, 7, 16, 17, 36, 44] and control for them explicitly in our model. We discuss how these variables could affect vendor prices directly or indirectly (e.g., through market power, customer satisfaction, etc.). We list these variables in Table 1.

The first variable, REPUTATION, is well recognized in the literature as affecting product prices (e.g., [54]). A firm’s good reputation can be a competitive advantage over other firms, enabling the firm to charge a higher price for its products [50, 55]. The second variable is AWARENESS. A high awareness among prospective customers can provide a firm a distinct advantage in selling its product [48]. The third variable is the operational competence of a Web site (COMPETENCE ), which has been found to affect customer satisfaction and loyalty [6]. A customer is typically satisfied with a well-designed page and fast download times, and such satisfaction engenders loyalty over time [42]. The firm may then be able to charge a higher price for its product. The next variable is the attention that a site holds (ATTENTION ). The more attention that a site gets from a customer, the more likely it is that the customer will purchase from the site or revisit the site [31]. Thus, the ability to get attention is an advantage that a firm can use to set higher prices.

This ability could also affect prices that a retailer sets [36]. The degree of rivalry in a market (COMPETITION) affects the power wielded by a retailer in that market [7]. The next three variables have to do with whether the Web site provides reviews of products, which are thought to affect prices [16]. First, the retailer, acting as an expert, may provide reviews of products (STORERVW ), thereby helping customers compare products. This occurs when customers purchasing a product in one category from a store also purchase products in other categories from the same store [53]. ## Data Collection

We des igned the data collection s trategy to best control for both product and vendor characteristics. We collected price data on products that are homogeneous so that the prices across vendors for the same product are comparable. To control for vendor characteristics, data from as many vendors as possible for these product categories were collected. Next, we discuss the identification of the product categories, and the vendors considered in each category. To address the product-specific characteristics that may lead to variations in prices, data on a large number of items were collected in each product category. The item selection process was randomized, as described below. Finally, we describe how the vendor characteristics were operationalized, along with their respective data sources.

## Identifying Product Categories and Privacy Seal Providers

The three categories—e-books, downloadable audiobooks (which we refer to as audiobooks), and textbooks—were selected to control for product-related characteristics. In each category, at least one vendor was a privacy seal–bearing vendor and at least one vendor did not bear a privacy seal. E-books and audiobooks are obvious choices for several reasons. First, both e-books and audiobooks are perfectly homogeneous in product format (e.g., PDF for e-books and MP3 for audiobooks, unlike physical books and audiotapes),<sup>5</sup> consumption tools (e.g., Acrobat Reader for e-books and Microsoft Media Player for audiobooks), and upstream suppliers (the same titles on all sites are published by the same publisher). Given the homogeneous nature of these products, they are most likely to experience strong price competition. Second, these are both information products whose delivery is made in real time over the Internet. This eliminates other costs such as shipping and handling charges and delays in delivery that could differ across vendors. Consequently, by choosing e-books and audiobooks, it was ensured that the attributes of a product remained identical regardless of the site from which it was being sold.

A practical limitation that stems from choosing these two homogeneous product categories is that it significantly limits the number of vendors available, especially those with a privacy seal. Therefore, a category for textbooks was also added to the list. Although textbooks do not possess all of the above desirable attributes of e-books and audiobooks, they are widely considered to be homogeneous products, and have been chosen in various studies on e-commerce pricing strategies for this reason (e.g., [10, 11, 14]). Of the three broad-based U.S. seal-granting organizations, TRU STe and BBBOnline Reliability are the seal providers for vendors in the selected product categories.<sup>6</sup>

## Selecting Products for Comparison

Data were collected between November 2008 and January 2009.<sup>7</sup> To collect information on e-books, audiobooks, and textbooks from seal-bearing and other vendors, we followed these steps: identification of vendors for each product category (and separating them into seal-bearing versus other vendors), random selection of book genre, random selection of books within the selected genre, and collection of price data for each book from every vendor carrying the book.

To identify vendors for e-books, we supplied the search terms “e-book” and “electronic book” to the Google search engine. The search returned sponsored links and organic results. We went through the first 60 entries in the organic results and all sites that appeared in the associated sponsored lists. From these, we ignored libraries, publisher Web sites, and free download sites. We arrived at a list of 17 vendors that sell e-books from multiple publishers. For each vendor site, we read the “About us,” “Contact us,” and the privacy policy page. Three vendors were subsidiaries of another vendor, and were dropped. This led to 14 vendors, of which one bore a TRU STe seal and another bore a BBBOnline Reliability seal. Table 2 lists the vendors in this category.

Our goal was to collect data on at least 100 books from each vendor. To do this, the first step was to compile a list of genres, which was done from the site of the e-book vendor bearing the TRU STe seal. This site had 59 genres. Each page within a genre contains information on around 10 books. We used a random number generator function to identify at least 10 genres, and eventually ended up with 12 genres (because of the probabilistic nature of the selection process). We then attempted to randomly pick one page from each of the genres selected. Data on all books listed on the selected pages were collected, leading to 123 items and their prices.<sup>8</sup> Each item was available in different formats (PDF, Palm Reader, etc.). We collected the price for the PDF version only because this was the most widespread format for e-books at the time of data collection. After the list of items was culled from the first seal-bearing vendor, we collected the prices for the same items (for the same format) from the remaining vendors. We finally arrived at a list of 1,320 prices from the 14 vendors that sell e-books.<sup>9</sup> Note that some vendors did not carry all the items identified.

## Collecting Data on Vendor Characteristics

We discuss how we operationalized each of the potential variables affecting price premium that are listed in Table 1. The four variables—REPUTATION, AWARENESS, COMPETENCE, and ATTENTION—were measured using statistics that are provided by the Web site Alexa (www.alexa.com). To measure the REPUTATION of a vendor, the number of in-links to a vendor’s site was used. In the online context, a firm’s reputation is built when other sites reference the firm on their site, whereby an in-link from another site is viewed as a form of recommendation for the firm [67]. AWARENESS for a site was measured using the statistic that provides the percent of global Internet users who visited the site in the most recent three months. Operational competence (COMPETENCE) was measured by the average load time of a vendor’s site. When a particular site loads faster than other sites during a customer’s visits, the site is perceived by the customer to be superior compared to the other sites, which in turn translates to a competitive advantage for the firm [6]. We measured the variable ATTENTION as the average number of pages viewed per user session for the vendor.

The next variable, LIBRARY, was measured by the count of all the products on a vendor’s Web site. Some vendors’ sites display the total number of products the vendor carries when a user browses the Web site. For other sites, using the search facility without specifying any search term resulted in the full catalog of products being displaying. The variable COMPETITION was measured by the number of competitors included in our analysis for each product category considered in this research. For PRODUCTVAR, we included three binary variables (PRODUCTVAR1, PRODUCTVAR2, and PRODUCTVAR3) for the three product categories considered and a fourth binary variable, PRODUCTVAR4, that was set to 1 if the vendor sold products in any other category. All the remaining variables were measured as binary variables by examining each vendor site. Table 5 lists the measurement and the source for each vendor characteristic.

## Empirical Model and Analysis

To validate our hypothes is , we first considered a linear model f (.) relating price premium and the presence of a privacy seal that accounted for all the control variables identified earlier:

$$
\text { PRICEPREMIUM } = f (\text { PRIVACYSEAL }, \text { Control   Variables }) + \varepsilon .
$$

The dependent variable, PRICEPREMIUM, is the excess price paid over the fair price of a product [49]. The average price of an item across all vendors is a reasonable proxy for the fair price of an item [24]. This is particularly appropriate in our case because the items selected in each category are homogeneous across all vendors. Data for all three product categories were pooled for our analysis.

Results from an ordinary least squares (OLS) analysis showed that the variance inflation factors (VIF) of several independent variables were higher than 5. This indicated that, because of multicollinearity, the estimates of coefficients and their errors will depend on the sample and may not represent the population estimates [41, p. 300]. Other studies have similarly found that some vendor characteristics are correlated with each other (e.g., [16]). To reduce multicollinearity in the model, we adopted a principal components approach to determine how groups of correlated variables can be combined into uncorrelated factors [41, p. 418]. We conducted a principal components analysis on the independent variables. The minimum eigenvalue for extracting a factor was specified to be 1 [32, p. 376]. Only orthogonal factors are extracted from a principal components analysis, so that the final factors will be uncorrelated to each other. Six factors emerged as a result (Table 6).

Following the recommendations of Gefen and Straub [25], we used two rules of thumb to determine which independent variables loaded significantly on a factor. The first rule of thumb is that loadings below 0.40 are not significant. The second rule is that, for an independent variable that loads above 0.40 on multiple factors, there must be a difference of 0.1 or more between its highest loading on one factor to its nexthighest loading on a different factor for the independent variable to load significantly on the first factor. The loading of this independent variable on the other (second-highest loading) factor is then ignored. Independent variables that did not pass the two tests are dropped from further analysis.

## Discussion

The ease with which data on customers can be collected, analyzed, and shared using current technologies has made privacy-related concerns an important consideration for both firms and their customers. Our work adds an interesting dimension to the literature on privacy in online environments. Prior research has recognized that the privacy of a customer has become an economic commodity, as exemplified by Hann et al. [28], who attempted to quantify the economic value of privacy by examining the monetary value respondents (e.g., students) place for trading off their privacy concerns. To our knowledge, ours is the first empirical work investigating the economic effect of a privacy seal. Further, we use objective price data, which goes beyond experimental and survey-based research that studied the relationship between privacy seals and individual behavior such as the willingness to disclose personal information (e.g., [34]) and the intention to purchase (e.g., [8, 38]). Our research provides hard economic evidence that vendors bearing a privacy seal can charge a premium for their products.

Our findings attest mainly to the proposition that customers value the assurance a seal provides—namely, that a vendor will adhere to the privacy-enhancing actions implied in a privacy policy statement. The privacy seal provides such an assurance to customers by shifting (at least partially) the burden of proof associated with demonstrating potential privacy violations from the customer to the seal provider. Our finding thus lends credibility to the notion that in the event of misuse of a customer’s information, the customer will be more likely to prove (with the assistance of the seal provider) that the posted privacy policy was violated when transacting with seal-bearing vendors than to prove such violations occurred when transacting with vendors not bearing a seal. When developing the hypothesis, we theorized that customers perceive a seal-bearing vendor to be less likely to misuse customers’ data, either intentionally or inadvertently, because the vendor is audited periodically by the privacy seal provider. Our finding bolsters this explanation. Because the presence of a privacy seal enables vendors to charge a higher price, we infer that the expected cost borne by the customer from a privacy violation is significant, so that the privacy assurance through a privacy seal is a value-added service to the customer.

Our finding reinforces the notion that seal programs can provide a viable marketbased mechanism for providing privacy protection [58]. Comparing seal-based mechanisms to a mandatory standards approach (where governments enact strict privacy standards, thereby providing a very high level of trust to consumers) using analytical models, Tang et al. [58] found that the seal-based approach can be potentially more efficient when some customers are sensitive toward their privacy and the potential loss to customers is not very large. Our findings demonstrate that for the product categories considered in this research, there do exist a sufficient number of customers who value privacy and are willing to pay a nominally higher price for the assurance provided by the existence of the seal on vendors’ sites. Furthermore, the presence of a privacy seal mitigates the effort required by customers to read and understand the implications of privacy policy statements posted on a site. Because the presence of a privacy seal leads to a higher product price than the absence of a privacy seal, the underlying implication is that enough customers also pay attention to the privacy policy, either directly or indirectly, through the services of the seal provider, so it is worthwhile for some vendors to subscribe to such a service. Thus, the seal programs, implemented through a trusted third party, are enabling industry to adopt a self-regulatory approach to foster trust between customers and sellers.

## Conclusions

In this paper, we pres ented em pirical evidence of the effect of displaying a privacy seal on prices of online vendors. We collected price data on products from three homogeneous categories (e-books, audiobooks, and textbooks) sold by seal-bearing vendors and vendors without a seal. Our analyses show that seal-bearing vendors for these product categories charge a price premium for their products as compared to vendors without a seal. We are also able to quantify the magnitude of this privacy seal–induced premium, which is 1.5 percent of the price charged by a vendor not bearing a seal, after accounting for other variables that affect prices.

An interesting issue that arises is whether all firms should adopt privacy seals if adopting such a service can enable them to charge a price premium. Clearly, the majority of online vendors have not chosen to do so yet. A reputable and well-established firm may undertake the efforts needed to implement its stated privacy policy and expect its reputation to provide the necessary assurance to its customers. Finally, privacy assurance is only one of several aspects of service that customers value, as the empirical results show. Vendors have a multitude of ways in which to differentiate themselves online, and adopting a privacy seal is one such way.

Of course, our research addresses only one aspect of the larger set of issues associated with privacy seals, and raises several questions that deserve further research attention. An important question is what characterizes vendors that subscribe to privacy seals and what characterizes vendors that do not subscribe? Will vendors find it profitable to charge a premium in the long run? What are the substitutes for privacy seals, and could they obviate the need for such seals completely? When should a firm obtain a privacy seal? If a firm does not obtain a seal when its main competitors do so, could it mean that the firm’s data management policies, processes, and culture are creating a significant hurdle for complying with the requirements of seal providers? These are questions for future research. Finally, future research should also look at the effect of regulatory and technological mechanisms to assure customer privacy.

One limitation of our work is that we were able to collect data on only a small number of vendors, as the number of vendors carrying a privacy seal is small. This was because we did not find other categories of homogeneous products that were sold by both seal-bearing vendors and vendors not bearing a seal. Further, the privacy seal– based premium identified for products included in our analysis may not necessarily generalize to other products, especially differentiated products where it will be difficult to separate out the price premium due to privacy assurance and price premium due to product differentiation. We note that privacy policies for the Internet are still evolving, as are adoption of privacy seals. In time, many more categories of products will be sold over the Internet, and several vendors might adopt privacy seals. This should eventually provide an even richer data set to analyze issues related to privacy seals and price premium.

Our findings should be of interest to researchers and practitioners alike. This paper contributes to the growing body of work on privacy-related research and adds another important dimension to extant findings. Our results would help firms make more informed decisions regarding their privacy portfolio in order to cater to their customers’ needs. Our findings should be of interest to privacy seal providers also as they develop their marketing strategies involving pricing and targeting decisions. This work should also help policymakers assess how a market mechanism could alleviate privacy concerns of customers.
