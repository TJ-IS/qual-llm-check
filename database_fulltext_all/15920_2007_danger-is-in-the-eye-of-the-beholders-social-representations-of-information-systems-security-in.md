# Danger is in the eye of the beholders: Social representations of Information Systems security in healthcare

Emmanuelle Vaast <sup>\*</sup>

School of Business, Long Island University, Brooklyn Campus, 1, University Plaza, H 700, Brooklyn, NY 11 201, USA

Available online 29 June 2007

## Abstract

This paper investigates the social representations of Information Systems (IS) security of diferent communities working in a healthcare organization. It considers questions of IS security as socially constructed and dependent on how people make sense of their context of work.

The social representations perspective presented in this paper is especially useful to make sense of IS security from the point of view of various communities. The paper illustrates the relevance of this perspective by analyzing the diferences and similarities in the way members of seven occupational communities (e.g., physicians, nurses, IS professionals) working in the same healthcare organization represent IS security. The paper finally draws the strategic implications for research and practice of considering the social representations of IS security. In particular, security and awareness programs should be customized to acknowledge that members of various communities ‘‘know’’ diferent things about security and that they react diferently to various security initiatives. - 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Social representations; Security; Privacy; Occupational communities; Healthcare; Interpretive qualitative study

## 1. Introduction

Information Systems (IS) scholars and practitioners have long considered seriously security and privacy issues (Conway et al., 1972; Loch et al., 1992; Straub, 1990; Ware, 1984). These issues have recently become even more critical for today’s organizations (Dhillon and Backhouse, 2000; Dutta and Roy, 2003; Greenaway and Chan, 2005; Karat et al., 2005). Organizations have become increasingly dependent on technology to achieve their missions. At the same time, new technologies (such as web-based applications and wireless devices) have triggered new breaches to security and privacy (Di Pietro and Mancini, 2003; Katos and Adams, 2005; Ryan and Bordoloi, 1997). Hackers, viruses, and legal liabilities have also made businesses more cautious about security (Mercuri, 2004b; Panko, 2004).

In this context, addressing IS security issues has become of paramount importance to managers and IS professionals (Gordon et al., 2004; Panko, 2004). Some answers to security challenges have been technological (Volonino and Robinson, 2004). Security oficers (i.e., most often, IS professionals) have adopted IT tools such as encryption, firewalls, access management, and backup systems in order to deal with these issues. Emerging thoughts, court rulings, laws, and standards (such as ISO 177799) have directed attention not only to technological but also to organizational dimensions of security threats (Calder and Watkins, 2003; Chan et al., 2005; Ma and Pearson, 2005; Mercuri, 2004b). In particular, in a recent survey, IS professionals considered that end-users’ ignorance was the most significant threat to IS security (Bennet, 2004). Security oficers have thus also increasingly acknowledged the importance of bringing awareness to security issues (Dhillon, 2001).

The purpose of awareness policies is to make end-users ‘‘aware’’ of IS security issues by informing them of potential breaches to security and of the risks associated with these breaches. These policies thus rely on the implicit assumption that if end-users were sufi ciently informed, they would develop the same awareness of security issues as, say, IS professionals.

This paper questions this implicit assumption. It introduces a social representations perspective to investigate how members of diferent occupational communities consider IS security. Social representations correspond to a stable and socially shared set of common knowledge and ideas that agents elaborate and communicate to make sense of and act in their environment (Jodelet, 1989; Moscovici, 1973, 1984; Vaast and Walsham, 2005). In a social representations perspective, members of various communities are not either ignorant or knowledgeable about IS security. Rather, members of these communities know diferent things about IS security. They are diferently aware of what IS security involves and of what may threaten it. In a social representations perspective, occupational communities are thus viewed as ‘‘communities of knowing’’ (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995) that have various yet related representations of IS security.

The paper aims at demonstrating the relevance of a social representations perspective to understand and manage IS security. It first presents the concept of social representations that benefited from more than 40 years of research in social psychology. It then explores the representations of IS security of members of seven occupational communities working in the same healthcare organization. The analysis of similarities and diferences in representations of IS security finally helps draw strategic implications for the theory and prac tice of IS security.

## 2. Social representations of IS security in healthcare

## 2.1. A social representations perspective <sup>1</sup>

A social representations perspective provides a theoretical lens that helps understand the knowledge of occupational communities from the point of view of members of these communities (Pawlowski et al., 2004; Vaast et al., 2006).

Serge Moscovici (1961) introduced and popularized the concept of social representations in the field of social psychology. Moscovici was interested in understanding how, in the French society of the early 1960s, a theory of the social sciences such as psychoanalysis was transformed into common, folk, knowledge. He thought of the process of difusion of scientific knowledge into common knowledge as transformation and enrichment, rather than as mere debilitation. He thus wanted to rehabilitate common thinking and knowledge and refused the traditional separation between expert (or scientific) knowledge and laymen (or common) knowledge (Moscovici and Markova, 1998).

Moscovici referred to social representations rather than to ‘‘collective’’ representations, the term used by Durkheim (1898), for he wanted to explore the diversity and plurality of representations in modern society (Doise, 1993; Moscovici, 1988). Investigating and contrasting the ways in which diferent communities represent the same object help to explore the specific representations of each community as well as the relationships among them (Doise, 1993; Emler and Ohana, 1993; Moscovici, 1961).

Social representations are socio-cognitive (Abric, 1996), which means that they are not merely imposed on the individual agent by the community, but rather, that they are generated by agents’ own reflexive process and experiences (Markova, 2000). Hence, social representations do not exclude individual variations of the way people represent their world (Billig, 1993). However, adopting a social representations perspective helps investigate how members of specific communities tend to share certain views and ways of interpreting the world that are diferent from other groups.

Another key characteristic of such a perspective is that social representations and practices are related to each other and mutually influence each other over time (Abric, 1994b; Vaast and Walsham, 2005). Social representations provide frames of interpretations that then influence actions and relationships within and among communities (Davidson, 2002; Orlikowski and Gash, 1994).

The third characteristic of such a perspective is that social representations act as sensitizing concepts (Liu, 2004). Social representations involve understanding ideas and concepts from the points of view of the persons involved, and not from an external or superior perspective (Pawlowski et al., 2004).

Fourth, social representations are ‘‘polyphasic’’: they are shared by members of a community, but not necessarily by members of other communities (Moscovici and Markova, 1998).

Finally, social representations are not static. They are dynamically generated and re-generated over time. Social psychologists have been interested in this process of representing (Flament, 1994). Representations are subject to change over time as members of diferent groups start representing an object in diferent ways according to experiences, interactions, and communication processes. More specifically, the social psychology literature discussed anchoring and objectification as the two main processes through which people represent their reality (Orfali, 2002). Anchoring makes the meaningless meaningful by anchoring it to something else. Through anchoring, people build representations of new objects that are built on their existing representations of familiar objects (Jodelet, 1991). For instance, representations of familiar illnesses help people understand the emergence of a new illness in terms of the more familiar ones. In the case of objectification, a new object is integrated into common sense reality. Objectification gives new meaning to objects. It builds an image of a new object and this image makes the new object part of the social reality of the community. Through objectification, a new illness (e.g., AIDS) will acquire a specific, concrete image, distinct from that of other illnesses. Anchoring and objectification are dialectically related and both contribute to the emergence, reproduction, and transformation of social representations over time (Orfali, 2002).

## 2.2. A social representations perspective of IS security in healthcare

Adopting a social representations perspective contributes to the understanding of IS security. In particular, such perspective can help advance from what Goodhue and Straub (1991) called individual’s ‘belief’ in the adequacy of security. In their seminal study, Goodhue and Straub (1991) considered that end-users’ belief in the adequacy of security was a function of the industry, of the company’s specific action, and of individual factors (e.g., computer literacy). A social representations perspective complements this approach by introducing an intermediate level, the level of the community. A social representations per spective deals with the relationship between people’s representations of IS security and their membership to specific communities, and in particular to occupational communities (e.g., managers, IS professionals, technicians). In other words, in a social representations perspective, the social representations of diferent occupational communities frame individual beliefs and interpretation of IS security. Such perspective is thus especially useful to advance toward a socio-organizational apprehension of IS security (Adams and Sasse, 1999; De Paula et al., 2005; Dhillon and Backhouse, 2001).

A social representations perspective can help investigate IS security from the perspec tive of the diferent communities involved. In a social representations perspective, instead of being ‘‘objective’’ issues that call for undisputable knowledge and action, security and privacy issues are viewed as constructed as agents represent them and, thus, as likely to change over time with social – and not just technological – changes (Katos and Adams, 2005; Palen and Dourish, 2003).

Moreover, when considering social representations, there is no one and unique defini tion of privacy and security within the organization. Members of diferent occupational communities, faced by diferent contexts, engaging in various activities, and interacting with various parties, represent IS security in diferent ways. Such polyphasic characteristic of social representations of IS security may impact communications and relations among communities.

Representations of IS security are also subject to change over time. These changes are partly related to ‘‘objective’’ changes in the environment (such as new law, technologies, and viruses). However, these objective changes are meaningless until people make sense of them and integrate them into their social reality (Berger and Luckmann, 1966). In particular, in a social representations perspective, social representations of IS security are likely to emerge, be reproduced, and change through the related processes of anchoring and objectification.

A social representations perspective seems especially relevant to investigate IS security in the healthcare industry. For one, in healthcare organizations, many diferent occupational communities (e.g., physicians, nurses, technicians) work and interact with one another. These communities difer in training, activities, and methods and, yet, they are all engaged in some way in patient care. The relationships among these communities are not tension-free (Adams and Blandford, 2005). In a social representations perspective, conflicts are related to diferences in perspectives and interpretations of diferent commu nities (Moscovici and Markova, 1998).

Moreover, healthcare professionals and organizations have become highly dependent on the use of sophisticated systems (see, for instance, the trend towards Electronic Medical Records, EMR) (Chiasson and Davidson, 2004). The adoption of new technologies in healthcare has deeply transformed medical practices as well as the relationships among members of healthcare practitioner communities (Barley, 1986; Schneider and Wagner, 1993).

The healthcare industry has also been facing pressing security challenges in the last few years. For one, EMR, among other systems, provide opportunities for care improvement as well as threats to the privacy of patients’ information (Huston, 2001). Moreover, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996 outlines medical security and privacy rules and procedures to be applied by all healthcare providers in order to simplify and protect the administration of healthcare billing (Baumer et al., 2000; Mercuri, 2004a). HIPAA aims at securing individuals’ ‘‘protected health information’’ (PHI). To become HIPAA compliant, healthcare providers have implemented policies that involve, in particular, computer security guidelines, patients’ consent forms, and training programs on HIPAA do’s and do nots (Fedorowicz and Ray, 2004; Mercuri, 2004a; Turner and Foong, 2003).

Such a context made healthcare a particularly appropriate industry to contrast diferent occupational communities’ representations of IS security.

## 3. Methods

## 3.1. General empirical approach

The main purpose of the empirical investigations was to illustrate the relevance of a social representations perspective by exploring how members of various occupational communities working in the same healthcare organization represented IS security. More specifically, investigations aimed at studying the similarities and diferences of representations of IS security by diferent occupational communities. They also attempted to make sense of such similarities and diferences with regard to the work, organizational context, and relationships among these communities. Finally, investigations aimed at understanding if, and how, the social representations of IS security of diferent occupational communities had changed over time.

Investigations that adopt a social representations perspective logically take on an interpretive epistemological stance (Berger and Luckmann, 1966; Klein and Myers, 1999; Walsham, 1995) but they may rely on a wide variety of approaches to collect and analyze data (see, in particular, Breakwell and Canter (1993) and Doise et al. (1993) for reviews and exam ples). To the author’s knowledge, no empirical investigations of the social representations of IS security in healthcare had previously been published. Because of its exploratory nature, this research followed a non-experimental design, with qualitative data collection and analysis. The data collection process was based on interviews with members of diferent occupa tional communities followed by a content analysis of interview transcripts.

## 3.2. Field setting

Eastern Hospital (a pseudonym) is located in a large Northeastern US city. Eastern Hospital is taking care of both inpatients and outpatients. Overall in 2005–2006, about 650 physicians, 800 registered nurses, 250 medical residents, 200 technicians, 80 clerks, and 80 administrative associates (including managers and IS professionals) work at East ern Hospital. Eastern Hospital does not benefit from massive funding and has not yet been able to invest heavily in the computerization of patients’ information. Eastern Hospital has thus not yet implemented EMR, but it has already adopted several systems that stored and retrieved patients’ information (e.g., lab results, medication orders). Occupational communities have diferentiated access to these systems, and access is regulated by personal login. Nurses and physicians access these systems through shared desktops on the diferent floors. Other occupational communities, such as clerks, managers, and IS profes sionals, work with their own computers (but still have to log in to access the systems). Finally, two other occupational communities (social workers and technicians) have a more limited access to and use of these systems to do their jobs. Table 1 describes the seven occupational communities, their jobs, interactions, and access to systems.

Investigations focused on the social representations of these seven occupational communities for three main reasons. First, and most importantly, members of each of these communities work together and/or are engaged in practices that are clearly diferentiated from the practices of the other communities. Hence, members of these various occupational communities are likely to have developed diferent representations of their work and environment. Second, members of these communities are involved diferently in the use or management of IS and are thus likely to represent IS security in diferent ways. Third, members of these communities interact with one another on a regular basis. Thus, one could expect that diferences in representations impact the relations and communications among their respective members.

Following this tradition, during interviews, I introduced the research by mentioning that it was about ‘‘the issues related to Security of Information Systems at Eastern Hospital’’. Such label was vague on purpose and no definition of ‘‘Security’’ or ‘‘Information Systems’’ was provided, in order to avoid biasing respondents’ subsequent discourse. Moreover, the interview guide was semi-structured so that interviews could flow according to respondents’ own concerns while simultaneously covering similar sets of topics. The interview guide focused on: (1) background information on respondent’s job and situation; (2) respondent’s use of IS; (3) definition of IS security (e.g., what does it mean for Eastern Hospital to have secure IS?); (4) threats to IS security (e.g., what triggers breaches to IS security?); (5) management of IS security (e.g., what can help manage IS security?); (6) peers and future perspectives on IS security (e.g., how are others reacting to IS security threats and how is IS security likely to evolve in the next few years?); (7) anecdote or critical incident related to IS security (e.g., what anecdote or incident that happened at Eastern Hospital comes to the respondent’s mind?). Interviews lasted on average 30 min (from 20 min to more than 1 h), were recorded and entirely transcribed.

I interviewed a total of 39 members from seven professional communities. Table 2 provides the respondents’ demographics.

Although my original objective was to interview more respondents, it proved impossible to do more than these 39 interviews. For one, people working in healthcare organizations are notoriously busy. What is more, my research dealt with the critical issue of security, and researchers already noted the dificulties of collecting information on IS security issues in today’s organizations (Kotulic and Clark, 2004).

Given the exploratory nature of investigations, a grounded qualitative data analysis strategy was adopted (Denzin and Lincoln, 2000; Miles and Huberman, 1984; Strauss and Corbin, 1998). The analysis of interview transcripts started with a coding process based on Strauss and Corbin’s (1998) guidelines for the development of grounded theoriz ing, which included open, axial, and selective coding. After reduction, overall, 27 topics emerged in the transcribed interviews.

A second, independent, rater coded interview transcripts from respondents of each community to test the reliability of the coding. Inter-coder agreement rate, operationalized as presence or absence of topics in interview transcripts, was 82.3%. Given the exploratory and interpretive nature of this research, this rate was deemed acceptable. Topics were then grouped into six larger categories: two categories encompassed how the respondents had defined IS security (security of patients’ information and security of systems), two categories concerned the perceived context of IS security in the hospital (tech-related and healthcare related), two categories dealt with expressed threats to IS security (external and internal ones). Fig. 1 summarizes the 27 topics grouped in six categories placed under an ‘‘umbrella’’ model of IS security.

Data analysis continued by comparing the mean frequency of occurrence of the categories and topics that appeared in the discourse of members of diferent communities. The mean frequency of occurrence of topics was used as a proxy for the ‘‘expressive value’’ of concepts that are part of social representations (as in Abric, 2001; Pawlowski et al., 2004; Zanchetta et al., 2005). The expressive value corresponds to the idea that topics that are important in social representations will tend to appear often in respondents’ discourse. By the same token, discrepancies in the frequency of occurrence of topics in the discourse of various communities may indicate diferences in the way members of these communities represent the same object (e.g., IS security). Tables 3, 5–7, and 8 show the mean frequency of occurrence of the diferent topics and categories<sup>2</sup> that emerged from the coding of the interviews. Table 4 presents the significance levels of the diferences in the frequency of occurrence of categories of topics among the seven occupational communities. Because of the small and unequal size of the samples, a non-parametric procedure was appropriate (Sprent and Smeeton, 2001). The statistics provided here do not aim at capturing once and for all the representations of these occupational communities. Rather, more modestly, these statistics are considered as indicators of contrasts in the way diferent communities represented IS security. They complement the qualitative analysis of respondents’ actual discourse.

## 4.1. Defining Information Systems security at Eastern Hospital

Table 3 summarizes the mean frequency of occurrence of the topics that members of diferent occupational communities associated with the definition of IS security. Table 4 shows the significance levels of diferences of occurrence of the diferent categories of top ics in the discourse of members of the seven occupational communities.

These tables reveal, on the one hand, that the diferent occupational communities shared a minimal understanding of IS security as related to the security of patients’ information (cf. Table 4, p = .217). On the other hand, they also suggest that IS professionals defined IS security in a way that contrasted with the definitions provided by members of the communities directly involved in patient care.

For IS professionals, IS security was mostly a technological issue: it was defined as the security of systems (hardware, software, network security, and data integrity). An IS professional declared:

In my view, you have IS security when you have your computer to work its best.

Members of the occupational communities directly involved in patients’ care tended to associate IS security with the security of patients’ information (including the respect of patients’ privacy, of professional access to patients’ information, and compliance with HIPAA regulations).

The various communities involved in patient care defined the security of patients information in slightly diferent ways. For instance, for nurses, the security of patients’ information involved mostly the privacy of patients’ information, whereas for physicians it especially meant making sure that the professionals involved in patient care would access the right information (and only the right information) at the right time. However, overall, there was a relative consistency in the frequency of occurrence of the topics related to IS security as the security of patients’ informa tion among members of communities involved in patient care. ## 4.2. Context of Information Systems security at Eastern Hospital

Table 5 presents the mean frequency of occurrence of topics related to the context of IS security at Eastern Hospital. Table 4 displays a similar pattern of levels of significance as for the definition of IS security.

In a similar vein as for the definition of IS security, IS professionals associated technology-related issues with the context of IS security at Eastern Hospital. Regarding the other internal threats, members of all occupational communities mentioned employees’ behavior as a major source of internal threats to IS security. However, more remarkable than the high frequency of occurrence of this idea in most respondents’ discourse was the variety of what members of diferent communities considered to constitute a significant breach of IS security. In particular, IS professionals often mentioned ‘‘employees’ ignorance’’ as causing breaches to IS security. An IS professional explained:

People are learning, but there is a lot of room for education. [. . .] I would say that you have to educate people about downloading music, visiting sites that bring a lot of negative things into the computers, weather bugs or other things, or free download.com.

IS professionals considered that such ignorance could be dealt with by ‘‘educating’’ end-users on the risks involved by a careless use of network resources.

In contrast, managers, nurses, social workers, and clerks mentioned much more frequently employees’ curiosity as a threat to IS security. They worried about the fact that employees who had access to Eastern Hospital’s medical systems could use it to look for information on patients they were not in charge of. Physicians, on the other hand, did not mention employees’ curiosity as a threat but, rather, their lack of time. They considered that, because most employees who worked on the wards were overwhelmed with work and had to deal with a tight and constantly moving schedule, they were likely to forget to close some programs or to leave some patients’ charts publicly available for short periods of time.

Somewhat related to the question of lack of time was the issue of password management. At Eastern Hospital, employees accessed systems through their personal user id and password and diferent communities had diferent access to the systems. In particular, clerks and nurses could order some lab tests and results online, whereas physicians did not have a direct access to the ordering system. Physicians thus usually had to give orders through the mediation of clerks and nurses. Clerks and nurses complained that, sometimes, physicians took their password, in order to access the ordering system directly. A nurse confided:

Physicians have an access to systems that is diferent from the one that nurses have. Depending on your title, it determines how much access you get. We know that physicians go get the charts they are not supposed to get and [they] try to change things because they forgot to do something. Sometimes, they steal someone else’s password.

For clerks and nurses, employees’ taking another employee’s password constituted a threat to IS security. Interestingly, physicians did not mention such a threat but rather, the reverse situation constituted by an employee giving her password to another one. A physician explained:

We [physicians] cannot access the systems in order to print all the forms we need to say what needs to be done [on a patient]. So we ask them [nurses] to do it, but sometimes nurses are so busy that it happens sometimes that nurses can give doctors their code. [. . .] I guess that would be a breach [of IS] security.

## 5. Discussion

## 5.1. IS security from the point of view of diferent communities

A social representations perspective helped understand IS security as embedded in the material and social context of work at Eastern Hospital and consider how this context was interpreted by members of diferent communities.

Investigations confirmed the need to take into account the context of work practices to understand IS security issues at Eastern Hospital. For instance, ignoring the chronic lack of time of healthcare professionals would lead to overlooking breaches of IS security tha emerge from the consequent non-application of security procedures (e.g., forgetting to close a program when moving away from a shared desktop).

Moreover, investigations also revealed that members of diferent communities represented their context and IS security in slightly diferent ways according to what they were already familiar with. Anchoring processes seemed especially prevalent in the formation of social representations of IS security. As noted above, some healthcare professionals (physicians and nurses, in particular) associated the topical issue of IS security with another issue that they were more familiar with: the difusion of patients’ informa tion from any medium, be it computer-based or not. These healthcare professionals anchored their representations of IS security to their traditional work practices in relation to patients. In contrast, IS professionals anchored IS security to the object of their own professional practice, that is, to the implementation and management of systems and computer networks.

## 5.2. Polyphasic representations of IS security at Eastern Hospital

What seemed striking from the analysis of interviews was the clear disconnect between how IS professionals represented IS security, on the one hand, and, on the other hand, how the other occupational communities represented it. In a nutshell, for IS professionals, IS security was first and foremost a technology issue, with threats such as hackers and viruses that directly afected systems. Most of the other communities represented IS security as the security of patients’ information, related to the mission of the healthcare organization in which they were working, and afected mostly by behaviora threats.

Such contrast was even more striking because IS professionals and the other occupational communities expressed consideration for each other’s practice. In the words of an IS professional:

I find that most people here are cooperative. They want to protect their data, and they are looking for our help.

Also, a clerk declared:

In IT, they know what they are doing. They do a good job with our computers and systems.

Despite this mutual respect, it was dificult for IS professionals to convince end-users to stop downloading free software from the web: end-users and IS professionals did not have the same representations of what was risk-inducing behavior with regard to systems use.

Diferences in representations between IS professionals and the other communities made cross-community communications dificult as members of diferent communities did not have the same understanding of what was important with regard to IS security. Obstacles to communications were made even stronger since they remained mostly invisible, as social representations are usually taken for granted and remain untold in the course of routine activities (Moscovici and Markova, 1998).

In addition to the obvious contrast between the representations of IS professionals and of the other occupational communities, the investigations also revealed more subtle divergences in representations. In particular, as suggested above, physicians, on the one hand, and nurses and clerks, on the other, had developed slightly diferent representations of what created a threat to IS security. Divergences in the interpretation of the same event (e.g., employee A asking employee B’s password and employee B giving away her password to employee A) reflected tensions between communities of physicians, nurses, and clerks. Nurses and clerks considered that some physicians abused their position in order to get patient-related information, disrespecting Eastern Hospital’s policies and pressuring the clerks and nurses that worked with them.

## 5.3. HIPAA laws and IS security: an objectification process

Objectification is the process by which a concept (such as IS security) acquires a specific and concrete signification that makes sense to people in the course of their regular activities (Orfali, 2002). At Eastern Hospital, there was evidence that members of the various occupational communities had objectified the concept of IS security by making it equivalent to being HIPAA compliant. Eastern Hospital had become HIPAA compliant a few years before and its members were familiar with the policies and procedures involved in HIPAA compliance (i.e., not talking about patients’ cases in public areas, avoiding the public display of patients’ charts, etc.). Such objectification of IS security as being HIPAA compliant made most communities aware of some threats to IS security, but it also bounded their awareness to the breaches mentioned in the privacy rule. Moreover, many respondents considered that the consent forms signed by both Eastern Hospital’s employees and patients strictly defined what they could be held responsible for. This emergent equivalence between guaranteeing IS security and being HIPAA compliant thus also bounded Eastern Hospital’s employees’ sense of responsibility and accountability.

## 6. Conclusion and strategic implications

This research introduced a social representations perspective to examine IS security. It contrasted the ways in which members of diferent occupational communities working in the same healthcare organization represent IS security. The analysis of empirical investigations led to understand better the diferent perceptions of threats to IS security as well as the ways diferent communities react to these threats. The analysis also indicated that diferences in representations are related to communication processes among community members.

This research is not without limitations. In particular, the research design had led to identify instances of anchoring and objectification, i.e., two key dynamics through which people build their representations. However, due to the exploratory nature of this research, collected data were cross-sectional rather than longitudinal, which did not allow to retrace the actual processes of emergence of representations. Future research could explore the processes through which new representations unfold. Moreover, the analysis of empirical data relied on the use of the mean frequency of occurrence of topics in discourses as a proxy for presence of these topics in the representations of diferent communities. As noted by Nicolini (1999), however, such an approach to elicit social representations depends on respondents’ overt statements, that is, on what respondents are willing and able to say about a subject. Also, a social representations perspective insists on the relational dimension of social representations: representations emerge according to relations within and among communities. The empirical investigations of this research however relied on interviews with individual members of various communities. Hence, the collected data could only account for these relations in a limited way. Further research could investigate these relations more deeply, possibly by means of sustained participant observation.

Despite these limitations, this research responds to current calls for a better understand ing of security in the everyday practice of users (De Paula et al., 2005) and holds strategic implications for research and practice of IS security. Its main original strategic findings are as follow.

First, this research posits security issues as deeply embedded in the overall social and physical context of work. A good understanding of this context is important for security oficers to be able design and implement efective security systems and policies. Such systems and policies should be well-suited to the specific environment of the organization, to the particular situation of the diverse communities involved in the implementation, management, and use of IS, and to the way members of these communities represent their work and environment.

Second, this research suggests that IS security holds diferent meanings for diferent occupational communities. It is paramount that security oficers get a minimal grasp of these meanings in order to be able to design and implement efective security policies. This research thus documents the idea that IS professionals have to get an understanding of what users think and know about IT (Schwartz and Hirschheim, 2003). It also advanced this idea by diferentiating communities of users that may hold diferent representations of security and by suggesting the need to take into account the potential impact of diferences of representations on behaviors and inter-group relations.

Third, this research adds to the literature that examines breaches of security originating from end-users. The literature has already shown that such breaches may come from conscious malpractice (Dhillon, 2001) or from end-users being unaware of the consequences of their actions (Adams and Sasse, 1999; Karat et al., 2005). This research suggests that some breaches of IS security come from diferences in the representations of various communities and from the subsequent misunderstandings and communication issues that arise among their respective members. Further research could take in consideration the representations of people who belong simultaneously to various communities. It could examine how being a member of various communities involved in IS security may afect the repre sentations of IS security of members of one or more communities.

Finally, this research also holds significant implications for security training and education (Hsu and Backhouse, 2002). It urges security oficers to communicate broadly (i.e., to all involved communities) about security issues. Such communication is necessary to facilitate the emergence of a minimal shared understanding of IS security issues and of consis tent practices across communities. This research however warns against standardized communication policies or awareness programs. To get their message across, security oficers should customize it to their potential audiences by building explicit bridges between their concerns and those of their target communities.
