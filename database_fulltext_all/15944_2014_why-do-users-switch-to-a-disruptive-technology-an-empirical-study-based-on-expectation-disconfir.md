# Why do users switch to a disruptive technology? An empirical study based on expectation-disconfirmation theory

## A B S T R A C T

A general technology switching model (TSM) is proposed to explain why IT users switch from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one. Seven research hypotheses derived from this model are validated using a field survey of feature phone users in Korea. The results indicate that users’ switching intention is determined by their expectations regarding the disruptive technology and dissatisfaction with the incumbent technology. User dissatisfaction is influenced by disconfirmation of previously held beliefs regarding ones incumbent technology and ones expectations for the disruptive technology. Switching cost is revealed not significant in the decision to acquire a disruptive technology.

## 1. Introduction

Today technology innovation is viewed an increasingly incremental element in driving productivity and competitiveness for business units. In the face of technology innovation, every producer tries to come out with differentiating products, attempts to enter the initial market, and intends to seize market share by the most advanced innovation of whatever product he offers [39,53]. A technology and resulting business innovation comes along to radically change the business landscape and environment. These innovations are loosely called ‘‘disruptive’’ [19]. A disruptive innovation is a new product that introduces a different set of performance attributes relative to what already exists, and this set of attributes is initially attractive to an emerging customer segment [1,18,26]. From a previous summary on disruptive innovativeness [18,16,15,49,22,54], Danneels [22] defined disruptive technology as a technology that changes the bases of competition by changing the performance metrics along which firms compete. It seems that disruptive technologies tend be associated with the replacement of incumbents by entrants. Although disruptive technologies initially underperform established ones in serving the mainstream market, they eventually displace the established technologies. Thus, users’ switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one will eventually bring the entrant firms that supported the disruptive technology to displace incumbent firms that supported the prior technology. This is why it is important to study users’ switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one.

Disruptive technology is regarded a specific type of technological change, which operates through a specific mechanism, and has specific consequences. However, it has been pointed out that the specific measures to define a disruptive technology need to be further refined. Even so, the term of ‘technology innovation’ is estimated to be a major issue for telecommunication industries, where companies regularly come up with new products and technologies. A smartphone is selected as a disruptive technology in this market comparing to a feature phone. A smartphone is a converged device for mobile computing and communication, which allows users to install and use the applications on their own, based on their needs and interests [50]. IPhone, Blackberry, Samsung Galaxy, etc. are most famous smartphones on market these days. In Korea, following the introduction of the iPhone to the Korean market in late 2009, different cell-phone brands and telecommunication companies promoted various smartphones. By July, 2011, smartphone users in Korea increased to 15,000,000, which is 30 percent of the total population. In such a shifting point of paradigm, where users of the current technology is considering whether to continuously be a loyal customer or to be an adopter of a disruptive technology, it is necessary to find out their choice making determinants during the period of paradigm shifting.

Technology switching is not entirely an alien concept in IS research. It has been examined variously as a post-adoption behavior, where users consider changing to another service provider or product after they evaluate the experience of using the service or product. The post-adoption behavior in IS research examines IS continuance usage by employing the expectationdisconfirmation theory (EDT). Developed from EDT, the post adoption model of IS posits that IS users’ continuance decisions are made following an initial adoption phase, influenced by the initial use, and can potentially lead to an ex post reversal of the initial decision, that is, to the discontinuance of the IS [9]. However, changing from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one is not simply a post-adoption behavior in literature, who explains continuance behavior by including unique post-adoption variables considering an IS. The switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one aims to employ both pre-acceptance and post-adoption variables considering different technologies.

Consequently, this paper aims to explain the switching phenomenon in the context of a disruptive technology field. Specifically, this paper intends to (1) generate a model to explain why users switch to a disruptive technology; (2) explore the factors influencing users’ intention to switch to a disruptive technology; and (3) explain the role of switching cost in the context of IT switching. The proposed model is based on EDT, which is further refined using auxiliary theories and empirical findings from prior IS use research [9]. In order to do so, this paper studies users’ switching behavior from a feature phone to a smartphone in the Korean telecommunication market. Structural equation modeling (SEM) is used to confirm the validity and analyze the causal relationship of the suggested model.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Customer switching behavior

While customer retention has been a focus of research, recently, the loss of customers has been recognized as a primary concern for at least three reasons [40]. First, for market share and revenues, both the number of new adoptions and number of discontinuers at any period are regarded significant [43]. Second, evidence shows that negative interpersonal influence by service discontinuers is generally more persuasive than is positive interpersonal influence from continuing customers. In addition to triggering further discontinuance by other subscribers, potential new adopters might be influenced against subscribing (e.g., [4,36,35,38]), despite positive influence from other sources [42]. Third, it is more expensive to acquire new customers than to retain existing customers because of the search costs associated with identifying new customers, start-up costs involved in setting up new accounts, and time spent by customer service and technical support personnel in initiating new customers to the service.

Regarding the importance of discontinuance behavior, research has started to focus on customer switching behavior. Users’ behavior of IT switching in this paper is defined as the behavior of replacing an incumbent IT with a disruptive one. In contrast to a large body of research on IT usage, few studies have focused on IT switching [12]. Literature in MIS refines itself by targeting at technology acceptance and continuous usage. Technology acceptance model (TAM) [23] and Post adoption model of IS usage (PAM) [9] are most widely adopted theories that support for large volumes of research in related fields. TAM reveals a user’s decision when facing a new technology on whether to accept it or not. It focuses on initial acceptance of a technology, although later been applied in both acceptance and continuance contexts. TAM only adopts pre-acceptance variables such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and intention to use. One of the biggest legacies of TAM is its inability to provide a reasonable explanation of acceptance–discontinuance anomaly based on a common set of pre-acceptance variables. Based on TAM, PAM is suggested to explain users’ IS continuance usage. It includes unique postacceptance variables, such as satisfaction, and confirmation. By employing such post-acceptance variables, PAM is able to explain users’ post-adoption behavior, such as continuous or discontinuous usage of an IT. However, both models suggest the adoption or continuance usage of a defined technology. The real situation is that users will consider changing to a new technology after the performance evaluation of the current one he is using. That is why the switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one should be taken into consideration, because it suggests evaluating the post-acceptance variable of confirmation on an incumbent IT and pre-acceptance variable of expectation on a disruptive technology at the same time.

Customers’ switching behavior has been studied in marketing literature which analyzes brand or service switching [3,33,7,34,29]. Rather than developing a general model of switching behavior, antecedents of switching behavior in the context of service switching were identified. Table 1 lists relative research.

Summarizing the previous literature, (1) switching behavior is observed in service industries, with most service providers offering the same or similar services. (2) While many of the studies explore the antecedents of switching intention, a large portion of them emphasize the significant role of switching cost in forming or constraining customers switching intention. (3) Switching intention seems to explore a new field of customer relationship, claiming that satisfaction–loyalty relationship may be broken down with the mediating effect of other variables. Though several studies have been conducted on switching behavior in marketing, few have addressed users’ switching behavior in ITs. Adapted from switching literature in marketing, this paper aims to develop a general model for explaining users’ switching intention to a disruptive IT.

## 2.2. Expectation-(dis)confirmation theory (EDT)

Customers’ IT switching behavior is viewed as a rational postadoption behavior following customers’ experience with IT usage. Post-adoption behavior is an important area of IS research because it is considered a significant indicator for IS success and use. Studies of post-adoption behavior mainly fall into two streams, either on the IS continued usage or its discontinuance. Theoretically, both streams are based on EDT, a theory widely used in the consumer behavior literature to study consumer satisfaction, post-purchase behavior, and related factors.

EDT posits that expectations, coupled with perceived performance, lead to post-purchase satisfaction. This effect is mediated through positive or negative disconfirmation between expectations and performance. The four main constructs in the model are: expectations, performance, disconfirmation, and satisfaction. If a product outperforms expectations (positive disconfirmation), post-purchase satisfaction will result. If a product falls short of expectations (negative disconfirmation), the consumer is likely to be dissatisfied [37]. Though EDT has been widely used to study consumer satisfaction, post-purchase behavior, and service marketing in general [37,2,21,41,48,55], it has been pointed out that EDT has several limitations. For one thing, conceptualization of expectation differs across EDT studies. Some studies define expectation in terms of pre-consumption beliefs while others define it as beliefs weighed with an evaluation of outcomes in an expectancy theoretic sense. For another, EDT ignores potential changes in consumers’ expectation following their consumption experience and the impact of these changes on subsequent cognitive processes [10]. Therefore, when adapting EDT to explain users’ switching behavior, we propose that expectation as a preconsumption belief which forms before a consumer buy the disruptive technology. In addition, we suggest that the constructs in original EDT should be extended to view at different phases of IT usage.

## 3. Technology switching model

While consumer research found that EDT plays a significant role in explaining customers’ post-adoption behavior, we believe that it is difficult to apply EDT directly to the switching behavior to a disruptive IT. The constructs of expectation and perceived performance from EDT are based on the perception of the same product. Confirmation is the perception resulting from the evaluation of the experience of using that product. The switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one considers the time period when users weigh the benefits and losses among different technologies and make a choice decision. Therefore, it is more likely that the expectations and performance arise from perceptions of different ITs in the context of technology switching model (TSM). Furthermore, evaluation of the disruptive technology results from a pre-consumption expectation before usage, which leads to a final comparison to performance evaluation of the incumbent IT. Fig. 1 explains the phenomena in detail. The original construct of expectation and perceived performance in EDT, although evaluated from different time series, is tested on one specified IT. The IT switching model we are suggesting aims to focus the performance on the incumbent IT, thus forming a disconfirmation of the incumbent IT through the gap between its expectation and performance evaluation. The IT switching intention would then be expected to stem from the expectation regarding the disruptive IT, because of rapid innovation, customers have higher expectations regarding new technologies.

Fig. 2 depicts a Technology switching model (TSM) that this study proposes for examination of users’ switching behavior to a disruptive IT. As shown in Fig. 2, different from the original EDT model, the major theme of this model aims to explain users’ IT switching behavior by testing their expectation and performance evaluation between different ITs.

The hypothesis discussed below is logically adapted from EDT. The original disconfirmation–satisfaction relationship is retained only by viewing satisfaction as the reverse side of dissatisfaction, which would cause the direct influence on the behavioral intention to switch. Dissatisfaction is regarded as an affect, which has been theorized and validated in TAM-based research as a significant antecedent of intention concerning IS usage (e.g., [24,32,47]). These studies indirectly support the dissatisfaction–discontinuance relationship. This leads to our first hypothesis:

H1. Dissatisfaction on the incumbent IT will have a positive effect on the intention to switch to a disruptive IT.

EDT posits that customer satisfaction is jointly determined by expectation of the IS and the confirmation of the evaluation of the performance after actual usage. Disconfirmation refers to the extent to which users’ pre-usage expectation is contravened during actual usage experience [11]. Disconfirmation can be divided into two categories. One is the case where performance exceeds expectation. In this case, customers feel satisfied because they feel the products successfully meet their needs and achieve their expectation. On the other hand, performance may be perceived lower than expectation, which in turn brings on dissatisfaction. Customers perceive that the product fails to achieve the expected benefits of the IS usage. Disconfirmation discussed in this paper refers to the case where perceived performance lags expectation. Hence:

H2. Disconfirmation of the incumbent IT will have a positive effect on dissatisfaction on the incumbent IT.

Expectation is pointed out as an additional determinant of satisfaction in EDT, because expectation provides the baseline or reference level for customers to form evaluative judgments about the focal product or services [10]. The construct of expectation in original EDT considers customers’ expectation of a product or service before its usage, and compares it with the post-usage performance to form a confirmation. However, expectation in this research is defined as the expectation of a disruptive IT. Previous EDT theorizes that a lower baseline level of expectation tends to enhance one’s satisfaction, while high expectation easily leads to dissatisfaction. Considering expectation for the case of a disruptive IT, we propose that a high baseline level of expectation of the disruptive IT will enhance one’s dissatisfaction with the incumbent IT. This is because users will anticipate a higher benefit from the disruptive IT, and feel the incumbent IT failed to meet their needs. Meanwhile, once a customer forms a high expectation of a disruptive IT, he/she will be willing to try using it. Thus, this influences them to switch from the incumbent IT to a disruptive one. Hence:

H3. Expectation on a disruptive IT will have a positive effect on dissatisfaction of the incumbent IT.

H4. Expectation on a disruptive IT will have a positive effect on intention to switch to a disruptive IT.

Switching cost, which is highly subjective and even emotional and difficult to assess [51], has been abstractly viewed in the literature as the ‘‘difficulty’’ or ‘‘disutility’’ involved in changing over or switching (to a new product/service/system) [25]. Burnham et al. [14] developed a switching cost typology that identifies three types of switching costs, and examined the antecedents and consequences of switching costs. The three types of switching cost are suggested as (1) procedural switching cost, primarily involving the loss of time and effort; (2) financial switching costs, involving the loss of financially quantifiable resources; and (3) relational switching costs, involving psychological or emotional discomfort due to the loss of identity and the breaking of bonds. Adapted from the research in Burnham et al. [14], this paper separates switching cost into two types, procedural switching cost and financial switching cost. Relational switching cost, because it usually arises when users switch to different service providers, is excluded from our research. In the case of mobile phone market in Korea, when users switch to a disruptive IT, they may maintain their relationship with their current service provider, thus, avoiding the relational switching cost.

A number of marketing studies have explored the notion of switching costs in consumer decision making and most of them examined its role in affecting customers’ switching intention [51,28,30]. Shapiro and Varian [44] pointed out that consumers will evaluate the benefit gained after the switching behavior to see whether the switching cost is worthwhile or not. It is believed that consumers will not switch if they perceive that the cost they incur is greater than the benefit that they will gain [31]. Bansal and

Taylor [7] regarded switching cost as an aspect influencing consumers’ attitudes toward switching behavior. Since switching cost will be perceived as bringing difficulties to the process of switching, consumers will have a weak switching intention. Hu and Hwang [29] reviewed the typology of switching cost in literature, and summarized the type of switching cost in Taiwanese mobile phone industry as procedural switching cost, financial switching cost and relational switching cost. They also test the relationship between these three types of switching cost and switching intention.

Based on the causal relationship between switching cost and switching intention, we propose that:

H5a. Financial switching cost will have a negative effect on intention to switch to a disruptive IT.

H5b. Procedural switching cost will have a negative effect on intention to switch to a disruptive IT.

Since the direct effect of switching cost on switching intention has been studied extensively, this research investigates the interaction effect of switching cost on the dissatisfaction-switch ing intention and expectation–switching intention relationship.

As previously stated, consumers dissatisfied with the incumbent IT will be likely to switch to a disruptive IT. However, considering the switching cost, consumers will weigh the benefit of switching, and sometimes give up the switching intention. This is because cost spent in switching may outweigh the benefit customers expect from the new IT.

IT switching is regarded as an inherently risky behavior, whose outcomes are often unknown. Thus, consumers who are cost sensitive and are afraid of taking risks may not be open to switching to a disruptive IT even if they are slightly dissatisfied with the incumbent technology. Hence, switching cost can be expected to weaken the positive effect of Dissatisfaction on the incumbent IT on IT switching intention. This lead to the following hypotheses:

H6a. Financial switching cost will have a negative moderating effect on dissatisfaction on the incumbent IT—intention to switch to a disruptive IT relationship.

H6b. Procedural switching cost will have a negative moderating effect on dissatisfaction on the incumbent IT—intention to switch to a disruptive IT relationship.

Between the expectation–intention relationship, switching cost is also expected to have a moderating effect. Consumers with high expectations toward a disruptive IT will be more willing to switch from the incumbent IT. However, considering the actual cost of the disruptive IT, one may choose to stay with the current IT. Hence, switching cost may likely weaken the positive effect of expectation on the disruptive IT on IT switching intention.

H7a. Financial switching cost will have a negative moderating effect on expectation on a disruptive IT—intention to switch to a disruptive IT relationship.

H7b. Procedural switching cost will have a negative moderating effect on expectation on a disruptive IT—intention to switch to a disruptive IT relationship.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Measurements

Multi-item measures for each construct were created through the following process. First, a draft of the questionnaire was prepared by reviewing the literature [34,29,10,11]. All of the items were translated into Korean and slightly modified to suit the context of mobile phones. The scale of items was measured on a seven point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) through neutral (4) to strongly agree (7). Second, we conducted field interviews with academicians and graduate students on the syntax and structure of the questionnaire and made modifications accordingly. Respondents were asked to assess the terminology, clarity of instructions and response format. Improved by literature review and field interviews, 22 items for 6 variables were finally selected. Disconfirmation of incumbent IT is measured by three items (DCF1-3), which examine users’ perception of the congruence between expectation on a feature phone and its actual performance. Expectation of disruptive IT by three items (EXP 1–3). These items identify users’ anticipation of a smartphone. Dissatisfaction of incumbent IT by four items (DIS 1–4), which capture users’ affect with (feelings about) a feature phone. Intention to switch to disruptive IT by three items (INT 1–3), identifying users’ intention to switch to a smartphone. Financial switching cost by five items (FCST 1–5). These items examine the cost involving the loss of financially quantifiable resources, and Procedural switching cost by four items (PCST 1–4), examining the cost involving the loss of time and effort. All items used are reflective.

## 4.2. Data collection

To test the model, a questionnaire survey was employed. The survey instrument was sent to undergraduate students, graduate students, and MBA students in two universities in Seoul, Korea. Data were gathered from a quota sample of 266 respondents of feature phone users. They were first asked whether they are using a feature phone or not; if they replied in the affirmative, they were asked to participate in the survey. The questionnaire requested the respondents to think back to the past experience of their feature phone usage and answer the questions accordingly. For each question, respondents were asked to circle the response which best described their degree of agreement. After eliminating 19 useless data, 247 questionnaires were analyzed. Respondents’ ages ranged from 20s to 50s, and the majority of respondents had undergraduate degree. Students were 39.3 percent of the total respondents, while 48.1 percent were employees. Most of the respondents earned less than 2,000,000 KRW (1800 USD) per month and 31.6 percent of the respondents spent between 60,000 KRW (55 USD) to 80,000 KRW (75 USD) on telecommunication fees.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Measurement Model

We conducted unconstrained confirmatory factor analysis by using AMOS 7.0 to evaluate convergent validity for six constructs. The purpose of convergent validity is to ensure unidimensionality of the multiple-item constructs and to eliminate unreliable items [13]. Items should load at least 0.70 on their respective hypothesized component and all loadings need to be significant (p < 0.05) [6,46]. On the basis of the above criteria, FCST3 and FCST5 were eliminated. For each item that examined, the value of standardized factor loading of the item to its respective construct was significant (p < 0.05), and all loadings ranged from 0.733 to 0.977, as shown in Appendix A.

Reliability for all items of a construct should be evaluated jointly by investigating composite reliability (CR) and the average variance extracted (AVE). For a construct to possess good reliability, CR should be at least 0.70 and the AVE should be at least 0.5 [5,8,27,45]. As shown in Appendix A, CR and AVE of all constructs in the final model were acceptable. The fit statistics for the model were good. The chi-square of the model was 259.085 with d.f. of 155, the ratio of chi-square to d.f. was 1.672, GFI, 0.908, AGFI, 0.875, NFI, 0.936, CFI, 0.973, and RMSEA, 0.052 were acceptable (Appendix A).

## 5.2. Structural model

After the measurement model was found to be satisfactory, the structural model was evaluated. It was well converged. The results indicated that the chi-square of the structural model at 265.451 with d.f. of 158, the ratio of chi-square to d.f. at 1.680, GFI at 0.906, AGFI at 0.875, NFI at 0.934, CFI at 0.972, RMR at 0.074 and RMSEA at 0.053 were acceptable.

For the moderating effects of financial switching cost and procedure switching cost, this research follows a hierarchical process in which the main effects model and the interaction model with the interaction construct are compared [17]. For estimating the moderator effects size, this research compare the squared multiple correlation (R-squared) for the interaction model with for the main effects model [20]. The effect size is estimated by computing Cohen’s $f ^ { 2 }$ following the guidelines of Chin et al. [17]. The difference between the squared multiple correlations is used to assess the overall effect size $f ^ { 2 }$ where 0.02, 0.15 and 0.35 have been suggested as small, moderate, and large effects respectively [17,20]. Medium and high effects indicate that the resulting path coefficients are meaningful and important.

$$
f ^ {2} = \frac {\left[ R ^ {2} (\text { extended   model }) - R ^ {2} (\text { initiative   model }) \right]}{\left[ 1 - R ^ {2} (\text { initiative   model }) \right]}
$$

The results of structural model analysis are reported in Table 3 and Fig. 3. The results of the main effects model showed that disconfirmation of incumbent IT and expectation of disruptive IT were significant in determining dissatisfaction on incumbent IT, and dissatisfaction on incumbent IT and expectation of disruptive IT were significant in determining intention to switch to disruptive IT, i.e., H1–H4 were supported. However, financial switching cost and procedure switching cost were not significant in determining intention to switch to disruptive IT; H5a and H5b were not supported. In summary, 4 out of 6 hypotheses proposed in the main model were supported.

## 6. Discussion

Based on EDT, this paper aims to investigate why users switch to a disruptive IT. The results generally support previous EDT related research [7,34,10,11]. The model expresses a theoretical rationale that the basic intention to switch to a disruptive IT is jointly influenced by users’ dissatisfaction of the incumbent IT and the high expectation of the disruptive IT. Inconsistent with previous research which found switching costs being the primary antecedents of switching intention [34,29,14], this paper finds switching cost neither as a direct antecedent nor a moderator in the decision to acquire a disruptive technology.

The empirical findings of the main effects model indicate that the key determinants of dissatisfaction on incumbent IT are disconfirmation of incumbent IT $( \beta = 0 . 5 0 8 )$ and expectation of disruptive IT $( \beta = 0 . 1 2 2 )$ . Disconfirmation of incumbent IT has strong positive effects on dissatisfaction on incumbent IT, while expectation of disruptive IT has less positive effects. The empirical findings confirmed the hypotheses concerning the basic relationship adapted from EDT, indicating that users’ disconfirmation regarding the incumbent IT will strongly influence his affect toward the IT [10]. At the same time, once the user looks forward to a disruptive IT or gains a higher expectation of the disruptive IT, he will become dissatisfied with the current IT. This suggests the disconfirmation of incumbent IT is more influential on the users’ dissatisfaction than the expectation of disruptive IT. This indicates that the users’ satisfaction is determined by their actual experience with incumbent IT. Once an individual started using an IT, he would measure the tradeoff between its advantages and disadvantages, and conclude with a comprehensive evaluation of his satisfaction.

The key determinants of intention to switch to disruptive IT are expectation of disruptive IT $( \beta = 0 . 5 8 3 )$ and dissatisfaction with incumbent IT $( \beta = 0 . 1 6 9 )$ . This implies that for respondents, the higher expectation of the disruptive IT is more influential than their dissatisfaction with their current IT in their decision to switch. This provides an important implication for IT practice. Ignoring users’ dissatisfaction of the incumbent IT may not cause users to switch to a disruptive IT. Focusing on arousing users’ expectations for the disruptive IT may be the most effective way to induce switching. In the promotion phase, companies should increase consumer expectations for the disruptive IT. Even when customers are satisfied with the incumbent IT, once their expectations of the disruptive IT are stimulated, they may be willing to switch to it.

The results show that the main effects of financial switching cost and procedural switching cost were not directly significant. This result is inconsistent with most switching cost related research [7,29] where switching cost was revealed a salient antecedent explaining users’ switching intention. This implies that the switching cost is not a significant barrier for consideration in the context of disruptive technology. This is because most of the companies and marketers usually prepare for large promotion campaigns when a disruptive technology comes to the market, particularly in the launching phase. Especially in the case of Korea telecommunication market, competitors are providing diverse programs, such as payment by installments, where a customer can divide the payment for 24 months. In this way, customers may not severely perceive the large effect brought by the switching cost.

The empirical findings of the interaction model show that both financial switching cost and procedural switching cost were not significant moderators in the dissatisfaction–switching intention and expectation–switching intention relationship, indicating a general inability of switching cost to weaken the association between expectation and switching intention, as well as dissatisfaction and switching intention. This is consistent with previous literature, where switching cost reveals no significant moderating effect [34].

## 7. Conclusion

The goal of this study was to investigate why customers switch from the incumbent IT to a disruptive one. To do so, we identified salient determinants of customers’ intention to switch to a disruptive IT, and analyzed the role of switching cost in moderating the causal relations between constructs. Toward that goal, EDT was adapted and modified to investigate user behavior during the period when customers were choosing between their incumbent IT and a disruptive one. Data collected from a field survey of feature phone users provided empirical support for the proposed model. The results indicate that while dissatisfaction continues to influence customers’ switching intention, customer expectation of the disruptive IT has a relatively stronger effect on the dependent variable. Customers’ dissatisfaction, in turn, is determined primarily by users’ disconfirmation of the current IT, and secondarily by expectation of the new IT. Further, switching cost has no significant direct or indirect influence on users’ switching intention.

Noteworthy contributions of the study include drawing attention to the substantive differences between switching behavior to a disruptive IT and switching service providers, distinguishing constructs in original EDT considering different phases of usage, and theorizing and validating a general model of IT switching behavior. As is stated in Bhattacherjee et al. [12], switching between similar products is viewed as an instance of adopting an incremental innovation, while it is in contrast to radical innovation adoption of a brand new IT. Thus, the understanding and exploration of IT switching can provide insight into the dynamics of innovation adoption. Practically, technology switching model proposed in this study provides guidelines for firms in the face of technology innovation. As this study aims to explain users’ switching behavior from an incumbent technology to a disruptive one, such users’ switching behavior will eventually change the business landscape between business competitors in the long run. Thus, the model proposed offers suggestions to firms in different positions in dealing with technology innovation.

This study also suffers from several limitations. First, as there is no clear metrics for defining a disruptive technology, it is necessary clarify its measurement in the future. Systematic review of literature on disruptive innovativeness is needed and a measurement of disruptive technology may be expected. Second, since the target technology selected in this research is smartphone; it may be difficult for us to generalize the model across a wide set of technologies. We believe, however, that the switching behavior from a general hand phone to a smartphone is not exceptional in that it embodies a trend where users shift toward more convergent and disruptive technologies. Third, except for the affect belief adapted from EDT, the only external variable examined in this research was switching cost. Switching cost is chosen because it is widely considered to be the most salient belief related to customer switching behavior. Considering the distinctive features of our technology in the development of social network services,

future work might investigate social and psychological factors (e.g., perceived personal innovativeness, etc.) to explain users behavior in greater detail. Lastly, the cross-sectional research here allows us to explain users’ switching behavior from a current IT to a disruptive one. However, a longitudinal research on whether the smartphone adopters are satisfied after their switching behavior and continuously use the technology is an open question for future work.
