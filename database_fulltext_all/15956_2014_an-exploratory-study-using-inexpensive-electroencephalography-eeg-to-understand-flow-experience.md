# AN EXPLORATORY STUDY USING INEXPENSIVE ELECTROENCEPHALOGRAPHY (EEG) TO UNDERSTAND FLOW EXPERIENCE IN COMPUTER-BASED INSTRUCTION

## Abstract

Flow is an optimal experience that results in intense engagement in an activity. In computer-based instructional environment, flow can be used to examine learning performance. We used questionnaire survey and electroencephalography (EEG) analysis to examine the influence of challenge-skill balance on the flow experience and influence of flow experience on learning performance in a computer-based instructional environment. The results showed that the flow experience of learners depends on challenge-skill balance of learning materials. The research explored the possibility of using an inexpensive non-medical EEG device to research the association between flow experience and challenge-skill balance in educational information systems.

Keywords Flow, Computer-based Instruction, E-Learning, Electroencephalography (EEG), Learning Performance

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Information technology is widely used in education to improve learning performance. E-learning, which is an efficient approach for acquiring knowledge, has flourished since the early 1990s with the progress of information systems and network technology. Although e-learning has prevailed in recent years, specifically because it offers considerable benefits, a number of challenges and limitations must be addressed for e-learning implementation [1]. Generally, in the context of traditional classroom, instructors can observe the conditions of learners directly; however, e-learning is not a face-to-face approach.

To explore the learning conditions of students in an e-learning environment, academics consider the concept of flow. Csikszentmihalyi proposed the concept of flow as an optimal experience resulting in intense engagement, distorted sense of time, and heightened motivation [2]. Novak, Hoffman and Yung [3] indicated that people who are in a state of flow are completely immersed in an activity. Flow experience elicits the feeling of pleasure and enthusiasm. Motivation to be involved in activities increases in the state of flow. Previous studies have applied flow theory to various fields, such as sport, gaming, shopping, website surfing, and e-learning. This study focused on computer-based instruction.

Previous studies usually measured flow using self-reporting questionnaire surveys. Participants were asked to recall their flow experience when engaging in activities. However, flow is a changeable state rather than an overall state [4]. Flow may appear in the process of an activity only briefly. An issue with self-reported flow measurement scales is that people perceive various affective states in different time spans. Self-reported flow experience scales assess the experience with the entire activity rather than with a specific time point. This is a disadvantage when system developers attempt to determine the effect of a specific circumstance of an educational information system on the flow state of a learner. To address this issue, academics desire to find ways to measure changeable flow experience of the entire activity. Electroencephalography (EEG), a psychophysiological method, may be an effective way to examine the affective states of learners during an instructional process.

The current research hopes to explore whether the brainwave signal data collected by an inexpensive non-medical purpose commercial EEG headset can help researchers examine flow experience using a psychophysiological approach. This study examined the flow experience of learners in computerbased instruction and explored the relationship between learning outcome and flow experience through an analysis of psychophysiological data collected using a self-reported questionnaire survey.

## 2 THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 Flow Theory

Csikszentmihalyi originally introduced the idea of flow experience in 1975 to report individuals’ experience with concentration and enjoyment [5-8]. People may feel control over and focus completely on a specific activity with a high level of enthusiasm and fulfillment [2, 9]. Individuals “flow” in intrinsically rewarding activities. When in a flow state, people are fully engaged in their task and feel pleasure [3].

Previous studies discussed individuals’ flow experience in various activities, such as information technology usage [10], online information searching [11], online shopping [12], working [13, 14], leisure [5-8], sport [15], musical performance [16], and online game playing [17, 18]. Flow experience is an important issue for educational systems developers, since all learning systems want learners to engage in the learning process. Many previous studies have focused on antecedents of flow experience of learners [19-23].

The initial models of flow proposed by Csikszentmihalyi focused on the challenge-skill balance aspect. The flow state can be represented as a “channel” that occurs in the condition of challenge-skill

#

balance. Csikszentmihalyi [5] proposed the three-channel model of flow to discriminate human affective states (i.e., flow, anxiety, and boredom) in various activities. A flow state appears when perceived challenge and perceived skill are balanced. If a challenge is beyond the skill of a person, a state of boredom would occur; conversely, if the challenge increases in difficulty and the skill of a person is insufficient to meet the challenge, the overwhelming activity would generate anxiety. The area in which a balance between challenge and skill occurs is referred to as the flow zone.

## 2.2 EEG

EEG is the process of recording brainwave activity, and it has been described as a “window on the mind” [24]. In studies on brain-computer interfaces, EEG is a type of psychophysiological measurement used to examine the relationships between mental and bodily processes. Typically, EEG is measured by recording the voltage of electrodes on the scalp; the electrodes are placed in assigned positions distributed on the head [25].

EEG is used widely in health and medical research, such as research on epileptic seizures [26] and sleep disorders [27]. Some literature [28-33] has focused on attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). In recent years, researchers have applied EEG in other fields, including computer interface design, computer game development, and neuromarketing research. For example, some brain– computer interface research has focused on using EEG activity to control external devices, such as robots, virtual environments, or spelling devices [34, 35]. Ariely and Berns [36] indicated that in the near future, business can use neuromarketing to develop marketing strategies. In a study on gaming, Russoniello, O'Brien and Parks [37] analyzed the EEG brainwave signals of players and revealed that playing casual games helps elevate the moods of players. Chanel, Rebetez, and Bétrancourt applied EEG to assess game players’ emotion [38].

Because EEG is a psychophysiological measurement that is closely related to the affective states of people, researchers have investigated the relationship between EEG and flow experience in gaming [25, 38, 39]. However, the relevance of EEG and flow in computer-based instruction environments has not been addressed sufficiently. To broaden the view of the concept of flow in computer-based instruction, we explore the possibility of using EEG signals to determine whether flow experience influences learning outcomes.

## 3 HYPOTHESES

## 3.1 Flow and Challenge-Skill Balance

Balance between challenge and skill is a primary concept of flow [2]. People devote themselves to their tasks and feel joy and delight if their skills are sufficient to manage the challenge of the activity. When their skills match the challenge of the content, learners may achieve a flow state, which enhances learning performance and learning satisfaction.

Previous studies have focused on challenge-skill balance proposed by Csikszentmihalyi [40]. Custodero [6] argued that the first criterion for flow experience in learning is that skill and challenge must match. Hoffman and Novak [41] proposed a model of flow state for users to navigate online environments. In the model, challenge-skill pairing was proposed as an antecedent of flow. Sweetser and Wyeth [42] created a model of game flow that considers challenge-skill balance. Shin [8] argued that learners achieve flow experience when they perceive a balance between challenge and skill. For flow to occur in learning, Gute and Gute revealed that learners’ skill must match the challenge of the learning task. When learners perceive that their skill surpasses the challenge of the learning, they experience boredom. When they perceive a challenge as too great for their skill, they experience anxiety [43]. Some previous studies even refer to flow experience as a state of optimal enjoyment, as defined by a person's perception of high skill and high challenge for a given activity [6], and argue that flow experiences can be characterized by a balance between challenge and skills [44, 45]. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Challenge-skill balance has a positive effect on flow in computer-based instruction.

## 3.2 Flow and Learning Performance

Previous studies have reported the influence of flow experience on learning performance. Rossin, Ro, Klein and Guo [23] examined the relationship between flow experiences and learning outcomes. Ho and Kuo [46] indicated that flow experience has a positive effect on learning outcomes. Hwang, Wu and Chen [47] designed an effective learning system for students and found that the proposed game approach promotes the flow experiences of learners in web-based problem-solving activities. In addition, several studies have applied the concept of flow in cooperative learning to examine whether learners can experience flow and whether it can enhance learning in groups [48-50]. Flow experience was also used to determine the benefits of e-learning in technological companies [46].

Flow experience positively affects the satisfaction of learners [51]. Egbert [44] revealed that flow experiences can lead to optimal learning. Shin [8] indicated that flow is an effective predictor of course satisfaction in online learning. Joo, Lim and Park [52] also examined the effect of learning flow on the learning satisfaction. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Flow experience has a positive effect on learning outcomes.

## 4 EMPIRICAL STUDIES

We conducted two studies in this research, a questionnaire survey and an electroencephalography (EEG) analysis, which is a psychophysiological method that provides a reliable and useful measurement of the affective states of learners. The independent variable (challenge-skill balance) is a vital antecedent of flow experience. The dependent variables (learning satisfaction and learning performance) are consequences of flow experience. Table 1 shows the methods and objectives of the two studies.

In Study A, we used a traditional paper-and-pencil questionnaire to measure the flow experience of participants during the computer-based instruction process. According to the original flow concept, the challenge and skill balance perceived during activities plays a critical role in achieving flow experience. Hence, Study A set the context for computer-based instruction to discover the effect of challenge-skill balance on the flow states of learners. Study A also examined the connection of flow experience with learning performance and satisfaction.

The experimental procedure of Study B was the same as that of Study A, except that an inexpensive EEG brainwave headset was used in Study B. Because EEG brainwave attention can be regarded as a crucial determinant of learners’ engagement, we assumed that brainwave attention correlates closely with flow experience during the learning activities of computer-based instruction. The main purpose of Study B was to explore the possibility of using an inexpensive EEG device in flow experience research. Study B compared the attention score captured by EEG with the self-reported flow experience. The values of self-report flow experience scale were used as criterion to check the validity of EEG brainwave data.

## 4.1 Study A

## 4.1.1 Participants

Participants in Study A were college students enrolled in the introductory computer courses in the department of business administration of a university in Taiwan. All participants joined the study voluntarily and acknowledged their right to decline their participation. Participants were required to spend approximately two hours to complete the study, including the time for exercise and for taking the questionnaire survey. Among the 189 respondents who voluntarily participated in the survey, 41 were eliminated because of incomplete responses. The study analysis was based on the remaining 148 usable responses (38.5% male and 61.5% female). The gender ratio in Study A was similar to that in the department, that is, according to the student enrollment data, 39.58% of the students in the department were male. Besides, t-test results revealed no significant gender difference in challengeskill balance (p=0.32, 0.40, 0.08 for easy, medium, and difficult level lessons), flow (p=0.81, 0.63, 0.50 for easy, medium, and difficult level lessons), and learning performance (p=0.23, 0.71, 0.46 for easy, medium, and difficult level lessons). Regarding the age of participants, 93.9% were nineteen or twenty years old. The average age of respondents was 19.54 years with a standard deviation of 0.74.

## 4.1.2 Procedure

First, the participants completed a pre-learning quiz. Subsequently, participants completed three lessons of computer-based instruction. Each lesson included learning material on Excel operations with easy, medium, and difficult content. The three lessons contained multimedia contents. No teacher was physically present during the lessons. The participants had essential knowledge and learning experience in using Microsoft Office software.

After completing each learning lesson, participants were asked to exercise. Subsequently, they were asked to complete the measurement items assessing challenge-skill balance and flow experience. The procedures were repeated for all three lessons. The time interval was about 30 minutes (including exercise) per lesson. The time interval for completing all three lessons was about two hours, including time to complete the questionnaire survey.

After completing the three lessons, participants were to report their satisfaction with the entire learning process using a paper-and-pencil questionnaire. Finally, they completed a post-learning quiz comprising questions from the pre-learning quiz (12 multiple-choice questions: four questions each for easy, medium, and difficult levels, all of which were taught in the three lessons). The improvement in quiz scores determined the participants’ learning performances. Appendix 1 lists the sample questions in the quiz.

When all procedures were completed, each respondent received a small souvenir worth approximately 4 USD.

## 4.1.3 Measures

The study used the scale developed by Shin [8] to measure the flow experience dimensions of enjoyment, telepresence, focused attention, and time distortion. The measurement scale developed by Shin [8] was adapted from previous studies on flow [53-55]. The engagement dimension of the Shin [8] measurement scale focused on learners’ peer discussion on bulletin board systems; however, the experimental procedure of the current study did not offer peer discussion for learners and did not provide bulletin board systems. The items measuring “engagement” in Shin [8], such as “when the need arises, I put a question on the electronic bulletin board”, were not appropriate for this study; thus, the current study omitted the engagement dimension of the Shin [8] measurement scale. Involvement is frequently mentioned in flow measurements. In Hoffman and Novak’s conceptual model, involvement is a dimension of flow [41]. Hsu and Lu used “totally involved” to describe and measure the flow [18]. Thus, we referenced the discussion of flow by Hoffman and Novak [41] and the measurement items developed by Saxena, Khurana, Kothari, and Jain [56] to develop a three-item measurement of involvement as a dimension of flow. The three items are, “While learning, I am totally involved in what I am doing”; “While learning, I concentrate on each and every point of the lesson”; and “I am fully attentive during the learning.”

Three items measured each dimension of flow on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree” and 7 = “strongly agree”). A participant with a high score is more likely to experience a flow during the computer-based instruction learning process.

To measure challenge-skill balance, participants were asked to rate the balance of challenge and skill on a 7-point scale from -3 (too easy) to 3 (too difficult). It was used as dichotomous balance/imbalance variable or interval variable. When it was used as a dichotomous variable, a neutral score (0 point) was regarded as challenge-skill balance, whereas positive or negative scores were regarded as imbalance. The challenge-skill balance was also used to calculate the degree of balance as a 4-point interval variable ranging from 4 (high degree of balance) to 1 (low degree of balance). When participants reported challenge-skill balance, the degree of balance was assigned 4 points. When participants reported that the learning was too easy or too difficult, the degree of balance was assigned 1 point.

To assess learning satisfaction, Study A included six items measured on a 7-point Likert scale used by Shin and Chan [57].

## 4.1.4 Reliabilities, validation, and common method variance

In Study A, Cronbach’s alphas were calculated to measure the reliability of the scales. The Cronbach’s alphas were 0.94, 0.85, 0.94, 0.82, 0.96 and 0.88 for enjoyment, telepresence, focused attention, involvement, time distortion, and satisfaction, respectively. All reliability values exceeded 0.70, thus they were within the acceptable range.

We assessed convergent validity by examining the average variance extracted (AVE) of each dimension. The results showed that all AVE values were above 0.5 value, as suggested by Fornell and Larcker [58]. Therefore, we confirmed the convergent validity of the measurement scales.

Discriminant validity refers to the extent to which evaluations of various constructs differ [59].We examined the discriminant validity through correlation among measurement scales. As Table 2 shows, the square root of AVE for each measurement scale was greater compared to the correlation coefficients, indicating that the discriminant validity was accepted.

For the medium level lesson, the result of t-test analysis indicated significant difference between the challenge-skill balance and imbalance groups in flow experience. Regarding the difficult level lesson, only 33 participants among 148 participants reported a state of challenge-skill balance. A significant difference in flow experience was observed between the challenge-skill balance and imbalance groups for easy, medium, and difficult lessons (Fig. 1). The participants in the challengeskill balance group reported higher flow experience compared to those in the imbalance group. The results supported Hypothesis 1 in that challenge-skill balance had a positive effect on flow.

After verifying the influence of challenge-skill balance perception on the flow experience of learners during learning, the next step was to determine the effect of flow experience on learning outcomes. Study A examined the relationship between flow experience and learning outcomes using correlation analysis. The correlation analysis results indicated that flow correlated positively with learning performance in all three lessons at easy, medium, and difficult levels. The results support Hypothesis 2 in that flow experience had a positive effect on learning performance. Appendix 2 lists the correlation coefficients between learning performance and flow dimension of the three learning lessons.

We divided participants into two groups based on their performance improvement and conducted t-test analysis to investigate the difference in flow between the performance improved and unimproved groups. As Table 6 and Fig. 2 reveal, participants with improved learning performance had higher levels of flow status compared to those with unchanged learning performance. Significant differences in flow were observed when comparing the groups on the easy and difficult level lessons. However, no significant difference in flow was observed in the medium level learning lesson between both groups. The results showed that participants with improved performance also experienced higher level of flow states compared to those with unchanged performance for both easy and difficult lessons. These results indicate that flow states are linked to learning performance for the easy and difficult level lessons. The results partially support Hypothesis 2 in that flow experience had a positive effect on learning outcomes.

We conducted a correlation analysis in Study A to determine whether flow is an antecedent of satisfaction. Since Study A measured only satisfaction with the entire computer-based instruction learning process, we merged the flow experience of three learning lessons, creating the aggregate flow experience of the entire learning process. The correlation analysis results showed that aggregate flow correlated positively with learning performance (r=0.28; p<0.01) and learning satisfaction (r=0.72; p<0.01).

The results of statistical analysis showed that challenge-skill balance was related to flow experience and that flow experience results in superior learning outcomes. When learners perceived that the difficulty of learning content was beyond or below their original skill, they did not achieve a flow state during learning. The result is consistent with that of Csikszentmihalyi [40], Hoffman and Novak [41], and Shin [8] who indicated that challenge-skill balance plays a critical role in achieving flow experience. Thus, Hypothesis 1, which proposed that challenge-skill balance has a positive effect on flow experience, was supported.

In addition, Study A examined the relationship between flow experience and learning outcomes. The results showed that flow experience correlated positively with learning performance and learning satisfaction. Study A also showed that participants with improved performance had a higher level of flow experience compared to those with unimproved performance. These findings support Hypothesis 2, which suggested that flow experience has a positive effect on learning outcomes.

A questionnaire survey can measure the self-reported subjective flow experience of participants only after completing each lesson. However, flow states change occasionally. EEG recording can examine the attention of learners at all moments during learning. The Study B examined the attention of learners measured by EEG brainwave signals to approach the real condition of a psychophysiological state at various difficulty levels during computer-based instruction learning. Self-report flow experience scale was used as the criterion to check the validity of EEG brainwave data.

## 4.2 Study B

## 4.2.1 Participants

Study B focused on the relationship between flow experience and the attention of learners, which was measured by EEG brainwave signals. Twenty respondents (10 males and 10 females) were invited to participate in Study B. All participants were college undergraduate or graduate students, with ages ranging from 19 to 27 years old (M=23.75, SD=3.25). The participants participated in this experiment voluntarily and acknowledged their right to decline their participation. No participant in Study A participated in Study B.

## 4.2.2 Procedure

The experimental procedure of Study B was the same as that of Study A, except for the use of an EEG brainwave headset that was used to measure the attention of participants. Fig. 3 shows the experimental procedure. In the beginning, participants entered the laboratory individually and listened to a briefing of the experiment procedure. Subsequently, participants were led to a laptop computer and asked to wear the brainwave headset (Fig. 4).

Participants were then equipped with NeuroSky Mindeset EEG headset. Once wearing the brainwave headset, participants had to stay calm and relax for at least three minutes. They were then asked to complete the pre-learning quiz. After that, participants completed three lessons of easy, medium, and difficult levels. Their brainwave signals were recorded as well. After completing each lesson, the participants completed questionnaires assessing challenge-skill balance and flow experience. The time interval to complete all three lessons was about two hours, including time for learning, quiz, and questionnaire survey. The respondents received a reward worth approximately 4 USD for their participation in the study.

## 4.2.3 Measures

Traditional EEG devices adopt the 10-20 international system as the standard positioning scheme. It includes 19 (can be 70 or more) wet electrodes for EEG measurements. These traditional EEG devices are expensive and are not easy to use, since the traditional EEG devices use electrolyte gel to wet the electrodes. Wearing a traditional EEG device usually takes one or more hours.

To measure individuals’ EEG attention, Study B used the Mindset EEG headset developed by Neurosky Inc., which captures neural activity using three dry electrodes, one at the forehead FP1 point of the EEG International 10–20 system and the other two on the left ear. The electrode at the FP1 point is the main sensor to collect data. The two electrodes in the left ear are reference points. The NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset adopts dry electrodes, which are gel-free sensors; thus, no conductive liquid is needed. According to patent documents [62-64], dry sensor module of Neurosky Mindset headset includes an instrumentation amplifier for amplifying and adjusting the biomedical signal to generate an output signal; a band-pass filter for filtering the output signal; a notch filter for eliminating a noise component; and an amplifier. It strengthens the raw brainwave signal; filtering protocols that eliminate known noise frequencies, such as muscle, pulse, and electrical devices; and notch filters that eliminate electrical noise [62-67].

Neurosky reported benchmark tests of the dry EEG by comparing EEG signals measured by the NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset with signals from the Biopac system, a well-known wet electrode

#

EEG system widely used in medical and research applications. According to Neurosky Inc., the two EEG signals of the Neurosky system and the Biopac system are very similar [68].

Electromyogram (EMG) contamination of frontalis and temporalis muscle is often an issue in EEG recording and might not be easily isolated [69]. The Neurosky Mindset EEG headset already considers the influence of EMG contamination. The Neurosky Mindset EEG headset is powered by the ThinkGear AM (TGAM) EEG sensor. The sensor module contains a chip that filters EMG noise [70].

The brainwave data provided by the NeuroSky headset included an attention indicator derived from the EEG values to indicate the mental focus of participants (Neurosky Inc., 2009). The values of the attention indicator ranged from 0 to 100. The attention indicator is based on beta wave [71-74]. The Neurosky headset provided the participants’ attention scores for each second.

Previous studies conducted usability evaluation of the NeuroSky MindSet EEG headset to measure individuals’ attention. Luo and Sullivan reported the design of the NeuroSky MindSet EEG headset [75]. Rebolledo-Mendez, Dunwell, Martínez-Mirón, Vargas-Cerdán, de Freitas, Liarokapis, & García-Gaona assessed the usability of the Mindset EEG headset in measuring players’ attention in the Second Life (http://secondlife.com), a 3D virtual world where users can socialize and connect with others using voice and text chat [71]. Based on the positive correlation between Mindset EEG headset measurements and self-reported attention levels, they suggested that the MindSet EEG headset provided accurate readings of attention. Crowley, Sliney, Pitt, & Murphy conducted two psychological tests (a Stroop Colour-Word Interference test and a test implementing the Towers of Hanoi) to evaluate the effectiveness of using Neurosky to measure individual attention [76]. They demonstrated NeuroSky EEG headset’s suitability as a means of measuring an individual’s attention.

The study assessed attention by measuring general cognitive resources devoted to the learning task. Attention is the cognitive process of selectively concentrating on one aspect of the environment while ignoring other things. The main sensor of the NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset is on the FP1 point on the forehead. Thinking, emotions, mental states, and concentration are primarily in the pre-frontal cortex where the FP1 is. Thus, NeurosSky Mindset EEG headset can detect individuals’ cognitive attention. Study B used the average attention score of the learning lesson and high attention percentage as indicators in the data analysis. High attention percentage was defined as the percentage of the time interval when attention value is greater than 40. The high attention percentage is a method proposed by Neurosky Inc. and used by Crowley, Sliney, Pitt, & Murphy [76].

In addition to EEG measurement, Study B applied the same paper-and-pencil questionnaires used in Study A to measure the challenge-skill balance, flow experience, and learning satisfaction of participants.

## 5 DISCUSSION

The paper empirically investigated the phenomenon of flow experience in a computer-based instruction environment. The links among challenge-skill balance, flow experience, and learning outcomes were examined using a questionnaire survey and a psychophysiological method (EEG recording). The empirical research results showed that challenge-skill and the flow experience of learners are related. In addition, after learners achieved a flow experience during learning, their learning performance and learning satisfaction improved.

One purpose of the paper was to explore the possibility of using inexpensive EEG devices to understand information systems usage behavior. Cognitive neuroscience usually uses the term ‘‘event related potentials’’ (ERP) to name the process of using EEG to measure brain electrophysiological response to a stimulus [77]. In addition to EEG, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) can also be used to explore people’s cognitive response [78]. Now, fMRI devices are still very expensive and used mainly for medical purpose. EEG is less expensive compared to fMRI [77]. The cost to equip an ERP lab is still significantly higher than that of using questionnaire survey. The development of inexpensive and easy-to-use characteristics can help popularize the use of EEG in research of individual information behavior.

In this paper, we used an inexpensive EEG device (cost less than US\$ 300 per set) to measure EEG brainwaves. The results revealed that flow had a positive correlation with attention, indicating that the perception of challenge-skill balance influences the attention of learners. The findings of the study confirmed the relationship between challenge-skill balance and flow experience using traditional questionnaire and psychophysiological measurement. The results also revealed that psychophysiological measurement could be used to conduct information systems behavior research. Nevertheless, the paper found that brainwave attention is not equivalent to flow experience, although they are related. Flow experience is a complex concept composed of several sub-concepts rather than a pure concept of attention. More efforts are needed to measure flow experience using psychophysiological methods.

## 5.1 Implications for theory and practice

This study offers several contributions to the related literature. First, we confirmed that challenge-skill balance is an essential antecedent of flow experience. Although previous studies [41, 42, 79] investigated this type of relationship, they did not focus on the context of e-learning or computerbased instruction. This study examined challenge-skill balance in a computer-based instruction environment. Additionally, positive correlations between flow experience and learning outcomes were observed. Thus, learners with higher flow experience exhibited superior learning performance and satisfaction during learning.

Another contribution of this research is that it used EEG brainwave signal recording to conduct flow research. The study by Pearce, Ainley and Howard [4] proposed that flow experience is a dynamic process rather than an overall state. Hence, using only a questionnaire survey to measure the flow concept does not adequately capture the real condition of flow experience when learners participate in computer-based instruction environment. This study recommends EEG recording to measure flow experience instead of traditional paper-and-pencil questionnaires. The results of the brainwave data analysis showed that flow experience was positively correlated with learner attention.

This study provides an approach to examine the factors that influence learning performance. It is necessary for educators or computer-based instruction system developers to design suitable learning materials according to the aptitudes of learners. The optimal approach is to offer various levels of difficulty that learners can choose based on their ability. In brief, novices must select easy learning content because difficult content may lead to a decline in attention. With the advancement of knowledge and ability, learners can choose the next level of learning content. Therefore, they can achieve flow experience easily and maintain their enthusiasm for learning. By contrast, expert learners will concentrate on difficult level learning materials. Following this would make learning more meaningful and enjoyable.

## 5.2 Limitations and suggestions for future research

The results indicated a number of limitations and suggestions for future studies. First, this study focused on challenge-skill balance as a vital antecedent that influences flow experience in a computerbased instruction environment. However, numerous factors (such as instructional factors and platform related factors) must be considered in future studies.

Second, both Study A and Study B used a self-reported questionnaire to measure flow and challengeskill balance. The results of empirical survey revealed that challenge-skill and the flow experience of learners were related. However, the experiment design cannot rule out the possibility that flow experience precedes and affects challenge-skill balance. Learners with high flow experience may also consider the learning task more challenge-skill balanced. To make sure that challenge-skill balance is an antecedent factor that affects the flow experience, future studies should manipulate the level of challenge of learning material to match learners’ skills.

Third, this study used a psychophysiological method to measure the flow experiences of learners. The indicator of attention provided by the NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset is not a precise indicator. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) can be used to find the frequency components in a time domain signal [80]. Furtyre studies can analyze raw data of brainwaves (including delta, theta, alpha, low beta, midrange beta, high beta, and gamma) to further explore the relationship between flow experience and brainwave activity in learning.

Fourth, small sample size is a weakness of study B. The results of statistical analysis cannot easily interpreted due to a very limited sample size. Sample size is usually small in EEG research. The complex procedure involved in EEG research and the difficulty to recruit voluntary subjects are two obstacles to recruiting a larger sample size. Further studies should utilize larger sample sizes to confirm the effectiveness of inexpensive EEG device in behavior research. By accumulating the results of different EEG studies, academics may realize the possibility of using EEG devices as an alternative tool in behavior research.

Fifth, we defined high attention percentage as the accumulated time interval percentage when attention value is greater than 40. This method was proposed by Neurosky Inc. and had been used by Crowley, Sliney, Pitt, & Murphy [76]. Further studies may conduct sensitivity analysis with various threshold values (for example, 30, 40, 50, 60…) to make sure the threshold value of 40 is appropriate.

In addition, using simple and low cost non-medical EEG devices has some limitations. For example, asymmetrical activity over the frontal cortex was associated with emotions and motivation [81]. However, NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset is a sample of a low cost EEG device that uses only one dry electrode at the forehead FP1 point of the EEG International 10–20 system to record EEG data. Thus, it is not possible to measure asymmetrical activity over the frontal cortex using NeuroSky Mindset EEG headset. An EEG device with electrodes at both left and right sides of head may overcome this issue. Nevertheless, an EEG device with many electrodes may raise the cost of device or decrease the easy-to-use characteristic. The characteristics of full function, low cost, easy-to-use, and precise data may not co-exist in a non-medical purpose EEG device now. However, it is a development direction for future EEG product.

Due to the advances in technology, psychophysiological measures, such as brainwave recording and eye tracking, which have become available, can help discover more unexpected phenomena for future studies on information behavior. This study explores the possibility of using brainwave recording in flow experience research. Brainwave devices can capture individuals’ cognitive attention. Further study may use eye tracking devices to capture individuals’ visual attention. EEG is not the only electrophysiological measurement.

Csikszentmihalyi [40] argued that people experience a state of anxiety when their skills are lower than the challenges they encounter. However, in practice, advanced learning materials may be necessary to improve learners’ knowledge and ability. A flow state might not be achievable because of the difficulty of advanced learning materials. Thus, it is crucial to improve the learning performance of learners who are not in a flow state. Further studies must focus on the learning performance of low flow learners who encounter learning materials that are more difficult than is their ability. Educational practice may be useful in addressing the possibility of inconsistency between flow experience and learning performance.

As mentioned above, flow may appear in the process of an activity only briefly. Self-report flow scales can provide only the flow feelings of the whole activity. If researchers wanted to examine the subjects flow experience at a specific time point, EEG measurement may be a solution. Further studies may try to use EEG to measure the change of flow experience during different time point of the activity.
