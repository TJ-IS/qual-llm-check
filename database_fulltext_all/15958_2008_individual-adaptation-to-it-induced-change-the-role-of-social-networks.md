# Individual Adaptation to IT‑Induced Change: The Role of Social Networks

Se bas tiÁn Bruque , Jos É Mo yano , and Jaco b Eisen be rg

Sebas tián Bruque is a Senior Lecturer of Management in the Department of Business Administration, Accounting and Sociology of the University of Jaén (Spain). He currently conducts research on organizational factors affecting IT adoption and social networks effects on workers’ acceptance of technological changes. His research has appeared in the European Journal of Information Systems, Internet Research, Journal of High Technology Management Research, and Technology Analysis and Strategic Management.

Jos é Moyano is a Senior Lecturer of Management in the Department of Business Administration, Accounting and Sociology at the University of Jaén (Spain). He currently conducts research on the effect of the ownership structure on firm performance. He also has interests in the role of technology in organizations, with current emphasis involving social networks’ influence on employees’ technology acceptance. His research has appeared in the Administrative Science Quarterly, Journal of Management Studies, International Journal of Management Reviews, and Technology Analysis and Strategic Management.

Jacob Eis enberg is a Senior Lecturer of Management at the University College Dublin (UC D) School of Business (Ireland). He received his Ph.D. from Colorado State University. His research interests include team creative performance, multiple organizational identities and work identifications, and the role of affect in creative behavior and decision making. He is also interested in examining the moderating role of national culture in these areas. He has presented at many international conferences, including the Annual Academy of Management meetings, and his research has appeared in several journals, including the Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, Communications of the Association for Information Systems, Human Performance, and Creativity Research Journal.

Abs tract: In order to better understand the sociopsychological factors involved in employees’ adaptation to new technology in organizations, we examine the role that two types of social networks—supportive and informational—play in individual adaptation to IT-induced change in a large financial company. Using survey data from 371 employees working in 133 different branches of the organization, we find that several aspects of the social networks relate to quality of employees’ adaptation to the new technology as assessed by the company’s departmental directors. Specifically, the size of the support network as well as the strength and density of the information network significantly predict employees’ adaptation to the new system. We conclude the paper by discussing theoretical implications for the relevance of social network research for members’ adaptation to organizational changes as well as outlining specific implications for practice.

Key words and phras es : individual adaptation, informational networks, IT-induced change, social networks, supportive networks.

The pervas ivenes of information technology (IT) utilization in the modern organization makes the effective use of IT by organization members a necessary condition for effective performance and successful business operation [45]. Consequently, a major challenge facing organizations is that of adapting to major technological changes that influence daily work practices. Given the rapid pace of workplace IT-induced changes in the recent decade and the fact that many organizational functions strongly depend on effectively using IT, the degree to which organization members adapt to a new IT system can have major a impact not only on the effectiveness of the operations that are directly based on IT but indeed on the performance of the organization as a whole.

Adaptation to workplace changes is the process by which individuals learn, negotiate, enact, and maintain the behaviors appropriate to a given organizational environment. Appropriate adaptation indicates some degree of fit between the behaviors demanded by the environment and those produced by the individual such that the individual is able to achieve valued goals [2, 4].

IT-induced change is a particular case of a workplace change. Significant transformations in people’s work take place when organizations adopt new IT. These change situations have been termed technochanges [54]. An example is a new accounting system that eliminates many specialist accounting jobs and requires business unit managers to master new accounting codes, new approval routines, and new ways to request and analyze financial data. Technochange situations differ sharply from other organizational changes as they can be risky and hard to carry out successfully [54]. One of the reasons major technochanges are fraught with uncertainty rests with the complexity of human responses to major changes in their immediate environment. While the functioning of new devices or technologies can be predicted, experience tells us that this is not the case with human reactions to these changes. As Griffith puts it: “Given the complex systems within which new technologies are implemented, it is even more difficult for implementers of modern technologies to anticipate users sensemaking and its effect” [34, p. 472]. Our aim in the present study is to examine the roles that various social networks (supportive and informational) play in members adaptation to IT-induced change.

Given the importance of interpersonal communications during organizational changes (e.g., [64]), we suggest that one of the mechanisms that affect individual adaptation to IT-induced change is the structure of members’ social supportive and informational networks. In the process of trying to assimilate an IT-induced change, individuals use psychological, social, and instrumental mechanisms that have not been sufficiently considered in the literature on adoption. Because new technologies are disruptive, at least to a degree, organization members are confronted with the need to make sense of their new organizational environment and to restore their cognitive equilibrium (e.g., [34, 84]). Some of the most commonly employed mechanisms for reducing uncertainty and for regaining psychological balance involve communicating with one’s peers [84] who make up the individual social network. Social networks are also a valuable source of knowledge sharing, encouragement, and performance feedback, which can assist with a better individual adaptation to IT-induced change.

There is some empirical evidence for the relationship between technology change and social networks structure [11]. However, the effects of individual social networks on individual adaptation following an IT-induced change have evaded close scrutiny by academics and practitioners alike. In the current study, we address this gap in the literature and argue that examining members’ social networks provides new and useful insights into this issue.

## Motivating Rationale, Theoretical Background, and Hypotheses

## Adaptation to IT-Induced Change

In the field of IT management, relatively little attention has been paid to adaptation to IT-induced change, in contrast to IT adoption processes. With regard to IT adoption, there is presently a large volume of research that approaches the issue of adoption with an assumption that the individual (or organization) can accept or reject a particular technology or use it in a different way from what it was designed for (e.g., [16, 43]). However, we contend that much of this research suffers from a “pro-innovation” bias, which causes scholars to overfocus on the quantity and pace of adoption, with a presumption, often implicitly made, that “more and sooner” is also better. Prior research indicates that perceptions and dynamics of users and implementing agents often differ considerably (e.g., [35]). Therefore, a valuable alternative approach that may help to overcome the pro-innovation bias is to explicitly study the outcomes of the technological adoption in individual behavior and performance, focusing on the quality aspect of the innovation process [43].

In the present study, our departure point is that an adaptation focus is a necessary theoretical complement to what literature on adoption has unveiled so far in relation to the introduction of IT innovations in organizations. We argue that this is the case because there are several meaningful differences between adaptation and adoption. First, adoption is an end result of a decision-making process that is often characterized by clear time boundaries, whereas adaptation is usually a dynamic, cyclical, and longterm process. Methodologically, although it may be possible to pinpoint an adoption decision as occurring in a single point in time, adaptation to a proposed change develops and changes over time [40]. Second, and building on the previous point, adaptation is best treated as a continuous-type variable that differs from the traditional dichotomous nature of adoption (i.e., adopters versus nonadopters). Some scholars have pointed out that analyzing the continuous nature of technology assimilation processes is more valuable than merely analyzing the adoption decision itself (e.g., [23]). Thus, our conceptualization of adaptation is closely related to what Fichman and Kemerer [22] term deployment. Third, in focusing on adaptation, we want to shift the focus from examining factors affecting top managers’ decision-making process of technology adoption to examining the factors affecting members’ (i.e., users) adaptation to a given technology change. To that end, we examine members’ individual differences since the degree of individual adaptation to IT change in organizations tends to be intimately related to individual characteristics [40]. Our third point has some elements in common with previous studies of technology adaptation, most of which examined how processes such as customization and addition of new features occur when new technology (or innovation) is introduced in a given social context (e.g., [65, 71]). However, while we share the focus on users’ perspectives, we differ from this stream of research in that rather than looking at how users’ reactions change the technology use (i.e., adjust or adapt it to their needs), we focus on how the users adapt themselves to the new IT system they have to use. Therefore, in contrast to prior research such as Fichman and Kemerer and following the urges of leading scholars in the field (e.g., [34]) to pay more attention to users’ psychological dispositions, we focus on the “typical end user” rather than the IT manager. Furthermore, in Fichman and Kemerer’s studies, the level of analysis is organizational (even though respondents are individuals) and the nature of the innovation is very distinct (database management systems, four-generation languages, and computer-aided software engineering tools).

We further observed that prior research on adaptation to IT-induced change has disregarded several factors that may influence individual adaptation to such a change. Often, the reasons for organization members’ resistance to various sorts of changes, including IT-related changes, have to do with perceived loss of a desirable aspect of the “old” organization or perceiving a threat for losing some individual assets such as power, influence, and security. Likewise, IT-induced changes may fail to succeed due to members’ resistance to change, which may be related to task mismatches [54] and to a dysfunctional individual adaptation to IT-induced change. Individual adaptation to new IT is particularly important when an IT-enabled change has to be assimilated by all employees. In these cases, organization members do not have discretion in regard to the new system: they must use the new technology because it replaces the previous one and it would be impossible to continue doing their work without it. Employees subjected to such technology change, however, have the discretion to use the new technology more or less effectively, to vary in the degree to which they take advantage of the possibilities offered by the new technology, and, consequently, determining to which degree the technological capacity indeed translates into effective behaviors in their workplace.

To conclude, individual adaptation to IT-induced change can be affected by idiosyncratic individual or work team dispositions (e.g., knowledge, skills, abilities, experience, age, work team size) as well as by broader human capital aspects, which include relevant social ties [29, 32, 36].

## Social Networks’ Effects on Individual Adaptation to IT‑ Induced Change

In the case of IT-induced organizational changes, Orlikowski and Gash [64] posited that relevant social groups have shared frames and that differences in these groups frames can affect how actors interpret and act on IT systems. When an IT-induced change takes place, environmental triggers may result in interpretive shifts within an organization [19].

One of the promising approaches to discerning patterns in socially driven interpretive shifts is by considering the role of social networks in shaping individual- and grouplevel reactions to IT-induced change. Increasingly, organizational behavior scholars have examined the favorable work outcomes of organizational social networks (e.g., [20]). We aim to further contribute to this growing area by using a social networks framework in order to explain the role that employees’ social environment plays in individual adaptation following an IT-induced change. Interestingly, almost all studies that empirically examined the role of social ties in relation to IT-induced change confined themselves to observing only within-organizational social relationships. Given the relative scarcity of research examining individual adaptation while focusing on employees’ social interactions in the social world outside the organization, several authors have called for a systematic investigation of these issues [47, 70]. By empirically examining the explanatory power of social networks, we seek to demonstrate that looking at the broader context of members’ social relationships is a fruitful strategy that expands our conceptual understanding of the social forces affecting individuals adaptation to IT-induced changes [47, 74].

To analyze the effect of social networks on individual adaptation to IT-induced change, we have adopted Ibarra’s conceptual framework for network classifications [41] and, consequently, distinguished between two types of networks—informational and supportive. Informational networks are those relationships that are developed for provision and exchange of information and advice needed to accomplish work-related tasks. Supportive networks are comprised of affective ties carrying either positive or negative emotions that are not necessarily task related.

## Hypotheses Development

## Supportive Networks

Sociocognitive theory (SCT ) proposes that individuals’ behavior is the result of the interaction between individuals’ social environment and their cognitive abilities [6]. A growing body of research demonstrates that members’ performance at work is also affected by extraorganizational factors, such as family and friends’ support (for example, a study by Madjar et al. [53] found that employees’ creative performance was affected by nonwork relationships). In the technological adaptation process, individuals behave as social actors who are influenced by the environment inside and outside the organization to which they belong. Closely related to the SCT , the actor metaphor [72] indicates that individual cognitions and attitudes that relate to IT-induced change are shaped by sources that are internal but also, and sometimes not less importantly, external to organizational boundary. Among the social-environmental sources influencing individuals, the structure of their supportive social network may play a substantial role.

The size of the individual supportive network is one of the most important attributes of individual’s social network. Individual supportive networks are networks that provide an appropriate arena to share sentiments, seek understanding, vent frustration, and build up self-esteem [50]. Previous research indicates that supportive networks are an important predictor of individual attitudes and behaviors. Supportive networks have been identified as predictors of psychological well-being [80]: it was found that a wide supportive network is positively linked to decreased perceived stress, decreased depression, and decreased likelihood of suffering distress [15, 80]. Furthermore, support networks in work groups that allow for sharing of members’ positive and negative emotions may promote important organizational outcomes, such as group cohesion and a climate conducive of creativity and innovation [86].

Supportive relationships can be especially crucial for employees undergoing an IT-induced change since one of the prevalent effects of such a change is a heightened sense of anxiety and uncertainty about the future among employees (e.g., [74]). Experiencing such uncertainty can lead to various dysfunctional outcomes such as stress, job dissatisfaction, low trust in the organization and commitment to it, and increased intentions to leave the organization [63]. The larger the size of the supportive network, the less likely these dysfunctional outcomes are. Individuals who enjoy a large supportive network have a higher likelihood of getting the required support and doling out encouragement. Indeed, lower uncertainty or, alternatively, higher confidence regarding one’s future are strong characteristics of a high adaptation state [37].

Social support stemming from a larger support network is another contextual resource that plays a critical role in the process of coping with change-related stress and anxiety [27]. According to coping theory [49], coping is a complex, multidimensional process that is sensitive to the environment and its demands and resources and strongly associated with the regulation of emotion, especially distress, throughout the stress process [24]. We suggest that organization members who face stress and anxiety in such effectively overloading situations that are often found in an organization undergoing IT-induced change [46] benefit from a larger supportive network, as its presence improves their coping abilities.

People who are able to cope with anxiety in an IT-induced change environment perceive themselves as having more cognitive and emotional resources and show higher levels of self-efficacy [78], which is one of the most significant predictors of positive adjustment to any major technological change [17, 31] and, indeed, of task performance in various organizational contexts [30]. Self-efficacy depends on the interplay between behavioral and environmental influences on a person in a given situation. According to SCT , environmental forces influence self-efficacy perceptions [51]; in the context of an IT-induced change, higher self-efficacy regarding coping with the change would lead to employees’ perception that they have the ability, motivation, and resources to adjust well to the new technological environment. Subsequently, higher self-efficacy is likely to increase the amount of effort individuals invest in coping with the new environment and their persistence in the face of difficulties [30, 87], resulting in higher likelihood to perform better under the new system [30]. Supportive networks foster self-efficacy beliefs through verbal encouragement, coaching, and by providing performance feedback [5]. This results in higher perceived self-efficacy, which enhances motivation and performance attainments [7]. A larger supportive network may increase the likelihood for the individual to enjoy these aforementioned benefits (i.e., verbal encouragement, coaching, and performance feedback). Furthermore, support that comes simultaneously from many people may be more credible and therefore more effective than support coming from just one person or a small group of people.

Therefore, we suggest that the size of employees’ support networks will relate positively to their adaptation effectiveness by improving change-related self-efficacy, by reducing perceived uncertainty, and by enhancing members’ overall coping resources. This leads to the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: The larger the size of the individual supportive social network, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

## Informational Networks

Informational networks constitute another, distinct type of social network. In contrast with supportive networks, informational networks provide concrete task-related assistance, especially useful when seeking to attain career-related outcomes that require access to such information [58]. Informational networks may play a relevant role during the process of change in organizations because informational ties can be used to better manage new knowledge [49] and to achieve assistance and guidance [77].

The informational network structure is conducive and will have implications for the individual adaptation to IT-induced change. We suggest that the relative number of ties among the members in the informational network (density) is related to individual adaptation to IT-induced change. Informational networks in which many members have ties to one another (high-density informational network) should have higher levels of information sharing, collaboration, and cooperation that are necessary for successful task completion [4]. In contrast, networks in which individuals do not interact with many other members (low density) do not facilitate exchange of job-related ideas or of tacit knowledge among actors [37].

Social network scholars argue that learning how to master a new job requires regular access to reliable and readily available information sources who are willing to assist and are familiar with the particular job or role requirements in question [33, 58]. During an IT-induced change, individuals need to learn to adapt to the change through deepening their understanding of the new standards and procedures rather than through obtaining diverse, nonredundant information about the change. Dense networks may prove more effective in providing this type of reliable and consistent information about the IT-induced change. In dense informational networks, individuals can make use of a closely woven network of ties, with many links among the members, to keep each other informed about the technical problems they encounter and the solutions they find. A related benefit of dense informational networks is that people can avoid duplicating the work of others in obtaining valuable information on what their contacts have been engaged in [69].

Another feature of the informational network structure that may have a significant effect on individual adaptation to IT-induced change is the strength of the ties. The strength of the ties represents ego’s degree of informational proximity to each alter inside the network. Strong ties in informational networks among actors involved in knowledge transfer facilitate a more effective knowledge sharing because strong ties may predict actors’ motivation to exchange knowledge [60]. During an IT-induced change process, members may need in-depth frequent access to complex knowledge, which is better transmitted through strong ties [38]. Compared to weak ties, strong ties are more efficient in promoting information flow and are more useful for fine-grained information transfer and are characterized by greater depth of information search [26, 82]. During an IT-induced change, workers connected by strong relationships may benefit from dense networks making use of the closure mechanism [13], which is opposite to the brokerage mechanism that emphasizes the importance of bridging ties connecting different people [62]. In groups where the closure mechanism is more pervasive, we find more solidarity, stronger reciprocity norms, and greater intergroup trust than we find in groups forming less dense and strong networks [61]. Solidarity and trust tend to promote exchange of psychological support, guidance, and encouragement, which are related to personal skill development [48]. Hence, members facing an IT-induced change may benefit more from a strong and dense network of informational ties than from a weak and less dense network. This rationale leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2a: The stronger the ties of the individual informational network, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

Hypothesis 2b: The denser the individual informational network, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

## Nonnetwork Effects on Individual Adaptation to IT‑ Induced Change

While the primary interest in the present study lies in examining social networks factors affecting adaptation to IT change, we respond to the call of organizational scholars (e.g., [66, 79]) to examine the effects of idiosyncratic individual or work team dispositions within the same empirical frame to gain a richer and more valid view of organizational phenomena. Prior research has called attention to variables such as age, education level, IT-related education, gender, and work team size.

## Age

Organizational researchers have found that individual dispositions influence task performance [57] and that some of these variables may relate to individual adaptation to IT change. One variable that often features in task performance studies is age. Several studies report that beyond a certain point (approximately early adulthood), there is an inverse relationship between age and cognitive ability [57, 59]. Thus, older individuals take longer to assimilate new information than younger ones. The implication of this relationship to adaptation to new systems is that older members may adapt worse than younger ones to an IT-induced change.

Hypothesis 3: The younger the individuals, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

## Education Level and IT-Related Education

Education level may also be related to adaptation since Lynch and Black found that workers with a lower education level have fewer chances of acquiring the skills that a rapidly evolving labor market requires [52]. Taking into account that there is a positive relationship between the education level and the coping skills the individual utilizes while facing a job-related transition, it is foreseeable that workers with a higher educational level will adapt better to an IT-induced change. In addition, in the particular case of an IT-induced change, IT-related education is crucial for enabling individuals to take advantage of the opportunities offered by the implementation of a new IT system [12]. Therefore, it is plausible to expect that the higher the individual IT-related education, the better the individual adaptation to an IT-induced change.

Hypothesis 4a: The higher the education level, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

Hypothesis 4b: The higher the IT-related education level, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

## Work Team Size

The last factor that we examine is work team size. Individuals who work in large work teams are more likely to possess IT-related abilities compared to workers in small settings [28]. As is the case with other human capital factors, larger teams have more members who can communicate with each other, thus potentially gaining access to a broader range of knowledge and skills that may subsequently prove useful while facing an IT-induced change.

Hypothesis 5: The larger the work team the individuals belong to, the better the individual adaptation following an IT-induced change.

## Research Setting

All data were collected from employees of a financial firm located in Spain. In 2004, this firm had 421 employees distributed in 133 branches spread across 108 Spanish cities and towns. The technology change took place between January 2002 and June 2004 and consisted of a radical change of the platform used. The change consisted of both technical changes and process changes.

## Technical Changes

Before 2002, transactions such as consultations, withdrawals, deposits, money transfers, loans, and so on were made using a numeric reference for each transaction. After the change, the interfaces and the architecture of the system incorporated object-oriented software embodying a direct way to introduce and manage operations, avoiding the need to use a numeric list of references. At the same time, the new system increased the capacity to manage interrelated data, giving rise to a more effective executive information system.

The new system uses a compatible operating system, which allows an easier communication with the other applications used in the firm and replaces the old proprietary teleprocess transaction management system. Changing the information system architecture included a complete renovation of the hardware as well.

## Process Changes

The new system had a significant impact on employees’ tasks and routines. Before the change, employees had to remember the list of transaction references to fulfill a transaction. When the client asked the officer to complete a transaction (i.e., a money transfer), the officer had to introduce the right transaction numerical reference in the system using a keyboard and a lean terminal. Once the employee had memorized the reference list, he or she would not be exposed to new relevant changes in his or her routines. After the change, the list of references was not used anymore because the new system includes a dynamic set of menus, run in Windows, which cover all possible transactions. The new hardware includes a PC connected to the firm servers through a wide area network. After the change, employees could, for the first time, access the Internet as well online help systems, knowledge sharing platforms, corporate databases, financial simulating programs, e-mail, professional chats, and forums. We learned that although the system was planned to facilitate employees’ work, some employees perceived that it was a big (and sometimes threatening) change given that, after the implementation, they were exposed to a large amount of new information. The new facilities forced employees to keep updated in relation to a broader range of tasks, products, and services ranging from traditional money withdrawals to marketing products such as cars, bikes, or computers, which were linked to customers’ personal loans. The new system gave rise to new process structures, in which interrelated processes could be actually connected, which subsequently forced the employees to participate in collaborative tasks and routines and, therefore, to share new risks and responsibilities with other coworkers. At the same time, the employees had to get used to checking the firm’s sources of information (e-mail, knowledge management platforms, distribution lists) on a frequent basis.

A cascading training system was developed to spread knowledge and skills throughout the organization: eight employees (termed A instructors) were trained by the technology supplier in the first stage. In the second stage, these eight A instructors trained 50 employees (termed B instructors), who subsequently provided training to the rest of the staff (third training stage).

## Method

## Data Collection

We ask ed B instructors as well as departmental directors to collect data on employees adaptation to the IT-enabled change (dependent variable). Through this process, we obtained two external and independent measures for the degree of technology adaptation (one from B instructors and the other from departmental directors).

In order to collect data for the independent variables, we used a survey that was administered through structured personal on-site interviews with company employees where we obtained demographic information as well as social networks data.

To ensure a high response rate, the company’s human resources (HR) management and the board of directors explicitly supported the research process. Before carrying out the interviews, the HR department sent out a letter to all employees asking them to take part in the interviews. After this initial communication, a research group comprised of two of the authors and a research assistant visited all firm branches as well as its central services. Data collection took place between November 2004 and March 2005, six months after the training period was over.

Using individual on-site employee interviews to administer the survey, we obtained 371 valid questionnaires out of the total of 421 employees. Because the actual number of active employees during the investigation period was 405 due to sick leave and maternity leave, the final response rate amounted to 91.6 percent.

Before engaging in hypotheses testing, we ran the Casewise Diagnostics analysis on the dependent variables (using a regression subcommand in SPSS) in order to identify observations with excessively large residuals. We identified six values that were more than 3 standard deviations above or below the average value for the relevant variables. Thus, these are observations whose dependent variable value is unusual given its values on the predictor variables. Given the relatively small number of cases concerned and in order to eliminate undue influence of a small set of outlier observations on the regression coefficients, we removed these six participants from the data set, which resulted in a final sample of 365 participants. All 15 departmental directors took part in the research. The average age of the staff was 40.8, 63 percent were men, and 49 percent of the staff had at least a university degree, with only 13 percent having had an IT-related education.

## Measures

Item Generation Procedures and Scale Formation for the Dependent Variable

Individual Adaptation to IT-Induced Change. According to Ashford and Taylor’s [3] definition, individual adaptation outcomes following a job transition are a set of behavioral habits or routines, reorganized in the individual’s best attempt to better fit the environmental demands. If adaptation is effective, these routines will keep individuals on track in the new environment with respect to the goals (standards) they value and their behavior will be judged as satisfactory by the organization.

We started by reviewing the literature in order to identify a valid measure that we could use to assess individual adaptation to IT-induced change. Having failed to identify any published or empirically validated measures for individual adaptation to job transitions, we proceeded to develop a theoretically based adaptation scale. We initially generated six items that correspond to the definition proposed by Ashford and Taylor [3], with IT-induced change as the specific job transition category of interest.

During the pretest procedure, which we describe in detail in the Independent Variables section (see below), we also evaluated how well these six items fit the definition provided on individual adaptation to IT-induced change (see Appendix A). Participants were the same as those who pretested the whole questionnaire. Following this procedure, we made wording changes and eliminated two items that were viewed as irrelevant or redundant according to our discussion with respondents. The resulting seven-point Likert-type scale had the following four items (translated from Spanish): (1) the employee has quickly become familiar with the new IT platform, (2) it was not difficult for the employee to adjust him- or herself to the new IT platform, (3) the employee has skillfully used the tools and applications the new IT platform provides, and (4) the employee has accurately managed all the facilities the new IT platform provides. We used several strategies to assess the content, predictive, and convergent validity of this new adaptation scale. To assess content validity, we implemented a pilot study, described below, following the research of Lankau and Scandura [48] and Schriesheim et al. [73].

Pilot Sample. Ninety-two Academy of Management association members who were working in universities and colleges in Australia, Canada, Germany, Greece, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, South Africa, South Korea, Spain, and the United States were invited to participate in a Web-based survey aiming to test the content validity of the proposed scale. Participants’ names and e-mails were randomly selected from among the 225 members of the Academy’s Technology and Innovation Management Division and the Research Methods Division using the directories of the respective university Web page. An e-mail message was sent to 92 researchers asking them to participate in the pilot sample and 31 of them provided full responses (a 33.69 percent response rate).

The Web survey consisted of two sections: in the first section, respondents were provided with definitions for the adaptation to IT-induced change construct as well as definitions for two other constructs that might be related to adaptation—the use of the new IT platform [17] and individual in-role performance [85]. Then, participants were asked to indicate on a five-point Likert-type scale how well each of the listed 12 items fit with the definitions that were provided for each of the three constructs (measures). The four adaptation items were intercalated among the other eight items, which were previously used [17, 85] to measure the two related constructs, namely technology use and in-role performance. Technology use was measured with the following four items [17]: (1) the employee has been using the new technology platform outside of ordinary working hours, (2) the employee has been using the new technology platform for a long time every day, (3) the employee has been frequently using the new technology platform, and (4) the employee has been using the new technology platform outside of working days. To measure in-role work performance, we used the following four items [85]: (1) the employee has been meeting formal performance requirements of the job, (2) the employee has been adequately completing assigned duties, (3) the employee has been performing tasks that are expected of him or her, and (4) the employee has been fulfilling responsibilities specified in his or her job description.

In the second section, respondents were asked to place an “X” in one of four columns in order to categorize each item as measuring either (1) adaptation, (2) IT use, (3) in-role performance, or (4) none of these constructs. The items were presented on the Web survey in the same order as in the previous section. Results were consolidated into a data matrix following the procedure of Schriesheim et al. [73]. Pearson correlations among the constructs in two randomly selected subsamples were calculated, obtaining results that were highly significant and comparable to previous research (r = 0.728, 0.855, and 0.985, respectively, for adaptation to change, IT use, and in-role performance; $p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ [67].

Next, a factor analysis was conducted on all 12 items, using a criterion of eigenvalue greater than one [48]. Results of the varimax rotated components indicated two factors accounting for 100 percent of the total variance (see Appendix B). Use of IT items loaded together on the first factor with positive and high loadings $( > 0 . 8 )$ , while adaptation items loaded on the second factor (loadings > 0.8) [25]. In-role performance had negative or zero-order loadings for both factors. These results give support for construct validity: since factor analysis reveals two related yet substantially different constructs, IT use and individual adaptation, this indicates convergent validity. The result that in-role performance is not related to either, a conclusion that fits with the theoretical definition of the three constructs, indicates discriminant validity. Complete factor analysis results are available from the authors on request.

After conducting pilot samplings, we asked departmental directors and B instructors to evaluate individual adaptation to the IT-induced change the firm faced between 2002 and 2004. To help respondents identify the IT-induced change, a brief description of the change was presented to departmental directors at the beginning of the evaluation form. To provide a single measure of adaptation outcome, the four-item average was calculated. Reliability was measured using intrarater as well as interrater reliability. Intrarater reliability was assessed through Cronbach’s alpha $( \propto = 0 . 9 5 )$ . Interrater reliability was assessed by comparing the measures provided by departmental directors and B instructors. Correlation between the four-item average for B instructors and departmental directors was 0.44 $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . These results are similar to (or higher than) previous research using a similar interrater reliability measure [57, 67]. We also performed a confirmatory factor analysis using EQS, specifying one single higher-order factor that yielded a good fit to the data (e.g., [33]) (goodness-of-fit index [GFI] = 0.95; adjusted goodness-of-fit index $[ \mathrm { A G F I } ] = 0 . 9 0 $ ; normed fit index $[ \mathrm { N F I } ] = 0 . 9 8$ ; nonnormed fit index $[ \mathrm { N N F I } ] = 0 . 9 4$ ; comparative fit index $[ \mathrm { C F I } ] = 0 . 9 8 $ χ<sup>2</sup>/degrees of freedom $[ \mathrm { d f } ] = 2 . 8 6 $ ; all factorial loadings > 0.80 and significant with $p < 0 . 0 1 ; R ^ { 2 } > 0 . 7 5 )$

## Independent Variables

To measure network attributes (size, density, and strength), we drew upon existing scales [41, 42, 58]. After translating the instrument from English to Spanish, we pretested the questionnaire in order to verify the clarity of instructions, the amount of time required to complete the measures, and the psychometric properties of the scales of the Spanish version. This pretest took place between September and October 2004 and involved surveying three company employees and one B instructor as well as two employees of other financial firms. Following the pretest, we interviewed the participants to obtain detailed feedback on their reactions to the survey instruments. Based on the feedback obtained during the pretest phase, we reworded some items as needed. Finally, we assessed the revised scale for stability using a test-retest procedure (see [55] for an extensive review on this issue). A total of 30 participants (26 master’s degree students and four university researchers) filled out the questionnaire with a two-week gap between test and retest, achieving a satisfactory level of reliability, consistent with previous research (Spearman’s rho > 0.8).

We followed a standard two-step name generator/interpreter methodology to elicit and characterize respondents’ (egos’) relationships [75] with others (alters). To assess supportive networks, respondents were asked to give the initials of people “that you know you can count on, who are dependable in hard times” [41]. The question used for the informational network referred to “people who have been valuable sources of information for you on job-related subjects” [41, 58].

Because we were assessing network connections inside and outside the workplace, we emphasized during the interview that the people listed did not necessarily have to be members of the organization [18]. There were no initial limitations on the number of initials to be elicited. Following a procedure employed in similar research designs, additional data were collected regarding only the first five persons (alters) for each network [14, 58]. We asked the respondent to place each initial in a cell of a table with two columns. In these columns, respondents indicated the strength of the relationship and a measure of density (see Appendix C).

Size of Supportive Network. We measured size of the supportive network as the number of alters listed in this network [10, 58]. The average number of alters named was 4.43. However, most interviewees named less than five initials (84.4 percent).

Density of Informational Network. Following similar procedures to those used in other studies of social networks [42, 58], we asked members to indicate the number of links between the network members (see the last column of the tables in Appendix C). Density was measured as the sum of the actual links between the members of the network (excluding ego) relative to the total number of possible links. The formula used was $\Sigma T _ { \scriptscriptstyle i } / n ( n - 1 )$ , where T is the number of links from alter j and n is the total number of alters identified [49, 67].

Strength of Informational Network. We computed strength of the informational network by averaging responses to the question about an ego’s degree of closeness to each alter [41, 58] using a seven-point Likert scale (“My relationship with this person is very close”: 1 = totally disagree; 7 = totally agree).

Age. Member’s age at the time he or she was surveyed.

Education Level. Education level was measured using a four-point classification: 1 = no schooling, 2 = primary school, 3 = secondary school, 4 = university degree.

IT-Related Education. IT-related education was measured using a dichotomous scale (0 = no formal IT studies, 1 = engaged in formal IT studies).

Work Team Size. Work team size was measured using the following item: “How many members are there in the work team you belong to.”

The results of this study throw light on the role of social networks in individual adaptation to IT-induced change. Specifically, we found that the size of the supportive network has a positive effect on individual adaptation to IT-induced change (Hypothesis 1). Likewise, we found that both strength and density of the informational network are significant predictors of individual adaptation to IT-induced change (Hypotheses 2a and 2b). The complete model accounted for a total of 22 percent of the variance out of which about half, 11 percent of total variance, was explained by social networks variables (supportive network size, informational network density, and strength). These results are comparable to the results reported in other relevant research when analyzing the effects of sociological or psychological factors on individual outcomes (e.g, [68]).

## Discussion and Conclusions

Instead of focusing on organizational coping mechanism during IT-induced change [8], this study sheds new light on individual adaptation to IT from a user perspective, which should prove useful for both theory development and managerial practice. This study should help to understand better that the factors that contribute to individual adaptation to IT-induced change cannot be only explained by individual learning ability or technological mastery, but that they are also anchored in how members interact in their social networks. Although this idea appears implicitly in some well-established theoretical models, such as Bandura’s [4, 83] social cognitive theory, the theory of reasoned action [23], and the theory of planned behavior [1], there has been a need to empirically test the implications of these theoretical models in the context of social networks and their impact on individual adaptation to an IT-induced change.

This study also introduces a new outcome variable—individual adaptation to ITinduced change—which has not been previously examined as a dependent variable in social networks or in IT management literature. A further contribution is that, contrary to the types of social networks that were typically studied in past research (complete, bounded networks), the networks analyzed here are egocentric [55] and include social ties inside as well as outside the workplace [14, 81]. Study of social ties inside and outside the firm, especially in the case of supportive networks, has several advantages such as a broader coverage of personal relationships and improved generalizability of findings [14].

An additional methodological contribution of the present study rests in our choice of operationalizing and measuring the dependent variable. Many previous studies of social psychological factors affecting employees’ adaptation employed self-report measures and several researchers called for more studies employing behavioral, rather than attitudinal, measures. We have addressed this concern by using a design that assessed employees’ adaptation using externally rated (i.e., by instructors and departmental directors) indicators of members behaviors at work rather than attitudinal (self-report) measures [2, 3]. A clear advantage of such a design is that it eliminates worries regarding common-method bias, common to designs where both the dependent variable and independent variables are based on self-reported data.

To further advance and facilitate the use of managerial evaluations to measure individual adaptation to IT-induced change, we constructed and successfully tested a new scale for measuring how well employees adapt following such changes. The scale, which demonstrated good reliability and validity properties allows future research to build on and extend the findings of the present study and should prove useful for researchers interested in assessing adaptation to IT-induced change.

Our results indicate that the size of the supportive network is related to better individual adaptation following an IT-induced change. Social support may serve as a buffer against the undesired psychological outcomes experienced during change and thus improve adaptation. Thus, individuals with a larger supportive network are better able to cope with potentially overloading and uncertain events, such as a pervasive, IT-induced change. A person who feels supported by his or her social network may experience less anxiety and stress, less likelihood of suffering depression, and a higher self-efficacy when addressing professionally challenging situations. Supportive networks deliver the positive effects of expressive (emotional) networks by affecting deep-rooted psychological mechanisms (increased coping skills, reduced anxiety, and depression) involved in complex processes such as adaptation [74].

Our results also provide some interesting clues about the role that the informational network plays in individual adaptation to IT-induced change. First, our findings point out that an informational network made up of strong ties facilitates the exchange of information (and knowledge) among individuals in the network. This improved flux of information and knowledge within the network can subsequently foster individual adaptation to the IT-induced change.

Second, we can tentatively conclude that, in contrast to what is suggested in some early papers on social networks (e.g., [33]), dense networks are actually more effective for individual adaptation to an IT-induced change. This effect may be explained by the fact that during an IT-induced change, individuals may benefit more from a high-density informational network than from a low-density informational network. A dense informational network may be more effective if the members use it as a tool to resolve doubts, obtain opinions, and deepen their understanding about the new system. In fact, the informational network may arise as a means for better understanding the new changes rather than just as a source for routine information.

## Managerial Implications

In a world where IT-induced change is a major source of concern for many organizations, managers should pay attention to context variables that promote a better fit between IT and organization members. There are also some relevant implications for HR management as well as for IT-induced change management. There have been few studies so far that applied social network perspective to the analysis of HR development and management [9]. Some authors [39] point out that network analysis can add empirical rigor to diverse areas such as organizational change and HR training and development. However, no prior research has analyzed the impact of the individual social network on recruiting, selection, and training practices. In light of our results, it would be interesting to consider the structure of individuals’ social network (mainly the supportive network) during staff selection. Some indirect effects of a large and dense social network can be estimated using new selection techniques (usually based on theoretical frameworks such as emotional intelligence) that identify psychological traits such as stability and extroversion, which serve as proxy to members’ potential social network. However, the positive effects of the size of the supportive network and strength and density of the informational network have not been directly included in the current corpus of HR management tools, especially when the firm is planning an in-depth, pervasive, and major IT-induced change.

Once the individual has been hired, managers should promote not only formal communication of information and technical training but also informal sharing of knowledge and expertise among organization members. In light of the analyses discussed here, creating and promoting dense groups of workers may be a good policy to foster individual adaptation to IT-induced change. To achieve this objective, managers should promote confidence among group members, backing constructive behaviors and encouraging organizational citizenship behaviors by combining individual-level formal and tangible rewards with informal and group-level rewards (e.g., [44]). In the same sense, a policy of building cohesive work teams in the workplace [21] can foster the development of new skills, behaviors, and attitudes that would eventually facilitate individual adaptation.

Our results also suggest that when engaging in job design and work organization, members’ social networks should be regarded as another professional asset or as a human capital dimension. In the case of the supportive social network, our results suggest that managerial practices that support and enable life–work balance may at the same time also contribute to maintaining high individual organizational performance in the long run.

Finally, in relation to individual demographic variables that may influence individual adaptation, our results indicate that HR managers should look for age in order to ensure a higher level of individual assimilation of new IT-related practices and routines.

## Limitations and Future Directions

While considering the implications of our findings, we should bear in mind possible limitations in our research project. In this last section of our paper, we present the fruits of our reflections on the design of our study, which include acknowledgment of some of its limitations and, at the same time, pointing to interesting future research directions.

The first limitation has to do with the cross-sectional character of our study. Although the objectives of the investigation have been fulfilled with a cross-sectional approach, a longitudinal research design might have broadened the number of dimensions analyzed, providing a more complete picture of the adaptation process, and not only of the adaptation. Further research may provide new interesting insights regarding the mechanisms that explain the whole adaptation process before, during, and after the change has been completed, and how social variables influence such a process.

The second limitation of this work is that we did not include some important factors that affect members’ adaptation. We focused on two types of informal information sources, delivered through social networks. However, in most instances where an ITinduced change takes place, organizations provide systematic (or “formal”) instruction and support to help users adapt to the new technology. Although we did not capture this source of learning in our current study, we do acknowledge that formal training should improve adaptation of users to the new system and, if effective, it should also increase self-efficacy and improved coping. We also did not analyze other social network effects, such as the advice network effects on individual adaptation (e.g., [69]). Based on our conceptual analysis, we suggest that future studies examine, simultaneously, the role of both formal and informal support systems in improving individual adaptation. Such a design will allow teasing out the relative contributions of both formal company instruction compared to the contributions of social networks.

The third limitation is that while we have posited and argued that self-efficacy and coping are instrumental for effective adaptation and that these psychological mechanisms would be positively affected by supportive and informational social networks, we did not assess them explicitly in our study. It would be beneficial if future studies explicitly test the proposed role of self-efficacy and coping as mediating the effects of social networks on adaptation.

Moreover, in future studies, it would be interesting to investigate several other relevant organizational factors such as leadership style and self-monitoring [56] in order to provide a more complete picture of the role that individual and group-level psychological processes play in mediating and moderating the relationship between social networks and individual adaptation to IT-induced change.

In our study, our departure point was that an IT-induced change triggers a process of sensemaking and leads to a need to learn and adapt to a new system, which in turn drives users to learn how to use the new IT system and engage with it—that is, adaptation. Clearly, members have to possess a need (be it intrinsic or extrinsic) to adapt to the new reality facing them (i.e., the new IT system introduced) before they are motivated to engage in adaptation activities. However, we know relatively little of the specific antecedents, nature, and consequences of the sensemaking process in such instances. For example, future studies could examine whether certain members experience a stronger need to make sense and whether such members especially benefit from access to certain type of social networks.

Understanding that individuals’ social supportive or informational connections affect individual outcomes in relation to IT-induced change is likely to strengthen management information systems research. Our social framework of individual adaptation has the potential to generate empirical inquiry that goes beyond analyzing the direct effects between social networks and technology, thus investigating how the social effects on adaptation to an IT-induced change arise and can be affected by other individual or organizational factors. In addition to serving as a reliable analysis of social effects on adaptation to change, this study also provides a platform enabling managers to take into account the workers’ social networks while managing a technochange. To sum up, this study, the first to investigate social networks influences on individual adaptation to IT-induced change, moves research on information systems and adaptation to ITinduced change forward by enhancing the understanding of social networks’ effects on technology management.

We are trying to determine what specific items are most related to the variable: individual adaptation effectiveness to IT-induced change. Individual adaptation effectiveness has been defined by Ashford and Taylor as “a set of behavioral routines and habits, reorganized in the individual’s best attempt to better fit the environment’s demands as he or she has negotiated them. If adaptation is effective these routines will keep individuals on track in the new environment with respect to the goals they value and they will be judged as satisfactory by the organization” [3, p. 27].

Two other variables that may be related to this construct are general in-role performance, defined as: “behaviors that are recognized by formal rewards systems and are part of the requirements as described in job descriptions” [85, p. 606], and use of IT, defined as “duration and frequency of use of technology” [17, p. 150].
