Because of the important role being played by social networks, many organizations are turning to the use of social network systems to help manage these social networks and the accompanying social capital. When a social networking system is implemented in an organization, it may serve as a signal to organizational actors that social capital, heretofore largely ignored and invisible, will hereafter represent an important resource for the organization. In a case study of two organizations in the process of adopting an SAP-based social networking system, our research examines how groups of actors develop strategies to control the social networking system as well as the symbolic capital that emerges during the adoption of the system.

## Introduction

Cross-functional, short-term, ad-hoc project teams form the backbone of modern organizational work practices. Participation in various project teams provides an individual with a cornucopia of internal organizational relationships forming an informal structure often referred to as a social network. The reliance upon these social networks to accomplish work tasks has become so extensive (Cross and Parker, 2004) that official organizational charts rarely reflect the actual relationships that individuals have with others in the organization. These social networks provide individuals access to resources – other individuals and their knowledge – that might otherwise be hard to find. They also provide individuals the ability to accrue advantages such as power (Brass, 1984), leadership (Sparrowe and Liden, 1997), mobility (Boxman et al., 1991), employability (Fernandez et al., 2000), performance (Burton et al., 2010) or learning (Borgatti et al., 1998). The totality of the resources embedded in an individual’s social network constitutes the individual’s social capital (Coleman, 1988; Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). Not only does social capital provide value to individuals, but it has been found to be an important enabler of collective action in organizations (Lazega, 2006).

Because of the important role being played by social networks, many organizations are turning to the use of social network systems to help manage these social networks and the accompanying social capital. Organizational Social Network Systems (henceforth, SNS) are designed to bring visibility to the informal relationships in an organization, to support crossfunctional collaboration (Cross et al., 2002), to identify relations between individuals and to provide a medium for informal interactions unbounded by hierarchy constraints. Similar to their predecessors in the consumer world, such as Myspace and

Facebook, organizational SNS promote networking and the benefits it can bring: SNS can improve employee productivity through the intensification of interactions, (Sparrowe et al., 2001), can improve the socialization effectiveness of new employees (Gonzalez et al., 2013), and can facilitate new relationships internally and externally (Deans, 2011; McAfee, 2009; DiMicco et al., 2008; Majchrzak et al., 2009). According to McKinsey, the economic impact of social media on business could exceed \$1 trillion, mainly resulting from more efficient communication and collaboration within and across organizations (Chui et al., 2012) (in Kane et al., 2014, p. 1). In addition to these gains in productivity and networking, SNS are being implemented because of their ability to generate collective intelligence, to facilitate collaborative work (Cross et al., 2002) and to foster innovation (Bradley and McDonald, 2011). As such, SNS are an important component of an organization’s IT portofolio and strategy. Decisions about how to use SNS, as well as appropriate SNS policies, are important for CIOs (Leidner et al., 2010).

Even as SNS provide a graphical visualization of an individual’s social network, they concomitantly increase the potential symbolic power of the social network: not only does the individual’s network provide access to knowledge and people, the network may now also provide symbolic value. Hence, when a SNS tool is implemented in an organization, it may serve as a signal to organizational actors that social capital, heretofore largely ignored and invisible, will hereafter represent an important resource for the organization (Feldman and March, 1981). This may then motivate organizational actors to develop strategies to control this newly visible capital in such a way as to increase or redistribute their informal power (Brass and Burkhardt, 1990) and their social prestige (Bourdieu, 1994, 2000) by actively forming and displaying their relationships in the system. Using a case study of two organizations, our study draws upon the concepts of Information as Signal and Symbol (Feldman and March, 1981) and Symbolic Capital (Bourdieu, 1979) to explore how groups of actors develop strategies to control their symbolic capital in response to their organization’s adoption of SNS. Strategic use of IS represents an issue that has been heavily studied in the IS literature through the last 20 years (Gable, 2010). Focusing on ‘‘individual-based IS strategies’’ where individuals may define ‘‘how to use the information and systems provided by the organization to carry out and develop their roles’’ (Ward, 2012, p. 168), our study treats a new emergent SIS tendency in the context of SNS.

This paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide the theoretical foundations of this research. Then, we detail the case-study methodology employed, the data analysis method used in this study and the two case sites. Next we present the within-case and cross-case analyses. In a final section, we discuss the implications, limitations and recommendations for future research.

## Theoretical foundation

## Information visibility and information as signal and symbol

The notion of information as signal and symbol developed by Feldman and March (1981) emphasizes the symbolic dimension surrounding the deployment and use of information in organizations. In this view, it is the visibility of information itself moreso than the content of information, that drives information use and perceptions. Farly research considered the willful act of making information use visible to others in order to provide a signal of legitimate managerial and decision making practices (Feldman and March, 1981). This line of work indicates that individuals will often project an image of using computer-based information in order to convince others of their managerial competence regardless of whether, or not, they actually need the information to guide their actions. Research has found that managers often make intuitive decisions and then search for information to support their decision, in order to provide legitimacy for their decision making process (Simon, 1977). Recent work on knowledge mapping systems suggests that human resource managers may use the knowledge mapping systems to project an image of control over the organization’s human resources, even if they themselves recognize the questionable accuracy of the information contained in the system (Dudezert and Leidner, 2011). In this case, the real value of the system was not in improving managerial decisions or actions, but in signaling HR’s efforts to manage human resources by making the information visible.

Even while some managers may willfully choose to make information visible in order to signal managerial effectiveness or increase their own power base, others may retract from such visibility, feeling a loss of power when information about their knowledge or work becomes overly visible to individuals higher up in the hierarchy. Such was the case for nurses, as eloquently described in Bowker (1997), many of whom felt that some of their most important roles – bringing a sense of hope to patients and relieving patient stress with humor – were not accounted for in a system designed to make visible the nurses’ everyday activities. Likewise, organizational librarians feel a sense of power loss when systems take over their customary role of tracking down, filtering and passing along information to managers (Schultze, 2000). These works and others underscore the political issues that surface as hidden information becomes visible (Star and Bowker, 1995; Star and Strauss, 1999). To the extent that information is identified as a political tool (Allen et al., 1979), it may sometimes be deliberately withheld from others (Suchman. 1995) and other times. deliberately shared to serve some personal motive (Wasko and Faraj, 2000). In both cases, the information itself holds symbolic value for the owner of the information who seeks to control its dissemination in order to achieve the desired signal. To further understand the symbolic value of information, we next turn to Bourdieu’s work on Symbolic Capital. Other theories – such as institutional theory (DiMaggio and Powell, 1983; Meyer and Rowan, 1977), Bourdieu’s theory of practice (Bourdieu, 1977), structuration theory (Giddens, 1976), and various theories on power and politics (Jasperson et al., 2002) could offer insights into the results of information visibility.

## Theory of social space

In his theory of Social Space (1979) articulated in his work, La Distinction: critique sociale du jugement, among others, Bourdieu places an important emphasis on forms of capital and in particular, symbolic capital. For Bourdieu, each actor belongs to a social field. A social field is a place of competition for position and struggle for power. In order to maintain their social position in their social field, actors must possess capital. Power accrues to those who are able to gain and control capital in a social field. There are different types of capital in organizations (Bourdieu, 1994): economic, cultural and social. Economic capital refers to material resources. Cultural capital is related to knowledge, individual know-how, skills gained from school and different cultural resources (books, paintings, etc.). Social capital corresponds to the resources embedded in the network of personal relationships. Each individual belongs to different social fields where he assumes a different social position depending on the resources and the type of the capital held. In the intellectual field, for example, it is more important to possess cultural capital than economic capital whereas in a business field, economic capital is valued over cultural capital. Because our work concerns social capital and its conversion to symbolic capital as information about social capital becomes visible, we describe it in more detail.

## Social capital

Social capital is a capital developed by social links between actors. It is embedded in an interconnected network of individuals (Borgatti et al., 1998; Putnam, 1995, 2001), forming a group that is well suited for the development of trustwor thiness values (Coleman, 1988), individual well-being (Putnam, 1995) and reciprocity in terms of resources exchange (Bourdieu, 1985). As a result, the group can function as a resource for the individual who is part of that group; likewise, the individual provides a resource to the group such that collective ends can be easily achieved (Coleman, 1988) Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) propose a clarification of the social capital concept when they conceptualized it as a multidimensional construct with three distinct and complementary dimensions: structural, cognitive, and relational. Structural social capital refers to the connections, relationships and hierarchies the network forms (who is related to whom? And how?). Actors need to accumulate and to develop these links to extend their social capital (Hazleton and Kennan, 2000) The structural dimension of the social capital plays a significant role in deriving benefits from social capital resources, such as knowledge or emotional support for example (Lazega, 2006). According to Tsai and Ghoshal (1998), an individual’s position in the organization such as network centrality, gives him the ability to leverage resources embedded in those social relationships. When these structural relationships are dense and frequent, trust is more easily established among actors and the actors become more willing to share common norms and values (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998). Relationships of trust and trustworthiness play a crucial role in facilitating collective action (Burt, 1992; Putnam, 1995) and in improving the organizational innovation process (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998). The relational dimension of social capital refers to the quality of these interpersonal links in terms of values and obligations (McFadyen and Cannella, 2004), of trust (Coleman, 1988) and of trustworthiness (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998). The cognitive dimension refers to the sharing of goals and vision (Tsai and Ghoshal, 1998) as well as a common understanding of the context of work using common language and codes (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). This cognitive dimension of the social capital allows organizational members to create and share knowledge. Social capital has historically been less visible than either cultural capital or economic capital. However, the introduction of social media such as social networking systems provides greater transparency to social capital. As a result, social capital can become symbolic capital.

## Symbolic capital

Symbolic capital is any form of capital that has taken on intangible value independent of the real value of the capital per se (Bourdieu, 1998). However it is not enough that the possessor of the capital views it as inherently valuable or that the capital has real value, others who perceive the individual with the capital must also view it as valuable. Some examples of symbolic capital include diplomas from a prestigious universities, medals of honor, designer clothing, or certain zip codes, streets, or neighborhoods. Symbolic capital emerges through a process of social construction in which other forms of capital (economic, cultural and social) undergo a process of conversion to symbolic capital whereby they are perceived as valuable and socially recognized as legitimate (Bourdieu, 1989). For Everett (2002, p. 63) ‘‘the other forms of capital are converted to symbolic capital the instant they are deemed legitimate and it is only in the form of symbolic capital that the ultimate base of power – wealth – can exert power and exert it durably’’. This refers to the processes of sensemaking and sensegiving (Weick, 1995) that occur as people involved in sensegiving processes attempt to influence sensemaking processes where the interpretation and meanings of organizational context and activities are socially constructed. For example, some researchers show that top management such as senior managers (Maitlis and Lawrence, 2007) or university presidents (Gioia and Chittipeddi, 1991) may use diverse sensegiving techniques to construct a meaningful framework about specific situations in the organization. In the context of a social field, it means that the accumulation of symbolic capital shapes other actors perceptions of the ‘‘natural’’ state of social order. This then creates legitimacy for those holding power and domination in the social field in the sense that this social domination and influence is seen by the other actors of the field as ‘‘natural’ ‘‘acceptable’’ and ‘‘legitimate’’. The recognition of symbolic capital requires a shared understanding and shared meaning by other actors of what constitutes value and therefore what is considered legitimate, valid and useful. While the possession and control of a symbolic capital becomes a symbol of the individual’s power and status, symbolic capital may also be a limited resource in which particular groups that are unable to acquire symbolic capital are excluded (Doherty and Dickmann, 2009). Doherty and Dickmann (p. 303) cite as an example the symbolic capital held within their organizations by individuals who participate in graduate or fast track recruitment schemes used for the development of the future top management. Actors will vie for those resources that confer symbolic capital even if the resources do not provide economic value or a physical/cultural asset.

In social fields, social capital has generally been the least visible of the capital and hence, has had the least symbolic value. Yet the emergence of social media provides the capability to make social capital highly visible and the ability to send signals well beyond one’s immediate field and hence to confer symbolic value to social capital (Karoui and Dudezert, 2012). Bourdieu’s theory largely assumes that people wear, consume, and/or participate in symbolic activities such that others surrounding them observe them in such acts. However, with social media, an individual’s engagement with symbolic activities is displayed well beyond one’s immediate field. We next examine organizational SNS and the types of information potentially made visible by such systems.

## Organizational SNS

A recent development in social network analysis has been the development of social network analysis systems (SNS) to help organizations better understand, and manage, the internal relations of its employees. Based on graph theory (Angot and Josserand, 2003; Tichy et al., 1979), SNS map and display the informal social network of an organization, thereby making the informal interaction patterns between individuals visible. These informal exchanges are the core of organizational knowledge and expertise (Hansen, 1999). SNS thus allow individuals to learn who does what and who knows whom in the organization (see Fig. 1). Moreover. SNS are well suited for improving collaboration and knowledge sharing in an organization particularly in cross-functional projects (Cross et al., 2002) where work patterns can be displayed. Individuals can easily navigate through SNS interfaces to see who is working on which project and which departments are involved in cross-functional projects (see Fig. 2).

In addition to reinforcing existing relational links, SNS allow individuals to establish new social links. These new connections may increase individuals’ social capital in their organization (Ellison et al., 2007) and consequently, their power: Because the system makes connections visible to others in the organization, individuals located at the intersection of multiple interconnected nodes may accrue their power and prestige, and may have further opportunities to obtain rewarding benefits like control and information (Burt, 1992). The added visibility of the individual’s position in a network works to the favor of those that project a well-connected image. As a result, individuals may consciously manipulate the system to either increase their own social capital or decrease the value of others’ social capital. As such, SNS may be considered as an opportunity for actors to attempt to influence the growth and maintenance of their social network and to try to use their connections to demonstrate and increase their power base. Thus, our research question focuses on how groups of actors develop strategies to use SNS and control the symbolic capital that emerges during organizational SNS adoption.

## Method

In light of the exploratory character of our study and our desire to investigate a contemporary phenomenon within a real-life context (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1994), we opted for case-study methodology. In order to expand and generalize theories (analytical generalization) as opposed to provide statistical generalization, we aim to use the cases to discover the relevant features, factors or issues that might apply in other similar situations (Myers, 2009). Our study compares and contrasts the SNS adoption of two public sector administrative offices in France (see Table 2). The selection of our two cases was guided by the principle of theoretical replication (Yin. 1994): in the first case, we encountered organizational actors that readily acknowledged, and embraced, the value that social capital visibility could bring to the organization whereas in the second, we found an organization whose actors treated social capital visibility with scepticism and anxiety.

Given our desire to study users' reactions to the potential of SNS to alter the meaning attached to their social capital and how these reactions might influence the subsequent implementation, as opposed to studying the impact of the system once fully implemented in organizations, we chose to collect data at the earliest point of adoption. A new system adoption process begins once actors are informed of the new system deployment project (Larif and Lesobre, 2004; Beaudry and Pinsonneault, 2005). Once employees in an organization become aware of their organization’s consideration of a new system, they start to develop their initial perceptions of the change the new system will bring (Herold et al., 1995, p. 158). These perceptions of the new technology developed during the pre-implementation phase impact the two other phases (implementation and post-implementation phases) and may influence actors’ behaviors toward the new technology during both of these phases (Hartwick and Barki, 1994; Karahanna et al., 1999; Muhlmann, 2001). ## The SNS context

The SNS in our study is a system developed by SAP. The system allows individuals to create links with others, to display formal (hierarchical) and informal links, and to send invitations to other employees to link. The system also enables a wide variety of information to be managed. It can manage photos, data and links (URL). For example, the SNS may show photos of the collaborators on a project as well as their contact information, competence, and collaboration activities and responsibilities. It also enables statistical analysis to create reports on information implemented in the SNS. For instance, one can create charts to display how many people are involved across projects. This system has been used internally at SAP but had not yet been tested in outside organizations prior to our study. Some screenshots of this SNS were presented in Figs. 1 and 2.

## Research cases context

Our two cases are public sector administrative offices in France. It is important to consider some fundamental elements unique to the French public administration sector that might hinder or abet the SNS implementation process. In the public administration context, there exists a trivariate power structure among the Mayors, who are elected, the public administrators who are chosen by the Mayor and the public servants who are essentially tenured in office. The Mayor is elected by citizens but relies on public administrators in the office to organize and effectuate collective action. Although the Mayor has decision making authority over major initiatives, the public administrators also dispose of a power of influence over the Mayor by virtue of their role as guarantor for the legality of the mayoral decisions. Because the public administrators are not elected, but rather are chosen and employed by the Mayor, there is a co-dependency power between the Mayor and the public administrators. In contrast to the public administrators who are the department heads and are chosen by the Mayor, the public servants working in the departments are non-elected and hold their positions for life. These latter employees sometimes feel free to refuse hierarchical order and to complain to the elected representatives. As electors them selves, they thereby wield a certain power themselves over the Mayor and his/her public administrators. The public servants typically view the role of public office as building consensus for collective action to serve the interests of citizens. In contrast, the Mayor and public administrators typically view their role as managing the collective action of the office in such a way as to ensure the efficiency and productivity of the public servants. Finally, there is also a certain external power in that the mayors, public administrators, and public servants all work under specific constraints related to the transparency of actions toward citizens and a heavy traceability of procedures. While transparency of action and procedure is expected, at the same time, the public employees are expected to not communicate any information about their work to those outside their department. All these constraints present a barrier to cross-functional collaboration and are important to bear in mind as we describe the cases.

## Data collection

Data were collected via semi-structured interviews in the mayoral offices of two cities in France (see Table 2). We chose these two organizations because both were in the midst of an SNS adoption. The first one, Northern Suburb, did not yet have an SNS but was in the process of evaluating the appropriateness of SNS. Our research in Northern Suburb involved assessing the current perceptions of various stakeholders regarding the potential value an SNS might bring to the office. Our research was supported by the Head of the mayoral office (Directeur Général des Services) and the Head of Human Resource Department. Over the course of 5 months (from April to August 2011), 22 interviews were conducted with individuals from different hierarchical positions and different functional areas. Our sample was heterogeneous in terms of hierarchical levels, familiarities with social media technologies and department affiliation. Some screen shots of SNS technology were shown to each interviewee to ensure that our use of the term SNS was consistent with what the individual was visualizing when we discussed with them their perceptions of SNS.

The second city, which we refer to as Sommerville, was in the process of prototyping an SNS. Our research in Sommerville was part of a project financed by the French government. The objective of the project was to provide French Public Administration with the latest technology in web 2.0. Following the development of a prototype, an SNS tool was presented to a steering committee<sup>1</sup> in charge of the SNS implementation. Forty organizational actors,<sup>2</sup> who were selected by the steering committee members, used the SNS during the 3-month test phase during which time we conducted in-depth interviews with 16 users (from October to December 2011). The sample is heterogeneous with respect to administrative responsibility:

we interviewed directors, department heads, director assistants, and administrative assistants. Data triangulation was achieved in both organizations by attending several steering committee meetings as observers and by participating in several meetings about the SNS with top executives involved in the project. Multiple stakeholders (from 4 to 10 actors) were present at these meetings and engaged in discussions over the merits of SNS.

The interview guide we followed is available from the authors (Appendix). In order to avoid elite bias (Miles and Huberman, 1994), we conducted interviews with the idea of ‘‘triangulation of subjects’’ (Rubin and Rubin, 2005), interviewing a variety of people representing diverse views on SNS implementation. Moreover we were able to evaluate actors perspectives on SNS implementation at two different stages: with Northern Suburb, we explore how actors perceive such a system implementation prior to using it whereas at Sommerville, we examine how actors react to such a tool after having used it. This enables us to contrast actors’ strategies in anticipation of an SNS as well as in reaction to an SNS. Interviews lasted on average one hour and each interview was recorded and fully transcribed, In total, we conducted 38 interviews across the two organizations and participated in 4 steering committee meetings with top executives involved in the SNS project resulting in 171 pages of single-spaced transcripts and notes.

## Data analysis

Our data analysis entailed open, axial and selective coding using Nvivo to help organize the analysis. Through our interviews, concepts related to informants’ social capital were repeatedly identified. We aimed to identify whether actors are aware of the social capital that already exists in their organization and how SNS strategies emerge during pre-adoption (in the case of Northern) and adoption (in the case of Southern). By using the ‘‘open coding’’ technique (Corbin and Strauss, 1990), we constructed the initial themes according to the social capital dimensions: structural, relational, and cognitive (Nahapiet and Ghoshal, 1998). We also sought to identify in the transcripts the informants’ perceptions of SNS and any emergent strategies aimed at influencing the SNS implementation (refer to Table 1). This category converged on two subthemes, one being a strategy to use SNS for collaboration and cross functional projects and the other, a strategy to use SNS for control and monitoring. Axial coding involved looking for relationships across the categories. This coding led us first to develop a new aspect of the structural dimension of social capital – hierarchy based structural capital and non-hierarchy based structural capital. Second, it allowed us to highlight a new phenomenon that accompanies SNS introduction, that is, the emerging symbols from actors’ interpretations of the status quo. These symbols are related to the concepts of structure, control, supervision and transversality. Structure – allocation of responsibilities and entities’ organization – is the essence of control and supervision of employees’ activities (Weber, 1947; March and Simon, 1958) in contrast with transversality which supposes collaboration and interactions across entities and departments. This round of coding enabled us to establish connections between information visibility and the forms of social capital. During the selective coding phase, we returned to the data to ensure that all relationships were supported by more than one informant and examined the data in depth to determine patterns in the relationships, leading us to observe different groups in the organization and their different perspectives of social capital and of the SNS.

## Case 1: Northern Suburb mayoral office

## Within-case analysis

## Perspective of social capital at northern

Social capital in the Northern Suburb mayoral office takes on different meanings to different sets of organizational actors. Those who hold high-level positions and/or have a high degree of seniority tend to regard their own social capital as a personal asset that provides information/knowledge advantages to them. These actors are maintaining multiple connections with their colleagues in a high-level position from other departments. Their structural social capital is well-established and contributes to the expansion of their cognitive social capital because it helps keep them informed about what is happening in the organization. These actors have a keen understanding of organizational structure and have a mental overview of the organization because they know who is who and who does what: ‘‘Given our position, the cross-functional overview of who does what, obviously we have it.’’ (Director of Budget and Public Procurement). The director of the Sustainable Development department explained: ‘‘Every Tuesday, I have meetings with all the other directors. And thanks to these meetings, I am always informed about current affairs and all that happens in the workplace.’’. In other words, structural social capital based on hierarchical links allows actors to get an overview of their organization’s actions, plans, projects and vision. Actors seem aware that their positions enable them to maintain a large network of structural links that allow them to leverage resources. The director of the Cultural Center said: ‘‘I have been here for 30 years, I know everybody and everybody knows me, I have a good knowledge of all that happens in the organization. Hence information access is easier.’’ Not only are they able to leverage the resources that comprise their social capital, they are also keen providers of resources to others. Some of them are aware of their own centrality: ‘‘Agents don’t hesitate to contact me because they know that I know all the workings (formal and informal ones) of the organization.’’ (Cultural Center’s director) and of the benefits of their structural social capital.

Moreover, some actors have the privilege of maintaining various cross-functional responsibilities even if their hierarchical positions are intermediate or low. Aware of the benefits of cross-functional positions, actors seek to extend their non-hierarchy based structural social capital. In order to be assigned to cross-departmental tasks, actors need to be known by a large number of colleagues: ‘‘when the new documentation portal has been set up, I had arrived. Nobody knew me and it was so hard for me to get things . . . I then created a blog to foster interactions with others and give them information about the portal.’’ (Computer Scientist). In addition, the archivist said: ‘‘I would like there to be in the new tool, information about what we do in order to be better known . . . this may create new opportunities for cross-functional collaboration.’’ These actors realize that developing their structural social capital will in turn allow them to leverage various resources such as peers’ recognition (relational dimension of social capital), information on new projects and a better overview of ‘‘what happens’’ in the organization (cognitive dimension of social capital).

In contrast to the higher level, higher seniority and cross-departmental actors who view social capital in positive terms, other organizational actors, particularly those who are in isolated departments that do not interact with other departments, express regrets about the lack of links connecting them to other actors. For example, one informant explained, ‘‘in my work I’m really isolated because I’m a librarian . . . I don’t have any links with my coworkers, they never come to ask me for a help or for example for an article they need . . . No one keeps me informed about the Mayoral office’s news or when new projects are decided. . .’’. These actors appear to lack the structural and cognitive dimensions of social capital: being isolated reduces the chance for some to connect with others (structural social capital) and to build their cognitive social capital by acquiring an overview of the organizational vision, objectives and aspirations. However, even though they bemoan their lack of socia capital, they nevertheless regard social capital itself as valuable.

In spite of the perceived differences in existing levels of structural and cognitive social capital in Northern Suburb, the relational dimension of social capital is well established and shared by actors in the organization. This manifests itself through the spirit of cooperation that exists within the Mayoral office where actors consistently point to ‘‘a spirit of unity’’, a ‘‘working as a team attitude’’, ‘‘a good atmosphere’’ and a ‘‘spirit of collaboration between Department chiefs’’. Lower level actors claim to ‘‘not really have a boss: we work together’’ Even while feeling that they work alongside their hierarchical superiors, rather than for them, the lower level actors nevertheless recognize the importance of hierarchy and whatever autonomy they have to make suggestions or express their viewpoint must be done with “respect for hierarchical protocol". It is in fact the shared meanings about hierarchical restrictions, obligations and the degree of their autonomy in the organization that creates challenges for the idea of cross-functional collaboration. Cross-functional collaboration implies to the organizational actors a breaking of the hierarchical protocol and thus presents ‘‘a sensitive problematic for us because we remain very hierarchical.’’ In other words, actors are supportive of the creation of greater structural and cognitive social capital, but only to the extent that it does not diminish the organizational equilibrium and the value of relational social capital.

The potential introduction of SNS at Northern Suburb is envisioned as part of the Mayor’s dematerialization project. This project has two major objectives. One of the major objectives is to create transversality, by which is meant collaboration

The second major objective is to provide greater visibility to the city projects being done and the actors assigned to those projects. The first of these aims is largely oriented towards forming relationships (structural dimension of social capital) and reinforcing them (relational dimension of social capital) while the second is oriented more towards the sharing of objectives and vision (cognitive dimension of social cap ital). Because of the peaceful atmosphere of the workplace and shared values of trustworthiness (relational social capital), most actors appear supportive of the use of SNS to assist with the objectives of transversality and project visibility. Nevertheless they expressed markedly different objectives depending on actors’ social position. Social groups have emerged supporting different strategies with respect to the SNS. While the more powerful of the two groups is pushing to incorporate SNS into the existing way of doing things in order to reinforce the status quo, the less powerful group envisions using SNS to change the status quo.

The hierarchy and isolation of departments ensures that cognitive social capital is effectively controlled by those with hierarchy-based structural social capital and prevents departments from interfering in the affairs of other departments. Can the organization withstand a threat to this delicate balance of collegiality with constraints? Our informants appear open to new ways of working and new ways of collaborating across departments and partners: informants claim a desire ‘‘to develop a transversal collaboration among Departments’’ and to ‘‘enhance transversal missions and to create more links’’ but then express fear that it cannot happen because ‘‘transversality is far away from hierarchy foundations and there is a protocol to respect’’. This contradiction between the creation of relational social capital with existing hierarchy-based structural social capital is a central issue facing the top management of the Northern Suburb mayoral office. According to the Human Resources department head ‘‘Collaboration spirit is a new way of working for Directors and some Departments heads who are used to operating in a vertical logic structure . . . some Directors face some difficulties in switching to transversal rules’’. Those facing a difficult transition to transversality are calling for a hybrid model of collaboration, just at the intersection of the cross-functional work's foundations and hierarchy's restrictions, This hybrid model would mean that actors, who were invited to participate in a collaborative project, would first have to gain approval from their supervisor and, when asked for help from another department, would be expected to copy their superior on any exchanges. In other words, actors would only be allowed to develop new connections if they acted within the confines of the existing structural social capital. As one department head explained: ‘‘My members’ team are free to develop cross functional work practices but with respect to some rules. Here hierarchy is very important it is what I try to teach to my new collaborators. For instance, as their chief I have asked them to put me in copy to emails they send as I have to know about that.’’ Hence, this group of actors supports the visibility aspects of SNS but is reluctant to embrace the collaborative aspects for fear that it will disrupt their hierarchy-based structural social capital, e.g. the hierarchical protocol.

These actors at the top of the organization with hierarchy and seniority in their favor and with a well-established social capital have embraced the new tool for two main reasons. First, they have seen in SNS an opportunity to strengthen the context of trustworthiness by sustaining the development of cross-functional links among actors according to the hybrid model of collaboration. Second, they are attracted to the monitoring aspects of the new system. They see in the SNS introduction a powerful means to consolidate their well-established structural relationships and an efficient tool to provide them with a better visualization of important projects in the organization (information about new projects, who works with whom, and who works on what, etc.). The new tool can help them to better understand the organization’s workings and can consolidate their cognitive social capital. They see that SNS ‘‘will be an efficient means to manage all our projects’’, will ‘‘provide better visibility of projects’’ and will provide ‘‘better views of who is where in the organization’’. Integrating this information into the new tool would help to legitimate control and supervision: with SNS implementation, those actors would be legitimated to control all resources including informal resources like social capital, thereby increasing power and influence and reinforcing social position in the organization. In this way, SNS is a symbol of the legitimization of control and supervision of organizational activities.

In contrast, for those engaging with cross-functional responsibilities and those occupving the more operational and lower level administrative positions of the office, the introduction of SNS reflects the importance that the hierarchy confers to cross-functional collaboration. Those actors consider SNS as a symbol of the top hierarchy support for the development of transversal work practices. Thus, SNS would allow them to extend and develop their non-hierarchy-based structural social capital: ‘‘it is a means to reconfigure their interpersonal exchanges’’. It is a real opportunity for actors in this second group to change the status quo, to change their social positions and to get access to more organizational resources. They want to see SNS implemented as an efficient collaborative tool which would allow them to develop new non-hierarchy-based structural power in the organization. ## Within-case analysis

## Perspective of social capital at Sommerville

In the Sommerville Mayoral office, there is a discrepancy in how social capital is valued, depending both upon hierarchical level and upon the degree of cross-functional collaboration required to accomplish one’s work. Individuals in higher level positions, such as directors and department heads, possess a good knowledge of what their coworkers do and a global overview of their department’s structure. For them, knowledge about who is doing what and who knows what is essential to their own work. This knowledge enables them to find information that otherwise would be difficult to locate. As one department head said: ‘‘I know very well the individuals working here, it is at the heart of my work . . . For instance some information I have we cannot get it unless we know very well our coworkers because it isn’t mentioned anywhere’’. These actors are aware that social capital is a resource that is not shared by all the agents, as explained by another department head: ‘‘. . .I know very well the organizational structure but for actors who are executants or from a category C (secretaries), they are not at an intermediate hierarchical position so they don’t know anything about the structure and the organization of our Mayoral office.’’ These actors hierarchy–based structural social capital provides them a lot of information about organizational workings, objectives and vision (cognitive social capital). For these individuals in the higher levels of the hierarchy, social capital is a prestigious and distinctive resource. It separates them from those lower in the hierarchy and is a privilege of their position.

Social capital is also highly valued by actors who are not at a high hierarchical position but who are embedded in crossfunctional groups that allow them to work with different coworkers from other departments. Such people express that their social position is quite useful and allows them to be aware of organizational developments in the mayoral office: ‘‘I am working as a cross functional Executive Assistant which facilitates the development of ties with other Departments . . . in addition I’m referent for the access to administrative documents so that makes me more visible to the other actors from different Departments . . , if I was only doing secretariat tasks I would not be able to know all that happens in the other Departments." Additionally. another Director’s assistant explained: ‘‘I’m directly attached to the director so I have a global overview of the organization and links between different Directions . . , we have a lot of information about the organization." For these actors, non-hierarchy-based structural social capital is a prestigious asset that confers to them cognitive social capital in the form of knowledge about the various projects and initiatives underway at the Mayoral office.

Yet actors who are not in high hierarchical positions or in cross-functional positions experience a sense of estrangement in their social relationships. Such individuals feel disconnected from the other departments and distant toward the other actors. Interviewees blamed geographical dispersion for this estrangement: ‘‘It does not interest me to know the other agents because we are working in several dispersed sites and we do not know each other. We only know some individuals that we fre quently see them because they work near.’’ They have a limited structural social capital and consequently lack an overall view of their office and where they fit in.

In the Somerville Mayoral office, there is an atmosphere of competition among actors, an individualistic culture and a climate of mistrust: as explained by the head of a department, ‘‘We are about 2500 actors, 22 or 24 Directions in 90 dispersed offices. All these are sufficient ingredients to have little specialists scattered in the city and who will, over time, lose the sense of community and will consider their pairs more as competitors than as collaborators’’. The director of Corporate and Legal Affairs furthermore explained that given the lack of relational social capital in the Sommerville Mayoral office, actors may reject the tool: ‘‘It is possible that agents do not want the tool. They may adopt the new system only if they are sure that its purpose is the one that is displayed and without misunderstanding. This [SNS] would be accepted if it is implemented to help people work better together, for a better transmission of information and to help agents to be better in their work without any deviation of the fina purposes. Then, it is a matter of trust and trustworthiness that cannot be decreed. Today is this level of trust sufficiently mature? I’m not sure’’. This organizational context will obviously affect actors’ responses to the introduction of SNS.

## Reaction to social capital visibility in an SNS

Depending on their perspective of their existing social capital, different groups of actors reacted differently toward the introduction of SNS. Our analysis suggests five main groups of actors, summarized in Table 3, who supported different strategies toward SNS use.

The first group refers to actors with top hierarchy positions (Top Executives), –these actors think that their high-level positions already enable them to control their network of structural links (structural social capital), to deploy appropriate actions to maintain the desired relational climate in the office (relational social capital) and to inform them about what is happening in the organization (cognitive social capital). As such, they express reluctance to accept the SNS, perceiving it as a threat to the existing hierarchy. According to one top executive: ‘‘SNS is a cross functional tool that should fit hierarchy foundations. It does not and our organization is not mature enough for that. Some chief executives fear cross functional collaboration because it may decrease their authority . . . I’m favorable for such tools but I know the reality of hierarchical administrations and it is difficult to evolve that culture’’. For this group, SNS is the symbol of a new organizational structure, one that they are not ready to embrace. Hence, this first group rejects SNS as unnecessary.

Their SNS’ adoption strategy is to guide the implementation of SNS to monitoring aspects of budgets and equipment (e.g., information on economic capital).

Department heads benefit from non hierarchy-based structural social capital to reinforce their social domination in their units. These non-hierarchy structural links provide them with information about informal organizational patterns. At the same time, department heads are aware of the impact of visibility of certain information and fear inequity issues: for these managers, information about who knows who and equipment or budget information – the information the middle managers wish to monitor – is not useful and they fear that this information might be used as an element of comparison to the extent that it will benefit actors who already have greater social and economic capital. As one of them explained: ‘‘. . .It will be as on Facebook, benefits are for those who have the more social links with others . . . Many coworkers will report on SNS that they are working with all departments in order to show to their chief that they are indispensable’’. For the lower level management, SNS introduction is a symbol of the legitimization of control and supervision of informal resources related to coworkers. Their SNS’ adoption strategy is to guide the implementation of SNS to the monitoring aspects of human resources – e.g., who knows what rather than who knows who.

The fourth group is comprised of cross functional executive assistants, who are in charge of directors’ agendas and such transversal functions as writing the municipal council’s minutes, referents and trainers. This group sees SNS as less a way to improve existing cross-functional collaboration and more as an opportunity to develop new cross functional links (non-hierarchy-based structural capital) by obtaining better knowledge about their coworkers and the organization (cognitive social capital). For them, SNS brings visibility to the structural social capital that they already enjoy as part of cross-functional teams. This non-hierarchy based structural capital can provide a source of power to those lacking in hierarchy-based structural power.

While the fourth group of actors welcomed the visibility provided by the SNS, the last group fears inequity issues and a loss of power related to SNS implementation. This group is comprised of administrative assistants who hold neither hierarchy-based nor non-hierarchy-based structural capital and who typically supervise a team of 3 or 4 secretaries, some switchboard operators, and those in charge of the mail. These actors do not have a high value of social capital in the organization to the extent that they feel disconnected and isolated in their daily life at work. Having limited structural social capital, this group’s actors feel that the SNS is neither an interesting tool for them nor a tool over which they have any influence. From their perspective, the SNS would be a tool containing much information that is useful for only a few.

## Discussion

Having examined each case separately, our discussion will now compare and contrast the two cases. The two Mayoral offices share several important similarities regarding perspectives of the individual value of relational, structural and cognitive social capital and the introduction of SNS. They also differ on several factors that influence the introduction of SNS. ## Similarities

In both cases, actors appear aware of the symbolic capital the new system can confer. We also uncover that different groups associate different symbolic notions to the introduction of SNS depending on their structural, cognitive and relational social capital level. In both cases, upper management considers that they are in control of their structural, relational and cognitive social capital: symbolic adoption depends on actors’ social capital perception. But for upper managers at Sommerville, the SNS presents the symbol of a new organizational structure that has to be integrated; they prefer to reject SNS rather than to disturb the status quo. At Northern, upper managers are able to reconcile the systems’ potential within the existing structural constraints. At both mayoral offices, we pointed out that the social group with well-established hierarchy-based structural social capital (middle and lower level of management) considers SNS as a symbol of the legitimization for control and supervision. They therefore develop strategies such that the SNS can provide them with additional information for their cognitive social capital to help them better monitor the organizational formal (in case of middle management) and informal (in case of the lower level of management) resources.

The social group represented by those who hold some cross functional responsibilities (cross functional executive assistants) considers SNS as a symbol of top management support for the development of transversal work practices. These actors are not hierarchically high but enjoy large non hierarchy-based structural social capital. They want to develop their non-hierarchy based structural capital in order to gain organizational advantages. Developing the cross functional links represents an opportunity to ‘‘to have more facilities to be closer to other colleagues and to establish new links of collaboration’’. Actors’ strategies depend on SNS symbolic adoption. At both Northern Suburb and Sommerville, actors with a well established hierarchy-based structural social capital (top hierarchy), recognize the value of these links and appreciate the resources it brings (helpful information for their cognitive social capital); those who do not enjoy such hierarchy-based structural social capital desire to develop their cross-functional relationships to develop their cognitive social capital which may help them to change the status quo. Actors appear capable of distinguishing between social capital’s forms such that those with less recognize that they would like more and those with more, find themselves threatened at the prospect of others acquiring more. Thus, actors seem to anticipate that controlling this social capital allows reinforcing or developing social positions in the organization. In particular, in both cases, those who enjoy hierarchy-based structural social capital (middle management) seek to develop their influence and power in the organization through developing their cognitive social capital. These actors for example appreciate the visualization functionality of the SNS because it ‘‘allows sorting out informal things that we cannot see in daily life and pushes to deeply go through information.’’ Actors in both cases are seeking access to a maximum of information with this tool (information on social relationships, on projects, on human resources, on budget, on equipment) in order to reinforce their structural position

Through the use of the new system, those with well-established hierarchy-based structural social capital would gain access to relevant information that was previously inaccessible. Thus, they would be able to improve their control of the organizational activities and resources and to reinforce the status quo. For other actors, working in cross-functional teams becomes a symbolic capital to the extent that SNS provides them the means to reveal their non-hierarchy based social capital and to develop it. Organizational actors use SNS to reinforce their power base and anticipate that controlling this social capital will allow them to reinforce or develop their social positions in the Mayoral Office.

## Differences

While the individual value of social capital was recognized in both cases, there appear differences in the two cases regarding the recognized value of social capital to the organization. At Northern Suburb, relational social capital is well-established. Actors from different hierarchical levels enjoy working together in a climate of trust. Both the upper and lower levels of the organization were supportive of adopting more cross-functional work collaborations. To this end, it was recognized that social relationships were an important foundation to develop cross-functional work practices. At Northern Suburb, social capital had value towards obtaining the organizational goal of transversality, not merely towards individual goals. In this Mayoral office, organizational actors believe that SNS can be implemented within the hierarchical protocol and will not disrupt the existing status.

By contrast, at Sommerville, there is a lack of relational social capital. We noted a climate of competition among actors and a context of mistrust. We also found little evidence that actors were aware of the potential organizational value of social capital. Actors are notably threatened at losing power. To preserve their social positions. they wish to avoid the inclusion of any information that might advantage another group over themselves. Nor was top management at Sommerville supportive of the introduction of SNS. The actors at Sommerville were concerned with inequity issues posed by the SNS. They do not trust the existing hierarchy, and sought to reinforce their own positions. Some of these actors are middle managers who, prior to SNS, had a privileged position of collecting information about work practices from employees and reporting these to upper managers. Their power rests on the invisibility of the work practices that they monitor. In Northern Suburb, SNS was largely viewed as an opportunity even by groups with varying interests, whereas at Sommerville, SNS was as much a threat as an opportunity, with two groups outright rejecting SNS introduction and three groups wanting to control information flow.

Given the culture of mistrust at Sommerville, an additional social group (administrative assistants) views the SNS as a symbol of their exclusion. The differences in the symbols attached to the SNS by the different social groups suggest that the symbols attributed to SNS underscore the existing tension and conflict among actors across organizational levels, rendering the introduction SNS challenging. Indeed, the symbols attached to SNS appear tied to the dominant organizational cultures: at Northern Suburb, the hierarchy is softened by a culture that values consensus and public service whereas at Sommerville, the hierarchy is further hardened by a culture fraught with power struggles and dominated by individual, as opposed to public service, aspirations. When companies implement SNS, they are looking for greater visibility on internal connections and collaborations. The organizing vision (Swanson and Ramiller, 2004) about SNS conveys that this visibility will allow people to better localize

SNS are like a glass lens through which individuals can better see other individuals and better see the formal and informal organizational structure. Visibility is considered as a significant step to developing collective action and collective work, enabling sensemaking and organizing (Weick, 1995). Nevertheless our work highlights that even with the same lens, individuals do not see the same things. They do not understand social capital and its potential visibility in the same way.

SNS are not only a material artefact. They are also symbolic artefacts that convey multiples possible interpretations. The interpretation of what individuals perceive depends on their initial vision. Consequently, in order to understand how groups of actors develop strategies to use SNS, we have to submerge into the symbolic world of SNS users to understand the origin of their vision. In this work we show that these ‘‘visions’’ of SNS depend on actors’ positions in the organization. More precisely, our research shows that actors will interpret what SNS will symbolize for them based upon their perspective of each form of their social capital. In this way, our research contributes to the emerging work on IS socio-materiality (Orlikowski, 1992, 2000, 2007). IS are material artefacts but they are also social artefacts influenced by the symbolic worlds of groups of actors SNS introduction in an organization reveals and transforms structures of signification. Boland et al. (2007) pointed out how changes in the digital representations of activities that are central to the functioning of a system can engender multiple changes to work practices. When they face a new IS, actors in an organization need to create new structures of signification and then new rules for work practices (Khalil and Dudezert, 2014). Our findings suggest that understanding actors perceptions regarding each form of their social capital can help to understand the structures of signification that influence actors’ strategies that emerge during SNS introduction. Future research can explore how the identification of these actors perceptions regarding their social capital can help to create new rules for work practices.

Second our study makes several theoretical contributions to Bourdieu’s Social space theory in the areas of IS, symbolic capital and social capital. Our work extends this notion of symbolic capital to the realm of the intangible, that of any form of social capital (structural relational and cognitive) which has significant implications. At the time of Bourdieu’s theorizing, actors in a field would signal capital to other actors in their field in largely tangible ways. For example, Bourdieu speaks about the wearing of luxury watches and the eating of caviar as symbols of economic capital, the acquisition of art as symbols of cultural capital, and membership in prestigious clubs as symbols of social capital. Symbolic capital was thus associated with physical objects and places. Whereas for physical objects, actors have little influence over the interpretation of the object per se, with an intangible object (information) as symbol, actors vie to manipulate the interpretation of the obiect, They work to influence the nature of the social information on display and actively manipulate both what is shared and what is not. In this case for example, information about the social position acts as the symbol. This was facilitated by SNS technology which is typically filled with information supplied by individuals. Hence they are able to actively manipulate the appearance of their hierarchy and non hierarchy-based structural social capital according to their social position in the organizational network. With social media, an individual’s engagement with symbolic activities is displayed well beyond one’s immediate field. Our study revealed that individuals were less concerned about how those in their immediate group interpreted the information in the SNS as they were with how those outside of their network interpreted the information. In this sense, the individuals were concerned with their field losing power relative to other fields rather than with gaining power within their field. Whereas Bourdieu’s theory emphasizes that individuals will pursue, willfully or even inadvertently, the forms of capital that are most necessary to succeed within their organizational field, be that a department, a particular profession, or a hierarchical level, our work demonstrates that when intangible capital becomes symbolic, actors must also work to influence the capital itself in order for their field to maintain its position of power.

Third, our work suggests that as individuals become aware of the potential to use technology to increase this symbolic capital, they will develop strategies to influence SNS adoption in such a way as to reinforce their own social position in the organization without yielding power to other departments or actors. In essence, they aim to increase their own symbolic capital and to reduce the gains in others’ symbolic capital. For example, those who believe that developing their non hierarchy-based structural capital is better, they will seek more connections. If they believe that connections with individuals in particular departments are better, will seek connections to those individuals. If they believe that it is harmful to be associated with individuals in certain departments, they may willfully exclude those connections from the system. This suggests that as individuals become aware of the symbolic value of their social capital and develop strategies to influence SNS adop tion, they become actively engaged in managing the appearance of their own social capital. This then has implications for impression management. Goffman (1959) argues that individuals see themselves in roles and often become so encapsulated with making sure that they appear to be correctly ‘‘playing’’ the role that they actually fail to engage in the most central of the activities associated with the role. As an example, he gives the student who is so consumed with appearing to be the perfect student, sitting attentively on the front row, taking copious notes, eagerly watching every movement of the professor, that the student fails to process the message the professor is communicating whereas a student who appears completely uninterested, slumped in a chair on the back row doodling while glancing around the room, might in fact be the one who is processing the most classroom instruction. In other words, Goffman warns that individuals who become consumed with appearing to properly fulfill a role often expend the best of their cognitive energy on the appearance of the role and fail to perform the actual tasks associated with the role. Our cases, while preliminary, suggests that with social capital, individuals might run this risk: it is telling that few of our interviewees discussed the value that the SNS might bring to the fulfillment of their work activities, but instead focused on what information they would, and would not, like for others to have access to. Indeed, they were already becoming consumed with the appearance of their social capital. This focus on what others would see about them suggests that impression management is an unintended consequence of the introduction of SNS in

organizations. Future research can further examine the relationships of SNS use to impression management, looking at how actors in organizations will manipulate their image and their position in the organization as they use the SNS.

Fourth, our study demonstrates that SNS can be viewed as a boundary object. SNS present an open view of the organization regardless of internal organizational boundaries. Indeed, social media could be conceived as designated boundary objects in daily life because of its capacity to facilitate information flow and gather individuals (Tim et al., 2013). Such systems serve as a facilitator for actors’ interactions across departments and plays an important ‘‘brokering role’’ (Fischer and Reeves, 1995) which contributes to the development of a ‘‘boundary spanning competence’’ (Levina and Vaast, 2005). Boundary spanning refers to activities occurring at organizational boundaries including knowledge transmission and interactions across sub-units (Pawlowski and Robey, 2004; Abott et al., 2013) and between the organization and its environment (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). By making such interactions visible, SNS encourages boundary spanning to the extent that crossboundary interactions are viewed as favorable in an organization and lead to the accrual of social, and symbolic, capital.

Lastly, our study shows that the SNS can also be considered by actors as a symbol of the ability of the more powerful within the organization to control the lower level workers. With SNS technologies, it is possible to not only represent tasks, budgets, and physical resources, but the informal resources as well. It is difficult to rationally manage that which one cannot measure or track, but as soon as one has the ability to track a resource, the managerial inclination is to manage and control the resource (Boland et al., 2007). For managers, SNS provides the occasion to open a discourse on informal resources and the total implication of the workers in the organization. Thus some actors (for instance middle-managers in the Sommerville case) see SNS implementation as a good way to monitor resources associated with those beneath them in the organization. In this way. SNS can become a tool of control over the worker, SNS can thus have a panoptical effect (Foucault. 1975: Zuboff 1988), i.e. it allows accumulating information on the worker and supervising him. Ironically, the more visible the informa tion about the lower level organizational actors becomes to their superiors, the less visible will be the ways that the superiors use this information. In other words, these systems are an opportunity for managers to collect various data on their employees (Who knows what? Who works with whom? Who knows whom? Who does what?). Access to such information allows managers to more closely supervise subordinates’ activities, thereby potentially reducing the perceived autonomy of the subordinates. Lower level actors may be left unaware of the ways that their superiors are using the information about them. In this way, the SNS can become a symbol of oppression: not only do their managers control their physical resources, but they now also have control over their informal resources. In one of our cases, the organizational culture itself appears to have alleviated this concern among lower level workers whereas in the other case, the organizational culture exacerbated this concern. Future work can look more deeply at the role of organizational culture in liberating the lower level worker from this potential panoptical outcome of SNS.

## Limitations and conclusion

As with any research project, ours is not without limitations. First, our study was conducted in the context of two French public organizations characterized by predominantly hierarchical cultures. Research in the private sector or in organizationa one with more supportive or innovative cultures (Wallach, 1983; Denison and Mishra, 1995) might reveal different insights into actors’ responses toward social capital visibility and SNS. Second, this study has focused on the early stages of SNS adoption. We have been able to get the actors' immediate responses to the SNS but we are not able to comment on their longer-term behaviors or to the sustainability of the SNS. Recent research suggests that users’ emotional responses at the early phases of the introduction of new IT directly and indirectly influence subsequent usage (Beaudry and Pinsonneault, 2010). Nevertheless, it would be helpful for future work to compare actors’ early responses towards SNS with their subsequent behaviors. Third, the technology examined in this study is relatively new to organizations and has not had a chance to become routinized in either the public or private sector. Individuals may respond differently to technology that is well established in an industry than to technology that is very new.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our research contributes to a deeper understanding of how individuals in organizations respond to the adoption of organizational SNS. Through two case studies of organizations in the process of adopting SNS, we are able to delineate groups of actors who hold differing perceptions of the value of social capital. These differing perceptions influence the strategies they develop to maintain their social position in the organization as their social capital becomes visible to others. Ultimately, the symbolic value of the capital may begin to outweigh the real value of the capital itself. Managers would do well to consider the potential responses to the introduction of SNS and work to ensure that the signals being sent by the introduction of SNS are tied to outcomes the organization desires to achieve.
