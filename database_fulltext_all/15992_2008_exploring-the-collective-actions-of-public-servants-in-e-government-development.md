# Exploring the collective actions of public servants in e-government development

Tzu-Chuan Chou <sup>a,⁎</sup>, Jau-Rong Chen <sup>b</sup>, Ching-Kuo Pu <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Information Management, National Taiwan University of Science and Technology, 43, Sec.4, Keelung Road, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC <sup>b</sup> Department of Business Management, Mingchi University of Technology, 84 Gungjuan Rd. Taishan, Taipei, Taiwan, ROC <sup>c</sup> Department of Information Management, National Kaohsiung First University of Science and Technology, 2, Jhuoyue Road, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, ROC

Available online 20 February 2007

## Abstract

The importance of e-government has been widely recognized. However, the roles of public servants on e-government development have not yet been convincingly demonstrated. The main purpose of this paper is to explore the collective actions in the development of e-government in terms of public servants' information literacy. Drawing on a case study of Kaohsiung City, Taiwan, both the motivational and structural dimensions of collective actions are examined. Based on evidence from the case, this study proposes nine possible collective action problems and their effects that influence cooperative behaviors of public servants. The implications and future research directions are also discussed. © 2007 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: E-government; Collective action problems; Information literacy

## 1. Introduction

The premise of public administration is undergoing a transformation with the redesign of non-innovative bureaucratic governmental structures to accommodate an emerging generation of e-government [54]. However, the introduction of IT and the transformation toward egovernment has brought dramatic changes in the governing of public administration and bureaucratic systems [39,40]. In addition, effecting change in the public sector is a massive and complex undertaking [19]. Therefore, despite the promotion of the benefits of e-government, the trend toward e-government may lead to various challenges to governmental organizations.

One of the challenges is, as pointed out by Horton [21], that the transformation of the public services was meeting resistance from existing staff because they felt threatened. Public servants are the “major players” in government, but they are also considered to embody inertia, inefficiency, red tape, and poor coordination [24]. From another perspective, public servants' cooperation and direct participation in a transition have long been regarded as a way to decrease employees' resistance to change and increase organizational adaptability. Indeed, public servants are like a double-edged sword, either facilitating or impeding the development of e-government.

To that end, the roles of public servants on the development of e-government have not yet been convincingly demonstrated. In particular, recent public administrative studies (e.g., [6]) suggest that we should see “the Internet as a public good”. A public good is one that is not used up or depleted in its consumption [50] and is typically associated with two characteristics: non-rivalry and nonexcludability. Essentially, e-government uses the Internet and other information and communication technologies (ICTs) as enablers to deliver their public services in a smarter way, improve citizen-state relations, and transform the scope of administrative actions and political processes. Accordingly, for public servants, e-government provides “information public goods,” which should be made available and accountable with as few barriers as possible.

However, one major problem associated with this viewpoint is that public goods create inefficiencies within an organization [57]. As suggested by Brito [11], when large collectives offer public goods as their sole incentive, both the “imperceptible effect” and the “freerider problem” are likely to jeopardize collective actions. Kollock [28] also points out that many of the most challenging problems we face, from the interpersonal to the international, are collective action problems (also known as social dilemmas), in which individual rationality leads to collective irrationality. That is, individual rationality is not sufficient for the collective [36]. Therefore, providing public goods from e-government will potentially lead to public servants' non-cooperation and free-riding, with the result that the collective pursuits of e-government are often difficult to achieve.

In addition, the enhancement of public servants and people's information literacy has been emphasized in many e-government action plans. Information literacy can be defined as the ability to recognize information needs and identify, evaluate, and use information effectively [12]. For example, in e-Japan Strategy (www.kantei.go. jp), nurturing high-quality human resources is one of the priority policy areas by which Japan is pursuing e-government. The policy shows that the enhancement of information literacy will form the essential foundation for the promotion of the IT revolution; therefore the information literacy of government officials should be enhanced. Sridhar [53] indicates that e-government vision requires a community that is information and technologically literate to access the information public servants require. Koniger and Janowitz [29] also suggest that improving the competency of the individuals in information processing is extremely crucial.

Therefore, both public servants' information literacy and collective action are considered important to the efficiency of e-government. In addition, we argue that public servants may have different levels of information literacy and, in turn, may have different interpretations of e-government, based on their underlying assumptions, expectations, and knowledge [37]. These different interpretations may lead to different levels of perception of that e-government as a public good. This research direction is typically overlooked by e-government researchers. Our study posits that e-government research must explicitly incorporate both public servants' collective action and information literacy. Specifically, the linkage between public servants' collective action and information literacy should not be ignored, because IT plays a vital part in the information-handling process of e-government.

Thus, the main purpose of this paper is to explore the possible collective action problems in the e-government introduction process in terms of public servants' information literacy. That is, drawing on collective action theory and information literacy, this paper explores how collective action differs in terms of different levels of information literacy. More specifically, this study intends to answer the following questions:

• Are collective action problems emerging in egovernment development due to pubic servants information literacy?

• What is the nature of these collective action problems?

• How do these collective action problems influence the cooperation and development of e-government?

This analysis is based on an in-depth case study from Kaohsiung City, the biggest commercial harbor and the second-biggest city in Taiwan. Our exploration of public servants' collective action behaviors will help gain an understanding of why or why not public servants work together toward the achievement of collective goals to the development of e-government. The implications of collective action behaviors for the development of egovernment and an agenda for further research into egovernment are also discussed. The primary contribution of the paper is in extending our understanding of the impacts of public servants' information literacy and collective action on the development of e-government. The next section discusses the background characteristics of public servants and the theories of collective action and information literacy. This discussion provides a roadmap for authors to analyze our case data. This is followed by a description of the case. Subsequently, the analysis and findings of the study are discussed. The final section concludes by discussing future research directions.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Collective action of public servants

The development of e-government has gradually shifted from standard Web pages to having more of a customer focus, serving citizens and trading partners directly by providing services, information, and transactions on-line via the Internet [17,55]. Although public sectors are generally not involved in obtaining competitive advantages as business units are, they are now often viewed as the primary mechanism for creating more-efficient and better service organizations [46]. Having an online aspect to government provides a foundation for richer public dialogue and greater citizen input [2], and it also provides public services to gain and retain competitiveness in a complex and turbulent global political environment [60]. From public servants' viewpoint, e-government delivers public services in a smarter way and transforms the scope of administrative actions and the political processes.

In fact, the adoption of IT for Public Management is essentially an administrative innovation. However, many studies [8] find that administrators in public bureaucracy find change difficult to carry out. For example, Rainey [43] summarizes the common assertion that resistance to change in public bureaucracies is due in part to bureaucratic structure and configuration, bureaucratic culture and administrative orthodoxy, individual caution, and slothfulness. Therefore, exploring public servants' cooperative behavior in the e-government adoption process is now of critical importance for all governments, since the Internet provides or enhances public services to gain and retain competitiveness in a complex and turbulent global political environment.

It is important to emphasize that e-government provides information public goods, which can create inefficiencies within an organization. A public good is typically associated with two characteristics: nonrivalry and non-excludability. An organizational good is non-rivalrous in use if all employees benefit from it without reducing the benefits of others; a good is nonexcluding if it is impractical in terms of organization transaction costs to maintain exclusive control over its use [57]. The services provided by e-government are non-rivalrous in use if all public servants would enjoy the benefits without reducing the benefits of others. Also, a specific public servant cannot be excluded from receiving the benefits of e-government innovations. As discussed, providing public goods from egovernment will potentially lead to public servants' non-cooperation and free-riding and also the result that the collective pursuits of e-government are often difficult to achieve.

Indeed, employees' cooperation is of critical importance for an organization's effectiveness [22], and the importance of cooperative behavior among individuals and groups has been a dominant research question in behavior science [16]. In particular, cooperative behaviors are said to foster positive work climates, thus promoting greater commitment, stability, and interpersonal effectiveness among participants [51]. However, an organization's members acting on a rational and utilitarian basis may maximize their benefits by not making any effort on behalf of the group [11]. That's why social science (e.g., [36]) has brought forward a mountain of cross-disciplinary analysis concerning various facets of the “paradox” of cooperation among rational individuals in large groups in pursuit of a collectively desired good.

Olson's [36] well-known theory, collective action theory, suggests that the rational, self-interested individuals in large groups would not automatically join together to solve a collective action problem, despite it being in their best interest to do so. Collective action refers to joint action in pursuit of a common objective [35]. Collective action problems (or social dilemmas, free-rider problems) occur whenever individuals in interdependent situations face choices in which the maximization of short-term self-interest yields outcomes that leave all participants worse off than feasible alternatives [38]. That is, there is a behavior that is clearly dominant and that will guarantee a maximum personal outcome, regardless of what others do; however, if all persons act in this way, outcomes in the long run are poorer than they would have been had everyone cooperated.

Collective action can be applied in the analysis of those institutional settings of e-government from which individual decisions are coordinated to get some particular result [32]. Interpersonal cooperation is typically limited by collective action problems that arise when group members have the potential to free ride [36], and free-riding is a strategy in which an individual can maximize his or her gains by refraining from cooperation of others invest in the cooperative activity. Thus, although everyone may gain if all group members invest in the cooperative goals, attaining such largescale cooperation is often difficult to achieve without social mechanisms limiting free-riding opportunities.

## 2.2. Perspectives on public servants collective actions

Collective action theories have been used in examining various micro and macro social phenomena such as worker resistance [47], group heterogeneity [20], social capital [48], social exchange [18], collective organizations [7], and online social structure [59]. Most of these studies have accepted Olson's conclusion that such cooperation is almost always impossible without the provision of selective incentives (private benefits or cost exemptions that are available only to those who contribute toward the collective good), and studies have moved on to examine various ways in which such incentives can be provided, particularly by a centralized hierarchy.

In exploring collective action problems, these studies focus on two largely disjointed approaches, one focusing on the influence of social structure (macro level) and another focusing on the incentive for individual participation (micro level) [14,28]. Following Kollock's [28] suggestions, there are three issues to be considered in the individual motivational dimension: social value orientation, communication, and group identity; there are six issues to be considered in the structural dimension: iteration and identifiability, payoff structure, efficacy, group size, boundary, and sanctions. Although Kollock [28] suggests that we can solve the collective action problems in terms of the motivational and structural dimensions, we need to explore the potential collective action problems in advance. Thus, in this study, Kollock's perspective on the individual motivational and structural dimensions will be employed as the roadmap for authors to analyze our case data.

Social value orientation refers to the preference for certain outcome distributions between the self and the interdependent other [56]. Social value theory assumes that individuals differ in their likelihood of applying subjective weights to outcomes affecting themselves and others [41]. There are three identified orientations: (a) an individualistic social value, which is egoistic and oriented toward weighting one's own outcome positively and others' outcomes as zero; (b) a competitive social value, with an egoistic orientation toward weighting one's own outcome positively and other's outcomes negatively; and (c) a cooperative social value, which is oriented toward group enhancement and weighting both one's own and others' outcomes positively. It seems, then, that having employees with a cooperative social value of will facilitate the development of e-government.

Communication allows group members to collect information, to make explicit commitments and promises, and to offer moral suasion [28]. The direct effects of communication can be attributed to two forms of social influence: informational influence, by which communication can be used to convey information and formulate strategies for how to solve the social dilemma, and normative influence, by which communication can be used to convey information about group norms and values [4]. The process of communication allows the group to recognize the benefits of cooperation and conveys tacit norms that limit competition and individual payoff maximization [23]. Therefore, a higher quality and frequency of communication among public servants will facilitate e-government development.

To have a group identity is to identify as members of a common group and will increase the altruism of the members. Group members always share the collective identity, and collective identity carries with it positive feelings for other members of the group [42]. Also, identity is appealing as an alternative to material incentives [42]. Kelly and Breinlinger [25] have demonstrated that group identification is as important for participation in a social movement as cost–benefit considerations. Identity is the self-definition of the question, Who are we? and it affects issue interpretation and action in an organization by serving as a cognitive schema or collectively held frame for organizational and individual sense-making activities. Accordingly, in e-government development, public servants would implicitly define the question, Who are we in the new form of government? If public servants have defined a positive group identity toward e-government, the collective actions are likely to be achieved.

In the structural dimension, iteration and identifiability are the first to be taken into account. Iteration refers to repetitive interaction, and identifiability (also called observability, anonymity, accountability, and task visibility) refers to the degree to which others can observe and assess an individual's behaviors [58]. Kollock [28] suggests that if individuals will not interact in the future, if identity is unknown or unstable, and if there is no recollection or record of past interactions, individuals will be motivated to behave selfishly because they will not be accountable for their actions. Identifiability will influence cooperation in groups in such a way that members perceiving themselves as having a high level of identifiability will cooperate more than those perceiving a low level of identifiability [58]. That is, the absence of identifiability or anonymity leads to non-cooperation. Therefore, in e-government development, we should understand the levels of iteration and identifiability perceived by public servants in the e-government introduction process.

In addition, both formal and informal rewards need to be considered in studying public servants' collective actions. The payoff structure represents the informal rewards individuals receive from others in return for cooperation. Obviously, the greater the personal return from cooperation, the higher the level of cooperation [28]. In contrast, unlike payoff structure, the sanction refers to the organization's formal reward system, which rewards the cooperators for their actions and punishes defectors.

From another perspective, efficacy refers to the idea that individuals can have a noticeable effect on the outcomes; if they can make an efficacious contribution, cooperation rates can increase [28]. Efficacy concerns whether a single person's actions may have a discernable effect on the situation. In this regard, if public servants can perceive noticeable effects of their actions on e-government development, the collective action problems are likely to decrease.

Also, researchers found that increasing the number of workers performing a task reduced the average individual effort devoted to task performance [58]. Based on this, in e-government development, the perceived increase in group size leads to the decrease in cooperation. Group size is a quite subjective concept. However, the groups involved in pursuing e-government development are not necessarily in the formal organization structure; they also appear as informal teams. Also, the boundaries of the collective good have an effect on collaboration. The introduction of e-government may create a public good (or collective goods) of a new form of government that is available to anyone in the government, making it easy for individuals to free ride on the efforts of others.

## 2.3. Information literacy

Historically, literacy was interpreted as a basic ability to read, write, and comprehend. Information literacy refers to the ability to find, use, and communicate information [30]. Riedling [45] more precisely defines information literacy as the ability of people to know when they need information and to be able to find, evaluate, process, and use information to make appropriate decisions in their lives. Saranto and Hovenga [49] suggest that information literacy is broader in its concept than computer literacy, which has only focused on basic knowledge and skills in computer use. Take an example from Sorapure et al. [52]: in considering the use of the WWW as a resource for writing papers, Web literacy not only refers to finding relevant information but also to accessing the quality and value of information for a specific paper, then determining how to integrate the information together with other sources into one's own writing.

Information literacy has long been an area of great interest to librarians, but it has also been attached to many discussions in e-government studies (e.g., [53]), IT-related workplace experiences (e.g., [12]), and the health care industry [49]. As an example, Bruce [12] discusses information managers with an interest in training and educating their clientele to effectively use the organization's information services.

In the e-government development process, public servants must be literate with computer operations and other job-related processes. That is, e-government development should incorporate computer skills and training programs into every public servant's working experience. In addition, information literacy is envisaged as comprising not only “an understanding of the general concepts of information processing,” but also “how computer systems support and shape a person's job function” and “ the trade-off between investment and benefits, time expended, and time saved” [33].

Although we recognize the potential power that egovernment poses for governmental reform, we argue that public servants may not have the capability to realize the potential power of the e-government, and, in turn, the resultant collective action problems will have deleterious effects on e-government development. Based on this framework and empirical evidence, this paper will discuss the public servants' collective action practices and the theoretical implications of the development of e-government.

## 3. Methodology

Studying the collective actions of public servants in the e-government development process requires the context of real governmental organization. This study adopts an in-depth case research method for gathering evidence. The case research strategy allows the exploration of unforeseen relationships (levels of information literacy and collective action) and offers better insights into the inter-dependencies among the factors captured in the study [9]. We selected Kaohsiung City, the biggest commercial harbor and the second biggest city in Taiwan, for our case study.

In studying e-government, Taiwan is a particularly appropriate context for study, because it is characterized by information-intensive competition and rapid technological advancements. Taiwan established an Electronic Government Program (2001–2004) in April 2001. The program aimed to link all government agencies through networking and to present versatile Internet-based services as the e-government system (www.rdec.gov. tw). It clearly depicted a roadmap for e-government that included infrastructure development and penetration, online services development, and mobile government.

## 3.1. Data collection

The Kaohsiung City government consists of a secretariat, eighteen bureaus, three commissions, six offices, eleven district offices, and over twenty-five thousand public servants. The case study was conducted in the Kaohsiung City government over a period of 6 months. Qualitative data were gathered from document archives, face-to-face unstructured interviews, and field notes. Also, one of the authors has worked in the Kaohsiung City government for over 15 years, thus providing insightful personal observation and ensuring the quality of collected data. As suggested by Tan and Pan [54], the author's experience and contextual understanding provide additional background information that is valuable to the interpretation of the evidence found. We carried out twenty interviews with public servants from the Kaohsiung City government, with each interview lasting 60 to 90 min.

A purposive sampling strategy was employed to ensure that the interviews represented various types of public servants. Therefore, in-depth interviews were conducted with the MIS and non-MIS staffs in both MIS and non-MIS departments at the city government. The face-to-face interviews were unstructured; the standard set of questions used was designed only to help initiate and guide the interview process. All the interviews were tape recorded; notes were taken during the sessions as well. Additional observations were noted immediately after each interview was completed. Table 2 presents the characteristics of the informants, including descriptions of the sex, age, work experience, department, and education major.

All informants shown in Table 2 were selected and scrutinized carefully to ensure the quality of the collected data. The author also assessed their information literacy by using informants' self-reported information skills, including job descriptions, using IT in their jobs, computerization of their department, information technology intensity of their job, information requirement and access, problem solving using IT, information processing, etc. These questions can be used to distinguish public servants' abilities to recognize information needs and to identify, evaluate, and use information effectively. According to informants' self-reported information skills and educational backgrounds, we assessed their information literacy and assigned as very high, high, medium and weak, respectively.

We found that several public servants with high information literacy (most notably A1, B3, and B5) stated that they have or need to cooperate with other departments to work toward e-government development. As public servant B3 noted: “In pursuing egovernment, all activities need to coordinate and cooperate with other departments. For example, CA (Certificate Authority) is noting without inter-functional cooperation in our city government. In fact, we have a very good collaborative model to share our data with other departments.” Perhaps these public servants' jobs are more related to e-government than the others. Therefore, they favor win–win solutions, in which all interdependent members within the system, including themselves, receive better outcomes.

Obviously, these public servants have a cooperative social value toward e-government and attach importance to the achievement of e-government for both themselves and other people. In contrast, some public servants with low information literacy, like C2, D3, and D5, provided different viewpoints. For example, as servant D3 indicated: “Every one has his own job and should be done by himself, not by any other one else. It is MIS department's jobs and responsible to development egovernment. For me, e-government is just like document system. In fact, I prefer to write a document by my hand, because it is faster than to type a document.” The other public servants with low information literacy also show their disinterest in e-government. Thus, compared with those with high information literacy, the public servants with lower information literacy show less cooperation in completing public objectives, which may have a negative influence on the collective action of achieving e-government.

Social value orientations are known to influence outcome preferences, expectations, and strategic choices. Our case evidence suggests that, in e-government development processes, public servants with lower information literacy are likely to be concerned with their own job, and they tend to ignore or are uninterested in the e-government's outcomes and lack the sense to help each other. Traditionally, public agents' computer knowledge, experience, and educational levels are closely associated with alienated beliefs and attitudes toward IT [1]. Therefore, one possible way to change a public servant's social value orientations is to change their IT skills. That is, by changing their IT skills, they will have different interpretations of e-government and will thus change their cooperative attitudes. Therefore, how to conduct an on-the-job training program for those public servants with low information literacy would be a challenge.

## 4.1.2. Public servants' communication

Communication allows group members to collect information, to make explicit commitments and promises, and to offer moral suasion, which shows what is the right or proper thing to do. In our data analysis, we found that the public servants with higher information literacy (most notably A1, A2, A3, and B3) not only can communicate effectively and frequently about the egovernment issues but also perceive the value of egovernment. For example, public servant A3 explained: “We call many join meetings before we take a step forward to promote e-government. In particularly, it's the age of information and no more each department works independently. We know what e-government is and we need all departments to work closely, to coordinate, and to cooperation, in order to get our jobs done. Moreover, we do realize that to provide the best public service to our citizens is the ultimate goal of e-government.” Empirical studies (e.g., [31]) suggest that goal clarity increases motivation by linking staff efforts to mission-related tasks, by insulating staff from organizational politics, and by encouraging innovation and risk taking within organizations.

However, public servants with low information literacy seem to have less chances to communicate with other departments and have only a vague understanding of e-government. For example, as noted by C2: “We do have electronic official document exchange system to announce the issues about e-government. However, it's too many technical terms and we just cannot understand what it mean... We type and send our official documents by computer. But, we are not clear about the goals of e-government”.

The ability to communicate and the quality of communication is vital to the success of e-government. Also, by offering and extracting promises and by reinforcing prior normative orientations, the communication process increases the selection of cooperative strategies [4]. That is, the functions of communication can mobilize collective actions toward e-government. Based on our observations, both forms of social influence of communication (informational and normative influences) are rare in those public servants with lower information literacy. Also, the concept and goals of egovernment are not clear. By comparing these data, apparently those public servants with lower information literacy do have communication problems in regard to understanding the development of e-government, which, in turn, may lead to collection problems. E-government itself is a useful platform to help public servants communicate with each other and improve informational and normative influences. A critical issue is how to motivate public servants, in particular those with low information literacy, to become active in the communication platform, in turn resulting in better communication.

## 4.1.3. Public servants' group identity

As mentioned, having a group identity is to identify as members of a common group; this collective social identity increases the altruism of the members. We found that the public servants with higher information literacy (most notably A1, A2, A3, B1, and B5) do develop a joint group identity toward e-government. That is, they acknowledge themselves as members of e-government. For example, as expressed by public servant A2: “Egovernment has becomes a critical issue. I do care about the performance of pursuing e-government for reasons. On the one hand, we can enjoy its substantial benefits. One the other hand, it represents the power of a country.” This positive attitude derived from their group identity can facilitate their cooperative behaviors.

In contrast, however, public servants C1, C2, D2, and D3 express a dissimilar perspective in developing the identity of e-government. For example, as indicated by D2: “We do not oppose to toward e-government. However, the biggest problem is our computer techniques. Learning computer would take me a lot of time and efforts, and our work loading and pressure will increase dramatically. In particularly, it's too risky to hand computer jobs by our disaccustomed computer skills. Even our supervisor would not take the risk.”

As previously mentioned, group identity represents the collective identities imposed on groups; the collective social identity increases the altruism of the members. Under the assumption that group members have a strong group identity, we can examine how perceiving themselves as members of e-government influences public servants' decisions with respect to cooperation. Inconsistent with those public servants with high information literacy, those with lower information literacy posit that they didn't consider themselves members of the new form of government. Thus, we conclude that public servants who have lower information literacy are less likely to have a group identity, and they didn't define themselves as members of e-government, resulting in collective action problems.

Many researchers (e.g., [27]) have assumed that the effect of social pressure on collective action is moderated by members' dependence on the group for social approval. From the e-government perspective, we should consider enhancing all public servants' dependence on e-government as much as possible in order to increase their group identity. If public servants dependence on the e-government increases, social sanctions will become more effective as catalysts for collective action. However, this may be not easy to achieve, because the antecedent of enhancing public servants' dependence on e-government is to increase their information literacy.

## 4.2. Structural dimension of collective actions

## 4.2.1. Iteration and identifiability

Iteration represents interactions between public servants, and identifiability involves the degree to which others can observe and assess an individual's behaviors. If individuals will not interact in the future or if there is no recollection or record of past interactions, individuals will be motivated to behave selfishly, because they will not be accountable for their actions. In our data analysis, several public servants (most notably A2, A3, and B3) stated that their jobs have been recorded clearly, and they highly interact with each other. For example, as stated by public servant A3: “We feel very tired to have meetings with the staffs of The Research, Development and Evaluation Commission (RDEC) of our city government. The results of each meeting are recorded clearly, and they will trace and review our progress in the following meetings.”

In contrast, public servants C1 and D4 indicated that their jobs have no relevance to e-government development. As noted by D4: “There are other departments in charge of e-government adoption. We have no relevance about this issue.” Since the jobs of public servants with lower information literacy are not related to e-government directly, their efforts and activities cannot be identified systematically. In turn, they don't need to interact with others and have no formal records of their interactions. In this case, these public servants are likely to behave selfishly and uncooperatively, and in turn will attenuate the collective actions. In the EG developing process, we can conclude that the civil servants with lower IL perceive a lower level of iteration and identifiability, which can lead to collective action problems.

Also, we need to be aware that the absence of identifiability or anonymity leads to non-cooperation. Efforts need to be made to solve the iteration and identifiability problem, such as making interaction more durable or frequent, increasing identifiability, and increasing information about individuals' actions [5]. E-government itself is a powerful tool for recording and transmitting information. For example, the establishment of a virtual community for public servants may increase iteration and identifiability. However, how to motivate public servants, in particular those with low information literacy, becomes an extreme challenge.

## 4.2.2. Payoff structure

The payoff structure refers to informal rewards received from others for an individual's cooperation. The greater the personal return from cooperation and the lower the return from defecting, the higher the level of cooperation. Our analysis shows that public servants (particularly A1, B1, and B2) expressed that they perceived positive payoff from their colleagues and other departments. For example, as indicated by public servant A1: “Our team is in charge of city government's informationization, including applications development, planning and consultation. Our efforts in crossdepartment communication have attained very positive feedbacks and cooperation.”

In contrast, public servants D2, D3, and D5 indicate that they do not perceive positive payoff from their colleagues and other departments. For example, as public servant D2 summarized: “We don't have our own IT/IS staffs. We do have hard time, because we need to do everything by ourselves or beg those IT/IS staffs. However, I think it is their duty to finish their jobs. When should we beg for them to do so? Even we win an award in promoting Electronic Official Document Interchange (EODI), the reward is quite minor.” If public servants with low information literacy cannot perceive a higher level of payoff from other servants, they will not delight in cooperating with others and will increase the collective action problems. Therefore, we can conclude that, in the e-government developing process, the public servants with lower information literacy perceive a lower level of pay-off for their cooperative behaviors, which can to lead to collective action problems.

Actors tend to base their decisions about participation upon other's decisions, and one's participation tends to encourage another's participation by creating a collective action to succeed [26]. Obviously, human interaction always shapes an actor's perceptions of opportunities for defection, and actors try to influence their neighbors [26]. Therefore, efforts should be made to shape an environment of positive participation and to reduce the opportunities for defection. However, this may not be easy to achieve if they do not appreciate help from others and ignore others' cooperation.

## 4.2.3. Efficacy

Efficacy refers to the idea that individuals can have a noticeable effect on the outcomes. One of the key reasons people do not cooperate is the fact that a single person's actions may have no discernable effect on the situation. As suggested by Brunner [13]), when group size increases, each individual perceives their own contribution as having little or no effect on the total provided by the rest of the group. Our analysis suggests that public servants (particularly A1, B1, and B4) perceived that their efforts can significantly contribute to the e-government initiatives. For example, as indicated by public servant A1: “In e-government adoption process, our department would lead the development in city government. We start the adoption process from our department, such as electric documents and KM system. In turn, other departments will mimic our successful experiences.”

In contrast, public servants C1, C2, D1, D2, and D3 indicate that they make no significant contribution to the e-government initiatives. For example, as indicated by C1: “Besides typing documents and Internet, my computer technique is poor! I can do noting and have no contribution in adopting e-government.” Accordingly, we can conclude that in the e-government development process, the lower information literacy of public servants leads to a perceived lower level of their contributions toward e-government, which can lead to collective action problems.

Obviously, government always suffers from the absence of economic markets for outputs and diffused goals [45], and it is difficult for each member to ascertain what returns are from his or her contributions. We should be aware that the perceived efficacy has a significant effect on the contributions to a public good [44]. More precise e-government-related job descriptions and promoting personal responsibility along with organizational recoding and a sanction system are likely to improve the perception of efficacy toward e-government. Undoubtedly, effective contribution measurement is always a problematic issue of public bureaucracies, which may lead to the difficulty in improving the perception of efficacy toward e-government.

## 4.2.4. Group size

Andreoni [3] focuses on the effect of group size on the set of free riders and the aggregate level of public goods. In fact, individuals treat the contribution of others as a perfect substitute for their own contribution; consequently, as group size increases, individuals become less likely to contribute themselves and more likely to rely on the contribution of others [13]. In our analysis, several public servants (most notably A2, A4, B1, and B5) stated that their departments are small and facilitate their cooperation in pursuing e-government. As indicated by A2: ”We need to form and attain some small workshops and learning groups to learn new techniques. As you know, the learning new tech is complicated. Our colleagues will share their experiences, not only to extend our knowledge but get close to each other.

In contrast, public servants D1 and D3 indicated that they are in a bigger department and always count on the help of others; they have no motivation to learn for themselves. For example, as indicated by D1: “We are in a large department and not everyone is good in computer techniques. However, some of the staffs have the capabilities to solve our problems.” Thus, we can conclude that, in the e-government development process, the public servants with lower information literacy perceive themselves to be posted in a larger group in performing their e-government-related tasks, which can lead to collective action problems.

Although we have no specific numbers of groups in judging their size, obviously, public servants with low information literacy are likely to perceive themselves as posted in a bigger department and count on the help of others. Especially as groups become large, individual contributions to the collective action tend to be greater than the perceived individual proportion of the public goods shared by each member [11]. Also, in large groups, members are more likely to tolerate instances of non-contribution. Therefore, Olson [36] asserts that the larger the number of members in a group, the less likely that the group will exhibit successful collective action. Accordingly, formally reducing the group size or informally segmenting the group (or department) into several learning groups is one possible solution to the large group size. However, the effectiveness of this solution is based on the public servants' incentive for learning and the public organizational sanction systems.

## 4.2.5. Boundaries

One of the basic assumptions of this paper is that the service provided by e-government is a public good. The boundary refers to the extent that public employees perceive that e-government provides that public good. The higher the perception of e-government, the lower the incentive to contribute voluntarily in developing e-government. Our analysis suggests that public servants (particularly A1, A4, B1, and B2) expressed the desire to be involved in the e-government adoption processes, which means that they do not see the results of e-government as public goods. As stated by public servant A1: “The Research, Development and Evaluation Commission (RDEC) of city government don't have any IT/IS staff in formulation e-government policy. All e-government initiatives are highly related to IT, and IT department is the window to pursue it. We need to take the responsibilities.” However, public servants with lower information literacy show that they are likely to take the results of e-government as public goods. Although they realize that the services provided by e-government are valued, no efforts have been made to involve themselves in the adoption of e-government. This was especially true for public servants C2, D3, D4, and D5. As public servant D4 said: “I have no idea about using computer and e-government. But, I know every one can use online government and it is quite convenient. So we do think without our efforts, every one still can use the services provide by e-government without any barriers. Thus, we can conclude that, in the e-government development process, the public servants with lower information literacy perceive a higher level of the services provided by e-government as public goods, which can lead to collective action problems.

Olson [36] suggested that individuals seek to reap the benefits of others' participation while evading the costs to themselves, because the individuals who cannot be excluded from the benefits of a public good have little incentive to voluntarily contribute once the good is produced. Therefore, if public servants perceive a higher degree of non-rivalry and non-excludability, they are likely to have little incentive to voluntarily contribute to the development of e-government, thus the boundaries of the collective good lead to collective action problems. In solving this problem, Noussair and Tucker [34] suggest the combination of the formal (see next section for detail) and informal sanctions on the contribution level for a specific type of public good. Informal sanctions refer to penalties that do not impose tangible costs on the offender, though they may decrease his or her utility [10]. For example, information sanctions such as social disapproval, ostracism, gossip, peer pressure, and public embarrassment are often applied to try to alter offenders' behavior. However, no empirical evidence shows the effects on employing formal and informal sanctions in the e-government development process.

## 4.2.6. Sanction

The sanction refers to the organization's formal reward system. The cooperators would be rewarded for their actions and defectors punished. That is, it is necessary to use selective incentives in encouraging cooperation and in contributing toward the public good [36]. In our collected data, several public servants (A2, A3, B2, and B3) expressed that the sanction system for e-government development is not strong enough, even they also like to be rewarded. However, they are still happy to get the job done even if the reward is not significant. For example, as observed by A2: “Personally, the sanctions are still the major incentive for our hard work. However, it' our duties and responsibilities to dedicate ourselves in e-government development, no matter what awards our supervised mangers would give to us.” Therefore, even though these public servants with high information literacy don't receive sufficient rewards for their efforts toward e-government, their job duty and obligation still motivate them to engage in developing e-government.

With respect to the same issue, public servants (C2, D2, and D3) place more emphasis on the need for more positive feedback to motivate their efforts in egovernment adoption. Since the sanction system for egovernment adoption is not significant, the data show that these public servants are quite reactive in egovernment development. For example, public servant D3 commented: “In pursuing Electronic Official Document Interchange (EODI), there is only a date for initiative. However, there is no compulsory of not doing. Basically, all we have to do is meet the minimal requirement: to learn how to type official document electronically. Nothing else!” Apparently, with no sanction, these public servants with low information literacy show no will to voluntarily contribute their efforts. Thus, we can conclude that, in the e-government development process, the public servants with lower information literacy need a more specific sanction system in promoting their cooperative behaviors, otherwise it is likely to lead to collective action problems.

Sanctions are actions taken on behalf of communities to enforce laws [61]. Noussair and Tucker [34] indicate that the sanctions are intended to lower the return on self-interested behavior and increase the incentive to follow the behavioral norm. However, public servants are also more likely to be characterized by an ethic that prioritizes intrinsic rewards over extrinsic rewards [15]. Overall, an effective sanction system will encourage all public servants positively, but it has a serious impact on those public servants with low information literacy. Therefore, the introduction of e-government should take an intrinsic reward system into account, and the design of the reward system should consider how rewards promote cooperation and how promoting cooperation allows everyone to win. Also, the sanction system must also consider how to set “right” quotas, to spur people to great achievement. More efforts are needed to design an appropriate sanction system in addressing these issues.

Actors tend to base their decisions about participation upon other's decisions, and one's participation tends to encourage other's participation by creating a collective action to succeed [26]. Obviously, human interaction always shapes actor's perceptions of opportunities for defection, and actors try to influence their neighbors [26]. However, this may not be easy to achieve if they do not appreciate the help from others and ignore the cooperation of others.

## 5. Conclusion

As we know, public servants' participation and involvement has been widely recognized as the key to the successful reform of public administration systems. The main purpose of this research has been to seek an understanding of collective behaviors of public servants in terms of their information literacy. Issues in the motivational and structural dimensions have been examined, and we show nine possible collective action problems. However, we also acknowledge that the assessment of information literacy is quite subjective, and it is possible that those proposed collective action problems are also job related. In future studies, we should therefore not only focus on the research needed in Tables 3 and 4, but also on the more objective assessment of information literacy.

Third, more recently, social processes that facilitate cooperation have become more prominent. Besides the size of the group, public servants' behavior is affected by many other structural variables, including the heterogeneity of the participants, their dependence on the benefits received, their discount rates, the type and predictability of transformation processes involved, the nesting of organizational levels, monitoring techniques, and the information available to participants [38]. From this perspective, the understanding of collective action problems helps to promote a normative environment that encourages cooperation between actors and, in turn, improves the efficiency of e-government development.

In short, we learned that the motivational and structural dimensions of the collective actions of public servants do influence the efficiency of e-government development. Notwithstanding, the purpose of this paper has not been to present generalized solutions to those collective action problems, but to awaken interest in the public servants' information literacy in the pursuit of e-government development.
