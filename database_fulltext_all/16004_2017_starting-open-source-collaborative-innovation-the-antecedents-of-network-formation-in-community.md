# Starting open source collaborative innovation: the antecedents of network formation in community source

Manlu Liu,\* Clyde Eiríkur Hull<sup>†</sup> & Yu-Ting Caisy Hung<sup>‡</sup>

\*Management Information Systems, Saunders College of Business, Rochester Institute of Technology, Rochester, NY USA, email: manluliu@saunders.rit.edu, <sup>†</sup>Department of Management, Saunders College of Business, Rochester Institute of Technology, Rochester, NY USA, and <sup>‡</sup>School of Individualized Study, Rochester Institute of Technology, Rochester, NY USA

Abstract. Speci<sup>fi</sup>c needs in the area of enterprise applications have led to a new type of collaborative open source innovation development across institution borders: community source. We use the Kuali community source network, a jointly managed, border-spanning organization that supplies the institutions that created it, to describe how community source works. This study builds a theoretical basis for understanding the individual and institutional factors affecting community source network formation and the decision by organizations to join a community source network. We identify eight antecedents of decisions about forming or joining community source initiatives: motives, learning, trust, norms and monitoring, institutional similarity, external funding, hostile external environment and information technology. © 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd

Keywords: community source, network formation, open source, collaborative innovation

## INTRODUCTION

Because innovation, in both products and processes, is essential to organizational surviva (Ireland et al., 2009), it is important to understand all approaches to innovation: developing it in side the organization, outside it or by innovating across company borders (Hull & Covin, 2010). Innovation not performed entirely inside the organization is increasing (Tran et al., 2010; Cui & O’Connor, 2012; Teo & Bhattacherjee, 2014). Cooperative or collaborative innovation in particular has enjoyed considerable evolution and increased use in recent years, perhaps more so than other approaches (Arora & Gambardella, 2010; Tran et al., 2010; Cui & O’Connor, 2012; Delerue & Lejeune, 2012; Fjeldstad et al., 2012; Han et al., 2012). This may be because advancements in communication technology have mitigated distance and other challenges to innovation across institution and geographic borders, or it may be that these technologies offer improvements over face-to-face communication (Carroll et al., 2009). In this study, we look at an emerging form of high-technology cooperative innovation made possible by these recen advances, a new venture in virtual form: community source. Community source, ‘an open source project that requires signi<sup>fi</sup>cant investments from institutional partners in both human resources and cash contributions’ (Liu et al., 2007), is a new collaborative form of inter-organizational entrepreneurship. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, discussed later, of community source are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, but compara tively few organizations currently participate in community source. To understand why such a powerful tool for ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently driving the innovation process is not widely adopted, we examine the antecedents of network formation in community source. Better understanding why (and why not) organizations choose to use community source should contribute to our theoretical un derstanding of innovation and the role of technology in advancing innovation. It will also help prac titioners better position their organizations to take advantage of this promising approach.

Much work has been carried out on the antecedents of innovation in general (Damanpour, 1991; Zahra et al., 1999; Jansen et al., 2006), less on the antecedents of cooperative innovation speci<sup>fi</sup>cally (Sampson, 2007; Hull & Covin, 2010; Cui & O’Connor, 2012) and virtually nothing on the antecedents of innovating through the speci<sup>fi</sup>c phenomenon of community source. Community source networks have several unique features when compared with other networks. There are multiple levels of participants in community source, including institutional and individual levels. Little research has examined how network formation is affected by different levels of participants. The aim of our study is to <sup>fi</sup>ll this gap by examining how network formation is affected by institutional and individual factors in community source. We consider the experiences of the Kuali Initiative, a community source project in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of higher education, to deepen our understanding of the antecedents of the high-tech approach represented by community source.

This study attempts to make the following contributions: First, to enrich the open source literature by providing a framework to understand the individual and institutional factors affecting the network formation in a new type of open source community. Second, to shed light on the antecedents of a novel form of technology-enabled cooperative innovation. Third, to provide guidelines for institutions and top management to form or participate in open community software development by considering individual and institutional factors. Fourth, to provide guidelines fo community source foundations to manage and grow a community source network effectively

In Section on Literature Review, we brie<sup>fl</sup>y review the literature on networks, community source software development and innovation. We introduce the research methodology in Section on Research Methodology. In Section on Community Source: The Kuali Case, we introduce the Kuali community source case A research model and statements are ther presented in Section on Research Model and discussed in Section on Discussion. In Section on Study Implications, we discuss the implications of this study and future research directions. The paper concludes in Section on Limitations and Future Directions.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Our review focuses on the literature around community source and related phenomena, starting with inter-organizational networking and network formation before turning to some recent work on open source development. While we draw upon literature relating to enterprise software in universities, we do not focus on this literature, as our focus is on the formation of a general model of community source network formation (CSNF) in the context of innovation. A noteworthy discussion of this literature can be found in Pollock and Williams (2013), and the book in which their chapter appears presents a thorough look at the enterprise resource planning (ERP) <sup>fi</sup>eld.

There are many other literatures we could have reviewed in depth. Because this study focuses on an information systems-enabled approach to innovation, we only touch brie<sup>fl</sup>y upon the innovation literature as it applies to the community source approach instead of reviewing the literature in depth. Also, although in-depth literature reviews on the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed individual and institutional antecedent factors to CSNF would certainly enrich our study, these reviews are beyond the scope of the present study and are left to future research.

## Networks

Inter-organizational networking represents a particular form of organizing, or governing, exchange relationships among organizations (Ebers, 1997). While networking can take different forms, all networks are characterized by recurring exchange relationships among a limited number of organizations that retain residual control of their individual resources yet periodically make joint decisions about their use. Transaction cost economics (Richardson, 1972) applies to the formation of such inter-organizational networks as well as to the formation of corporations: The networks give each member organization enough control to get the results it needs without using more costly internal or external solutions. Consistent with transaction cost economics, Hoskisson and Busenitz (2002) predict that when developing needed knowledge in-house is too expensive and external solutions are unacceptable or too costly, organizations will turn to cooperative innovation, and subsequent studies have con<sup>fi</sup>rmed this tendency (e.g., Hull & Covin, 2010; Cui & O’Connor, 2012). Inter-organizational networks may serve other purposes but do appear to be effective cooperative innovation vehicles for some organizations (Cui & O’Connor, 2012).

Many studies offer observations and examinations of inter-organizational networks (Sydow, 1992; Alter & Hage, 1993; Mizruchi & Galaskiewicz, 1993; Grandori & Soda, 1995; Auster, 2010; Fjeldstad et al., 2012). Some have focused on the antecedents of inter-organizational networks. Panteli and Sockalingam (2005) examined trust and con<sup>fl</sup>ict within interorganizational alliances. Paulraj et al. (2008) extended the stream of research in supply chain management by investigating the antecedents and performance outcomes of interorganizational communication. The antecedents of inter-organizational network formation include motives, learning, trust, norms and monitoring, equity and context (Brass et al., 2004). Learning capability and technological parity interact to predict the successful use of inter-organizational cooperation (Hull & Covin, 2010). Antecedents of interpersonal networks include actor similarity, personality, proximity, organizational structure and environmental factors (Brass et al., 2004). Some of these variables may also explain the formation of community source networks.

Antecedents of network formation are studied in multiple levels and multiple dimensions as well. Koza and Lewin (1999) examined the antecedents of network formation at the interpersonal level through a longitudinal case study of the creation of a professional service network meant to produce cross-border referrals in the public accounting industry. Arora and Gambardella (2010) looked at individual motivations and group af<sup>fi</sup>liation, <sup>fi</sup>nding that the decision to af<sup>fi</sup>liate with a group signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly impacts both short-term and long-term engagement with the group. Their <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest a possible virtual cycle related to the creation and sustaining of ‘swift trust’, with group af<sup>fi</sup>liation leading to higher levels of participation and trust, which in turn lead to higher levels of commitment to the organization (Jarvenpaa et al., 1998; Robert et al., 2009). Chang and Chuang (2011) also examined short-term and long-term engagement in virtual organizations, shedding light on how they affect the roles members take in virtual communities.

Networks have to address three conceptually distinct dimensions of legitimacy: the network as form, the network as entity and the network as interaction (Human & Provan, 2000). Failure to build legitimacy across the three dimensions may lead to network collapse. Both earning the trust of the individual actors and <sup>fi</sup>nding partners with similar knowledge bases may also prove important in community source, although this question has not yet been examined. We also note that studying the formation of a community source network, which if successful will exist for many years or even decades, is best performed through a strategic ethnography/Biography of Artefacts approach (Pollock & Williams, 2009, 2013), which involves looking at multiple entities engaging with the network over an extended period of time. This sort of study will give us the breadth of understanding needed to adequately understand network formation in this context.

## Community source

Open source software (OSS) has attracted considerable commercial interest because of its promise to cheaply and reliably address organizational needs (Agerfalk & Fitzgerald, 2008; Fjeldstad et al., 2012; Kilamo et al., 2012; Reed et al., 2012), particularly with respect to new online ventures (Jelonek, 2015). OSS and OSS development have triggered research on a variety of topics (Sharma et al., 2002; Hauge et al., 2010; Madon & Sharanappa, 2013). These include OSS seen as a new innovation model (von Hippel & Krogh, 2003), the motivations of OSS developers (Hertel et al., 2003) and OSS business models (von Hippel & Krogh, 2003). One of the trends in open source is more use of community development (Fitzgerald, 2006). Osterlie and Jaccheri (2007) focus on studying large, successful, community-driven OSS projects. Fitzgerald (2006) argues that the OSS phenomenon has evolved into a more commercially viable form where volunteers and commercial organizations collaboratively contribute to evolving the phenomenon. Benlian and Hess (2011) demonstrate that, when considering open source products, companies use different criteria than when considering purchasing from traditional vendors. Bergquist and Ljungberg (2001) point out that creating a critical mass of users as a community is important for the construction of a symbolic attraction surrounding open source development style. Krogh et al. (2012) develop a framework to better understand the motivation for contributors to join the open source community. Crowston et al. (2013) study the recruitment ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency issue for sustaining a collaborative community, <sup>fi</sup>nding that the non-compensatory nature of the typical open source community can make recruiting and retaining new members a challenge. Because the individual participants in community source are actually paid to do the work and because the organizational participants have <sup>fi</sup>nancial reasons to participate as well, this seems less of an issue for community source. Sharma et al. (2002) predict, correctly, both that for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t companies would have trouble adopting OSS and that OSS would eventually transform the software industry. From the studies we mentioned earlier, it is clear that the research in open source is focused primarily on creating and maintaining community, with some attention given to innovation as well.

Arguably, the arguments of Sharma et al. (2002) could be interpreted as predicting the evolution, from OSS, of community source as an approach that would work for organizations. Community source, <sup>fi</sup>rst discussed by Wheeler (2004), has become a popular trend in the development of scalable and <sup>fl</sup>exible information systems developed by multiple organizations in collaboration (Liu et al., 2007).

The promise of community source builds on the recognized value of open source. Community source is like ordinary open source in that many collaborators work together to develop desired products. But while ordinary open source typically emphasizes individual participants, community source focuses on institutional participants with individuals participating as assigned by their employers. Community source is more formal than ordinary open source because in the former the systems are developed under formal governance structures. Also, community source is more entrepreneurial than ordinary open source because the systems developed in community source are suited to organizational participation (Liu et al., 2014b).

In community source, organizations collaborate to develop systems in innovative processes. Enterprise application systems often have to be customized and are complex, costly and large in scale (Perry & Quirk, 2007; Pollock & Williams, 2009). For organizations whose main business does not involve software development, developing such applications internally would be prohibitively expensive (Hoskisson & Busenitz, 2002; Teo & Bhattacherjee, 2014). Commu nity source cooperation with other organizations to develop such systems can lower costs below those associated with either the internal or external approach by creating a new single source for the product that will reliably meet speci<sup>fi</sup>cations at a low price, making it the best option (Athaide & Zhang, 2011). Other potential costs, such as the risk of partners expropriating potential new products, may increase (Haeussler et al., 2012), but the overall costs of this option may still be the lowest. The technology, the organizational structure and the underlying values of the participant organizations are all signi<sup>fi</sup>cant factors in making community source an effective solution (Liu et al., 2014a). Like virtual teams, community source networks operate as virtual organizations, with project members belonging to geographically and culturally diverse parent organizations.

The structure, origins and potential cost bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of community source all suggest that this new form of cross-organizational collaborative entrepreneurship requires careful study (Liu et al., 2010: Liu et al, 2012). Several issues in community-based development of enterprise applications have been studied. On one hand, it is optimal for the community source project to attract as many participating organizations as possible in order to share resources, reduce costs and minimize risks (Liu et al., 2014a). On the other hand, the management of community source projects becomes increasingly complicated and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult with the increasing number o participating institutions (Liu et al., 2014). Achieving project success in community source poses new challenges that are not found in the development of commercial software or conventional OSS such as Linux (Liu et al., 2010). Community source can be understood as a hybrid developmental model, positioned between commercial software and OSS (Raymond, 2004). The community source model inherits strengths from both commercial software and OSS. However, despite its signi<sup>fi</sup>cant promise, community source development is not a panacea. Challenges associated with the community source model include dealing with limited access to development talent, managing levels of institutional commitment, coordinating across multiple regulatory regimes and determining pricing mechanisms (as one respondent called it, ‘the price of buying free’) (Liu, Hansen, & Tu, 2014).

So far, community source has focused on vertical integration to resolve supply chain issues, with partners cooperating to create and control a dependent supplier that will innovate as needed (Jean et al., 2012). The risk of knowledge appropriation is low because the entrepreneurial venture is focused on providing higher quality inputs at lower prices, rather than on competitive new products. As in other forms of open source projects, knowledge and products used and developed in the community source project are meant to be shared. Thus, community source projects resemble innovation intermediaries that are run by the organizations they serve. Both coordinate across their clients, share information with them, aggregate supply and demand, et cetera (Tran et al., 2010). In community source, participant organizations act entrepreneurially to create a shared intermediary to serve their own needs rather than creating new products for customers.

Community source attracts widespread and deep interest in higher education. Because universities are badly served by commercial application software, more and more of them are turning to open source (Wheeler, 2004). Wheeler (2007) argues that developing a collaborative capability is not an option but a necessity for effective college and university IT organizations. Some examples of prominent community source projects include the Kuali project (www. kuali.org), the uPortal Project (www.uportal.org), the Sakai Project and the Open Source Portfolio Initiative. The Kuali project began in 2004 as a <sup>fi</sup>nancial information system for higher education and so far is the most high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le and well-funded project in community source. In this paper, Kuali is examined through a strategic ethnography, a theoretically informed, multi site, real-world, on-going case (Pollock & Williams, 2013) to better understand the community source phenomenon. By examining data from many institutions across more than a decade from when the Kuali network was founded to the present, we are better able to see wider forces that are not easily detectable with a briefer snapshot of a single site (Pollock & Williams, 2009, 2013). The other open source projects are also impactful. uPortal (2000) is a \$3.2 million open source project of JA-SIC to create portal software for education; Sakai (www.sakaiproject.org), launched in 2004, is a \$6.8 million open source project to develop a next-generation course management system; and Open Source Portfolio Initiative (www.theospi.org), started in January 2003, addresses the software needed for student electronic portfolios (Hansen-Shinnerl, 2008). Each of these is an entrepreneurial venture created to supply the organizations that created it. Community source projects also appear in industries outside of higher education. For example, in 2007, a group of leading IT companies, including Google, T-Mobile, Intel, Qualcomm and Samsung, formed Open Handset Alliance and launched an initiative called Android to develop an array of innovative infrastructure platforms and software applica tions for mobile technologies.

The community source network studied here consists mainly of non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t universities. Steinberg (1993) suggests several objective functions for non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, such as maximizing budgets, inputs and social welfare; all of which are unrelated to pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. Also, organizationa research has shown that non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts are driven by resource enhancement (Pfeffer & Leong, 1977; Provan et al., 1980; Chang & Tuckman, 1990; Alexander, 1996; Kraatz & Zajac, 1996). Non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts may typically be more constrained than businesses in terms of scope of innovation, but innovation that lets them pursue their current mission more effectively with the same resources <sup>fi</sup>ts the constraints (Hull & Lio, 2006; Jaskyte, 2011). Lam (2007) found that universities are particularly valuable partners because they have more open knowledge structures with less focus on pro<sup>fi</sup>t, which allows partners to learn more from universities than from businesses. An entrepreneurial community source arrangement in which the partners are universities may be particularly effective (Liu et al., 2014b).

Finally, despite the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using community source discussed here, the number of organizations participating in this approach is relatively low. Little is known about what drives organizations to form and participate in community source organizations, or to choose not to participate despite the advantages.

## Innovation

Given the importance of innovation to the organization (Ireland et al., 2009), all approaches to innovation deserve scholarly attention, whether it be innovating inside the organization, outside it or across company borders (Lengnick-Hall, 1991, 1992; Hoskisson & Busenitz, 2002; Sampson, 2007; Chesbrough et al., 2008; Hull & Covin, 2010; Iivari, 2010; Pérez-Luño et al., 2011). It has been argued theoretically and demonstrated empirically that the modes of innovation – internal, cooperative and external – are functionally different approaches to innovation that arise under different circumstances (Damanpour, 1991; Hull & Covin, 2010). Despite clear calls to further study the less-understood modes, such as cooperative innovation (Zahra et al., 1999), the literature still tends to focus on internal innovation more than it should (Sampson, 2007). The existing literature lacks detail about the approaches used to pursue cooperative innovation and calls for more detailed study (Hull & Covin, 2010). OSS has been studied as a novel approach to innovation (Sharma et al., 2002; von Hippel & Krogh, 2003), but although community source represents a novel vehicle for cooperative innovation through a technology-based inter-organization network, it has not, to our knowledge, been previously studied as a means of cooperative innovation. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the question of why more organizations are not using this novel and effective approach to cooperative innovation needs to be addressed. By examining the antecedents of CSNE, we help address this question. Because we are using the strategic ethnographic methodology called for by Pollock and Williams (2009, 2013), we are able to examine this question across the current life span of the phenomenon, from when it was a theoretical and unproven method in 2004 up to the present day when it is a proven and well-established approach to cooperative innovation.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

To examine how network formation is affected by institutional and individual factors, respec tively, in community source, we follow the guidance of Pollock and Williams (2013) in using a Biography of Artefacts approach. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we present a real-world community source project called Kuali spanning from 2004 to the present and including over 100 participating organizations. So doing allows us to understand the phenomena, we are examining with greater depth than if we were to look at one organization at a single moment in time (Pollock & Williams, 2009, 2013). Our data collection and analyses focus on discerning institutiona factors and individual factors affecting network formation in Kuali.

## Overview of research methodology

The single case study methodology enables investigation of a contemporary phenomenon within its natural setting (Lee, 1989a; Yin, 2009). The longitudinal case study is appropriate for ‘how’, ‘why’ and ‘what’ questions (Dubé & Paré, 2003; Paré, 2004) as it fosters contextual understanding (Pollock & Williams, 2009, 2013; Yin, 2009). It is especially appropriate for the early stage of research on a topic in order to extend emergent theory (Eisenhardt, 1989). Network formation under community source is a long-term process because the network is formed in an open community, and new members keep on joining the network. The longitudina nature of our study allows us to observe the network formation process as events unfold (Pollock & Williams, 2009, 2013).

The data collection effort included semi-structured interviews with Kuali participants and observers, direct observation of project events, milestone review presentations and minutes of meetings, and formal project documentation. Interview transcripts, observational <sup>fi</sup>eld notes and documentary evidence were coded using NVivo. a qualitative analysis tool. The data collection and analysis were conducted in line with grounded theory methodology, including adherence to the principles of constant comparison and open, axial and selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). We chose to use Strauss and Corbin’s (1990) approach because it is a wonderful method of conceptual description and has given us an in-depth portrayal of network formation in community source. The description was conceptual in that it delineated multiple levels of antecedents associated with the network formation. The hardest part of grounded theory analysis is moving beyond description and into conceptualization and theorizing. Strauss and Corbin’s paradigm model helps us to think of cause and effect that we rely on to explain why and how the network formation in community source occurs. By using this paradigm, we are able to capture as much as possible of the complexity and activity in the real world (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

For the past 10 years, one of the authors has been observing the Kuali community through participating in Kuali annual conferences, talking to Kuali management teams, interviewing

Kuali members and observers and subscribing to a mailing list. This observation derives insigh from re<sup>fl</sup>ection on embedded, longitudinal, lived experience (Lewis, 1985; Myers, 1999).

The main respondents in this stage were deployment partners and commercial af<sup>fi</sup>liates. The fourth round of interviews was conducted when Kuali provided standard platform to integrate eight projects. ## COMMUNITY SOURCE: THE KUALI CASE

Indiana University started the Kuali (www.kuali.org) community source project in 2004 when it recognized the need to move its <sup>fi</sup>nancial information system to the Web. Indiana University joined with the University of Hawaii to begin building Kuali in August of 2004. In 2005, four new partners, Cornell University, San Joaquin Delta College, Michigan State University and the University of Arizona, joined as core investors. In April 2006, the Kuali project announced the availability of the Kuali Test Drive, which lets universities explore KFS. In 2015, Kuali had more than 100 development partners, including Carnegie Mellon University, Colorado State University, Cornell University, Indiana University and Massachusetts Institute of Technology. While this is unlike the typical entrepreneurial venture, it resembles the social entrepreneurship model in which ventures are started with the intention of solving a social or shared problem rather than making a large pro<sup>fi</sup>t (Madon & Sharanappa, 2013).

Kuali uses a community source approach to allow member universities to share the costs and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of system development. Although the Kuali project received \$200 000 from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation, member universities provide most of its resources. A Kuali development partner must contribute between \$500 000 and \$100 000, 25% in cash and 75% in personnel costs. Universities not in the development community can pay about \$25 000 per year to collaborate in the deployment and customization efforts. Universities that want the results without any involvement in the process can download the software for free.

As mentioned earlier, and consistent with transaction cost economics, Hoskisson and Busenitz (2002) predict that when the cost of developing needed knowledge in-house is pro: hibitive and external solutions are unacceptable or too costly; organizations will turn to cooperative innovation. The Kuali project perfectly illustrates this outcome. Kuali was created because universities’ <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems were outdated and dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to maintain, and commercial products were expensive and hard to customize (Pollock & Cornford, 2004). As a result, some universities found themselves operating expensive ‘shadow systems’ to provide needed features not provided by the available ERP packages. But building a new <sup>fi</sup>nancial system in-house is equally daunting and can only be considered by the largest universities. The border-spanning Kuali project provided an alternative to the ‘buy or build’ model. It pooled institutional resources to develop a <sup>fi</sup>nancial system through a new entrepreneurial-style organization, dramatically reducing the cost of managing <sup>fi</sup>scal data and processes in highe education.

Figure 1 shows the structure of the Kuali project and how community source projects in general could manage their development partners. The Kuali project team works in a virtua environment but a formal organization, which ensures close collaboration among partner universities. The project managers supervise the project staff and development staff. The Kuali functional council and Kuali technical council take care of functional and technical issues. The extended board, Kuali investors, Kuali functional council, Kuali technical council and the project managers report to the Kuali Board, the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision-making body.

The initial mission of the Kuali consortium was to develop a baseline system for <sup>fi</sup>nancial services (KFS). It was later expanded to other systems such as research administration (Kuali Coeus) and student management (Kuali Student). Since then it has gone on to include eight systems commonly used across higher education institutions. The service-centric approach of Kuali makes it possible to ‘develop once and use anywhere and on any platform’. Each new module produced by Kuali saves its members and other users time, money and effort. As a community source project, Kuali balances varying requirements from many universities. Thus, the Kuali system needs to be as customizable and extensible as possible. Because most organizations that adopt Kuali are well established, with many legacy systems, it is also important to make Kuali good at integrating with and absorbing legacy content.

## RESEARCH MODEL

Because community source projects require formal collaboration and frequent communication, individual and institutional networks are heavily relied upon in the projects. The individuals in the Kuali example include managers and developers from partner universities, while the institutions are partner institutions of Kuali. The analysis of the Kuali community reveals a vari ety of antecedents of CSNF consisting of individual factors and institutional factors

## Individual factors

Individual decision makers are a major factor in the development of virtual organizations (Fjeldstad et al., 2012; Li et al., 2012; Mukherjee et al., 2012). In community source networks, the individuals are decision makers and developers from partner institutions. The individual factors that we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as affecting community source networks include motives, learning and trust.

## Motives

Institutional ties in community source are similar to those in consortia, which allow universities to achieve common objectives, sharing cost and risk.

The goal of Kuali is for many universities to share resources to develop information systems while minimizing the risk and expense of system development. Top managers, whose motivation for joining the community source plays an important role in the decision-making process, make decisions about IT investment. Their main motives for joining are resource sharing and cost reduction. There are other motives, such as early deployment and risk control, but if cost is not a concern, institutions can pay more to speed up the development process and better control the risk.

The Institutions that decide to join the community source network have strong motives for implementing the systems.

… But I think the underlying assumption of us joining this partnership was that we would strongly consider implementing it. What else would you do it? – Development Team Leader of a Large Private University - University I, a Development Partner of Kual

The motives of top management play a critical role in joining the community source network.

…when I heard about the possibility of an open source <sup>fi</sup>nancial information system in 2003, I have to say, from the very beginning, I actually thought this was a remarkably sensible idea. And the reason for that was, my background, I’m a computer scientist… - Top Management of a Large Public University – University A, a Founding Partner of Kuali

Thus, it seems clear that using community source to solve an innovation need has swift appeal to potential members who are motivated. The question of what drives this motivation is beyond the present study but worthy of future consideration. More signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly, however, is that some possible participants are motivated to avoid community source. Motivation can be a negative antecedent as well, and just as some participants immediately recognized Kual as something they wanted to join, others immediately chose not to participate. One reason for this might be that vertical integration, collaborative or otherwise, is outside of the perceived scope of the university’s mission. More likely, although, is that the decision makers in these organizations simply were not motivated to pursue an alternative that appeared to them to be unorthodox.

## Learning

That Kuali developers help create the system is a bene<sup>fi</sup>t for partner universities. In the deploy ment stage, they can deploy the system at lower cost and in less time than non-partner universities because their developers have become Kuali experts. In addition, it appears early adopters may enjoy more in<sup>fl</sup>uence than late adopters. Universities that have developers with strong learning capabilities will bene<sup>fi</sup>t more by being early adopters. Not only will they have more say over the end result, they also have the opportunity to learn faster by being deeply embedded in the Kuali process. In contrast, universities that have developers with weak learning capabilities will not be able to exploit this opportunity. Therefore, institutions at which developers have strong technical abilities and learning capabilities have more incentives to join as a development partner.

…They (the developers) are critical, …You can actually have people go in and look to see how things work. We asked many, many questions where we weren’t sure. …Without it being open source, without having developers on the team, you don’t get that. – Development Team Leader of a Small Teaching College – University G, a Development Partner of Kuali

… With Kuali, as opposed to a vendor solution, the investment we make in software is an investment in our own people. The individuals at our institution become the subject matter experts regarding the design and functionality of the software. Through this, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that we are nimble and better able to react ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and effectively to the rapid evolution of technology. – System Developer Leader of a Public Research University – University K, a Development Partner of Kuali

Although there is some overlap with the cost-reduction motive, this factor seems to be separate in the minds of participants, more closely linked to the ability to bene<sup>fi</sup>t from participating than to the desire to enjoy these bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. The bene<sup>fi</sup>t from participating seems to be largely the opportunity to learn more and develop a deeper understanding of systems. Organizations, which do not employ developers with the ability to learn from participating in such systems, or which suffer from high levels of developer turnover such that the institutional learning from the experience would be minimal, might not see participating in Kuali as a learning opportunity but as a threat – both a distraction and an opportunity for rival universities to poach their talent. Without signi<sup>fi</sup>cant learning capability, an organization is poorly suited to cooperative or interna innovation and is better off buying the solutions it needs (Hoskisson & Busenitz, 2002; Hull & Covin, 2010).

…we aggregate a community there that knows each other, trust each other, learns from each other, support each other… One of the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is we really got our developers knowledgeable about the system through learning… – Team Leader of KFS of a Public Research University, University L, Development and Deployment Partner of Kual

Universities compete with one another on academics, not in ancillary services. While one of these universities might not hesitate to improve its academic rankings at the expense of the others, the collaboration-based learning in this case is focused on managing costs, not on a differentiating feature and, thus, seems less likely to inspire future competition. Despite this, trust in partner organizations remains an issue, which may be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant as it seems to be tied to the learning advantages, among others, offered by participation in community source.

## Trust

The way Kuali is managed illustrates the importance of trust in the decision to join a community source project. To become a development partner, a university that joins Kuali needs to contribute a sizeable amount of money and resources, which will be lost if the community source project fails. Decision makers would not join a community source network if they distrusted the other participants. It may be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for them to trust the Kuali community source foundation if their university has not worked with it before. However, they can consider the community source partners and trust based on existing information, such as the identity and reputation of the partner universities and speci<sup>fi</sup>c individual participants, their technical capability or the quality of the base Kuali system.

We joined Kuali in late stage. The objective for our <sup>fi</sup>rst trip to Kuali Days was actually <sup>fi</sup>nding out the reason why we should not go down the Kuali path. After we talked to quite a few well known universities including Indiana, Cornel etc, we felt that we need to seriously look at this path. What they (these well- known universities) shared with us helped us to make the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision to join Kuali…– Functional Team Leader of a State University - University J, a Development Partner of Kual

This trust may happen quite quickly for some institutions.

Sometimes the <sup>fi</sup>rst impression you give is important. I had an institution join Kuali Days for one day, and decide to join. I think that is because of the immediate trust to the community. – Top Management, Kuali Foundation

Although Kuali is attracting more and more institutions to join the network, quite a few institutions are still observing without making the decision to join. Most of the observers lack trust in the community source foundation and its existing members.

There’s always a question of the open source site, community sources even, more sketchy in this area, of who wrote it, how good is it, who’s going to keep it up, as soon you download it, is it out-customed and now I have to <sup>fi</sup>nd the resources to maintain it and keep it valid. Those are the kinds of questions that you have to sort through. – Top Management from a Private University- University U, an Observer of Kuali

…and then the viability of it long term. Any vendor can go out of business or choose to leave the business, right? So there’s always a risk to some degree but it’s lesser for a big company like Oracle…–Top Management from a Private University – University W, an Observer of Kuali

Decision makers with more trust in the community source partners and foundation – in its ability perhaps more so than in its motives – are more con<sup>fi</sup>dent of the project’s success and are more likely to decide to participate. We note that, as discussed earlier, this openness and willingness to trust other universities may be limited to universities or, possibly, non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. O it might be more related to whether the cooperation is intended to reduce costs or to provide competitive advantage, with parallel organizations to Kuali operating successfully among businesses so long as the community source projects focus on cost management rather than differentiation. In either case, however, it seems clear that trust is a positive antecedent and distrust a negative one for community source participation.

## Institutional factors

We found that CSNF is also affected by institutional factors: norms and monitoring, institutiona similarity, external funding, hostile external environment and information technology.

## Norms and monitoring

The Kuali monitoring system is well designed. Kuali systems are developed under a formal governance structure, and the participants agree that this hierarchical structure helps solve problems. Institutions are more willing to join the Kuali network and cooperate because socia controls arising from norms and monitoring govern the collaboration.

As a community college, we must comply with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant regulations, often more so than a university. Our experience has been that while vendors would like, in theory, to accommodate our needs, we often are not a priority and we do not have a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant voice in the application development. With Kuali, we not only have a voice, but we also are designers and architects. We work with leading institutions, not as partners in name only but as full partners in development. – Top Management of a Small Community College – University H, a Development Partner of Kual

The Kuali board plays a critical role in enforcing the norms and monitoring system so that the large and small universities can have equal access to resources.

We have very clear development scope for KFS. We are very careful not to develop the system out of scope. For example, Cornell University used to ask something which is out of scope. We did not do it, Finally Cornell build the part they asked for by themselves and contribute back to Kuali later on. …If we decide to do outsourcing in future project, <sup>fi</sup>xed scope is critical. – Functional Council Leader of the Kuali Board

The development partner can make the request to build certain features they want in the system. The request will be voted on by each development partner. The Kuali board will make the <sup>fi</sup>nal decision for whether to accept this request. – Team Leader of KFS Development, the Kuali Foundation

It thus seems clear that strong norms and careful monitoring are important to creating and maintaining successful community source networks. They may not be suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to attract partners who would otherwise stay away, but it seems certain that weak norms and careless monitoring will prevent prospective members from joining.

## Institutional similarity

Institutional similarity encourages network formation because similar entities tend to interact more than dissimilar ones. As mentioned earlier, equity and context, two dimensions Brass et al. (2004) see as separate, here emerged as part of actor similarity. In this study, an acto is treated as an institution instead of an individual. Therefore, institutional similarity is an institu tional factor because we look at institutional partners’ similarity. The institutions that are attracted to Kuali have similar features, such as feeling comfortable with sharing resources and being willing to take the risk, collaborate and be innovative. Because the Kuali partners are similar in many dimensions, including equity and context, our study may not be positioned to evaluate the effects of these dimensions on the formation of community source. Diversity in context and equity might strengthen a community source project, and future studies of commu nity source that are better able to evaluate these dimensions would enrich our knowledge of how community source works.

However, we can evaluate the effects of institutional similarity. In Kuali, universities might each propose to add developer talent or cash, but the real value is their ability to deliver quality code, work well with others, honor deadlines/work assignments and contribute intellectually. If status and context indicate the ability to deliver, seeking partners similar in these dimensions seems reasonable. An institution with different values or staff members who do not work well with others could be problematic if it were to join and would likely sense this and choose not to participate. Institutional similarity in a community source network can be a sign that the insti tutions in the network will cooperate well with each other.

In the process of doing that now with eight projects, some people ask, “What’s the secret sauce (for sustaining this large community)? How does this work? How does this hold together?” I think the secret sauce is really shared values among partner institutions. Institutions coming to Kuali share the same value and have the same destiny. We are not beholden to anyone else. – Top Management of the Kuali Foundation

Kuali community is like a family since we share same value. The family environment really connects us together … – Team Leader of KFS of a Private University, University E, Develop ment Partner of Kual

Several partners started the Kuali project with investments and external funding. When new potential partners showed interest in joining, the Kuali foundation chose carefully which to admit. Kuali Foundation selects additional investors that have the requisite ability and the right philosophical <sup>fi</sup>t to join the network. For example, 2 years after Kuali began, University of Southern California (USC) was accepted as a development partner, but only after extensive review determined that USC <sup>fi</sup>t well into the Kuali project. The similar participants have a philosophical <sup>fi</sup>t to let them put their needs into a correct context.

… An institution can be a negative <sup>fi</sup>t when values are not aligned or their staff members do not work well with others. Often this is an issue of “<sup>fi</sup>t” and “philosophy” more than saying some developer or administrator is good or bad … – Top Management of the Kual Foundation

## External funding

In the traditional system-collaboration model, universities working together on a project to bene<sup>fi</sup>t themselves do not receive external funding. But external funding is one of the unusual features of a community source project. The Kuali foundation received an award of \$2.5m from the Andrew W. Mellon Foundation to start the project. Universities that joined Kuali bene<sup>fi</sup>ted from this external funding, and external funding is a motive for universities to form a community source network. Absent a seed, community source may not grow. But more external funding is not always better. If the amount of external funding is too small, it may not be enough incentive to form a community source network. If the external funding is too great, the partners can make small investments when joining, decreasing their commitment. If the potential loss if the community source project fails is small, the partner universities may not try as hard to ensure the project’s success. Thus, just the right amount of external funding is needed to start a community source network. In Kuali, \$2.5m in external funding was enough to start the project, leading universities to feel more secure about joining. An initial investment from each partner institution encourages them to collaborate closely to ensure success of the project.

…An issue was whether universities could take the steps necessary to harness these new schools, to pool our resources, to agree on a system design, and then to make it happen… And there’s nothing like funding to help spur consortium efforts. …And we came up with a recommendation to the Mellon board that funding such projects was an extremely good idea. That then led to considerable funding by the Mellon Foundation…– Top Management of a Large Research University- University B, a Founding Partner of Kuali

## External environment

Before Kuali appeared, very few vendors provided <sup>fi</sup>nancial systems to universities, so this resource was scarce. Universities that bought from these vendors were dependent upon them for software upgrades and maintenance. Kuali provides universities an alternative <sup>fi</sup>nancia system with lower cost and better quality, impacting the market of enterprise application system vendors. This cooperative innovation would not have been possible without a large number of similar institutions facing the same problem with the same resource constraints. If the environment had been more supportive of universities, the Kuali Foundation might have had more trouble attracting partner institutions.

Most universities have to buy expensive software not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed for higher education. Others are forced to develop software in-house, at high cost. Collaboration emerges as a very appealing option.

© 2016 Blackwell Publishing Ltd, Information Systems Journal … There’s a road map. It’s a small set of investors at this point, but just like other Kuali projects, institutions came together and said, “We are not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed with what vendors offered. We want to do a certain thing.” (We) established a work plan, and now that project is moving forward. – Top Management of the Kuali Foundation

The current vendor set a high price for implementing ERP systems in higher education, which forced universities to seek an alternative solution.

…They (A large state university) killed it (the project of implementing an ERP system) at 29 (million dollars). The new project (implementing an ERP system) that made the papers again in 2012 was \$81 million. When we started with Kuali in 2004, one of the big ideas behind Kuali was that we could help institutions put more of their money toward the mission of higher education by using the community models that reduced the administrative expense.–Top Management of a Commercial Af<sup>fi</sup>liate - Company X

If an institution’s <sup>fi</sup>nancial system is largely dependent on a few indifferent or unfriendly vendors, the institution might have more incentives to join a community source network so that it can deploy the community source system earlier and reduce its dependency upon predatory or indifferent suppliers. If, on the other hand, an institution’s suppliers are supportive and responsive, with high levels of competition for its business, then a community source solution is unlikely to happen at all.

## Information technology

Virtual team communication and distributed computing architecture, which simply did not exist 10 years before Kuali began, were essential to its creation and are essential to the creation of any similar cooperative arrangement. Even a group of universities that existed side by side would have had trouble creating Kuali with earlier technology – they would still have had to rely on face-to-face meetings and in-person collaboration. The forms of cooperative innovation available to them at that level of information technology would have been costly enough to force them to choose between the still-expensive external and internal options. But advances in information technology have changed the transaction cost equation and, by enabling community source, have made cross-border innovation affordable.

The development of the Internet networks and the development of regional optical networks were increasingly providing the level of high speed connectivity for universities and this greatly accelerated the work of community source development communities. Internet tools were maturing, and these encouraged cooperation.–Top Management of a Large Public Uni versity – University A, a Founding Partner of Kuali

As a small community college, we do not want to have any modi<sup>fi</sup>cations. Moving Kuali Ready (system) to cloud base is nice. We can simply use the software…–Top Management of a Small Community College – University H, a Development Partner of Kuali

Information technology has changed from that available in times past, and it continues to evolve. Community source is a new form of entrepreneurship enabled by advances in information systems. It could not have existed more than a few years before it actually came into being (cf. Wheeler, 2004). New variations on community source may develop in years to come, but universities, particularly those at the level that Kuali admits, are likely to be on the cutting edge. Many of the founding universities were among the <sup>fi</sup>rst organizations to connect to the internet. Now, small community colleges that might not have had access to the necessary technology even 10 years ago are able to blithely participate in community source using cloud technology. Other organizations in other industries and in less developed nations may not have access to the most current technologies. In some industries, suf<sup>fi</sup>cient technological parity may not exist (Hull & Covin, 2010), suggesting that organizations that can use the latest technology to form the most advanced sorts of cooperative arrangements may not <sup>fi</sup>nd enough partners to work with. Thus, widespread use of current communication and information technology within an industry may encourage the use of community source, while its use is unlikely in ‘low-tech industries or settings.

## DISCUSSION

The CSNF model adopts several factors suggested by Brass et al. (2004) and better re<sup>fl</sup>ects the CSNF. In Brass et al. (2004), the antecedents of inter-organizational network formation are motives, learning, trust, norms, equity and context. In a study of why businesses shunned open source solutions, Goode (2005) found that many of these antecedents were cited as reasons not to try the open source solution. While joining a community source project is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different in terms of commitment and control, these remain issues likely to affect whether an organization chooses to enter a community source project.

Collaboration of participants with no past relationships, as in a newly formed community source network, needs presumptive, swift trust from the start. We believe that swift trust can exist among participants of a virtual organization and is particularly important in virtual ventures such as community source projects. In our discussion of motives in Section on Motives, we referred to swift appeal, a parallel concept with adoption of an innovation instead of trust in a stranger as the outcome. The concept might appeal, but without equally swift trust in the other participants, the cooperative innovation of community source will have a hard time gaining members. Thus, distrust of prospective partners could incline organizations to avoid collabora tive arrangements such as community source. It is among institutions inclined to trust, and trust swiftly, that the community source approach is likely to arise.

As noted in our discussions of institutional similarity, community source seems more likely to arise among people at institutions that are similar along many dimensions, including reputation, norms and values. Exploring the relationship between institutional similarity and swift trus formation at the individual level is bevond the scope of this study. but institutional similarities on these points might make the formation of swift trust between individuals based on categories and stereotypes such as employer reputation easier. The universities in the network have similar status, power and cultures. To the degree that there are differences, we subsume equity and context into the concept of institutional similarity. Equity includes the status and power of institutions (Ostrom, 1990; Ring & van de Ven, 1992); context includes cultural, historical and institutional context. In Brass et al. (2004), actor similarity is an antecedent of interpersonal networks because actor similarity was de<sup>fi</sup>ned as individual similarity. In this study, institutiona similarity is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as similarity among institutions, and we examine it as one of the institutional factors affecting CSNF.

Even if institutional members are similar and individuals trust each other, problems will arise in the course of collaboration. Collaboration through digital means may be even more problematic: Even within single organizations, social media structures not carefully monitored may lead to con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the traditional structures that spawned them (Koch et al., 2013). Hierarchy (Williamson, 1975) is one solution for settling disputes, as are reciprocity norms (Ostrom, 1990) and rules of behavior (Kogut, 2000). Network structure can help enforce desired norms and rules.

In the Kuali case, three additional factors including external funding – which we argue can be the catalyst that starts groups of organizations on collaborative ventures such as community source – the external environment and information technology are also observed to play impor tant roles in CSNF. Therefore, we include these three factors in the CSNF framework.

The external environment impacts the formation of a network. In this case, unresponsive suppliers were an external threat to all the participants, which provided them a strong motivation to band together to pursue community source. Other external threats might produce similar results.

Information technology is a key factor as well, because community source is based on technology that did not exist more than a few years before community source was introduced. Information technology parity seems an important element of the information technology factor, given that all participants must have similar levels of information technology to participate.

## STUDY IMPLICATIONS

Our study offers both theoretical and practical insights. On the theoretical level, this study builds a basis for understanding the individual and institutional factors affecting CSNF and the decision of whether or not to join such networks. Community source is an effective and costef<sup>fi</sup>cient approach to innovation (Liu et al., 2014b), which is remarkably little-used. Now, we better understand why this is the case. Community source represents a still-novel approach to the broader and growing (Cui & O’Connor, 2012) category of cooperative innovation, the antecedents of which are still not fully understood (Hull & Covin, 2010). Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings, although they may not be perfectly generalizable, offer a basis for better understanding the antecedents of cooperative innovation in general as well. The existing literature in inter-organizational network formation does not capture the important features of the community source network. The framework developed by Grandori and Soda (1995) can be used as a basis for developing testable comparative models of inter-<sup>fi</sup>rm organizational coordination. However, this framework does not take the multiple levels of factors into consideration. Brass et al. (2004) reviewed research on the antecedents and consequences of networks at the interpersonal, interunit and interorganizational levels of analysis. But this model does not include critical factors in the community source network such as information technology and external environment. In particular, the role new information technology can play in enhancing a company’s innovation processes has yet to receive the attention it deserves in the innovation literature, an imbalance our study helps address. The CSNF model developed in our study contributes to the existing literature by categorizing the antecedents of inter-organizational networks into individual factors and institutional factors, and by recognizing the important role that information technology and external environment play in inter-organizational network formation. Further, this study adopts longitudinal study, which was seldom adopted by the existing networking literature. Network formation under community source is a long-term process. The longitudinal study allows us to have deeper understanding about the network formation process in community source. Therefore, we believe that the CSNF model is superior to previous models of new network formation.

On the practical level, the model developed in this study offers guidelines for planning an institution’s innovation strategy, particularly when the factors we describe indicate that community source is an effective solution. However, the community source approach might not be practical for all institutions. The CSNF model can help top management of institutions make wise decisions regarding if and when to engage in community source. For example, institutions should evaluate their developers’ learning capability to see if their developers can become experts in the community source approach. The ‘institutional similarity’ factor can help institutions understand the importance of sharing similar values with the development partners in a network if they are to join it. This study also provides guidelines for community source foundations to manage and grow their community source networks effectively. Community source foundations face the challenge of managing the growing community (Liu et al., 2014a). The CSNF model helps community source foundations to select partners with the right ‘<sup>fi</sup>t’ and sustain the community effectively.

## LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Several limitations and related future directions are as follows: First, our research framework and model are based on a strategic ethnography. Measures need to be developed to have more empirical validation. In future studies, quantitative survey data could be used to validate and improve the model. We would expect some degree of difference between different stakeholders in this network, as they may be more or less susceptible to particular aspects, and quantitative data would allow us to uncover these variations. Alternatively, we could use a natural experiment or natural controls (Lee, 1989a, 1989b) by observing institutions that consider joining, measuring the factors in our model and estimating the likelihood of each one joining. Over time, we would be able to re<sup>fi</sup>ne the predictive power of the model. This would also allow us to explore not only the formation of networks but the chances of their succeeding, a topic clearly needing furthe study.

Second, the perception of what is relevant for the formation of the community may have changed through time, as we tend to reinterpret the past in hindsight, changing our perception of what was relevant. Because our data extend back to when Kuali was forming, this is not a severe limitation, but regardless of how soon after events an interview is conducted, it will al ways offer a hindsight perspective on the topic.

Third, data collection in this study was con<sup>fi</sup>ned to higher education sectors and companies, which have af<sup>fi</sup>liations with higher education sectors. Whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could be generalized to all types of companies is therefore unclear. Universities and businesses are, arguably, different, particularly in knowledge creation and dissemination (Pollock & Cornford, 2004; Lam, 2007), so our results may not apply in industries where knowledge is more jealously guarded, and the risk of knowledge expropriation is high (Haeussler et al., 2012).

A promising avenue for future research is to study the antecedents of community source projects outside higher education. In other settings we may see different levels of equity and context, which will oblige us to adjust the model accordingly. Two ways for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t companies are likely to differ from non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t universities are the drive to make a pro<sup>fi</sup>t and the drive to innovate beyond their present scope (Hull & Lio, 2006). Many questions about for-pro<sup>fi</sup>t companies using community source remain: How do they choose to use community source? Can they work together to make community source happen? How does participating in community source affect a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s innovative output?

Through our examination of the Kuali case, we have found several antecedents of the use of community source as an open source mechanism for cooperative innovation. This case involves non-pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, but does demonstrate proof-of-concept for organizations in similar situations.

The present study offers both academic and practical conclusions. The model that emerged from our research suggests that several key factors should be considered when trying to estab lish or grow a community source project. Our study sheds light on what factors deserve the credit. The framework we have devised will, we hope, enrich the open source literature and, more speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the community source literature, by ordering the factors affecting CSNF, both individual (motives, learning and trust) and institutional (norms and monitoring, institutiona similarity, external funding, hostile external environment and information technology). Multilevel <sup>fi</sup>ndings such as ours are not commonplace, but often, as we believe they have carried out here, provide richer insight.

To have the best chances of bene<sup>fi</sup>tting from community source, the individuals involved in the process must be motivated and able to learn from the process and willing to trust their external partners. This last may be the choke point for businesses contemplating community source, although community source seems little different on matters of trust from other, more traditional collaborative approaches such as joint ventures or other formal alliances.

To have the best chances of bene<sup>fi</sup>tting from community source, the institutions involved in the process must have the necessary norms and monitoring in place across the network, have institutional similarity, be facing environmental pressures and use the necessary information technology. External funding appears to help and may function as a needed impetus to engage businesses in forming a community source network that they will maintain after it forms because of its obvious pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. This suggests a public policy implication as well: Modest government funding to start community source networks may be a powerful driver of innovation, leading to business growth, which leads in turn to more jobs.

As mentioned earlier, collaboration across company borders for purposes of innovating is a well-established phenomenon that is becoming more popular (Cui & O’Connor, 2012). As technology advances and continues to make it easier to pursue cooperative innovation, it is to be expected that new variations, such as community source, will become increasingly common. By highlighting the Kuali example, we hope to serve the innovation literature by shedding light on the larger issue of how organizations will collaborate to innovate in the future. While our focus here is on community source, we hope that our study will help direct more attention in the traditional innovation and technology management literature to the ways in which information systems can improve – and are improving – the available processes by which organizations pursue innovation.

Future directions for research in this area include testing and perhaps improving upon the model we have developed here, in the context of community source speci<sup>fi</sup>cally and more generally in the contexts of strategic alliances and cooperative innovation.

While academics can – and, we hope, will – test our model with quantitative data, the detailed case we present here may serve as a guide to practitioners hoping to establish community source organizations of their own and to practitioners involved in managing and growing com munity source organizations. Testimony from other practitioners who have succeeded will serve them well. Our model, based on our analysis of multiple interviews with Kuali organizers and participants, should help them choose when to consider forming a community source project, who to recruit for it, how to gain their trust and how to structure it. This practical guidance should increase the odds of their success.
