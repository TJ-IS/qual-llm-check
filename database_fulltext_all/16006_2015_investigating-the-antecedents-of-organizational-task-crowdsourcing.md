## Abstract

Crowdsourcing is gaining traction as a possible approach for organizational task solving. However, few studies have explored and statistically tested the antecedents behind organizations’ intention to crowdsource. This paper examines the antecedents of firms’ crowdsourcing intention based on transaction cost theory and the resource based view, and test them using survey data from 161 organizations. Results show that the perceived benefits of cost reduction, brand visibility, and access to specialized skills positively affect firms’ intention to crowdsource while codification cost and proposal evaluation cost negatively influence firms’ crowdsourcing intention both directly and indirectly through diminishing perceptions of cost reduction.

Keywords: Crowdsourcing, Transaction Cost Theory, Resource Based View, Intention to Crowdsource, TaskCN

## 1 Introduction

Crowdsourcing is a phenomenon in which an organization (i.e., seeker) recruits a large group of undefined individuals (i.e., solvers), referred to as the crowd, to work on organizational tasks through Internet-based platforms (Howe 2008). As the crowds are becoming increasingly connected and informed (Sawhney et al. 2005), they can contribute their distinct expertise to solve organizational problems (Estellés-Arolas and González-Ladrón-de-Guevara 2012; Howe 2008). Thus, firms are starting to leverage the wisdom of the crowd and realize the benefits of a crowdsourcing strategy (Zwass 2010).

Evidence of this trend can be seen from the emergence of crowdsourcing platforms, such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, InnoCentive, and TaskCN (Kaufman et al. 2011; Yang et al. 2009). For example, Amazon Mechanical Turk hosts more than 100,000 tasks every day<sup>1</sup>. TaskCN is one of the largest crowdsourcing platforms in China for performing tasks such as website development, advertisement posting, and business proposal writing. Even established market leaders such as Google and Dell have recognized the potential of crowdsourcing. In 2008, Google funded a \$10 Million crowdsourcing project (Project 10^100) that called for ideas from the crowd to change the world (Yang et al. 2010). Dell launched the IdeaStorm platform to collect innovation ideas from the public in 2007 (Bayus 2013). Till September 2014, more than 548 ideas have been implemented <sup>2</sup>.

However, seeker firms encounter challenges in carrying out crowdsourcing activities. They may face issues in selecting the tasks to crowdsource and of appropriately formulating them so as to obtain satisfactory solutions. Particularly, ambiguously defined tasks can mislead solvers, resulting in seeker firms receiving unsatisfactory solutions (Afuah and Tucci 2012; Doan et al. 2011). Crowdsourcing necessitates firms’ employees to work with crowdsourcing platforms to specify task requirements, provide feedback for further improvement of solvers’ proposal, and select the final solutions (Sieg et al. 2010). For certain tasks, it may be arduous to transfer specifications from seeker firms to solvers to allow

These activities may incur costs for seekers (e.g., in terms of employee time and effort) contrary to the purpose of crowdsourcing, i.e., lowering the cost of solving organizational tasks. Prior research in this area (Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Schenk and Guittard 2011) has mainly investigated what motivates firms to crowdsource tasks, and through conceptual or case studies. In a conceptual paper, Schenk and Guittard (2011) proposed that crowdsourcing enables firms to mobilize external competencies for internal tasks at a lower cost. A descriptive study of Mechanical Turk (Kittur 2010) suggested that crowdsourcing can be a less expensive way to solve firms’ problems and a viable approach to obtain creative solutions. However, previous research on seekers’ crowdsourcing behavior consists mainly of conceptual (Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Schenk and Guittard 2011), or case studies (Kittur 2010; Yang et al. 2008) proposing the benefits of crowdsourcing for seekers. Thus, there is an absence of theoretically-driven empirical research that can explain the antecedents (including the costs) of seekers’ participation in crowdsourcing (see a thorough review by Zhao and Zhu 2012). These research gaps provide motivation for this study.

This paper aims to address this knowledge gap by developing a model to answer the following research question: what drives (or inhibits) firms to crowdsource internal tasks? Drawing on the resource based view and transaction cost theory, a context specific model was developed to explain seeker firms’ intention to crowdsource. The model proposes perceived resources obtainable that will enhance firms’ intention to crowdsource and costs that will reduce their intention to crowdsource. The model is tested through a field survey of 161 seeker firms in TaskCN, one of the largest crowdsourcing platforms in China. The results are expected to contribute to research and practice by enhancing understanding of the determinants of firms’ intention to crowdsource internal tasks.

## 2 Conceptual Background

This section reviews and identifies theories i.e., the resource based view and transaction cost theory,

#

which could shed light on firms’ intention to crowdsource. Subsequently, relevant constructs for the model are conceptualized, informed by these theories and previous related literature.

Previous literature (Afuah and Tucci 2012; Schenk and Guittard 2011) has noted similarities between crowdsourcing and outsourcing, as both employ external talents to work on organizational tasks. For both strategies, uncertainties exist in the process of managing external parties and in the final outcomes. Given these similarities, theories used in the outsourcing literature could be applicable in the context of crowdsourcing (Schenk and Guittard 2011). Thus, we review theories employed in the outsourcing literature and examine their relevance to understanding crowdsourcing behavior. Three broad perspectives have been commonly used to explain the determinants of outsourcing, i.e., strategic, economic, and relational perspectives (Heng et al. 2009). The first perspective is concerned with what strategy organizations formulate for outsourcing and how they allocate resources to pursue the strategy and obtain a desirable performance. It includes lenses such as the resource based view (Barney 1991), the knowledge based view (Grant 1991), resource-dependency theory (Pfeffer and Salancik 1978), firm strategy theory (Rumelt 1984), and game theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern 1944). Second, the economic perspective is related to the strategic view and considers how benefits of outsourcing can be achieved in an efficient way, with minimal costs. It includes lenses such as transaction cost theory (Coase 1937), agency theory (Eisenhardt 1989), and incomplete contracts theory (Hart 1988). Third, the relational perspective is concerned with how organizations develop relationships with external partners and their implications for outsourcing (Dibbern et al. 2004). It includes lenses such as social exchange theory (Blau 1964), social capital theory (Nahapiet and Ghoshal 1998), and institutional theory (Powell and Dimaggio 1991).

Crowdsourcing is considered a strategic as well as an economic choice for organizations (Yang et al. 2008). Firms need to weigh the strategic and economic benefits against the costs before they decide to engage in crowdsourcing activities. Thus, we consider the strategic and economic perspectives as relevant for this study. Further, in the context of crowdsourcing, the relationship between solvers and seekers is transaction-based and short term (Sun et al. 2012; Yang et al. 2008). Seekers may not be able to form a

#

relationship with particular solvers since for each task there are usually a large number of solvers and the same solvers may not participate across tasks <sup>3</sup>. Therefore, compared to the strategic and economic perspectives, the relational perspective may be less relevant in this context (cf. Sun et al. 2012). Since crowdsourcing involves leveraging the crowd to solve organizational problems rather than solving them internally (Howe 2008), this is similar to firms’ decision to make vs. buy. Among the theories under the economic perspective, transaction cost theory is concerned about the decisions of organizations whether to buy particular products/services from external sources or make them internally (Williamson 1985). Thus, we consider it appropriate to model the costs in our model.

Also, crowdsourcing serves as a means for firms to access resources that are unavailable within the organization (Leimeister et al. 2009) and to obtain novel solutions from the crowd (Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Schenk and Guittard 2011). Given these characteristics, we consider the resource based view a suitable theoretical lens to understand firms’ crowdsourcing decision, since it posits that a firm will look to external sources for resources that are lacking inside (Barney 1991). As a complementary lens, the resource based view (RBV) together with transaction cost theory is used here to explain seekers’ intention to crowdsource.

However, several characteristics differentiate crowdsourcing from outsourcing. First, crowdsourcing aims to recruit undefined individuals to work on organizational tasks (Howe 2008) rather than recruiting selected external vendors in outsourcing. All solvers in the crowdsourcing platform are invited to tackle problems through an open call, unlike in outsourcing. This could be a channel for firms to advertise their brands (Libert and Spector 2010) (e.g., brand visibility). Second, the quality of solutions obtained from crowdsourcing has less possibility of being controlled or guaranteed as compared to outsourcing. Solvers in crowdsourcing platforms may not be specialized in a particular area and some solutions, even if novel, may be difficult to implement (Poetz and Schreier 2012). This is different from outsourcing where the firm can carefully monitor the development and implementation of the solution to ensure desired quality. Third, in general, multiple solution providers simultaneously work on the same task in crowdsourcing (e.g., solution diversity) while not in outsourcing. As a result, firms have more choices of solutions in crowdsourcing and need to expend resources to select the best solution (e.g., proposal evaluation cost). Overall, the differences indicate that firms’ crowdsourcing requires investigation in its own right. In this study, we have adapted the theories used in outsourcing literature to the context of crowdsourcing by theorizing the effects of context specific factors (i.e., solution diversity, brand visibility, and proposal evaluation cost), which will be explained next.

## 2.1 Transaction and Production Costs in Crowdsourcing

Transaction cost theory (TCT), initially developed by Coase (1937) and extended by Williamson (Williamson 1981; Williamson 1985), is mainly used to identify the conditions under which firms will performed by the market (buy). It argues that these decisions are made by balancing two types of costs i.e., production costs and transaction costs, to achieve efficiency, which is important for organizations to sustain competitive advantage in the market (Williamson 1981). Production costs are the costs required to make the product or to provide the service, such as the cost of capital, labor, and materials. Transaction costs are the coordination costs for the activity such as searching and planning, information communication, negotiating and process monitoring, and performance evaluation. TCT proposes that firms should perform tasks internally when transactions costs exceed external production savings and outsource when internal production costs are high and present comparative disadvantage (Liang and Huang 1998).

Transaction costs are proposed to depend on various contingencies, such as asset specificity and uncertainty (Williamson 1985). Asset specificity refers to durable investments that are undertaken in support of particular transactions, the opportunity cost of which investment is much lower in best alternative uses or by alternative users (Williamson 1985). The party who has invested in the asset will incur a loss if the party who has not invested withdraws from the transaction. Transactions that are

#

supported by high asset specificity should be governed by hierarchical structures (make internally), whereas transactions that require only general-purpose investments will most efficiently be conducted over markets (buy). Uncertainty refers to the cost associated with unexpected outcomes and information asymmetry (Liang and Huang 1998). Greater uncertainty of a transaction generally implies a higher transaction cost, indicating that the activity should be conducted in the organization. This premise has been supported by empirical findings that projects with lower uncertainty will be outsourced (e.g., Aubert et al. 2004; Goo et al. 2007).

In the context of crowdsourcing, too, seeker firms are concerned about the costs of having their tasks solved. When making decisions about crowdsourcing, firms will consider the efficiency of the decision, i.e., whether production costs are saved and transaction costs are outweighed by the benefits from crowdsourcing. Specifically, the price paid to solve the task in crowdsourcing is the production cost for the seeker. Overall, firms would want to ensure that crowdsourcing results in cost reduction. Therefore, we include cost reduction as a salient motivator of intention to crowdsource our model. Additionally, transaction costs are incurred during crowdsourcing, due to asset specificity and uncertainty. In crowdsourcing, asset specificity is manifest in the form of codification cost. Specifically, firms need to spend time and effort in transferring contextual knowledge related to the tasks through careful task requirements codification. We term this as codification cost, which is tied to the transaction. Codification cost incurs in support of the transaction and is an opportunity cost if firms fail to obtain satisfactory solutions from crowdsourcing. Further, since uncertainty exists regarding the quality of solutions obtained from crowdsourcing (Roman 2009), firms may need to expend time and effort in evaluating the proposals from solvers and selecting the winners (Afuah and Tucci 2012). We refer to this cost as the proposal evaluation cost. Therefore, deriving from TCT and the related literature, we identify and include cost reduction, codification cost, and proposal evaluation cost as antecedents of intention to crowdsource in our model.

While TCT is theoretically and empirically useful, it has received criticism from strategic management researchers (Foss and Klein 2010). One of the salient criticisms of TCT is that it neglects differential capabilities of firms i.e., firm heterogeneity (Kogut and Zander 1992). Under the assumption of bounded rationality (as also acknowledged in TCT), the RBV argues that differential capabilities and resources of firms give rise to different production costs, and that such cost differences influence firms’ make or buy decision (Barney 1991). Thus, firms may internalise activities because they can carry out these activities in a more production (not transaction) cost-efficient way than what other firms are capable of (Foss and Klein 2010) and buy if needed resources are unavailable within the firm. Therefore, the RBV can complement TCT in explaining firms’ make or buy decision and we use them in concert to explain seekers’ intention to crowdsource in our model.

## 2.2 Resources in Crowdsourcing

The resource based view (RBV) argues that valuable resources determine firm performance and competitive advantage in the market (Barney 1991; Penrose 1959). As per the theory (Barney 1991), resources include assets, firm attributes, and information that can contribute to the enhancement of the efficiency and effectiveness of firms’ strategies. Valuable resources are neither perfectly imitable nor substitutable without great effort. Thus, sustained competitive advantage can be achieved when firms are able to protect against resource imitation, transfer, or substitution.

The value creation capability of different resources affects the boundary decisions of a firm (Penrose 1959; Stevensen 1976). Firms will try to retain activities in-house that take advantage of their strategic resources. Outsourcing these activities would deprive organizations of their competitive advantage and subsequent abnormal returns (Duncan 1998). However, when needed resources are not available for the activities, they will look to alternative sources for them (Chesbrough 2003; Conner and Prahalad 1996). This forms the basis for applying the RBV to examine outsourcing decisions in previous studies (e.g., Alvarez-Suescun 2007; Gonzalez et al. 2006; Han et al. 2008; Roy and Aubert 2002).

In the context of our study, we expect that firms will crowdsource if it provides access to certain beneficial resources that are unavailable internally. A valuable resource that firms can access through

#

crowdsourcing is brand visibility, i.e., a reputational resource (cf. Morgan et al. 2002). It has been noted that firms may be able to use the crowdsourcing website as a platform to promote the awareness of their brand (Yang et al. 2008). Also, open sourcing can potentially increase customers’ familiarity with the firm’s brand and lead them to accept the brand (Agerfalk and Fitzgerald 2008). Further, there are other knowledge-based resources that seekers can access through crowdsourcing, which we explain through the resource based view.

Particularly, as per the RBV, we expect that firms will consider crowdsourcing if it can fill the gap between the knowledge resources required for solving tasks and the resources available in the firm. The knowledge resources can occur in the form of specialized skills for task solution (Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Schenk and Guittard 2011) as well as heterogeneity in solutions from a crowd (Kittur 2010; Poetz and Schreier 2012). As crowdsourcing can render firms access to specialists in various fields, we term this benefit as access to specialized skills. We term the perceived benefit that crowdsourcing may provide firms with heterogeneous ideas, as solution diversity. In sum, we include constructs of access to specialized skills, solution diversity, and brand visibility as drivers of seekers’ intention to crowdsource in our model.

## 3 Research Model and Hypotheses

As per the above discussion, we consider both production and transactions cost related factors i.e., cost reduction, codification cost, and proposal evaluation cost, as well as resources accessible through crowdsourcing i.e., brand visibility, access to specialized skills, and solution diversity, as antecedents of seekers’ intention to crowdsource in our model (see Figure 1). Other than the direct effects, we also hypothesize that codification cost and proposal evaluation cost will indirectly impact the dependent variable through diminishing the cost reduction perceived through crowdsourcing.

## 3.1 Brand Visibility

Brand visibility, in this study, refers to the extent to which seekers believe that their brands or products will be seen and made aware to a large audience through crowdsourcing. Marketing literature suggests that for an unknown brand, building the brand’s knowledge in consumers’ minds is the crucial task for a firm (Campell and Keller 2003; Sprott et al. 2009). Since consumers have limited cognitive capacity and memory for brands (Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004), they tend to purchase products from familiar brands (Sprott et al. 2009). The exposure of product information to potential consumers should improve the brand familiarity and thus enhance their purchase (Campell and Keller 2003). For example, when users were involved in designing a particular product, they were more familiar with and likely to buy it (Franke et al. 2010; von Hippel 2001).

In the context of our study, seeker firms can expect that solvers who perform tasks for the firms in crowdsourcing platforms will be more likely to become aware of and possibly purchase their products/services (Zheng et al. 2014). Firms believe that the more people get involved in solving tasks for a firm, the more likely they will be able to form an impression about the firm and have a better awareness of their brand (Libert and Spector 2010). As suggested in previous marketing literature, brand image and brand visibility is one of the reputational resources for organizational long term success (Aaker 1989; Itami 1987; Morgan et al. 2002). Since firms believe that crowdsourcing allows firms to reach a large pool of potential solvers and increase brand visibility, according to the RBV, firms may adopt crowdsourcing to acquire the resource (brand visibility) to perform. Following the discussion above, the expected brand visibility could increase seekers’ intention to crowdsource. Thus, we hypothesize: H1: Brand visibility positively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

## 3.2 Access to Specialized Skills

We define access to specialized skills as the extent to which firms think they can acquire knowledge or skills related to specific domains through crowdsourcing. Firms can have access to a broader range of knowledge, skills, abilities, and opinions through crowdsourcing (Schulze et al. 2011; Soukhoroukova et al. 2012). Through leveraging specialized knowledge and skills from the crowd, firms should be able to tackle internal tasks more effectively. For example, Wilogo taps around 13,000 logo design talents to work on tasks proposed by seekers. Through this platform, seekers can approach skilled workers around the world and invite them to design specialized and potentially valuable logos that cannot be obtained from internal employees. Through a case study in the SAP community, Leimeister et al. (2009) suggested that firms could obtain better access to experts through crowdsourcing.

In general, crowdsourcing can allow seeker firms to access the crowd’s specialized abilities, which may be unavailable internally, which are expected to effectively solve their tasks. As per the RBV, the gap between the knowledge required for task solving and the knowledge available leads firms to believe that they can acquire external knowledge (Penrose 1959), e.g., through crowdsourcing. Therefore, we expect

H2: Access to specialized skills positively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

## 3.3 Solution Diversity

In this study, solution diversity refers to seeker firms’ perception of the range of different solutions proposed by solvers in crowdsourcing. The basic premise of crowdsourcing is to tap the wisdom of crowds (Howe 2008). Solvers’ heterogeneous skills and knowledge will contribute to the diversity and innovativeness of solutions obtained from crowdsourcing (Bayus 2013). It has been suggested that the crowd can provide more diverse and novel ideas for problem solving than relatively homogeneous professional employees in seeker firms (Soukhoroukova et al. 2012). For example, an experiment involving design of new products for babies (Poetz and Schreier 2012) found that external solvers generated ideas of significantly higher novelty than employees in the firm.

Potential solution diversity provides firms with more choices on how to solve their problems (Howe 2008; Zheng et al. 2014), potential for innovation (Chen et al. 2010), and resources for organizational performance (Damanpour et al. 2009). We argue that these choices will make seeker firms to believe that they have control on and are satisfied with crowdsourcing. Also, diverse perspectives from the crowd can allow firms to better understand their needs, reduce the cost of thinking of multiple possibilities, and find the best fit solution. These are invisible resources for organizational superior performance (Itami 1987). Thus, as per the RBV, the potential benefit of solution diversity should motivate firms to crowdsource tasks. We propose that

H3: Solution diversity positively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

## 3.4 Cost Reduction

We define cost reduction as the perceived cost savings to seeker firms from solving the tasks externally through crowdsourcing than solving them internally. Reducing cost is considered one of the key strategies for firms to improve their profitability and maintain competitiveness in the market (Coase 1937). Cost reduction has been found to positively impact firms’ decision to outsource (Dibbern et al. 2004).

It has been suggested that solving tasks through crowdsourcing can be cheaper (Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Howe 2008). Although compensation is required for rewarding solvers, Brabham’s (2008) case study at iStockPhoto found that most of the time the cost of crowdsourcing was lower than that of solving the task inside the firm. Further, an experimental study (Horton and Chilton 2010) reported that firms only need to pay a median wage of USD 1.38/hour to workers in Amazon Mechanical Turk. If seeker firms perceive a cost advantage from crowdsourcing (lower production cost), as per TCT (Williamson 1985), this should motivate them to crowdsource. Thus, we expect that the perception of cost reduction through crowdsourcing will enhance firms’ intention to adopt this approach. Therefore, we hypothesize

H4: Cost reduction positively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

## 3.5 Codification Cost

Various costs arise for seekers from activities in the crowdsourcing process (Afuah and Tucci 2012). Besides the economic cost of rewarding solvers, we propose that a cost incurred by firms is in transferring specific knowledge to solvers through clear codification of task requirement (Afuah and Tucci 2012). We define codification cost as seeker’s cost associated with codifying and describing tasks clearly in crowdsourcing. Codification cost includes time and effort spent in transforming the tacit knowledge of the task requirements into explicit information so that solvers can understand what kinds of solutions are expected.

Clearly defined tasks may be able to receive better solutions from the crowd. This is because clearly defined requirements can reduce the likelihood of irrelevant submissions (Soukhoroukova et al. 2012). Also, clear task requirements lower the possibility of conflicts between solvers and seekers on why particular solutions were not selected. However, to clearly define and codify tasks, firms may need to expend both time and effort, i.e., a codification cost. Past literature in knowledge contribution has found that individuals will refuse to share knowledge if the action requires a high level of codification cost (Kankanhalli et al. 2005).

Some tasks require more time and effort for codification if substantial tacit knowledge needs to be transformed into explicit knowledge. Such time and effort could become a sunk cost if firms fail to obtain satisfactory results from crowdsourcing. Thus, as per TCT, a high perceived codification cost indicates a high perceived transaction cost for crowdsourcing (Afuah and Tucci 2012), which should inhibit firms from adopting the crowdsourcing strategy. Hence,

H5: Codification cost negatively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

At the same time, codification cost incurred in crowdsourcing will make firms perceive that it may not be cost-effective to use crowdsourcing for task solving. In this sense, codification cost will decrease firms’ perception of cost reduction in crowdsourcing (Afuah and Tucci 2012). As per TCT, asset specificity will increase the perception of transaction cost which will decrease the intention of the target behavior (Liang and Huang 1998; Williamson 1981). Thus, codification cost should decrease the overall perception of cost reduction as well as behavioral intention. Therefore, we expect H6: Codification cost negatively affects cost reduction

## 3.6 Proposal Evaluation Cost

We define proposal evaluation cost as the cost associated with assessing submissions and selecting the winners from all solutions obtained from crowdsourcing. An advantage of crowdsourcing is its ability to attract a large number of problem solvers and ideas through an open call (Howe 2008). Nonetheless, this would incur the cost of having to screen and evaluate these submissions (Afuah and Tucci 2012). When there are a number of proposals obtained from solvers, seekers believe that they will need to expend considerable time and effort in evaluating them (Pisano and Verganti 2008). Given the uncertainty in solution quality, proposal evaluation cost could be a transaction risk or an opportunity cost of solving the tasks via crowdsourcing. The transaction risk may become salient to firms when they cannot find a satisfactory submission (Ye and Kankanhalli 2013). If it is costly to evaluate these proposals (a high transaction risk), firms may prefer to solve the tasks internally in order to reduce the transaction cost. As per TCT (Williamson 1985), a high perceived proposal evaluation cost will decrease the attractiveness of crowdsourcing for seeker firms. Thus, we expect

## H7: Proposal evaluation cost negatively affects seekers’ intention to crowdsource

As discussed above, proposal evaluation cost incurred or expected will add to the overall costs of crowdsourcing. Thus, proposal evaluation cost should decrease the perception of cost reduction of task solving through crowdsourcing. Further, if the solution is highly specific to the firm’s application, only the firm can evaluate it. This will further decrease firms’ perception of cost reduction (Afuah and Tucci 2012). Therefore, we expect

H8: Proposal evaluation cost negatively affects cost reduction

As explained later in Section 4.3, we also included various control variables in our model since they may partially explain the dependent variable.

## 4 Research Methodology

Survey methodology was employed to test the research model. The survey was conducted in TaskCN.com, which is considered as one of the most popular and established crowdsourcing websites in China. By the end of September 2014, TaskCN had over 3.4 million registered solvers and over 56 thousand tasks<sup>4</sup>. Different from other crowdsourcing platforms, e.g., InnoCentive, in TaskCN, solvers interact with crowdsourcing companies to receive additional information on the public online community, by private email, or through instant messengers (Zheng et al. 2014). In this context, feedback including brand related information is often delivered from the company to solvers.

This platform has been recognized as successful for facilitating solutions of a variety of tasks and has received extensive media coverage in China as well as researchers’ attention (Yang et al. 2008; Yang et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2009). The selection of this platform allows us a sufficiently large pool of solvers to test our models. Further, the existence of previous studies on this platform allows for cumulation of research in this area.

## 4.1 Instrument Development

Since all the construct items in our model were adapted from their original source to the study context, we conducted a systematic procedure of instrument development (DeVellis 2003). First, items for each construct were generated based on previous literature and refined through interviews with seekers. Second, all items for each construct were validated through unlabeled and labeled sorting exercises. In the two rounds of item sorting, the survey instrument had high Kappa score, agreement level, and hit ratio, i.e., greater than 0.8, suggesting sufficient construct validity (Jarvenpaa 1998). Last, prior to the main survey, the instrument was pilot tested in another similar Chinese crowdsourcing website, i.e., Zhubajie.com. The final instrument is shown in Table 1. All constructs were reflective and the items scored on a 7-point Likert scale anchored from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree.

## 4.2 Control Variables

To rule out confounding effects of differences in seeker firm and task characteristics, we also include them as controls in the model, which may affect their intention to crowdsource. These variables include seekers’ firm age, firm size, previous experience, task type crowdsourced, and industry. For instance,

#

smaller and younger firms may be more likely to crowdsource since they lack resources to solve tasks internally and are more concerned with the cost (Doan et al. 2011; Greengard 2011). Firm’s age is measured by the years elapsed since its foundation while firm size refers to the number of employees. Industry is classified by the type of products or services the firm provides. The type of industry was coded as dummy variables in the model. Previous experience refers to whether the firm has crowdsourced task(s) to the platform before. Previous experience is coded as a dummy variable with value 0 if the firm has not crowdsourced a task before, and 1 otherwise. Task type was coded as dummy variables in the model. The demographic information about the respondent firms is listed in Table 2. Among 112 firms crowdsourced in TaskCN, each on average crowdsourced 1.21 tasks in TaskCN (Min= 1 task, Max= 4 tasks, STD. =0.72). Only one has proposed two types of tasks, i.e., logo design and laborious tasks.

## 4.3 Data Collection

To survey the seeker firms in TaskCN, we got a list of registered firms with the help of a TaskCN website administrator and randomly selected 500 seekers from the list. We sent them invitations to participate in the survey through email. As a token of appreciation for their participation, a \$10 voucher was given to respondents. A total of 161 valid responses were received with a 32.2% response rate. The respondents included project managers (20.5%), IT managers (17.4%), individual entrepreneurs (37.3%), systems analyst (9.3%), system developers (7.5%), and sales supervisors (8.1%) who are in charge of the crowdsourcing activities for their respective firms. We verified with the website administrator that this distribution of respondents was representative of the seeker population in the platform.

Since a web-based survey design may suffer from non-response bias (Roztocki 2001), we tested for such bias by comparing the early and late respondents (Armstrong and Overton 1997) as commonly done. T-tests of the differences between the earliest 10% respondents and the last 10% respondents in terms of demographics revealed no systematic difference. Thus, non-response bias is not expected in this study. We have also conducted a Chow’s test to check sample homogeneity (Chow 1960). Our results show support for sample homogeneity, F (6, 142) = 1.65, p>0.05. This demonstrates that different groups of respondents answer the questions not different from each other.

## 5 Data Analysis and Results

For this study, structural equation modeling (SEM) was chosen to simultaneously analyze all paths among latent variables in one analysis (Gefen et al. 2011). Within SEM, Partial Least Squares (PLS) was chosen over co-variance based SEM as single item constructs (e.g., controls variables) are included in the model. This could result in a low model fit in co-variance based SEM. The SmartPLS 2.0 software was used to conduct the statistical analyses. All constructs in model were measured using reflective indicators.

## 5.1 Instrument Validity

To validate our instrument, convergent and discriminant validity tests were conducted (Hair et al. 2006). We assessed convergent validity by examining the Cronbach’s α (CA), composite reliability (CR), average extracted variance (AVE), and factor analysis results (Straub et al. 2004). The factor analysis results are shown in Table 3, where the factor loading of each item on its own construct was larger than 0.7 as desired. Results in Table 4 show that each reflective construct in the model has values of CA and CR greater than 0.7 and value of AVE greater than 0.5. Thus, all constructs satisfied the criteria, demonstrating sufficient convergent validity (Hair et al. 2006).

As expected, cost reduction, brand visibility, and access to specialized skills are positively related to seeker firms’ intention to crowdsource (H1, H2, and H4 are supported) while codification cost and proposal evaluation cost are negatively related to the dependent variable (H5 and H7 are supported). Contrary to our prediction, solution diversity had no impact on firms’ intention to crowdsource (H3 not supported). This could result from the nature of tasks crowdsourced in our sample. This suggests that older firms tend to have a higher intention to crowdsource while firms in the education industry are more likely to crowdsource their tasks than those in traditional services industries (the baseline).

## 5.3 Post-hoc Analysis

Since the surveyed seeker firms included those who have crowdsourced a task to TaskCN in the past and those who have not, this study post-hoc tested whether there is a difference in the antecedents of future intention to crowdsource for the two groups of seeker firms. Of the 161 firms in our sample, 49 seeker firms have registered but did not submit a task in TaskCN (inexperienced seeker firms) while 112 seeker firms have prior experience in crowdsourcing through TaskCN (experienced seeker firms). We split the sample into these two groups and tested the hypotheses separately on each group. Keil et al.’s (2000) approach was adopted to statistically compare the corresponding path coefficients for the two groups and compute the T-values shown in Table 8.

Table 8 shows that significant differences exist in the antecedents of intention to crowdsource between inexperienced and experienced seeker firms. Inexperienced seekers pay more attention to cost reduction, codification cost and proposal evaluation cost while experienced seeker firms give more importance to brand visibility, and access to specialized skills. We did not compare the coefficients for solution diversity since neither was significant.

The differences between the two groups could be due to changes in beliefs as seekers’ gain experience with crowdsourcing. According to the belief-adjustment model (Hogarth and Einhorn 1992), people will update their beliefs over time. Specifically, they will first develop a general perception of an action. Their beliefs will then be adjusted according to new information received. If the received information is positive, there would be a relatively small increase in the strength of current beliefs. If the received information is negative, there could be a considerable decrease in the strength of existing beliefs. Similar arguments can be found in cognitive dissonance theory (Festinger 1957). It suggests that as individuals gain first-hand experience, they evaluate the extent to which their initial cognition (or beliefs) is consonant or dissonant with actual experience, and revise their cognition or behaviour to achieve greater consonance.

In the context of our study, seekers would have a general perception of crowdsourcing before their participation. Their beliefs will be revised with their actual experience and information gained from crowdsourcing. They will evaluate whether their initial cognition is consonant with actual experience and revise their cognition and behaviours accordingly. Thus, it is possible that the feedback from the crowdsourcing experience significantly changed seekers’ beliefs such that the cost related factors became less salient (cost reduction, codification cost and proposal evaluation cost) and the resource factors became more salient (brand visibility and access to specialized skills). Our interviews with a few experienced seekers validate these results that they overall had a positive experience, such that the resources from crowdsourcing were apparent to them and the costs were not too burdensome. However, the extent of cost reduction they expected may not have materialized. We refrain from generalizing these results since the samples may not be large enough but suggest that the differences between the groups need to be investigated in greater depth in future.

## 6 Discussion and Implications

With the emergence of new Internet based technologies, firms are starting to leverage the wisdom of the crowd to solve internal tasks. To attract more firms to participate, crowdsourcing platforms should understand what motivates them to crowdsource (Zhao and Zhu 2012). This is essential as not every crowdsourcing platform has been successful in attracting and retaining firms to crowdsource their tasks e.g., the failure of CrowdSpirit<sup>5</sup>. Further, there is a lack of research that has theoretically modeled and empirically tested the antecedents (including costs) of firms’ intention to crowdsource (see a thorough review by Zhao and Zhu 2012). Therefore, researchers and practitioners alike are interested to understand how to encourage firms to crowdsource tasks (Howe 2008; Terwiesch and Ulrich 2009). From seeker firms’ viewpoint as well, participation in crowdsourcing may allow them to obtain the benefits possible through this approach. Deriving from the RBV and TCT, following Zhao and Zhu (2012) call for research, this study developed a model to explain firms’ intention to crowdsource. It found that cost reduction, brand visibility, and access to specialized skills positively affect the intention to crowdsource while codification cost and proposal evaluation cost negatively affect their intention. Also, cost reduction was found to partially mediate the influences of codification cost and proposal evaluation cost on the intention to crowdsource.

## 6.1 Limitations and Future Research

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of its limitations. First, our study is restricted in its ability to make broad generalizations due to studying one type of crowdsourcing platform. In other websites such as InnoCentive, seeker firms crowdsource tasks that require more creativity and expertise to solve, e.g., R&D problems. Future research could examine seeker firms in crowdsourcing platforms such as InnoCentive (Jeppesen and Lakhani 2010) to test if similar results hold or factors such as solution diversity become salient. Nevertheless, this study has established some degree of generalization since similar results have been found both from the pilot test in another Chinese crowdsourcing platform (zhubajie.com) and the main study in TaskCN. Thus, the findings may be generalizable to platforms that are similar in the types of tasks to be solved, incentives provided, and crowdsourcing mechanism used e.g., Wilogo.com, TopCoder.com, and Netflix.com.

Second, while this paper studied the influence of cost and resource factors on firms’ intention to crowdsource within a platform, future work can explore the influence of the characteristics of different crowdsourcing platforms on seeker firms’ crowdsourcing behavior. Particularly, researchers could investigate how crowdsourcing platforms perform in facilitating the process of crowdsourcing. For example, they can consider crowdsourcing platforms as knowledge brokers and utilize knowledge brokering theory (Hargadon 2003) to explore the performance of crowdsourcing platforms. Furthermore, researchers should conduct a longitudinal research on the antecedents of organizations’ actual crowdsourcing behaviors by collecting the number of tasks crowdsourced by companies in future.

Third, we found the partial mediation effect of cost reduction on the influences of codification cost and proposal evaluation cost on the intention to crowdsource. Future research should theoretically explore the underlying (both cost and non-cost related) mechanisms that link proposal evaluation cost and codification cost to intention to crowdsourcing. Furthermore, future study can explore the relationship between solution diversity and proposal evaluation cost since diverse solutions could increase the workload for firms to select a winning solution.

## 6.2 Theoretical Contributions

This paper contributes to the crowdsourcing literature by developing a holistic model to explain firms’ intention to crowdsource and empirically testing it with survey data. Specifically, this study contributes to research in several ways. First, the understanding of seeker firms’ crowdsourcing behavior in previous literature has been mainly based on conceptual or case studies (e.g., Burger-Helmchen and Penin 2010; Schenk and Guittard 2011; Terwiesch and Xu 2008; Yang et al. 2008). Following Zhao and Zhu (2012) call for research, this paper fills the literature gap by theoretically and empirically exploring the antecedents of firms’ intention to crowdsource. It found that cost reduction, brand visibility, and access to specialized skills positively affect firms’ intention to crowdsource. This study adds to crowdsourcing literature by validating that these are important considerations for firms’ crowdsourcing decision.

Second, previous studies mainly suggested the influence of benefit factors on firms’ crowdsourcing behavior (Doan et al. 2011; Kittur 2010). There is a lack of investigation of the influences of costs during crowdsourcing (Afuah and Tucci 2012). This paper identified the cost constructs for this context and tested them in the TaskCN platform. It found that codification cost and proposal evaluation cost negatively affect firms’ intention to crowdsource. The findings contribute to crowdsourcing literature by enriching our understanding of the cost concerns that will thwart firms’ intention to crowdsource.

Third, this study found that certain antecedents of crowdsourcing intention i.e., cost reduction and access to specialized skills, are similar to the determinants of outsourcing decision (cf. Jarvenpaa et al. 2003). However, several antecedents differ and are specific to firms’ intention to crowdsource, i.e., brand visibility, codification cost, and proposal evaluation cost. The context specific findings contribute to previous research by allowing comparison between the two forms of sourcing. Further, this study facilitates future research by developing and validating an instrument for studying firms’ crowdsourcing behavior.

Fourth, this study contributes to the literature on TCT and the RBV. It extends the applicability of transaction cost theory and the resource based view to the context of crowdsourcing. The explanatory power and support for the model indicate that the complementary perspectives of TCT and the RBV provide an appropriate theoretical lens to explain firms’ intention to crowdsource i.e., our results suggest that both cost and resource factors affect firms’ intention to crowdsource. Further, we modify the standard TCT and RBV formulations to account for firms’ intention to crowdsourcing by modeling the influence of context specific constructs, i.e., brand visibility, codification cost, and proposal evaluation cost.

## 6.3 Practical Implications

From a pragmatic perspective, this study offers insights to crowdsourcing platform administrators on how to attract firms to crowdsource their tasks in two main ways. First, this study provides guidelines for crowdsourcing platforms to enhance firms’ intention to crowdsource. In particular, the results suggest that crowdsourcing platforms should heighten the perceptions of cost reduction, brand visibility, and access to specialized skills benefits for the purpose of promoting firms’ participation in crowdsourcing. On one hand, crowdsourcing platforms should take the initiative to attract and retain a large and diverse pool of solvers to participate in crowdsourcing so that firms can access their specialized skills in multiple areas.

On the other, crowdsourcing platforms could communicate the advantage to firms that solving tasks through crowdsourcing is cost-effective. Inviting experienced seeker firms to share their crowdsourcing experience could be a viable approach for crowdsourcing platforms to advertise the benefits of crowdsourcing. Also, a forum could be set up to elicit firms’ feedback and comments on their crowdsourcing experience.

Second, crowdsourcing platforms should seek ways to reduce the costs for firms to participate, while remaining profitable. They should identify approaches to facilitate firms to codify their requirements description and provide suitable tools to support their task codification. In particular, they could help firms define their problems by providing specific advice and improve the process of requirement submission through offering tools to support them, e.g., providing samples and training for requirement codification. Apart from reducing codification effort, crowdsourcing platforms should strive to mitigate firms’ proposal evaluation cost. To do so, they can provide appropriate tools for firms to filter out irrelevant proposals e.g., by refined searching. They can also offer services to assist firms to select the relevant solutions according to the task requirements.

## 7. Conclusion

Considering the importance of seeker firms’ participation to the survival of crowdsourcing platforms, practitioners have expressed substantial interest and concerns about encouraging firms to crowdsource (Doan et al. 2011). At the same time, research and understanding has been lacking on what influences firms intention to crowdsource. To this end, we developed a model based on transaction cost theory and the resource based view to examine drivers and inhibitors of firms’ intention to crowdsource. Our findings indicate that cost reduction, access to specialized skills, and brand visibility enhance firms’ intention to crowdsource, while proposal evaluation cost and codification cost reduce firms’ intention to crowdsource both directly and indirectly. These findings add to the limited but growing body of research on crowdsourcing. They also offer suggestions for practitioners to provide appropriate motivators and help reduce participation cost in order to promote firms’ crowdsourcing.
