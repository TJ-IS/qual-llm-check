# Towards an ecological account of media choice: a case study on pluralistic reasoning while choosing email

Yusun Jung\* & Kalle Lyytinen<sup>†</sup>

\*Information Systems, Case Western Reserve University, 10900 Euclid Ave, Cleveland, Ohio 44106, USA, and <sup>†</sup>Information Systems, Case Western Reserve University, Cleveland, Ohio USA, email:yusun.jung@case.edu.

Abstract. The discourse around media choice has generated a diverse array of media choice factors originating from both the media-based and social interactionbased approaches. The multitude of these factors hints at the adaptive nature of media choice. Alas, how a user engages with such factors and adaptively carries out media choice has remained understudied. We undertake a <sup>fi</sup>eld study to explore the role of a plurality of choice factors and their interactions in shaping media choice processes and outcomes. In particular, we focus on how a user identi<sup>fi</sup>es relationships among plural choice factors while he or she works on his or her particular choice resulting in a similar outcome – email – given a large number of alternatives. Drawing upon a theory of affordances, we propose a systemic way of narrating the dynamics of media choice as a multi-dimensional process where a user explores her or his surroundings – a niche – as to establish media affordances that will then help her or him achieve a communication goal. We identify <sup>fi</sup>ve relational patterns of interactions among speci<sup>fi</sup>c choice factors: reciprocity, emergence, complementarity, re-exploration and actualisation. These patterns are shown to be emergent and highly interdependent. We conclude by reviewing future research avenues to formulate richer ‘ecological’ accounts of media choice.

Keywords: information and communication technology (ICT), media choice, the ory of affordances, media ecology

## INTRODUCTION

During the last decades, organisational settings have been increasingly saturated by a wide array of media alternatives, thanks to constant advances in Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs) (Ortiz de Guinea & Markus, 2009). Simultaneously, media choice has become deeply embedded in organisational routines, rendering users’ media uses increasingly adaptive, in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by plural antecedents. Indeed, organisations’ members have learned through sustained media use to establish a wide range of media choice ‘repertoires’ that creatively combine multiple and varied media capabilities (Watson-Manheim & Bélanger, 2007). Parallel with this change in the media landscape, the explanatory power of received media choice theories has weakened (El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1997; Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997). In response, researchers have proposed broader theories that try to capture the new realities of media choice. For example, Trevino et al. (2000) highlighted the complementary nature of the media-based and social interaction factors and discussed the salience of understanding their interactions. Donabedian (2006) illustrated users’ adaptive choice when he analysed how users hop among media while pursuing the ‘optimal’ choice. Dennis et al. (2008) proposed a theory of media synchronicity that belabours the new characteristics of media and how they in<sup>fl</sup>uence communication tasks. Kock (2009) explicated media use from an evolutionary perspective by emphasising the importance of understanding users’ new evolving ICT capabilities. Yet, how users recognise increasingly plural choice antecedents and what shapes their actual choices have remained largely unexplored.

In this paper, we address some of the outstanding challenges in explaining choice<sup>1</sup> in the new media landscape. To this end, we identify the plurality of media choice factors that are viewed to be deeply embedded in users’ routine and dynamically shape users’ choice. We engage in an in-depth investigation of users’ media choice by exploring a <sup>fi</sup>eld setting where three knowledge workers’ media choices resulted in the same outcome – email. We observe that these choices were dynamically in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by a multitude of factors. The effectiveness of these choices evolves with changes in communication tasks and users’ capabilities. Whilst recognising this increased adaptability in the choice behaviours, we adopt an ecological lens drawing upon Gib son’s (1977; 1979) theory of affordances. Accordingly, we introduce the concept of media affordance – a user’s conceived possibilities for communication as to ful<sup>fi</sup>l his or her dynamic communication needs – as a way to explain media choice. The lens posits that media choice encompasses a goal-oriented behaviour whereby a user establishes media affordances from media properties ‘at hand’ in her or his ‘niche’. By the term niche, we refer to each user’s surroundings equipped with an array of media to which the use has adapted through her repeated deployments. Consequently, during the media choice, a user exercises critical localised agency owing to his or her familiarity with the environ ment and the communication task. We propose <sup>fi</sup>ve relational patterns of interaction among plural choice factors that constitute the media choice process when such media affordances are enacted: reciprocity, emergence, complementarity, re-exploration and actualisation.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. First, we review the extant literature on media choice and propose an alternative theoretical lens of ecological psychology. We then describe the study design and report its empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Thereafter, we build up abductively the ecological account of media choice. We conclude by discussing the theoretical and practical implications of the proposed approach.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

## Evolving discourse around media choice

Early studies on media choice were dominated by an approach we call media-based choice. The <sup>fi</sup>rst theory following this approach was the media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1986), which approached media choice as a decision-making task that seeks to reduce uncertainty and/or ambiguity associated with communicating external information (Daft & Weick, 1984). The theory uses an invariant criterion of ‘good’ media – a task <sup>fi</sup>t to assess whether users can achieve effectiveness in their communication tasks. Accordingly, it has devised a scale to measure each medium’s capacity to convey information richness. By using this scale, it predicts each medium’s effectiveness in ful<sup>fi</sup>lling the needs of varying information-processing tasks. By matching ‘<sup>fi</sup>t’ between media richness and task characteristics, an organisation’s members can optimise their communication effectiveness. For example, a richer medium (i.e. face to face) allows for more immediate feedback, offers multiple cues, conveys the message in an unrestricted language and increases personalisation. It is more suitable for processing information of an equivocal nature. In contrast, a leaner medium (e.g. a textual document) offers limited feedback and fewer cues but is ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in reducing uncertainty.

Since then, a number of studies have sought to validate the key tenets of the media richness theory by seeking to replicate its predictions across multiple settings. Alas, the results have been largely contradictory. Interestingly, the theory’s predictions are more widely supported when a non-mediated, face-to-face condition is tested against a mediated communication condition (e.g. Chidambaram & Jones, 1993; Kahai & Cooper, 2003), but they are not supported when tested across a variety of computer-mediated conditions (e.g. Schmitz & Fulk, 1991; Rice, 1992; El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1997; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). Furthermore, studies conducted in richer <sup>fi</sup>eld settings have generated at most a weak support for the theory, calling into question its external validity (Trevino et al., 1987; Schmitz & Fulk, 1991; Rice, 1992; El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1997; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). These confounding <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that media richness and the idea of <sup>fi</sup>t re<sup>fl</sup>ect better the initial design features of earlier communication technologies, but the theory has gradually lost its power as new, versatile computer-mediated communication media have emerged and been widely adopted. Therefore, richer taxonomies re<sup>fl</sup>ecting advanced media traits have been proposed to overcome observed weaknesses such as theory of media synchronicity (Dennis et al., 2008). Given that media traits in the media-based approach not only describe media but also create the variance necessary to ‘test’ the espoused effectiveness of the medium, the question of how to operationalise the traits properly for theory validation has remained a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant challenge.

The social interaction-based approach emerged largely as a response to a growing recognition of the diminishing power of the media-based approach. In particular, the origina idea of ‘media richness’ was considered to be relatively poor in its capacity to articulate the true ‘richness’ of media choice and use in real settings (El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1997; 1998). The assumptions of the media-based choice approach were in particular criticised for decontextualising choice from ongoing social interactions and ignoring users’ varying characteristics in those interactions (Schmitz & Fulk, 1991; Fulk, 1993; Fulk et al., 1995; Vandenbosch & Higgins, 1995; Warkentin & Beranek, 1999). Yet, most studies suggest that such elements signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly affect media choice. Consequently, the proponents of the approach have argued that social interactions and related learning signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly shape users’ media perceptions and consequent choice (Carlson & Zmud, 1999). For social interactionists, media choice is not merely a mechanistic matching task; instead, it forms a dynamic, situated process shaped by the user’s agency, context and history (Lee, 1994; Markus, 1994). For example, email gets richer as people learn new ways to express personal cues (e.g. emoticons), and users can also use it for richer communications, because it allows feedback (Ngwenyama & Lee, 1997; Panteli, 2002). Thus, each medium and its uses are continually shaped while users share their learning of its (social) use (Te’eni et al., 2001). Yet, social interactionists also admit that these evolving perceptions are in the end grounded on the invariant materiality of media features (Mackay, 1988; El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1998). Hence, the interplay between <sup>fi</sup>xed materiality of media and their continued social shaping needs to be explicitly considered in media choice theories (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 2007; Leonardi & Barley, 2008; Markus & Silver, 2008). Although the approach articulates the necessary social construction of media, it can be too generic to explain variations in individual’s choice behaviours when the individual can exercise crucial localised agency in her or his choice. In other words, it is not clear how a socially constructed perception of media can govern highly individualised choice behaviours at a practical level and how such behaviours evolve or which factors in<sup>fl</sup>uence this.

More recently, a third ‘middle-ground’ approach that we call integrative/adaptive behaviourism has been put forward. It confronts the complexity of media choice by recognising that the choice is fundamentally a situated process where a user re<sup>fl</sup>exively monitors its accomplish ment (Giddens. 1984). Drawing a strict boundary between the media-based choice and the social interaction-based approach (i.e. Carlson & Davis, 1998; Lee, 1994; Kock, 2004) is therefore super<sup>fl</sup>uous: indeed, constructs identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in both approaches ‘coexist’ and shape users’ media choice while users execute their agency. This has resulted in attempts to formulate integrated approaches to account for choice. For example, Trevino et al. (1987; 1990) sought early on to integrate situational determinants (e.g. social distance or socia and emotional meanings in messages) into media-based choice models. Webster and Trevino (1995) and Trevino et al. (2000) argued further that media-based factors and social factors coexist while exercising varying in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the choice. For example, constructs associated with the media-based approach are more powerful in explaining choice among traditional media, whereas those with the social interaction approach are more prominent for under standing choices related to digital media. In addition, their interaction effects are signi<sup>fi</sup>cant, so that one set of factors can complement or substitute for the other set of factors. Therefore, Dennis et al. (2008), among others, have included appropriation factors affecting the <sup>fi</sup>t between media synchronicity and communication processes in their, media synchronicity theory as substitutes or complementarities. Because of their focus on variance-based explanations, however, they do not take into account the order in which these factors enter the choice process and in<sup>fl</sup>uence the outcomes, and consequently how the effects thereby can vary from one dynamic context to another.

Some attempts have been made recently to resolve these limitations. For example, Donabedian (2006) argued that media choice forms a dynamic process in which users tack between the rational domain (i.e. decisional ambiguity) and the social domain (i.e. speci<sup>fi</sup>cally by eyeing towards the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to group coordination). Kock (2004), in turn, proposed an ambitious evolutionary theory using the concept of media naturalness. He argued that users have a predisposition towards unmediated communication, because of their inherent ‘phenotypes’, and that this predisposition puts a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive load on users when they must use ‘unnatural’ media. Users seek to decrease these cognitive loads through adaptive repeated uses over time.

Fundamentally, the adaptive approach assumes a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant level of dynamism in media choice: users in any given communication situation will, through their agency, grapple adaptively with multiple factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing the choice. Earlier studies have also investigated the dynamics of such adaptation by viewing media choice as a situated process where users media perceptions are determined largely by a contingent history rather than by universal logic (King & Xia, 1997; Carlson & Zmud, 1999). Users learn to enact different meanings of the same medium, depending on their locale and self-re<sup>fl</sup>ection (Mackay & Elam, 1992; El-Shinnawy & Markus, 1997; 1998). Furthermore, past media uses do not necessarily consistently predict the next choice (Trevino et al., 2000). As Leonardi and Barley (2008) observed, users act by relying on their localised agency, which often results in unexpected ways of using artefacts. This calls for a deeper understanding of how to characterise users’ localised agency and how this agency constitutes a critical element of media choice. To commence our theory-building effort, following this line of thought, we next draw inspiration from ecological psychology.

## Theory of affordances

Ecological psychology seeks to explicate how a human ‘determines’ his or her action in a particular context. Traditional cognitive psychology presumes that all human actions are conditioned by what goes on in the human mind in the form of cognitive processing. This processing generates a set of mental schemata, which then ‘dictate’ how a human acts. In contrast, social psychology views human behaviour as being primarily in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by other human agents and social interactions. Although both psychological strands assume essentially that a cognitive process precedes any act, the ecological psychology claims that a stimulus from a ‘direct perception’ outside explicit cognition is suf<sup>fi</sup>cient to trigger action. In other words, no complex cognitive inferential system is needed at all times to construct a ‘meaning’ from the stimulus (Barker, 1968; Michaels & Carello, 1972; Lombardo, 1987; Heft, 2001).

Gibson’s (1977; 1979) theory of affordances has been instrumental in advancing such ecological accounts of human action. In his theory, action is situated in an environment – a bounded space holding a set of particular substances. The actor perceives selectively the ambient information that is meaningful to his or her action. Gibson called such meaningfu information for action affordances’ and defined them as possibilities for action, He stated ‘The affordance of anything is a speci<sup>fi</sup>c combination of the properties of its substance and its surfaces taken with reference to an animal’ (Gibson, 1977, p. 67). Substance refers here to inherent biochemical functions determined by its materiality. Surfaces are both what an actor perceives and where his or her action occurs (i.e. interface). Attributes of the substance are called properties, and usually, a substance has more than one property. The actor’s perception of affordances is obtained directly from his or her environment and does not rely on pre-existing knowledge or symbolic learning. For example, an actor can avoid running into a tree without possessing information generated by inferential systems, such as the distance to the tree, the velocity of the actor approaching the tree and the course towards it. The direct visual perception from the optical array as the tree becomes bigger is ‘suf<sup>fi</sup>cient’ to alter the actor’s course (Michaels & Carello, 1972).

Action constantly shapes how an actor relates with substances in the environment and her or his properties to do so (effectivities). For example, when an actor intends to crush a nutshell with a stone, the hardness and dimensions of the stone and that of the nutshell form additional properties that constitute the action. An affordance emerges only when the rock is harder than the nutshell; it has an adequate shape; and the actor’s arm is resilient enough to stand the weight of the rock. Gibson states:

An affordance is neither an objective property nor a subjective property; or it is both if you like. An affordance cuts across the dichotomy of subjective – objective and helps us to understand its inadequacy. It is equally a fact of the environment and a fact of behavior. It is both physical and psychical, yet neither. An affordance points both ways, to the environment and to the observer. [Gibson, 1979, p. 129]

Although affordance is central to explaining an actor’s action, the ontology of affordances has remained highly ambiguous. One stream argues that affordances are objective properties of the environment and have nearly deterministic consequences for action (Lombardo, 1987; Turvey, 1992; Greeno, 1994; Heft, 2001). Simply, affordances ‘reside’ in the environment, and action occurs naturally when an actor’s effectivities complement the properties of the environment. In other words, perceiving a property of the environment and its designated action is crucial to generate action. This view emphasises the role of perceptual systems, such as the ambien optical array, in accounting for the emergence of affordances. The second stream posits that affordances emerge dynamically from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c actor–environment system. Affordances are meaningful only when an actor with a behavioural purpose prevails in the environment. Action takes place because of the actor’s agency, not just because particular properties reside in the environment (Chemero, 2003; Stoffregen, 2003). Action is a multi-dimensional event: the actor’s effectivities and the properties of the environment build reciprocal relationships that complement the actor’s purpose. The actor actualises affordances to ful<sup>fi</sup>l her or his purpose in a situation. Therefore, any object in the environment offers multiple affordances, and the actor can actualise them dynamically depending on circumstances and her or his shifting goals. The reciprocity between an actor and her or his environment is about dynamic convergence that involves simultaneous adaptations to and modi<sup>fi</sup>cations of the environment whereby the actor builds up reciprocal relations in the environment while she or he tries to ‘adapt’ and thereby ‘survive’ in her or his niche.

Human action around media does not happen in the same way as when humans act upon stones or chairs with relatively <sup>fi</sup>xed physical properties; media have essentially simultaneous symbolic and material characteristics. Therefore, our intent is not to validate the ‘original’ theory of affordances in the context of media choice; rather, we use the theory as a sensitising theoretical lens to capture the role of users’ individual localised agency in relation to an environment that undergirds media choice. This behaviour is circumscribed neither by rationa media-based factors nor by contingent social interaction factors – rather, it encompasses both adaptively in speci<sup>fi</sup>c sequences over time. By doing this, we follow the second stream of ecological theory that emphasises an actor’s dynamism in her or his adaptive action.

## AN EXPLORATORY FIELD STUDY

We engage in an exploratory <sup>fi</sup>eld study to elicit ways to account for the inherent dynamism in users’ media choice. In seeking to formulate our initial theory inspired by the idea of affordance, we conducted an exploratory case study that focused in particular on why and how users entertain plural choice factors in media-rich environments while coping with multiple, distinc choices (Eisenhardt, 1989; Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

The professionals had roughly equal range of media options available; the extent of mediated communications required for a good job performance was similar; <sup>fi</sup>nally, their familiarity with ICT was similar. Overall, he preferred verbal media over computer-supported media. Case 2 was involved in <sup>fi</sup>scal analyses and consultation for institutional clients, such as banks and investment agencies. Case 3 worked for an information and technology support group at a large university. She also engaged in signi<sup>fi</sup>cant interpersonal communications to consult with faculty and to collaborate with her team. As an instructional designer, she used multiple media in her communications and deployed daily a rich selection of computer-assisted applications. Her primary concern was how effectively media content was delivered to and used by users.

## Data collection and analysis

We gathered data from each case through semi-structured, on-site interviews lasting minimally an hour and a half. (See Appendix A for an interview instrument.) In addition, we followed the subjects in their workplace for a normal workday as to observe their communications at different time points. Overall, we spent about 9 h observing each interviewee. We recorded the interviews and transcribed them verbatim for later analysis. We also took notes during each observation session and asked additional follow-up questions about their media choice behaviours after the session. We judged that the obtained data were adequate to inquire into the three issues of interest and for generating theoretical insights. Overall, the data collectior conveyed to us a good sense of dynamics in the individuals’ media choices as to detect consistent patterns and observe diversity.

We analysed the data in several ways as to ful<sup>fi</sup>l our research objectives. First, to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the presence of the plurality of media choice factors, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three media choice dimensions from the existing approaches, media characteristics, social in<sup>fl</sup>uence and communication context (Table 2), and tallied the presence or absence of factors in each dimensions associated with each subject’s media choices. This was accomplished by coding the transcripts along the pre-identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dimensions. We also examined the coding for its plausibility (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). Second, we conducted a cross-case analysis of subjects’ identical media choice of emai as to reveal the different underlying dynamics of each choice process and their variation. We selected the email choice for two reasons: (1) it has been the most widely studied electronic medium (offering a possibility for external validation and theoretical triangulation); and (2) each subject used email routinely to address multiple communication purposes (interna validity/typical communication process).

## FINDINGS

The three subjects attended at all times to more than one media feature in each medium and switched constantly between them. The subjects were well aware of the characteristics of each medium in their niches regarding their functional ‘effectiveness’ and social meaning. At the same time, they were receptive to communication contexts and their potential effects on communication outcomes. This familiarity not only enabled them to make fast and intuitive choices but also formed a highly dynamic milieu characterised by constant shifting between media preferences, augmenting habitual choices of speci<sup>fi</sup>c media over other media in his or her niche.

## Pluralism of factors shaping media choice

Choosing a medium for a practical communication goal was constantly complicated by the presence of multiple and often contradictory choice factors. Interestingly, frequently used media were more often associated with a larger plurality of factors. Apparently, subjects chose them in more diverse communication situations and developed highly adaptive use patterns for them. In a sense, knowing the media characteristics and their relevance to social contexts was rudimentary in the subjects’ niches. Subjects were apt to weigh in those factors differently depending on the situation. ## Three adaptive media choice processes of emai

During our cross-case analysis, we probed how each individual incorporated dynamically multiple dimensions during their media choice, which resulted in the same choice but for different reasons. Indeed, the three cases differed signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly by the number of factors considered, the way in which they are considered and the depth and intensity of reasoning resulting in the choice of email.

Case 1 exhibited a multi-level choice in which he constantly mixed and matched two media to carry out his task. In his choice, he wanted to distribute urgent market information. He recognised that email could handle multiple recipients with ease, i.e. it offered the ability to deliver messages to a large number of recipients at once. He was aware, however, that the email transmission could not guarantee clients’ receipt of the message – in particular because many of his clients were retired and did not check email frequently. Recognising the situational constraints, he improvised and used media combinations – prioritising his contact lists and following up by telephone – that could achieve his purposes adequately.

Case 2’s choice was in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by multiple factors. His primary concern was to defend himself from potential disputes associated with clients’ privacy and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality. He also wanted to deliver information without a threat of misunderstandings. He perceived that the capacity to reprocess email could address these concerns. In contrast to voicemail or a phone call, to destroy email communication records arbitrarily is not easy, which enhanced his trust of email communication. He was also con<sup>fi</sup>dent about using it for his needs, including typing, saving and retrieving messages so as to ensure an adequate communication outcome.

Case 3 occasionally conducted large-scale surveys. When she had a limited number of respondents, she had to consider a telephone follow-up to achieve a better response rate. How ever, doing so was not practical in large-scale surveys. The only option for paralle communication was email. She was concerned that pure text email messages were too simple to draw recipients’ attention and thus decided to use advanced Hypertext Mark-up Language-based formatting to incorporate richer symbols, such as eye-catching graphics and other typographical elements. With the richer representation, she could draw students attention and elevate response rates. This approach was possible because she had advanced media expertise.

By focusing on the subjects’ media choice events within their niches, we underscore the situated, locally constituted and habitual nature of media choice. During the choice event subjects reinforce iteratively their af<sup>fi</sup>nity with each medium, which builds up an array of media choice patterns for the next round. Consequently, they can shift from one medium to another or even combine several media without signi<sup>fi</sup>cant cognitive effort. To this end, we conjecture that localised agency becomes central in coordinating the plurality of media alternatives in users niches. The niche is filled with dynamism as it conveys constantly new features and variations It also undergoes constantly through shifting situational conditions. The subjects therefore need to exercise individual ‘re<sup>fl</sup>exive’ agency to dynamically adapt to the complexity of intertwined media characteristics and social in<sup>fl</sup>uence. While encountering novel situations, they must search for media that can be operated within their expertise so as to overcome the present constraints. Indeed, prior research has considered individual factors as an important antecedent of media choice, which enables or constrains users’ access to a variety of media options (Fulk et al., 1995; King & Xia, 1997; Te’eni, 2001). Users generally prefer media that are easy to use (Fulk, 1993; Carlson & Zmud, 1999) and familiar to them (Kock, 2004). Yet, individual agency drives moment-to-moment decision-making by shaping dynamically media choice processes; i.e. whether speci<sup>fi</sup>c factors truly in<sup>fl</sup>uence user’s course of action depends on his or her niche and how he or she observes and prioritises particular media. Therefore, theorising around media choice should also seek to synthesise systemic and emergent patterns of choice processes rather than just identifying generalisable, static factors that correlate with some likelihood with observed choice outcomes.

reciprocity, emergence, complementarity, re-exploration and actualisation (Figure 1). These patterns were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed on the basis of how users engaged with multiple choice factors while working on their choice. We coin a speci<sup>fi</sup>c construct, media affordance, to express the adaptation of the original concept of affordance to a socially conditioned media choice situation. It denotes possibilities for communication within a user’s media niche to achieve his or her communication goal. By doing so, we can envision media choice as a multi-dimensional process where a user adaptively explores his or her niche while she ‘searches’ for media affordances. How media affordances emerge and why a user adopts them are thus in<sup>fl</sup>uential in understanding media choice. Media affordance emerges when the medium possesses particular properties, when a user recognises these properties and when she has the ability to operate the medium properly. Media affordance, in turn, paves the way to media choice when the user believes that actualising the affordance makes reaching the expected effect possible. Ir contrast, if the media affordance becomes blocked by unforeseen situational factors, the user needs to re-explore, engage again in search and generate alternative and actualise another affordance that might better achieve his or her initial communication purpose.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst relational pattern, reciprocity, is initiated by a user’s desire to achieve a communication goal that arises within her or his particular task situation such as communicating with a client; a communication goal denotes what a user wants to do at the moment in a <sup>fl</sup>ow of tasks. She searches for media that have media properties relevant to her communication goal and also that she can handle without trouble. The reciprocity is thus shaped by and involves tacking between two factors: user effectivities and media properties. By user effectivities, we refer to users’ capabilities for perceiving essential media features within their niche that help them achieve their communication goal. Whereas the original conceptualisation of effectivity has an evolutionary and anatomic leaning, we adapt it to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of media choice that is more dynamic because of the organisational. contextual and technological influences. We thus adopt a more adaptive interpretation of effectivity to denote users’ operational abilities and situational awareness that will determine their skill and dexterity in operating communication media. Operational ability pertains to media expertise in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by previous experiences of using media as well as relevant cognitive and motor skills determined by training, education and inheritance. Situational awareness refers to a user’s evolving understanding of the requirements for successful communication and situational constraints that in<sup>fl</sup>uence access to or deployment of media properties. For example, case 2 was well aware of the situationa requirements of his task – to ensure privacy and con<sup>fi</sup>dentiality – and thus set keeping communication records as this achieved his communication goal. At the same time, he possessed an adequate level of media expertise to produce, save and retrieve digital text. The former here is an example of situational awareness and the latter of operational ability. Together, they enabled him to establish reciprocity between him and email.

Media properties form a material and localised approach of characterising media in that media properties have direct lawful relationships to communication. Here, the lawful relationship of a media property conveys the idea of dispositional capacity of the medium, in that the property forms a necessary condition for a communication event to take place, whereas it needs to be augmented with an actor’s purposeful engagement in the form of action. Because of its material and localised nature, the concept of media property differs from abstract media characteristics recognised in media-based approach such as media richness. Case 1 perceived email to be lean because of asynchronous textual communication, but its paralle transmission designating multiple recipients simultaneously forms email as a medium of group mailing in his situation. Likewise, case 3 recognised the property of parallel transmission as necessary for a large-scale survey and recognised email as a medium serving her goal. At the same time, these media properties are generic in the sense that other media can share roughly similar properties and thus can be used for achieving similar goals. For example, mobile text messaging also has a group-messaging property, although it comes with different design form factors and with the additional feature of user mobility. Locally, media properties are malleable in the sense that users can sometimes creatively constitute new ‘unexploited properties by combining multiple properties in novel ways (Stoffregen, 2003). Diverse functional interpretations of a medium thus depend on the imagination with which a user explores her or his niche and builds reciprocal relations with particular media properties.

The second relational pattern is the emergence of media affordances. In this pattern, a user relates a medium reciprocated from the relation between her or his effectivities and media properties with her or his communication goal and realises that the ‘planned’ communication is ‘doable’ with the chosen medium; whereas the reciprocity is mainly about relating properties of a user and media, the emergence is about relating the reciprocal relation to the user’s communication goal. Consider case 1; he sets a reciprocal relation between his effectivities to type and send a message and parallel transmission of an email, and the reciprocity enables him to achieve his communication goal. The goal is already implicit in the idea of an affordance: the user distinguishes the media affordance from a set of media properties and their lawfu relations in her or his niche given her or his communication goal (Sanders, 1997; Stoffregen, 2000; 2003). Thus, this event forms a foundation for the subsequent choice action but is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient for engaging the user in the planned communication. To actualise the media affordance leading to a real choice (i.e. a choice that achieves the user’s goal), the user must mitigate or overcome constraints that may block her or his action.

The third relational pattern is complementarity, which relates the ‘constructed’ media affordance to situational constraints. Here, a user perceives possibilities of and constraints for communication in terms of how effectively she or he can actualise a media affordance. Because situational constraints are an element of a user’s situational awareness, they do not just follow from the emergence of media affordances; instead, constraints surround the entire media choice process. Differ ent from case 2 who could actualise his initial media affordance, cases 1 and 3 found their initial media affordances to be limited in achieving their communication goals. Case 1 perceived paralle transmission to be essentially unidirectional and lean, so he could not secure his communications to guarantee adequate understanding of the messages among clients. Case 3 also recognised that the group survey messages were likely to be ignored and could yield a low response rate. Because of their unique situations, they perceived distinct situational constraints that blocked initia media affordance (as expressed in the two dash-dot lines in Figure 1).

Norman (1999) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed three types of situational constraints. Physical constraints directly con<sup>fi</sup>ne the material actualisation of affordances. For example, the absence of a counterpart is a typical physical constraint in synchronous interaction that redirects the user towards choosing another asynchronous medium (Straub & Karahanna, 1998). When the actor prefers one medium over others, duplicate alternatives that offer equivalent outcomes (e.g. email vs voicemail) invoke logical constraints. Conventions or social norms become cultural constraints. For example, organisational norms emphasising reliability usually encourage the use of text-based media. This can evolve into best practices (i.e. the an intranet-based ERP system system used by Case 2). Finally, communication is essentially a social process that takes place at the ‘colliding’ and ‘negotiating’ surfaces between self-presentation and the presence of others (Goffman, 1959). Indeed, the choice of ICTartefacts entails not only functional and social meanings but also a means of expressing the self (Pels et al., 2002; Pfaffenberger, 2002). For example, despite acknowledging his inef<sup>fi</sup>ciency with using some ICT artefacts, the investment of<sup>fi</sup>cer still sought to present himself as a competent consultant during a phone conversation. Such a desire for dramaturgical self-representation and awareness of how user’s media choice will be interpreted by others become occasionally a critical situational constraint.

The fourth relational pattern is re-exploration. When a user faces unforeseen situational constraints, he or she must overcome them by reconsidering the initial media affordance. Here, he or she needs to modify the environment by seeking for alternative media properties and constitute new affordances. Skilful users overcome constraints typically in two ways. First, they search for alternative reciprocal relations within other media properties. For example, the instructional designer identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the hypertextuality of email as useful in overcoming low response rates. Second, they can add supplementary media properties to complement and overcome observed constraints (Michael, 2003) by engaging in bricolagé. For example, case 1 had telephone follow-ups after the group email to obtain immediate response, whereas case 3 used supplementary media content to support her lectures. Combined with advanced Hypertext Mark-up Language-based formatting, email that is often considered a lean textual medium can now provide the user with a graphically enriched online survey.

The <sup>fi</sup>fth relational pattern is actualisation: ‘what happens at the conjunction of complementary affordances and intentions or goals’ (Stoffregen, 2003b, p. 125). After users recognise that an identi<sup>fi</sup>ed media affordance is not constrained by any situational element, they engage in a communication event by choosing and manipulating the medium. The actualisation is, however, not a corollary process to the complementarity of media affordance. Thus, the media choice in this context is a choice in material use rather than a distant decision-making in the mind. For example, in this step, the user can still struggle with particular media properties when they do not easily <sup>fi</sup>t his or her effectivities or he or she makes errors. Hence, successful media choice cannot be fully predicted without adequate consideration of user agency, media artefacts and situational constraints.

Although Figure 1 analytically depicts the media choice as a phased process, the relational patterns never move in a strictly sequential order. Rather, a user’s perception of media affordances takes place transiently as he or she constantly responds to emerging situations and draws upon his or her prior experiences. Therefore, we emphasise the multi-dimensiona nature of media choice and the dynamic content of relationships between choice factors. Unless the user is requested to use a new communication technology (i.e. a new set of properties enter his or her environment), the ongoing exploration of existing media does not pose a considerable cognitive burden. Even when users adopt a new medium, they do so selectively, as they adaptively choose media properties that will complement those already in use. Our study subjects, for example, developed distinct use patterns around scheduling applications depending on their communication tasks.

## DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Our study highlights the presence of a plurality of in<sup>fl</sup>uential media choice factors. It also recognises common patterns in media choice processes wherein these factors interactively operate. We interpreted these patterns through the lens of affordance. By doing so, we can derive an ecological account of media choice in situ whereby users are seen to incorporate adaptively multiple factors in their choice processes, which comprise of the emergence and actualisation of media affordances. It also recognises individual agency as a driving force that shapes media choice. Overall, the ecological account critically evaluates several unexplored assumptions that are relevant for future theorising around media choice in complex media environments.

First, media perceptions, although varying, are essentially bound to material characteristics of media. These perceptions are continually shaped as users explore media and discover their properties – each of which has a dispositional relation to users’ courses of action. Users recognise and leverage these properties contextually when they respond to their communication goals under situational constraints. In this way, media choice is grounded simultaneously on the materialistic foundation of each medium and voluntaristic rendering of users’ media perceptions. This continued reciprocity generates a seemingly in<sup>fi</sup>nite variety of media perceptions.

Second, familiarity towards media gained through repeated uses shapes users’ media choice Although new technologies are often conveyed as improving the convenience or ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency o users’ communications, users have a tendency to return to their customary choice patterns and adjust to new media with hesitation. This preference is also validated for its ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency during successful and unsuccessful media experiences. Experience involves generating and actualising media affordances whereby users garner an increasingly intricate understanding of communication situations. Prevailing media preferences thus enforce the preferences by also improving users’ effectivities. Therefore, (habitual) choices that appear inef<sup>fi</sup>cient from the media-based approach presuming an optimal <sup>fi</sup>t between media and tasks can be ef<sup>fi</sup>cient in the users’ niche and override the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of media-based calculus.

Third, users engage in choice locally. Media choice is an individualistic event governed by a user’s localised agency as a user acquires great familiarity towards her or his media options and communication tasks. Media perceptions and subsequent behaviours are thus essentially local and re<sup>fl</sup>ect speci<sup>fi</sup>c ways in which each user will con<sup>fi</sup>gure her or his niche. Although media choice is situated, the contexts do not exercise deterministic in<sup>fl</sup>uence; for example, the extent to which users coordinate their media choices with those of others depends largely on the individual agency. Users are therefore characterised not only by their abilities for perceiving and operating media but also by their awareness of ongoing communication situations. Factors associated with these situations include social norms and media perceptions, which can enter as situational constraints and affect subsequent choice.

The ecological account opens several vistas for future research. First, we need to explore further the grounding concepts and relational patterns (Figure 1). Indeed, Gibson’s naturalistic term ‘affordance’ has recently received extensive recognition and been adopted in varied and discipline-speci<sup>fi</sup>c interpretations (Norman, 1990). Several information systems researchers have used the term to denote typi<sup>fi</sup>ed social appropriations of IT artefacts (Bergman et al., 2007; Zammuto et al., 2007; Markus & Silver, 2008). In contrast, we emphasise the emergent nature of affordances rather than their generalised nature. As such, our conceptualisation recognises dynamic relationships among multiple factors and offers strength in understanding the use of applications through interventions. Future research needs to also elaborate on the affordance concept and associated relational patterns as to offer better accounts of the dynamics as well as to extend theoretical and empirical discourse on media choice.

Second, we need more con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory case studies of choice processes and outcomes across media choice contexts as to evaluate explanatory power of the proposed model. The proposed account provides an integrated dynamic framework of choice that aims to overcome the static contingency focus of the prior ‘middle-ground’ approaches (e.g. Trevino et al., 2000; Donabedian, 2006). Although we could formulate illustrative examples from the current case study, the proposed account was derived using a limited number of exploratory cases. Therefore, the model validation and expansion will bene<sup>fi</sup>t from a broader data corpus obtained through multiple case studies across multiple use contexts. In particular, studies focusing on highly virtual and nomadic environments appear promising – not only because these environments create unprecedentedly rich media ecologies but also because they also demand that users constantly con<sup>fi</sup>gure new media properties that reveal diverse media affordances (e.g. Knutsen & Lyytinen, 2008). Methodologically, we expect future research to carry out cross-case analyses as to reveal new crucial concepts and relational patterns. In doing so, the studies can help expand the proposed model by embracing richer de<sup>fi</sup>nitions and adding new dimensions. Future research could also seek to control for the expected richness of communications by investigating how contextual characteristics shape the emer gence of media affordances.

Third, we need to identify additional ‘dependent’ variables associated with media choice outcomes beyond communication/task effectiveness. The media-based approach focuses on the ‘correct’ perception of media that can lead to increased task effectiveness, whereas the social interaction approach rests on socially constructed perceptions of media. However, how media perceptions lead to choice actions and how these actions achieve ‘designated’ effectiveness are taken for granted in these approaches. That is, they often oversimplify media choice to be preconditioned by users’ static media perceptions and consider communication/task effec tiveness as the instrumental dependent variable that re<sup>fl</sup>ects the correct media perceptions. Theory developments in the adaptive approach (e.g. Kock, 2004; Donabedian, 2006), however, urge us to tap into situations where demands on rational ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency are not the sole choice criterion. Users were inclined to maintain their habitual media choices as long as these choices worked properly at the moment rather than pursuing optimal choice determined by strict theoretical calculus. A user’s consideration of task effectiveness is implicit in his or her reciprocity and re-exploration, and the communication effectiveness is what is being enabled, partially enabled or disabled, depending on the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of a set of diverse factors. In this regard, future research needs to address how task effectiveness can be positioned in alternative ways to understand how it in<sup>fl</sup>uences media choice.

The ecological account of media choice has several implications for practice. It is now a common practice to iniect a media environment constantly with new artefacts. However, it is also important to recognise that many of these artefacts remediate users’ choice pattern and use practices. For example, mobile ICT artefacts are popular and hugely successful because they allow users to reconstruct media niches that are more tolerant to situationa conditions; smartphones and tablet PCs now connect multiple media, where each medium used to be bound to separate physical forms and constraints. They provide a more seamles transition between tasks and related communications wherever a user goes. Understanding users’ media choice patterns by re-mediating the choice patterns in design principles can become a critical rule in designing user-friendly ICT artefacts in contrast to emphasizing convenience and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency This would counteract the tendency in the design of ICT artefacts, which – in the pursuit of maximum effectiveness and functionality – ends up with creeping featurism and increased complexity (Norman, 1990).

Users’ communications could also be improved by creating more diverse ways to carry out media choice rather than offering precise criteria for optimal media choice: by doing so, one can facilitate the development of multiple alternative patterns of media choice. For example, better training on media use and clear representation of associated tasks can facilitate reciprocity. Such training should not be merely normative but also experiential, helping users engage in adaptive adjustments of their media choice. In addition, media properties are often packaged into <sup>fi</sup>xed forms, which necessitate consideration of strictly de<sup>fi</sup>ned usability factors to render the designated functions as being ‘correctly’ perceived (Schneiderman, 1992; Nielsen ,1993). However, visibility and accessibility are not limited to interface; they also need to be extended into considerations of the physical and social spaces around media artefacts (Mittleman,

2009). Actualisation of media affordances depends, for example, on constraints in a user’s physical environment. Thus, it is important to allow a ‘moderate’ level of <sup>fl</sup>exibility so as to allow for the emergence of new affordances (Hall, 1966; Alexander, 1979; Gargarian, 1993).

When new media technologies (e.g. computer-mediated communications) are being adopted in organisational settings, the initial sensation of their advanced functionalities will eventually fade away, as members garner socially constructed repertoires of use. This is the saturation point where the rational approach and the social interaction approach lose their theoretical power. Among hybrid theoretical resolutions that we called the integrative/adaptive approach, our ecological account is unique in that it views media choice as a multi-dimensional dynamic process, as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed by the users’ complex behaviours in apparently ‘static’ organisational contexts. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings do not merely embrace the tenets of prior theoretical approaches but also provide a new lens to investigate media choice in real settings. We posit that deepening such understanding has become particularly important because media technologies of the modern and post-modern era (e.g. printing, electronic media and digital media) have been speci<sup>fi</sup>cally designed to extend human faculties (McLuhan, 1964) while remediating the patterns of older technology use (Bolter & Grusin, 2001). Therefore, we need more systemic approaches, as exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed here, to account for the presence of highly routine and yet varying local patterns while choosing and adopting next-generation media technologies.
