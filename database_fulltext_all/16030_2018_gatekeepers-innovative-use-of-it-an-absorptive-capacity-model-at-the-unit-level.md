Abstract: This paper examines the potential role of gatekeepers’ innovative use of

information technology (IT) in organizational units and its ability antecedents. Using absorptive capacity theory as our theoretical lens, we theorize a model of innovative use of IT at the unit level. The proposed model was empirically validated using a survey of 204 unit gatekeepers. This paper develops a preliminary model of innovative use of IT that can inform and guide future research, specifically focusing on the key role of gatekeepers and the abilities needed by users in this role that can help in using IT in an innovative manner.

Key words: innovative use of IT; absorptive capacity; prior IT knowledge; survey research.

## Gatekeepers’ Innovative Use of IT: An Absorptive Capacity Model at the Unit Level 1. RESEARCH MOTIVATION

Information technologies (IT) have traditionally been viewed as tools to automate work processes and improve worker productivity in organizations. However, with the advent of modern computing tools such as business intelligence and big data (Baesens 2016), it is now increasingly seen as a tool that can be used innovatively to solve new business problems or existing problems in creative ways (Tiwana & Mclean 2005), which may eventually be the key to derive significant future organizational benefits (Couger 1988; Zawacki 1993; Nambisan et al. 1999; Li et al. 2013).

The concept of innovative use of IT has existed in the information systems literature for over two decades, under different labels. For example, Saga and Zmud (1994) distinguished between “standardized use” or the use of IT in a standard and recurrent manner to accomplish organizational tasks quickly and to reduce variation in outcomes, and “emergent use” or the use of IT in novel and innovative ways beyond what the system was originally intended. The concept of emergent use is similar to that of innovative use. Indeed, other authors since then have used terms “innovative use” (Li et al. 2013; Roberts et al. 2016) and “trying to innovate using IT” (Ahuja and Thatcher 2005), and “innovation in IT use” (Nambisan et al. 1999) to convey the similar concept. An innovation is defined as “an idea, practice, or object that is perceived as new by an individual or other unit of adoption” (Rogers 2003). Innovative use of IT can therefor

##

be defined as the use of IT in novel ways to perform new tasks or existing tasks in a different way (Li et al. 2013). As an illustration in a workplace context, consider the case of a marketing analyst entrusted with the responsibility of evaluating the success of her organization’s various marketing programs, understanding which marketing program is most effective with which customer segment and what product category, and optimizing its marketing programs for its target market segments. The marketing analyst will typically be provided with access to a data warehouse containing the necessary data from prior marketing programs and a business intelligence system equipped with various analytic capabilities. Traditionally, the analyst would use “canned reports” designed by IT developers that are refreshed on a monthly or weekly basis to infer about the efficacy of current marketing programs. Such reports may get the job done, but the results may be far from optimal because these reports may not reflect real-time trends and because the canned reports may not address the analyst’s specific questions. In contrast, the analyst may also use analytic tools to extract new variables from the data warehouse that are not available in the canned reports, combine variables from different reports to generate unique views of purchase patterns of customers, and generate ad hoc queries to specifically target her desired query. Doing so can help her evaluate her company’s marketing programs more accurately, track these programs in real-time, and adjust the marketing programs to target segments more effectively, and thereby get her job done better. This latter type of IT use is called innovative use, which is qualitatively different from routine use of IT examined in the prior literature, and merits attention in its own right.

Despite the intuitive appeal of innovative use, prior research on this topic has been scarce, especially in contrast to extensive volumes of research on acceptance and routine use of IT and its antecedents (e.g., Davis et al. 1989; Venkatesh et al. 2003; Beaudry & Pinsonneault 2010; Hess et al. 2014; Li & Liu 2014; Peng et al. 2014; Rizzuto et al. 2014; Kroenung & Eckhardt 2015; Montazemi & Qahri-Saremi 2015; Bayerl 2016; Hibbeln et al. 2017). Some research has recognized IT users as a source of innovative ideas in organizations (Zawacki 1993; Nambisan et al. 1999; Kankanhalli et al. 2015), examined innovative capacity and innovative activity of IT professionals (Tiwana & Mclean 2005), and studied the effects of IT use on individual innovative capacity (Marakas & Elam 1997) and on organizational innovations (Joshi et al. 2010; Kleis et al. 2012; Trantopoulos 2017). However, prior research provides little insight into the key drivers of innovative use or the underlying mechanisms that drive the innovation process. This paper addresses this unmet gap in the literature by focusing specifically on the role of “gatekeepers,” referring to key users responsible for monitoring the IT environment, interacting with partners and vendors, and identifying potential opportunities on the innovative use of IT in the business units.

The research questions of interest to this study are as follows: (1) can units benefit from innovative use of IT by gatekeepers and (2) what salient abilities do gatekeepers need in order to facilitate innovative use of IT? To address these questions, we conceptualize and test a theoretical model of innovative use of IT at the business unit level by drawing on absorptive capacity as our theoretical lens. We extend this theory by illustrating the nomological relationships among different dimensions of absorptive capacity of gatekeepers and using them to understand gatekeepers’ innovative use of IT. The proposed model was empirically validated using survey data from 204 gatekeepers of organizational units regarding their use of organizational transaction processing systems (TPSs).

Because this is not a study of IT acceptance or the quantity or frequency of IT usage, but rather focuses on a specific type of use (innovative use of IT), we have consciously stayed away from traditional IT acceptance or usage research. Extant models of IT acceptance and usage such as the technology acceptance model (Davis et al. 1989) and the unified theory of IT acceptance and usage (Venkatesh et al. 2003) do not examine innovative processes in organizations, nor do they elaborate the role of key personnel in the innovation process, and are therefore not relevant to our research.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section describes absorptive capacity theory and extends it to postulate a theoretical model of innovative use of IT at the business unit level. The third section describes the research methods used to empirically test our research model. The fourth section presents the results of data analysis. The fifth section discusses the implications of our key findings, followed by its

implications for research and practice.

## 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND AND RESEARCH MODEL

## 2.1. Absorptive Capacity and the Role of Gatekeepers

Absorptive capacity theory is a theory of organizational innovation in the management literature that explains how organizations (or organizational members) learn, innovate, and improve their performance by absorbing and leveraging knowledge from their network partners (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002; Massey & Montoya-Weiss 2006; Todorova & Durisin 2007; Su et al. 2013; Alves et al. 2016; Jha & Bose 2016; Limaj et al. 2016; Saldanha et al. 2017). Absorptive capacity refers to an organization’s (or organizational member’s) ability to recognize the value of new external information, assimilate it within internal operations, and apply it to commercial ends (Cohen & Levinthal 1990).

Absorptive capacity is usually regarded as an higher-order capability that enables organizations to exploit lower-order capabilities (Malhotra et al. 2005; Deng et al. 2008; Carlo et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2013; Iyengar et al. 2015). In the IT context, Rai et al. (2006) suggest that a firm’s IT capacity is “a lower-order capability that can be leveraged to develop higher-order process capability” (p. 227) and absorptive capacity is one of those important higher-order process capabilities. Absorptive capacity helps organizations (or organizational members) to identify new technologies or practices that may help improve their current or future performance (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). Hence, absorptive capacity theory is relevant to understand the innovative use of IT in organizations.

Absorptive capacity has been conceptualized and measured at different levels of analysis in the literature (Zahra & George 2002; Roberts et al. 2012). It has been conceptualized as an individual ability (Griffith et al. 2003; Massey & Montoya-Weiss 2006; Lin et al., 2014), a business unit ability (Tsai 2001), and an organizational ability (Jansen et al. 2005; Pavlou & Sawy 2006). Furthermore, it has been measured as a singular construct (Szulanski 1996; Tsai 2001; Ko et al. 2005; Pavlou & Sawy 2006; Su et al. 2013; Iyengar et al. 2015) and as a multidimensional construct consisting of constituent dimensions such as acquisition, assimilation, transformation, and exploitation (Jansen et al. 2005; Limaj et al. 2016) and as potential absorptive capacity (i.e., acquisition and assimilation) versus realized absorptive capacity (i.e., transformation and exploitation) (Zahra & George 2002; Setia & Patel 2013; Alves et al. 2016). However, very little research has examined the nomological relationships among these constituent dimensions.

Prior related knowledge is a key determinant of absorptive capacity (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). The process by which prior knowledge helps organizations’ (or organizational members’) absorptive capacity and the nomological relationships among different dimensions of absorptive capacity are as follows. First, organizations (or organizational members) can use their prior knowledge to identify and evaluate the

importance or value of new knowledge acquired from external sources. Prior knowledge also permits an organization or its users to predict more accurately the commercial potential of new knowledge, especially in relation to identify the potential areas of improvement within the organization that can be benefitted from the new knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1990).

Second, the possession of related knowledge permits better assimilation of new knowledge within an organization’s operations and processes. The ability to assimilate new knowledge is a function of the richness and relevance of prior knowledge, because assimilation builds upon and extends prior knowledge in a cumulative manner. As Cohen & Levinthal (1990, p. 131) note, “learning is cumulative, and learning performance is greatest when the object of learning is related to what is already known. As a result, learning is more difficult in novel domains.”

Third, recognizing the value of new knowledge and assimilating that knowledge may help organizations (or organizational members) apply the new knowledge to productive ends (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002). High learning ability permits organizations to successfully apply new knowledge (Tsai 2001). This application of new knowledge, in turn, leads to improvements in organizational performance (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002).

The absorptive capacity of an organization depends on the presence of key individuals who stand at the interface between that organization and its external environment, or between subunits within the organization. These individuals, called “gatekeepers,” perform the valuable task of monitoring the external environment, sourcing valuable knowledge of potential benefit to their organization, and communicating them to internal employees for further evaluation and possible deployment. This gatekeeping activity is particularly critical for tacit knowledge that is often embedded within organizational processes and difficult for other employees to identify, let alone use. Gatekeepers are typically referred to as “innovators” in the organizational innovation literature (Rogers 2003). Without gatekeepers, organizations cannot learn on their own, and their innovation process will likely grind to a halt. This is because gatekeepers are risk-takers, and often have explicit organizational mandate to enable their risky gatekeeping function. This contrasts with the typical organizational culture that seeks to minimize risks to maximize efficiency and productivity in everyday operations. Nevertheless, by virtue of their ability to acquire new knowledge, assimilate such knowledge in existing or new processes, and apply the knowledge to organizational ends, gatekeepers are responsible for building absorptive capacity in organizations and driving the organizational innovation process (e.g., Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra &

George 2002). Cohen and Levinthal (1990) elaborate the role of gatekeepers as,

It may therefore be argued that the absorptive capacity of an organization, business unit, or department depends on the absorptive capacity of its gatekeepers. This expectation is consistent with microfoundations (Helfat & Petaraf 2015), an emerging concept in the strategic management literature, which argues that organizations’ ability to grow and manage strategic change depends on the cognitive capabilities of specific managers in an organization entrusted with the job of identifying strategic opportunities, evaluating them, and implementing plans to take advantage of such opportunities.

## 2.2. An Absorptive Capacity Model of Gatekeepers’ Innovative Use of IT

Some of the external knowledge acquired by organizations for improving business performance is related to the new use of IT or novel ways to use the existing IT to meet unanticipated or emergent business needs. Identifying the potential of IT and evaluating how it can be leveraged within existing business processes require specialized knowledg of both IT and internal processes (Karten 1988). Hence, many innovation-centric businesses employ IT gatekeepers to monitor their business environment, interact with and learn from IT providers, business partners, and other external sources, and identify the potential application of IT within their own departments (Wu & Wang 2007).

As discussed before, prior related knowledge permits unit gatekeepers to predict more accurately the commercial potential or value of new knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). In the IT context, such prior knowledge may include knowledge about functionality of IT modules, whether a particular IT can be accommodated within an organization’s existing IT infrastructure, and how similar IT is being deployed in other companies within the industry and with what results (Nambisan et al. 1999). Without such knowledge, it will be difficult for unit gatekeepers to recognize or gauge the value of new knowledge for unit’s IT use or whether it can be applied to the greatest advantage of their departments. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: Unit gatekeepers’ prior IT knowledge is positively associated with their recognition.

Absorptive capacity theory suggests that learning is cumulative, and prior related knowledge facilitates people in assimilating the new knowledge into existing operations and procedures (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). In the IT context, this means that unit gatekeepers with prior related IT knowledge are better able to assimilate new knowledge in unit’s IT use than those lacking related IT knowledge. For example, if unit gatekeepers are knowledgeable about the existing IT infrastructure in their organizations (e.g., servers, networks, operating systems), they are better positioned to assimilate new knowledge about IT use within their work processes. If unit gatekeepers are knowledgeable about the functionality of IS modules used, they will be more successful in learning how to use IT systems features in innovative manners. Therefore, we posit the following hypothesis: Hypothesis 2: Unit gatekeepers’ prior IT knowledge is positively associated with their assimilation.

According to absorptive capacity theory, the recognition and assimilation of new knowledge typically results in improved application of such knowledge to organizational ends (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002). In a study concerning business unit innovation, Tsai (2001) argues that high learning ability permits a unit to

successfully apply new knowledge. Similarly, in the IT context, unit gatekeepers with greater ability to recognize the value of new knowledge for unit’s IT use and assimilate it should be better positioned to apply it into their business operations. Conversely, if they lack the ability of recognition or assimilation, they will struggle to apply IT in an innovative manner to productive ends. Therefore, we postulate the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3: Unit gatekeepers’ recognition is positively associated with their innovative use of IT.

Hypothesis 4: Unit gatekeepers’ assimilation is positively associated with their innovative use of IT.

Lastly, absorptive capacity theory posits that gatekeepersʼ application of new and useful knowledge acquired from external sources helps them realize significant improvements in organizational performance (Cohen & Levinthal 1990; Zahra & George 2002). This is consistent with the microfoundations literature that suggests that cognitive capacity of individual managers can influence the performance of their respective organizations by helping them identify and deploy appropriate organizational strategies (Helfat & Peteraf 2015). Similarly, in the IT context, unit gatekeepers’ application of new knowledge to unit’s IT use (innovative use of IT in operation) will allow them to track organizational processes on a real-time basis, take remedial actions quickly if things go wrong, and take advantage of opportunities about new use of IT as they arise or create the opportunities within their units (Helfat & Peteraf, 2015), thereby improving the IT

performance of organizational units or departments. Hence, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 5: Unit gatekeepers’ innovative use of IT is positively associated with their unit IT performance.

Our proposed research model, illustrating the relationships among business unit gatekeepers’ prior IT knowledge, recognition, assimilation, innovative use of IT, and unit IT performance, is depicted in Figure 2. We also include routine use of IT and top management support as control variables based on prior research that suggests that routine use of IT and top management support in IT initiatives are important predictors of organizational IT performance (Davis 1989; Somers & Nelson 2001; Delone & McLean 2003; Ragu-Nathan et al. 2004; Kim et al. 2016). In the next section, we describe how we empirically tested the proposed model.

## 3. RESEARCH METHODS

## 3.1. Data Collection

Empirical data for testing our hypotheses were collected using a field survey of IT user representatives (gatekeepers) in 204 departments from 27 companies in China’s Guangdong province. These representatives used TPSs such as enterprise resource

planning (ERP) systems, customer relationship management (CRM) systems, supply chain management (SCM) systems, and manufacturing resource planning (MRP) systems for their work, and therefore had a significant degree of prior IT-related knowledge in such systems. Additionally, these employees are often asked by their managers to be on the lookout for new ways to use IT to improve the performance of their organizations.

The TPSs context was selected because Guangdong province is the hub of the global computer and consumer electronics manufacturing industry. This region supplies assembly operations for many global firms like Apple, Dell, and Lenovo, and TPSs are heavily used to monitor, manage, and improve manufacturing operations in this industry. Given increasing cost pressures in industrial manufacturing, these companies are forced to continually innovate using IT to improve business efficiency and performance. Companies in the Guangdong province exhibit varying levels of transaction processing competencies and different levels of IT performance, allowing us a good variance in the dependent variable to detect our effects of interest.

We personally contacted the top managers in industrial manufacturing companies in Guangdong province to solicit their support for this research. Twenty-seven companies agreed to participate in our research. We then asked these companies to distribute the survey questionnaires to gatekeepers in their organizational units who used TPSs. Each respondent represented a unique unit and was asked to respond as a key informant of the department. A total of 204 valid questionnaires were returned. Among these respondents,

81% had college-level education or higher, 80% were between the age of 21 and 40 years, and 72% were male. These demographics are typical of China’s industrial workforce, which tends to be college-educated, young, and predominantly male.

## 3.2. Measurements

Seven constructs were measured in our study: prior IT knowledge, recognition, assimilation, innovative use of IT, IT performance, routine use of IT, and top management support. Measures of these constructs, except recognition, were drawn from previous research, with modifications to fit the current research context. To ensure that there were no semantic discrepancies between Chinese and English, the original English-language measures were translated into Chinese, and the Chinese questionnaire items were back-translated into English and compared with the original items by two independent professional translators (Li et al. 2013).

Because we could not locate an appropriate measure for recognition, we designed a new measurement scale for this construct. For this purpose, we interviewed seventeen practitioners: four IT consultants, seven unit gatekeepers, five chief information officers (CIOs), and a university professor with extensive IT consulting experience. Interviewees were provided with the conceptual definition of the construct, asked to examine to what extent an initial set of measurement items for this construct matched its conceptual definition (for face validity), and provide suggestions for further improving item

wordings.

Table 1 provides the conceptual definitions of all constructs and sources of their measures.

Prior IT knowledge was measured using five items taken from prior studies (Boyatzis 1982; Spencer & Spencer 1993; Nambisan et al., 1999; Tippins & Sohi 2003), and adapted to our innovation using IT context. Recognition was measured using a new scale, as described before, based on the conceptual definition of this construct. Assimilation was measured using four items, drawn from previous research (Jansen et al. 2005; Pavlou & Sawy 2006), and adapted to our context. Innovative use of IT was measured using four items adapted from previous research (Zhou & George 2001; Li et al 2013). IT performance was measured using four items drawn from Ragu-Nathan et al. (2004), and adapted to our context. Routine use of IT was measured using three items adapted from previous research (Li et al. 2013). Lastly, top management support was measured using three items modified from previous research (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2004). All measurement items used a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

## 4. DATA ANALYSIS AND RESULTS

## 4.1. Measurement Model

Convergent and discriminant validity for all scales were assessed by exploratory factor analysis (EFA), using the SPSS program, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the LISREL program. EFA was conducted to provide an initial check of convergen and discriminant validity of our measurement scales. The results of the analysis are shown in Table 2. A seven-factor solution emerged, which explained 81% of total variance. All items had greater than 0.6 loading on their respective factors, while cross-loadings were less than 0.3 (with two exceptions: PK2 and RC4), which provided preliminary evidence for convergent and discriminant validity.

Next, we conducted CFA to further evaluate the construct validity of these constructs. Tables 3 and 4 present the CFA results. A minimum sample size of 150 is suggested for a measurement model with seven or fewer constructs and modest communalities (0.5) (Hair et al. 2010). The current measurement model has seven constructs and all communalities are over 0.5, and hence our sample size of 204 is acceptable. For model fit, if the sample size is less than 250 and the number of observed indicators is between 12 and 30, it is suggested that the normed chi-square $( \chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } )$ should not exceed 3, CFI should be 0.95 or higher, and RMSEA should be less than 0.08 (Hair et al. 2010). The current measurement model, with 27 observed indicators, has a $\chi ^ { 2 }$ value of 566.84 with 303 degrees of freedom $( p { < } 0 . 0 1 )$ ), normed chi-square $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f }$ value of 1.87, CFI of 0.98, and RMSEA of 0.07, all of which indicate acceptable model fit.

To examine convergent validity, we used three criteria suggested by Hair et al. (2009): (1) standardized loading estimates should be 0.7 or higher; (2) internal consistency reliability should be 0.7 or higher; and (3) average variance extracted (AVE) should be 0.5 or higher. As shown in Table 3, in the current CFA model, all standardized item loadings exceed 0.7 and are significant at p<0.05. As Table 4 shows, internal consistency reliability ranges from 0.85 to 0.96. Furthermore, AVE ranges from 0.59 to 0.88, which is greater than variance attributable to the measurement error (see Table 3). Therefore, the construct measurements in this study have good convergent validity.

To examine discriminant validity, we used Hair et al.’s (2009) recommendation that the average variance-extracted estimates for any two constructs should be greater than the squared of the correlation estimate between these two constructs. As Table 4 shows, the smallest AVE is 0.59, which is greater than the largest squared correlation estimate, 0.45 (i.e., the square of the correlation estimate between recognition and assimilation). Therefore, all our construct measures have acceptable discriminant validity.

## 4.2. Structural Model

We tested our five hypotheses using the structural equation modeling (SEM) approach using the LISREL program. The results of the SEM analysis are shown in Figure 3. Our hypothesized model demonstrates good fit with the observed data with a $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f }$ value of 2.34 $( \chi ^ { 2 } { = } 7 3 5 . 5 1$ ; df= 314), CFI of 0.96, and RMSEA of 0.08.

As Figure 3 shows, all five hypothesized paths in the research model are supported at p<0.01 significance level. Prior IT knowledge predicts recognition (β=0.68) and assimilation (β=0.59), explaining 46% and 34% of the variance in the respective dependent variables. Innovative use of IT is predicted by recognition (β=0.30) and assimilation (β=0.46); the two variables jointly explain 41% of the total variance in the innovative use of IT. IT performance is predicted by innovative use of IT (β=0.19), top management support (β=0.49), and routine use of IT $( \beta \mathrm { = } 0 . 1 5 )$ , jointly explaining 40% of the variance in IT performance.

## 4.3. Common Method Bias

Although perceptual scales have been used widely in survey research (e.g., Zhou & George 2001; Jansen et al., 2005), they are often subject to common-method bias. Since all our constructs of interest were measured perceptually using five-point Likert scales, we too had a possibility of this bias. We tried to reduce the effect of common-method bias by clearly reminding respondents in our cover letter that their responses were anonymous and would not be shared with any third party including their employers, that there were no right or wrong answers, and that they should answer the questions honestly (Podsakoff et al. 2003). We also tested for this bias by specifying a common-method factor that has effects on all measurement items but no relationships with the seven constructs in the model (Podsakoff et al. 2003). In the presence of this common-method factor, most of the path coefficients became smaller (e.g., the path from prior IT knowledge to recognition dropped from 0.68 to 0.62, and the path from prior IT knowledge to assimilation dropped from 0.59 to 0.48). But all coefficients remained statistically significant, suggesting that even if common-method variance was present among measurement items in our data set, such variance did not significantly influence the hypothesized relationships among our variables of interest (Podsakoff et al. 2003; Loi et al. 2009).

## 5. DISCUSSION

The motivation for this study was an observed gap in research on how users can innovatively use IT in organizations. While IT research has traditionally focused on the efficiency and effectiveness outcomes of routine use of IT, organizations are increasingly realizing today that innovative use of IT may be the key to their future survival. However, the mechanisms that organizations can use to promote such innovative use are not well documented. This paper develops and tests one of the earliest models of innovative use of IT in organizations, focusing specifically on the role of gatekeepers in business units. Drawing on absorptive capacity theory, we outline the key abilities that gatekeepers must possess to promote innovative use of IT (recognition and assimilation), the preconditions for such innovative use (prior IT knowledge), and its outcomes (unit IT performance). A unit-level analysis using data from a sample of 204 unit gatekeepers in industrial manufacturing provides support for the proposed hypotheses.

The first important finding of this study is the observed relationship between innovative use of IT by gatekeepers and IT performance. Although the importance of innovative use has been suggested in the literature (Couger 1988; Zawacki 1993; Nambisan et al. 1999) and prior research has examined the relationship between innovation and business outcomes (e.g., Tsai 2001) at the business unit level, but to the best of our knowledge, this study is possibly one of the earliest to examine the specific role of key innovation players such as gatekeepers. Other research on the relationship between IT usage and organizational performance (Delone & McLean 2003; Kim et al. 2016) has focused on routine use of IT, rather than on innovative use of IT. Traditionally, routine use of IT is presumed to improve organizational performance through automation and by providing better information to make better decisions. By controlling the effects of such routine use on IT performance, our results suggest a second way in which IT use can drive performance: through innovative use.

Second, we elaborate the mechanisms by which gatekeepers facilitate innovative use of IT and thereby business unit performance. Using the theoretical lens of absorptive capacity theory, we suggest that innovative use of IT follows three phases: recognition, assimilation, and application of new knowledge. We hypothesize the nomological relationships among these phases, as well as their antecedents and consequents, to postulate a theoretical model of innovative use of IT, which may serve to inform and guide future research in this area. We suggest that innovative use of IT in organizations is driven by gatekeepers who are entrusted, either formally or informally, the role of innovation leaders in their organizations or departments. Our research highlights a new role for gatekeeping IT users that has previously been overlooked in the IT literature.

A third key finding in our study is that prior IT knowledge is a predictor of recognition and assimilation. This suggests that not all organizational employees are equally capable of using IT innovatively; only those with prior IT knowledge can effectively recognize the value of new knowledge for unit’s IT use, assimilate such knowledge into organizational processes, and therefore apply IT innovatively for organizational improvement.

## 5.1. Contributions to Research

This study makes several contributions to IT research. First, it draws attention to the relationship between innovative use of IT and organizational performance – a previously under-examined area of IT research. While prior studies have examined the relationship between quantity of IT use and performance (e.g., DeLone & McLean 2003; Kim et al. 2016), we show that the quality of use (e.g., the extent to which it is used innovatively) may also contribute to organizational performance. This finding recasts IT users in a new light (as enablers of innovation), which offers interesting possibilities for reexamining the value of IT use in organizations.

Second, we provide a preliminary model of how specific organizational users (gatekeepers) can drive innovative use of IT. While most prior IT usage research has examined typical users’ use of IT in a routine manner based on motivational antecedents such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and social norms (e.g., Venkatesh et al. 2003; Li et al. 2013; Hess et al. 2014), we show that innovative use of IT by a specific category of users (gatekeepers) responsible for driving organizational innovation are driven by absorptive capacity theoretic constructs such as abilities of recognition and assimilation, which may serve as the starting point for more extensive theorizing of innovative use of IT in organizations.

Third, we illustrate how prior IT knowledge helps in using IT in an innovative manner. Prior research has noted that IT knowledge helps with innovative use of IT; however, the mechanism and empirical evidence were unclear (e.g., Nambisan et al. 1999). Our research reveals two mechanisms through which users turn their prior IT knowledge into innovative use of IT, that is, by aiding the recognition of the value of new knowledge for IT use and by helping assimilate that knowledge in organizational processes.

Lastly, our research demonstrates a new role for certain IT users in organizations as gatekeepers. Gatekeepers must have extensive prior IT knowledge to discover how to apply IT innovatively to realize significant improvements in organizational or unit performance. As users may vary substantially in their IT knowledge (Aggarwal et al. 2015), not every IT user is suited for this role. Nonetheless, this new IT gatekeeper role may contribute to understand how micro-level players in organizations (users) may influence macro-level factors (organizational or business unit performance) in the IT context.

## 5.2. Contributions to Practice

The findings of this study also contribute to IT practice. First, our finding that innovative use of IT can drive organizational performance will be of interest to IT managers who are continually pressurized to demonstrate or defend the business value of organizational IT investments. While routine use of IT has traditionally been viewed as a source of organizational efficiency and effectiveness, we show that innovative use of IT can be an alternate way of realizing organizational performance. Organizations should therefore encourage IT users to use IT innovatively, in order to discover new uses of IT or apply IT in new ways to improve IT performance.

Second, our finding that users can be a source of innovations, if entrusted with the authority or responsibility of recognizing the value of new knowledge, assimilating it, and using IT innovatively in organizational tasks, is of particular relevance to human resource managers in organizations. This calls for expanding the scope of responsibilities for at least some IT users beyond their traditional function as simply using the IT as directed, and placing them in gatekeeping roles to improve organizational performance.

Third, we remind practitioners that not every IT user is equally capable of using IT innovatively or serving in gatekeeping roles. Rather, only those users with significant prior IT knowledge should be entrusted with this responsibility, given our observed association of the relationships between prior IT knowledge, recognition, and assimilation. Therefore, organizations should recruit expert IT users for gatekeeping roles in order to best stimulate innovations in organizations. Alternatively, key users may be systematically trained on IT before they are placed in the gatekeeping roles.

## 5.3. Limitations of our Study

Like most other empirical studies, this study too suffers from a few limitations. The first limitation is the narrow set of constructs used to explain innovative use of IT. Our choice of constructs was restricted by our adherence to absorptive capacity theory as our theoretical lens. There may be many additional predictors of innovative use of IT that are not examined in this paper; we leave those predictors as opportunities for future research. Future studies may consider other theoretical perspectives and employ constructs from those theories to enrich or model or build more comprehensive models of innovative use of IT.

The second limitation is that our model only shows the importance of gatekeepers in building IT absorptive capacity of business units. However, there may be many other users, not in gatekeeping roles, that may also help facilitate innovative use of IT in business units. Our choice of unit gatekeepers was motivated by our research questions and the practical considerations of the survey. Nonetheless, future research may analyze the roles of other individuals in fostering organizational absorptive capacity, further elaborate the relationships between gatekeepers and other users in enabling organization-wide innovative use of IT, and the conditions under which gatekeepers innovative use of IT improves or does not improve organizational IT performance.

#

## 6. CONCLUSION

In closing, this study postulated an absorptive capacity theoretic model of innovative use of IT and empirically validated that model using a survey of gatekeepers in business units. We demonstrate that gatekeepers can indeed foster innovative use of IT in organizational units, elaborate two mechanisms by which innovative use of IT can occur (through recognition and assimilation), and identify antecedent (prior IT knowledge) and outcome (IT performance) of innovative use of IT. The primary contributions of this paper include developing and validating one of the earliest models of innovative use of IT in the literature, recasting the role of certain key IT users in organizations as gatekeepers, and identifying the abilities needed by IT users to staff the gatekeeping responsibilities. We hope that our research will stimulate further research interest in exploring innovative use of IT in organizations, and more generally, in the quality of IT use in contrast to the quantity of use.
