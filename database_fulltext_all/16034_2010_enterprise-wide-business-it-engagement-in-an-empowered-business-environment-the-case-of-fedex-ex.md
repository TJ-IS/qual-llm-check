## Abstract

In 2003, Rob Carter, CIO of international express courier FedEx, launched the ‘Six by Six’ (6 - 6) IT transformation program: a major rationalization and centralization effort to improve FedEx’s IT service delivery to its business partners. This teaching case deals with the 6 - 6 efforts made by the IT department for Europe, Middle East, Indian sub-continent and Africa (EMEA). The case focuses on two objectives of the 6 - 6 program: (a) creating a consistent IT environment and (b) increasing delivery bandwidth to the business. Historically, FedEx Express in EMEA had granted high levels of empowerment to loca business and IT people. Moreover, FedEx had always supported a ‘can-do’ mentality in both business and IT people. Consequently, the IT department was expected to keep the enterprise systems architecture resilient and supportive of longer-term enterprise growth, while keeping the IT development pipeline aligned with the often unpredictable stream of requests from empowered business constituents. The story begins in 2007, when a new Vice President for IT was appointed for the region. Part A deals with the IT department’s efforts to set up a central systems architecture and IT resource estimation review process. Part B deals with demand-side prioritization issues. The suggested teaching plan assumes that Part A is discussed before the students receive Part B. Instructors can use this case to introduce their students to a realistic view on the complex puzzle of establishing enterprise-wide business-IT engagement patterns through IT governance in an empowered and operations-focused business environment.

## Part A

pril 2007, Brussels, Belgium – It was one of the hottest April days ever recorded in Belgium as Stefaan Butaye sat in his office at FedEx Express watching the airplanes fly over from nearby Brussels Airport. Stefaan had been Managing Director (MD) of the Strategic Planning & Programs (SP&P) team in the IT department for FedEx Express in EMEA<sup>1</sup> for 3 years. He was reviewing the latest business requests for IT development work in preparation for that afternoon’s Supply Review Board (SRB) meeting. Still, he was feeling a little distracted. Beth

Galetti, the newly appointed Vice President (VP) of IT at FedEx Express EMEA,<sup>2</sup> had just asked him to brief her the next day on his team’s achievements over the past 3 years.

Three years earlier, in 2004, the previous VP of IT in EMEA, Eric Keane, had asked Stefaan to move from Customer Technology in Marketing<sup>3</sup> and join him in his efforts to re-invent the IT department for EMEA. Stefaan: ‘Eric drew me a clear picture at the time: the EMEA region had been developing at the speed of light and this had put considerable pressure on the IT department’s ability to deliver IT developments, and manage the region’s IT environment sustainably.

It was also a time when the FedEx global CIO, Rob Carter, expected a commitment from the EMEA region for realizing the goals of his major ‘Six by Six’ (6 - 6) IT transformation program, which had been initially geared towards the domestic operations (i.e. USA). Two of the six key program objectives would form the backdrop for Stefaan’s team: (a) create a consistent IT environment and (b) increase delivery bandwidth to the business, that is, improving the IT department’s capability to deliver what the business needed, when they needed it. During the ensuing 3 years Stefaan and his colleagues had implemented governance instruments that could engage business and IT people to stabilize and simplify the EMEA IT environment and expand the EMEA IT department’s capacity for reliably delivering IT developments to its business partners.

Stefaan knew he needed to get his meeting with Beth right from the first try. As he recounted: ‘When someone like a new VP is being installed, a lot of buzz is going around. I had been hearing that Beth wanted to focus on: ‘‘delivering what we promise.’’ So, how was she going to evaluate our achievements?’

## There’s a FedEx for that

Headquartered in Memphis, FedEx was founded in 1973 by ex-marine Frederick Smith. Ever since, ‘to FedEx something’ had become synonymous for sending a package overnight. By 2007, FedEx Corporation had become renowned for its worldwide network of companies delivering a wide range of express package delivery, supply chain, transportation, and related business and information services with consolidated revenues amounting to over USD 37 billion. Tables 1 and 2 give an overview of key FedEx financials.

At the turn of the millennium, FedEx Corporation had found itself at a crucial stage in its global development history. Intensified international competition and economic turmoil had resulted in flat profit margins and stabilizing growth rates in FedEx’s domestic market. To adapt to these tougher market conditions, FedEx had taken on an expansion strategy. They broadened and deepened their geographical and product portfolio reach to create four main service lines with operations spanning the globe: FedEx Express, FedEx Ground, FedEx Freight, and FedEx Kinko’s. Figures 1 and 2 provide an overview of the history and a description of these service lines.

To maximize the realization of synergies from this global collection of activities, FedEx Corporation drew up its worldwide reporting structure under the slogan: ‘To operate independently, compete collectively, and manage collaboratively.’ Each of the four service lines was considered a specialty operating company with its own responsibility to manage and expand its specific line of services internationally. Each operating company was led by a President. The operating company Presidents and FedEx corporate officers were responsible for global strategy and brand management of FedEx Corporation as a whole.

## 6 - 6 IT transformation

Value delivery from IT investments had never been an issue at FedEx. Everybody understood how IT enabled the company to compete at the amazing pace of a global, high-speed business. As early as 1979, FedEx founder and Chairman Fred Smith stated: ‘The information about a package is as important as the delivery of the package itself.’ Ever since, FedEx had relied heavily on the power of the innovative and advanced use of IT to support, develop and expand its operations and customer offerings.

## Joe Kinder

A snowstorm had shut down the city, but FedEx Express courier Joe Kinder had an important delivery of visas for a couple traveling to Russia to adopt their son. The weather had prevented the delivery of the package on the expected day, and the couple was due to leave for Russia the next morning. Foreign adoption procedures made the situation dire: without the visas, the couple would not be able to adopt the child. They called FedEx, frantic for help. Joe tracked the package down and, on his way home, despite the dangerous road conditions, he drove out of his way to the couple’s neighborhood to deliver the visas. The delivery allowed the couple to leave on time and bring home their new son. The happy parents expressed their thanks to Joe by later introducing him to their son and sharing with him how Joe’s last name, Kinder, meant ‘children’ in German, but it meant ‘family’ to them.

Courier James Bush, Jr. did not intend to go swimming during the working day, especially with harsh winds and rain covering much of Honolulu, Hawaii. However, when a gust of wind plucked a package from the back of his truck and flung it into the ocean, James did not think twice about diving in. He recovered the package and, soaking wet, delivered it to the customer.

Although her employer didn’t have a scheduled Friday pickup, a jewelry store employee contacted FedEx Express about a shipment that desperately needed to go out that afternoon with a ring for a wedding scheduled for the next day. FedEx Express courier Ed Walker of Scottsdale, Arizona, reacted quickly. With no time to explain the situation to his Manager or find an alternate vehicle, Ed picked up the ring in his personal vehicle and it was delivered as scheduled, in time for the wedding.

FedEx had achieved many competitive ‘firsts’ through the use of IT, such as the first PC-based shipment automation system and the pioneering use of the internet with FedEx.com. Box 2 presents an overview of some of these IT milestones.

In 2000, Rob Carter had taken office as CIO of FedEx Corporation. He reported directly to the FedEx President and Chairman, Fred Smith. By that time, IT had become all but ubiquitous at FedEx. Among others, IT was automating sorting activities, enabling flexible wireless servicing and shipment tracking, improving the customer experience through FedEx.com, and streamlining international customs clearing.

At the yearly IT Summit in Memphis in January 2003, Carter had announced his sweeping plan for the 6 - 6 transformation program. ‘IT is broken! y or at least, the business thinks we are,’ the CIO exclaimed. During the course of the summit, he unfolded his vision and plans. He believed strongly that decisive action was needed in order for FedEx to maintain its frontrunner position in leveraging IT.

Rob Carter challenged the FedEx IT departments to accomplish six improvement objectives by the end of 2006:

1. focus on the FedEx customer;

2. work as a trusted partner with the business;

3. create opportunities for every IT employee;

4. facilitate competing collectively by creating a more consistent FedEx IT environment;

5. simplify information access across FedEx businesses through FUSION;<sup>5</sup>

6. and, finally, increase IT delivery bandwidth.

In its first year, the 6 - 6 transformation program had been most prominent in the domestic market. The extensive communication efforts and drive coming from the global CIO and his team had also made the program the talk of the town in the international regions. This was especially so in early 2004, when Carter and his team expected the EMEA region to define their initiatives for realizing the promise of the 6 - 6 program.

## EMEA IT challenge in 2004

The FedEx Express business in EMEA had been expanding at double-digit growth rates. The region had realized this by growing on a country-by-country basis. FedEx Corporation had given local business management the leeway to decide opportunistically on acquisitions of local express operators and to set up new hub operations in many countries across the region. ‘At FedEx, each business decision triggers an IT event. Our operations-focused and empowered business strategy had clear repercussions for how IT and the IT department had evolved in the region,’ Eric Keane underscored.

At the time of Stefaan’s arrival in 2004, the IT department in EMEA observed four main functions. First, they maintained centrally a set of region-wide systems. Second, the central IT teams in Brussels were responsible for servicing the non-operations-focused business areas (e.g. Marketing, Finance, and HR) and for coordinating IT developments that were part of large multi-country or region-wide strategic renewal, innovation and compliance programs. Operations-focused business areas were mostly serviced by decentralized IT staff located at the operational hubs in the different countries. Third, Stefaan’s SP&P team was responsible for drawing up the annual budget for the whole IT department. This included getting an overview of IT development work required for operations and other business areas across the region. And finally, individual MDs of IT, reporting directly to the VP of IT in EMEA, were responsible for managing different pools of IT competencies. These were grouped roughly according to their contribution to different parts of a typical IT development life cycle. For example, Roger Van Beeck, MD for Applications and Architecture, managed a central pool of Developers, whereas the MDs of Business and Customer Technologies would manage the final implementation of developed systems, done by decentralized IT staff, in FedEx’s business areas and corporate customers (see Figure 3 for an organizational overview).

The decentralized business growth strategy was clearly reflected in how the budget of the IT department and requests for IT development work were dealt with in the EMEA region. Once a year, the Finance department for EMEA asked the IT department to draw up an overview of the expected IT expenditures in the region for the next fiscal year. This overview included the budget of the IT department and the expected expenditures by the business areas on new IT investments for their business initiatives. The budget of the IT department included running costs, such as wage costs of all IT staff, software licenses costs, and systems maintenance costs, and investment costs for region-wide systems. The systems investment costs related to IT development work for business initiatives remained on the budget of the respective business areas.

Each year, the SP&P team would collect the wish-lists from operations and other business areas for the coming fiscal year. To collect this information, someone from Stefaan’s team had to call key business and IT representatives from different business areas across the EMEA region. Based on the information collected, the Finance department could estimate the expected split of capital and operational expenditures on IT.<sup>6</sup> The IT department used this information to include the necessary provisions for IT resources in their yearly budget and plan. Based on this annual exercise, the business people expected that the IT department would have enough IT manpower available for all types of requests for IT work, for example, operations-focused work in a hub, work for other business areas, and multi-country initiatives.

During the year, new IT development work could effectively start after the Finance department released the reserved business budget for a particular ‘capital lease expenditure authorization request’ (CLEAR) submitted by a business representative.<sup>7</sup> These requests did not necessarily include only IT work. Most initiatives at FedEx needed work done by engineering and other technical staff. The

Finance department would base their approval of a business request on purely financial grounds. Did the CLEAR contain a sound business case (e.g. in terms of return on investment)? Did the request comply with the applicable accounting rules (e.g. for allocating operational and capital expenditures)?

If the CLEAR was approved, the business representative would then ask his IT contact to initiate the necessary IT work and purchases. CLEARs for operations-oriented IT development work did not necessarily pass by a central IT team; the different IT contacts would simply get started after seeing an approved CLEAR.

Getting expenditures released from the Finance department was not a problem if a financially sound and compliant business case had been made. Often problematic, though, were cases where the IT staff was confronted with a CLEAR that contained a request for IT development work that had not been included in the annual IT budget, or that had been underestimated. This was not an uncommon situation. Often, unforeseeable business opportunities and challenges required IT effort (e.g. new hub openings, new pricing decisions, local customs and regulatory changes). In these cases, although the business might have had their approved CLEAR from Finance in hand, their IT contacts did not always have the slack available to deliver the required IT developments. So, quite often actually, the business representatives would call ‘a guy’ they knew to get some work done for them quickly. Although most IT people were very forthcoming in meeting such unexpected requests, these jobs would frequently interfere with perhaps less urgent, but planned IT work. Consequently, delays had started to build up in mainly pre-approved development work and, of course, the business projects of which they were part.

The fact that IT development work often had to be executed quickly for unanticipated requests had also resulted in a very complex and disparate systems architecture. For example, in 2004, FedEx Express EMEA had over 30 different systems to deal with customs clearance. This meant that more and more of the IT department’s time and budget was being allocated to finding ways to integrate existing systems, perform maintenance activities, and basically prevent the whole thing from breaking down and bringing the business to a halt. There were stories going round of instances where adding a single data field to an existing region-wide system was being quoted at a cost of up to EUR 1 million.

As time went by, the business increasingly experienced project delays and high costs in most of its projects due to IT integration and resourcing problems. In particular, the delays and problems in highly visible multi-country and region-wide projects had put the perception of the added value of the IT department under considerable pressure. Stefaan recalls: ‘Many business partners in EMEA perceived us as an unreliable and costly supplier of their much needed IT solutions.

When Eric Keane became VP for IT in EMEA in 2004, he believed the region would benefit from better functioning central IT oversight to support the region’s growth sustainably, and in line with the 6 - 6 goals as set out by FedEx’s global CIO.

Eric knew it would take vision and good leadership to guide this revitalization. He reorganized the IT department to realize this and asked Stefaan to take charge of a newly created central group for EMEA: the SP&P team.<sup>8</sup> Eric expected Stefaan to come up with initiatives to improve central governance of the IT pipeline of development work. This would help put the department’s ability and reputation back on track to cater for the IT needs of the business.

Stefaan remembers: ‘From an IT supply point of view, the existing routines had created a rather haphazard IT work initiation process. While this process had served the EMEA business well in enabling its quick growth, the situation had become unsustainable in the long run. My team and I needed to develop governance processes and engagement practices that would not only create more central control over the IT supply pipeline, but also guarantee the needed agility for future strategic moves in the region.’

## Say ‘no’ first, so you can say ‘yes’ more often later

Stefaan was a strong proponent of an architecture-based approach to tackling this situation: ‘We had to increase our ability to steer all requests towards more cost-effective, better-scoped and less risky solutions. What’s more, we needed to do so without negatively impacting and even increasing our flexibility in delivering to the business what they needed, when they needed it. This meant halting the proliferation of the EMEA region’s convoluted IT environment. We first had to develop a vision within the IT department of a simplified systems architecture. This would be our yardstick for assessing both current and future requests for business solutions. We then needed to rally the local IT staff, the central IT teams, the operations and other business areas around the idea of collectively behaving like co-custodians of the enterprise systems architecture. This meant selling and enforcing some architectural discipline and consistency when the business asked for IT developments. In this way, we could increase our added value to the business in both the short and the long term. In sum, we were convinced we needed to be able to say ‘‘no’’ to the business first, so as to be able to say ‘‘yes’’ more often later.

Together with the other IT MDs in the EMEA region, Stefaan had created the necessary governance instruments to engage both the business and IT in realizing the selected 6 - 6 goals. A new IT development work request process, devised and governed by Stefaan’s team, became the backbone to connect these governance instruments. Figure 4 shows the process flow, and how it fitted within the ‘definition’ and the start of the ‘planning’ phases of the FedEx ‘cross-functional development process’ (xFDP), that is, the standard business and IT project life cycle at FedEx.

The SRB was central to the process. The SRB consisted of all MDs of IT in EMEA. As ‘SRB facilitator,’ Stefaan Butaye, was appointed to watch over the flow of the whole request submission and decision-making process, as well as chair the actual SRB meetings. The SRB reviewed all new requests for IT development work in the region in order to ascertain: (a) whether the proposed solution (re-)used standard solutions as much as possible, (b) whether the IT resources requested by the business were included in the annual plan, (c) whether the estimations were correct, and (d) whether the required resources (technical and human) were physically available to complete the request.

To optimally fulfill its role, the SRB was assisted by the, also newly created, ‘technical architecture advisory board’ (TAAB). The TAAB was a group of four or five people working with EMEA IT senior management to define, coordinate, and communicate the envisaged overall IT EMEA systems architecture, strategy, and standards (i.e. the ‘to be’ situation). To this end, for example, the TAAB oversaw the ‘Take Off’-project. This was an initiative to simplify the current constellation of systems across the region. The plan was to reduce the number of IT systems from over 165 to less than 100 by FY09.

According to the new process, when someone in the business needed IT development work done for a project, he had to complete an ‘express definition document (EDD). This person then became the ‘business representative’ for that request. The EDD stated the high-level functional requirements and business needs, and a first estimation of the financial and human resource implications of the request.

Once an EDD had been submitted, the ‘SRB facilitator’ had a key role in triaging the request to a specific ‘subject matter expert’ (SME). SMEs were IT people who were distributed across the region. Stefaan was wary of creating too much new bureaucracy, so the SME was not a newly created full-time position; any IT person, local or central, with sufficient experience and seniority could be asked to take on the role of SME for a particular request.

Through a couple of rounds of discussions, the SME and the business representative refined the latter’s EDD into a ‘statement of work’ (SoW). The SoW contained a further estimation of resource requirements, systems impact, dependencies and alternatives based on the business needs.

The SRB had three options when reviewing SoWs. It could immediately approve a request in its estimates; only then was the request allowed to go into the financial (CLEAR) and further business and technical project steps of the xFDP. The SRB could also disagree with the estimates in the SoW. The request was then put on hold; the SRB sent the SoW back to the business representative and SME to fine-tune the SoW before re-submitting it to the SRB. A third possibility was that the SRB agreed with the details contained in the SoW, but was unable to guarantee the availability of IT resources to complete the work within the expected timeframe. In that case, too, the SRB would put the request on hold.

The business representative then had the possibility of escalating the request to his senior ‘business sponsor.’ The sponsor could: (a) approve the release of extra expenditures to hire external resources and complete the request under the guidance of the IT department, (b) ask for IT resources to be decommissioned from other less important business requests for IT work towards the escalated request, or (c) withdraw the request.

The SRB process was launched at the end of 2004 and met fortnightly from then on. By 2007, in principle, no IT development work was initiated without an SRB number; the Finance department simply no longer accepted CLEAR submissions without an SRB number. Stefaan: ‘Of course, in the beginning we had to deal with a lot of resistance from our stakeholders, from both business and IT. But with Eric’s backing, we were able to persist. Since then, request by request, the SRB has simply become a fact of life for business and IT across EMEA.’

## Mission accomplished?

The 6 - 6 program was officially concluded in EMEA in April 2006 with an executive visit from the US. The delegation included Rob Carter. Carter and his team appeared to be particularly impressed with the realizations in EMEA on architectural hygiene and the IT supply pipeline through the SRB/TAAB construction.<sup>9</sup>

By the end of 2006, it had become clear that Eric Keane was going to move back to the US in a new position as senior VP for International IT. Anticipating a new VP for IT in EMEA, Stefaan had collected some input from key business partners on their perception of the installed approval processes. In preparation for his meeting with Beth Galetti the next day, Stefaan reviewed his notes on the feedback he had received at the time.

Andy Hogan, MD of Planning & Engineering in EMEA, commented: ‘I haven’t been hearing much about the SRB process in meetings of the Executive Council or other senior management forums. Still, come to think of it y to my mind, in recent years IT has indeed moved forward. Maintenance costs haven’t been rising the way they used to each year and I’m being asked less often to intervene in firefighting for projects gone bad due to poor IT scoping or estimations. That may also explain why I haven’t been asked to approve as many additional major expenditure releases for the same projects as I did some years ago.’

Jean-Marc DuPont, MD of Customer Service, agreed with Andy: ‘The process ensures that we are getting the right knowledge for the IT part of our projects applied as soon as possible in the project life cycle. This used to happen only much later in a project’s life cycle, or even not at all if the project passed through the informal network. Thanks to the new process, there are fewer surprises when development is done. This helps to make our projects smoother and more stable in execution and roll-out.

Despite some of the positive comments, Stefaan still had a feeling that some issues remained unresolved. He knew that some business MDs, especially in more operationsoriented business areas, perceived the SRB process as sometimes being too rigid and too slow compared to the pace at which the business wanted to make decisions. One business MD admitted that he could still bypass the SRB if he really wanted something done urgently. Other MDs considered the EDDs too technical for their staff to fill out. Some business representatives felt they needed to be experts or at least to have a dedicated coach in IT standards to complete the documentation.

Additionally, the development work for a lot of major and highly visible projects, such as the new GENIUS clearance system,<sup>10</sup> was still not under control in terms of reaching targeted milestones. And what about alignment with the business strategy? IT work was still being executed on a first-come, first-served basis. As one business MD put it: ‘If we had a hundred business cases, and they were all worth doing, we would still want to do them all.

As he was reminiscing about all this, Stefaan suddenly realized that an hour had already passed. He needed to get started on reviewing those SoWs for the SRB meeting that afternoon. But, what was he going to tell Beth the following day?

Stefaan had been hearing that Beth wanted to focus on ‘delivering what we promise.’ He wondered how she would perceive their past efforts. Had they addressed some of the root causes of what she considered to be ‘not delivering?’ Could he prove this? Also, what improvements could he suggest?

## Part B

Back in 2004, the aspired governance setup for IT development requests differed substantially from the one that was operational in 2007. Originally, Stefaan and Eric wanted to install a cross-functional decision-making process to support both business prioritization and IT resource allocation. Figure 5 and Box 3 represent the way they had initially conceived the process. This design had come out of a strategic off-site meeting with all Managers and MDs of the IT department for the EMEA region in response to the 6 - 6 challenge.

A ‘demand review board’ (DRB) would prioritize incoming requests for IT development work cross-functionally. The DRB consisted of MDs from all business areas in EMEA (e.g. Marketing, Finance and Engineering). They were asked to prioritize based on criteria such as strategic value, business process impact, economic value, and business and IT risks. An IT development work justification template was specifically designed to summarize the relevant information for each request in a standardized, thus consistent format. The agreed-upon prioritized list of requests would then be passed on to the SRB for further systems architecture and resourcing feasibility review.

In October 2004, Eric and Stefaan extended an invitation to all business representatives to participate in the DRB. They explained the rationale behind the DRB and the workings of the new IT demand prioritization process. The message positioned the DRB as an important EMEA response to realizing Rob Carter’s strategic 6 - 6 transformation program. The first DRB meeting was scheduled to take place the next month.

‘I remember receiving that e-mail,’ recalled Andy Self, now VP of Strategic Sales & Marketing in EMEA. ‘At the time, according to the annual IT resource budget we were assigned only half a FedEx IT FTE<sup>11</sup> or so to organize and execute the IT development work we needed in Strategic Sales & Marketing. This wasn’t much if you compared it to some of the other business areas. Naturally, I was very happy to hear that from then on resource allocation would be periodically prioritized cross-functionally based on a business justification.’

The November DRB meeting, however, had not gone exactly as planned.

Stefaan commented: ‘When we met with all the MDs, not everybody had brought the required documentation. For example, while a region-wide business area such as Strategic Sales & Marketing had their fully completed set of justification documents ready, most operations-focused departments had none ready.

Andy Hogan, MD for Planning & Engineering, also commented: ‘Stefaan’s team had clearly gone about it very professionally. They had defined a process, designed supporting templates and assigned job aids to help us complete these documents. But, honestly, when the DRB was launched, some of us were really holding our breaths. We really had no idea how the new process would actually forge a cross-functional consensus.’

Moreover, there seemed to be a ‘catch-22’ associated with the selection process.<sup>12</sup> In order to make decisions about which requests to prioritize, the DRB members felt they needed information on the status of the available IT supply pipeline. At the time, however, the IT department had very little real-time visibility on that side. Stefaan commented: ‘The business confronted us with the fact that we could not give them a clear overview of our IT development pipeline. We simply could not determine on the spot whether we had enough capacity to deliver the list of chosen requests; that discussion needed to take place in an SRB meeting. So, in the end, we started iterating back and forth between the SRB and the DRB without making decisions.’

The DRB ended up meeting only five times before being disbanded. It did not succeed in coming up with a stable prioritized request list. ‘It’s not that we didn’t try. Eric and Stefaan had really put their backs into making this work. And it is probably thanks to the energy they put in, that we kept going at it. But it just didn’t work,’ concluded Andy Hogan.

Looking back, Eric Keane summarized the experience as follows: ‘The DRB experience had not been a lost effort. We simply needed to go through this phase. It taught us that we should have approached things differently. Instead of trying to force our ways of prioritizing requests onto the business, we should have aligned with how the business actually prioritized all their projects.
