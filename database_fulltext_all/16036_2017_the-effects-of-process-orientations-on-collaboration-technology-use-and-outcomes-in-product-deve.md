# The Effects of Process Orientations on Collaboration Technology Use and Outcomes in Product Development

HILLOL BALA, ANNE P. MASSEY, AND MITZI M. MONTOYA

He received his Ph.D. from the University of Arkansas. His research interests focus on IT-enabled business process change and management, IT use, adaptation and impacts, and the use of IT in health care. His work has been published or is forthcoming in Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Management Science, and other journals. She received her Ph.D. from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. Her research focuses on computer-mediated collaboration and the role of IT, including most recently the effects of enterprise social media and 3D virtual environments on team performance. She is also interested in IT-enabled innovation and IT implementation processes. She has received research funding and support from industry as well as National Science Foundation, the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, and others. Her work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Academy of Management Journal, among others.

MITZI M. MONTOYA (Mitzi.Montoya@oregonstate.edu) is the Sara Hart Kimball Dean of the College of Business at Oregon State University. She received her Ph.D. from Michigan State University. Her research has focused on innovation processes and strategies and the role of technology as an enabler of distributed team decision making. With a focus on the dynamics and performance of globally distributed work teams, her research has appeared in Management Science, Journal of Marketing Research, Marketing Science, Academy of Management Journal, and MIS Quarterly, among others. She has been the recipient of grants from, among others, the National Science Foundation, U.S. Department of Defense, U.S. Department of Education, and corporate foundations.

ABSTRACT: Notwithstanding interest in implementing information technologies (ITs) that facilitate collaboration among employees, there has been limited research that examines how such technologies influence collaboration in strategic business processes, such as new product development (NPD), and how this influence is contingent on the nature of the process. We develop and test a model that posits that employees' collaboration technology use in NPD will influence collaboration outcomes, such as IT-enabled collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction. The model postulates that NPD involves multiple process orientations—exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity—and that these orientations have a moderating effect on the relationship between collaboration technology use and outcomes. We conducted two studies in a major pharmaceutical company ( $N = 1,749$ and $1,454$ , respectively) and found support for our model. We found that the effects of collaboration technology use on outcomes were stronger for employees involved in the NPD process phase that had an ambidexterity orientation. Our findings contribute to theory and practice related to the use of collaboration technology in the NPD process by offering insights on how process orientations influence the dynamics of the relationship between employees' use of collaboration technologies and associated outcomes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: ambidexterity, collaboration technology, collaboration capability, collaboration satisfaction, innovation, information technology use, new product development.

Information technologies (ITs) have fundamentally transformed the way individuals, organizations, and societies operate, interact, and communicate. In recent years, organizations across the world have started implementing enterprise-level ITs that facilitate social interaction, collaboration, and knowledge sharing among employees who execute operational and strategic business processes $[74]$ . Research in various disciplines, for example, social psychology, organizational behavior, and information systems (IS), and the practitioner literature have made it almost a truism that collaboration and knowledge sharing in the workplace are key to individual productivity and organizational success $[3, 6, 76]$ . For instance, Cisco, a large organization, implemented a wide range of collaboration technologies and associated processes in the late 2000s $[97]$ . Consequently, it was able to save about US700 million while increasing productivity by almost 50 percent in 2008 $[71]$ . Similarly, organizations such as IBM, P&G, and Unilever have implemented enterprise-level collaboration technologies to add collaborative capabilities in core business processes and to enhance interpersonal and intraorganizational collaboration $[41, 86]$ . Notwithstanding such large investments and interest, it is not clear whether collaboration technologies can be effectively integrated and routinized in core business processes $[41]$ . In fact, the practitioner literature notes the fallacy of collaboration technology that highlights the lack of evidence that they are indeed beneficial $[39, 42, 86]$ .

In the academic literature, there have been equivocal findings related to the effects of collaboration technologies on outcomes across a wide variety of contexts. Prior findings on the relationship between collaboration technologies and team performance outcomes (e.g., decision quality, number of ideas generated, decision time, user satisfaction) have been inconclusive $[26, 34]$ . For example, Fuller and Dennis $[34]$ found that collaboration technologies positively affected team performance when technology supported the task, whereas Malhotra and Majchrzak $[59]$ found that the direct effect of collaboration technologies on team performance was not significant. Easley et al. [30] found that use had a positive effect on team outcomes for certain tasks and no effects for other tasks. Further, much prior research has focused on outcomes related to teams (e.g., [95]) and organizations (e.g., [6]), and factors that predict collaboration technology use (e.g., [10]). Research has been limited on how collaboration technologies affect individuals in the context of strategic business processes. It is important to examine the influence of these technologies on individual outcomes in such contexts because it is ultimately the individuals who use these technologies to support and execute these critical business processes [5, 10].

Against this backdrop, our objective is to understand employees' use of an integrated collaboration technology platform in the context of a strategic business process—the new product development (NPD) process. NPD is a critical IT-enabled business process in today's hypercompetitive business environment $[29, 75, 77, 89]$ . The success of NPD largely determines long-term viability and success of an organization $[15, 53, 74]$ . With the recent advancements and proliferations of ITs in the workplace, an integrated, enterprise-wide collaboration technology is likely to play an important role in the NPD process. We define an integrated collaboration technology platform (ICTP) as an assemblage of hardware, software, and processes that enable communication, coordination, information processing, resource sharing, and joint decision making by organizational members both within and across organizational boundaries. It is an enterprise-level technology platform that integrates different communication media and/or channels, for example, e-mail, instant messaging, video conferencing, online meetings, document sharing, and workflow management capabilities. Further, it offers mechanisms for information and knowledge management (e.g., sharing and/or transferring) through its various features $[10, 70, 98]$ . In the NPD context, an ICTP enables and facilitates collaboration among the individuals, teams, organizational units, and external partners involved in product development activities $[74, 75]$ .

Prior research has suggested that NPD is a complex business process involving multiple business functions and competencies $[7, 11, 79]$ . Research has also underscored the cross-functional nature of NPD and the need for superior coordination and collaboration $[7, 53]$ . Building on the widely used exploration–exploitation framework $[40, 54, 62]$ , we suggest that different phases of the NPD process constitute activities oriented toward exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity (i.e., simultaneous pursuit of both exploration and exploitation). In fact, prior research has suggested that these process orientations are integral and critical to the NPD process because NPD involves individuals and functional units with competencies and activities that can be organized into these three orientations $[54, 87]$ . For instance, individuals and units that are engaged in exploration in NPD pursue new knowledge and competencies to bring new ideas into the process $[46]$ . In contrast, individuals and units engaging in exploitation build on existing knowledge and competencies to extend and improve existing ideas and capabilities $[46]$ . Further, there are individuals and units in NPD who pursue both exploration and exploitation synchronously—ambidexterity—to achieve certain goals $[43, 46]$ .

Given the differences in these three process orientations with respect to their goals, activities, and competencies, it is possible that an ICTP will be of differential importance to employees involved in NPD activities that have different process orientations. In other words, we suggest that ICTPs are likely to have systematically different values and meanings to employees who are engaged in activities based on different process orientations in NPD.

In this research, we seek to address the following research questions: (1) Does employees' use of an ICTP lead to improved collaboration capability and satisfaction in the NPD process? and (2) What role do NPD process orientations play in the relationship between use and collaboration capability and satisfaction? Drawing on theoretical perspectives from the literature on postadoptive IT use including collaboration technology use (e.g., [10, 14, 48]), coordination theory (e.g., [20, 22, 61]), and the exploration–exploitation framework (e.g., [47, 62, 72, 82]), we develop a research model to examine these questions. We test our model using two field studies (N = 1,746 and N = 1,451, respectively) conducted in a major pharmaceutical company that implemented an ICTP for employees involved in NPD (i.e., drug discovery, development, and production activities). We found strong support for our model. Our findings have important theoretical and practical implications.

## Coordination Theory

We use coordination theory as a theoretical foundation of our research model and hypotheses. While we do not include a detailed discussion of the theory because there have been multiple reviews of the theory in the literature (see $[20, 21, 22]$ ), we provide a brief discussion of the core tenet of the theory.

At its core, coordination theory provides a rich understanding of how tasks and resources are dependent on each other, and offers various coordination mechanisms to manage these dependencies. The theory offers a parsimonious and actionable definition of coordination as a process of managing dependencies between interdependent tasks and resources $[22]$ . It suggests that various forms of dependencies arise between tasks rather than individuals or units. For example, a task may need specialized skills and/or resources created by another task. Actors in organizations face coordination problems arising from these dependencies that constrain how tasks can be performed. This conceptualization of dependencies makes it possible to understand the effects of task assignment and reassignments to different actors and units. The theory provides a theoretical framework for analyzing and managing coordination in complex processes that involve multiple tasks and resources. It suggests that coordination mechanisms rely on other organizational functions, such as decision making, communication, and development of shared understanding and collective sensemaking $[20, 22]$ . Further, the theory offers a typology of dependencies and associated coordination mechanisms to manage dependencies. It suggests that coordination problems exist (i.e., unmanaged dependencies between tasks and resources) in organizational activities. Employees, groups, and organizational units need to perform additional tasks (i.e., coordination mechanisms) to manage these coordination problems $[20, 22]$ . Collaboration technologies have been suggested as a key enabler of coordination mechanisms that help address these coordination problems and improve coordination and collaboration among individuals, tasks, and resources $[20, 22]$ .

We leverage the notions of dependencies and coordination mechanisms to conceptualize our key constructs and justify our hypotheses. Building on the theory's assertion on the role of IT in enabling and managing coordination mechanisms, we suggest that an ICTP will facilitate and enable coordination mechanisms such as goal decomposition (e.g., decomposing tasks and associated goals to manage and maintain dependencies, negotiating mutually agreeable results), resource allocation (e.g., identifying necessary and available resources, choosing resources, and marking resources in use), and synchronization (e.g., order or reorder tasks and manage flow of resources) in organizational activities related to NPD. We further suggest that these ICTP-enabled coordination mechanisms will provide theoretical underpinnings regarding how ICPT use will lead to important collaboration outcomes.

## Collaboration Technology Use and Outcomes

Easley et al. [30] noted that much prior research on collaboration technology use has been conducted in controlled experimental settings (e.g., [26, 27, 34]), limiting applicability for developing and testing rich conceptualizations of use in field settings (e.g., [6, 10]) where collaboration technologies are integrated platforms implemented at the enterprise level. Further, prior research has conceptualized and operationalized the use of collaboration technology using traditional lean IT use measures, such as frequency of use (e.g., [10]). Drawing on recent work on post-adoptive IT use [14, 48, 92], we develop a richer conceptualization of ICTP use by employees in organizations.

An ICTP facilitates collaboration by offering rich mechanisms for communication, collaboration, and coordination among employees involved in NPD. Such a platform helps organizations manage employee knowledge by facilitating the creation, retention, and transfer of knowledge among employees involved in key business processes $[3, 67]$ . Consistent with coordination theory, we suggest that a collaboration platform can facilitate coordination and reduce conflicts among individuals involved in NPD by providing visibility into resource and task assignments and handoffs $[20, 21, 22]$ . To receive the intended benefits, employees need to use various features and deeply embed usage into their work processes $[5]$ . It is important for employees to be able to use features in a manner consistent with the platform's general intent. Jasperson et al. $[48]$ discussed the concept of a feature-centric view of technology (see also, $[38]$ ). Noting that features are the building blocks of an IT, Jasperson et al. suggested that analysis at the feature level of use is critical for understanding the impact of IT. In a similar vein, Burton-Jones and Straub $[14]$ conceptualized featurelevel use as exploitive use of an IT and incorporated it as an important facet of their rich conceptualization of IT use.

Given that an ICTP offers multiple features to serve the purpose of collaboration, coordination, and communication, we propose that feature use is an appropriate conceptualization of ICTP use. Examples of features include e-mail, instant messaging, asynchronous discussion tools, scheduling, video conferencing, documents storing and sharing, and integration with other external systems $[10, 27]$ . An ICTP not only makes these features available to users but also helps create, retain, and transfer knowledge content that is generated when users use and/or access these features $[25, 67]$ . Building on recent works on postadoptive IT use $[14, 48, 92]$ , we conceptualize ICTP use as feature-level use (see Figure 1). We suggest that the use of different features forms the overall perception of platform use. Also, we suggest that different features of an ICTP are complementary, and they cumulatively combine to serve the overall purpose of using a platform. While it is possible that an employee may not use all the features, we operationalize this construct in such a way that each feature will have a different importance (i.e., empirically derived weight) based on employee preference and usage (details are given in the method section).

## IT-enabled Collaboration Capability

An ICTP and its features provide users the capability—the potential for an indicated use—to collaborate $[10]$ . Thus, in order to assess overall effectiveness, it is important to measure the quality of this capability. Prior research has offered different collaboration outcomes, for example, decision quality, communication performance, and team performance $[27, 30]$ . However, these outcomes were conceptualized primarily in the context of teams involved in specific tasks and often using a particular technology. In contrast, drawing on coordination theory (e.g., $[61]$ ), we suggest that a holistic perception of IT-enabled collaboration capability is an important outcome of employee use. That is, use will lead users to form a holistic perception of the ICTP's fit for purpose. Building on prior research $[8, 35, 63]$ , we define IT-enabled collaboration capability as a user's evaluations of how well a platform supports collaborative processes, helps collaborators overcome work boundaries, and facilitates alternate forms of interaction.

Our conceptualization of collaboration capability is multidimensional, consisting of three dimensions—collaboration process capability, boundary spanning capability, and interaction capability. Developed from the collaboration literature (e.g., $[12, 27, 61, 69]$ ), these dimensions capture the nature and challenges of collaboration, tying them directly to the enabling intent of an ICTP. While specific outcomes (e.g., decision quality) may not transfer to other concurrent or future collaborations, holistic outcomes and appraisals such as collaboration capability will likely lead to increasing collaborations $[12, 24]$ . Thus, we suggest that collaboration capability is an important individual outcome in an IT-enabled setting.

Collaboration process capability reflects how well an ICTP supports the various simultaneous functions collaborators engage in as they work toward goal accomplishment. These functions include actions directly related to tasks (e.g., knowledge and skill sharing, learning, building consensus to make decisions and/or solve problems) [27, 61, 69]; activities focused on evaluation or planning that guide work [63, 69, 90]; and interpersonal actions that build relationships between and among collaborators (cf., [63, 66, 73, 96]). Further, these functions may include coordination mechanisms to manage dependencies across interdependent tasks that employees and organizational units perform.

While teams provide a mechanism to bring together people with different but complementary skills and tie them to a single goal (such as product development), for the collaboration to work, those involved must often manage work across various boundaries $[2, 60]$ . Given the multiple constituencies involved in NPD, we focus on organizational boundaries. This cross-boundary work includes collaborations within an organization (across geographical or functional boundaries), and collaborations between organizations (possibly across geographical or functional boundaries, but almost certainly across organizational cultures). In essence, boundary spanning capability reflects how well a collaboration platform eases the flow of work by helping collaborators displace these boundaries and manage task and resource dependencies effectively and efficiently $[28, 35, 44, 64, 85, 93]$ .

Lastly, interaction capability addresses how well the ICTP enables both formal and informal communications $[10, 27]$ . Prior research has largely focused on the role of collaboration technology in facilitating formal, more structured and planned communications $[35, 65]$ . Yet, today's ICTP features support informal, less structured and spontaneous interactions. Such interactions facilitate performance by aiding in the coordination of work and helping collaborators learn from each other and learn about task and resource assignments and reassignments $[13, 83]$ . For example, shared workspaces provide up-to-the-minute knowledge of who is doing what and using what resources, including current work progress $[60]$ . Similarly, by letting collaborators know who and what resources are available and accessible, presence awareness aids in the coordination of work as the need arises $[31, 58]$ .

## Collaboration Satisfaction

User satisfaction is one of the most widely used surrogate measures for assessing the success of an IT system $[4]$ . It is an important outcome in DeLone and McLean's $[25]$ IS success model and studies that uses this model as a theoretical foundation. Prior research has suggested and found that IT systems typically fail due to psychological and organizational factors rather than technological issues $[5]$ . From an individual's point of view, user satisfaction is considered an important psychological appraisal of an IT system's success. In keeping with this view, we incorporated satisfaction as an outcome of collaboration technology use. We conceptualize it as a holistic assessment of an individual's appraisal of collaboration capabilities enabled by an ICTP used to accomplish his or her NPD-related tasks. We define it as the extent to which individuals develop favorable affective reactions to collaboration capabilities enabled by an integrated collaboration platform in the workplace. Given that the core purpose of a collaboration technology is to enhance individuals' collaborative behaviors and capabilities, we focus on individuals' satisfaction with the collaboration capabilities enabled by an ICTP, rather than focusing on satisfaction with the technology itself. We suggest that such an assessment provides an accurate representation of the success of a collaboration technology in an organization.

## New Product Development (NPD) Process

NPD is the process of bringing a new product to market, including idea generation and idea screening, concept development and testing, business analysis, prototype and market testing, technical implementation, and plans for commercialization and launch $[15, 75, 89]$ . The process integrates disparate input from R&D scientists, engineers, marketers, and often external business partners to jointly develop and launch new products $[18]$ . Broadly, NPD has three distinct phases: (a) discovery, (b) development, and (c) production (see $[11, 53, 88, 99]$ for reviews).

Discovery is a phase in which employees spend a significant amount of time exploring new ideas for a product. This phase is sometimes referred to as the fuzzy front end $[51, 52, 68]$ . Typical activities include: opportunity identification, opportunity analysis, idea genesis, idea selection, and concept and technology development $[52]$ . Building on Rothaermel and Deeds $[87]$ , we suggest that the activities in the discovery phase of an NPD process are theoretically consistent with the notion of exploration proposed in the organizational learning and adaptation literature $[40, 55, 62]$ . In this vein, exploration refers to the search for novel or innovative ways of doing something and is associated with phenomena, such as search, variation, risk taking, experimentation, play, flexibility, discovery, and innovation $[40, 62]$ . Exploration involves learning gained through concerted variation, planned execution, and play $[7, 40]$ . Employees who are involved in the discovery phase of NPD will be mostly engaged in activities that are aimed at learning, improving, and acquiring novel knowledge $[87]$ . In fact, it is this novelty gained through exploration that provides competitive advantages to organizations that depend heavily on NPD. In the context of the discovery phase of NPD in the biotechnology industry, which is also our context, Rothaermel and Deeds suggested, “During this discovery period, the venture pursues an exploratory search involving basic search, invention, risk taking, and building new capabilities with the goal of developing new knowledge and capabilities” $[87]$ , p. 203).

The second phase, development, involves activities such as designing and implementing prototypes (or pharmaceutical candidates) based on ideas from the discovery phase. This phase is critical because the decision to launch a product depends on the success of this phase. In this phase, employees tend to be engaged in both exploration and exploitation simultaneously—a concept known as ambidexterity $[40, 43, 88]$ . Unlike exploration, exploitation refers to the routine execution of knowledge and is associated with phenomena, such as refinement, choice, production, efficiency, selection, implementation, and execution $[62]$ . Exploitation also refers to learning gained via local search, experiential refinement, and selection and reuse of existing routines $[40]$ . It involves improvements in existing components and building on the existing technological trajectory. The notion of ambidexterity in the development process suggests that both exploitation and exploration are required for success $[82]$ . Given that product ideas, features, and specifications $[52, 53]$ are primarily generated in the discovery phase, employees in the development phase are engaged in refining these ideas and specifications (i.e., improvements in existing components and refinement based on local search) and developing product prototypes based on the technological trajectory suggested in the discovery phase. However, in doing so, it is expected that employees in the development phase may need to perform some tasks, such as planned experimentation, learning from concerted variation, shifting to a different technological trajectory, that are conceptually similar to exploration. Given this, the development phase requires an ambidextrous process orientation.

Finally, the third phase of NPD is production, which involves activities such as mass production, commercialization, and patent applications, if applicable $[88, 99]$ . In this phase, the dominant process orientation will be exploitation as employees attempt to use existing knowledge (e.g., design and implementation details) to manufacture and commercialize the new product at scale. Generally speaking, once scale-up has been tested and is planned, exploration of new ideas and experimentation are not required. In fact, variations, experimentations, and shifting to new trajectory of technologies and knowledge are typically discouraged during production to reduce product variations and quality concerns. Hence, employees involved in production are more likely to have a process orientation dominated by exploitation.

Given that multiple constituencies involved in different NPD stages and different interdependent tasks are performed with shared resources, collaboration is central to the process $[23, 42]$ . Importantly, an ICTP can enable and facilitate factors known to be critical to NPD success—collaboration, coordination, and communication, process and project management, information and knowledge management $[53]$ .

## Linking Collaboration Technology Use and Outcomes

Collaboration among employees and external stakeholders (e.g., partners and alliances) is a critical success factor for NPD $[6, 67]$ . As an information- and knowledge-intensive process $[56, 74, 89]$ , NPD is facilitated by effective use of IT functionalities $[80]$ . Building on coordination theory and prior research on NPD, we offer theoretical mechanisms to explain why use of an ICTP is expected to have a positive influence on perceptions of collaboration outcomes in NPD contexts.

First, ICTP use should facilitate effective communication, essential to the success of any kind of collaboration and coordination needed for a complex process, such as NPD, that has substantial inherent interdependencies among individuals, tasks, units, and resources. Coordination theory offers insights on how these interdependencies can be managed and maintained through various coordination mechanisms. Effective communication is an integral facet of these mechanisms $[20, 21]$ . A common ground of mutually shared concepts, assumptions, and expectations have to be established and enlarged during communication(s) $[21]$ . NPD employees must also pool and process their complementary knowledge via group-level information processing $[45, 70]$ . By enabling communication $[6, 76, 77]$ , use of an ICTP can help in the development of a common ground, the sharing of domains of expertise, and in the pooling of resources. Drawing on the knowledge management literature (e.g., $[76]$ ) and media richness theory $[23]$ , Banker et al. $[6]$ noted that for NPD, collaboration technology facilitates these collaborative processes through the following functional mechanisms: socialization (e.g., providing a platform for conducting online meetings and communication through online chat rooms and threaded discussion), externalization (e.g., providing a collaboration workspace where employees can share and review design specifications and offer insights), internalization (e.g., providing visual and other aids to help employees understand messages and documents), and combination (e.g., facilitating various channels of communication, faster information transfer, organization of messages and documents). We suggest that these collaboration mechanisms complement the coordination mechanisms offered by coordination theory and help employees manage complex dependencies across tasks and resources found in NPD processes $[7, 11]$ .

Second, consistent with the theoretical underpinning of coordination theory, use of an ICTP should facilitate and enable coordination in NPD. NPD is a process with multiple phases and stakeholders who have specific roles in the process. A high degree of coordination among employees who are involved in various phases of the process is critical for an NPD process. According to coordination theory $[21, 61]$ , coordination is the additional information processing performed when multiple, connected actors pursue goals that a single actor pursuing the same goals would not perform. Coordination theory suggests that collaboration technology can facilitate this additional information processing by supporting multiple authors working together on the same document, helping people display and manipulate information more effectively in face-to-face or online meetings, and helping people intelligently route and process electronic messages $[21, 61]$ . We suggest that the use of an ICTP will provide various rich mechanisms to improve coordination among employees.

Third, ICTP use should facilitate constructive interpersonal relationships $[57]$ , necessary for successful collaborative interactions, such as task and resource assignments and conflict resolution. As noted earlier, NPD employees hold complementary knowledge that often must be integrated in order to arrive at solutions. Employees involved in NPD are often from different business units and/or different groups within respective units—and, in some cases, from external partners. Further, many employees are not collocated. They may also have different backgrounds, competencies, professional orientations, and normative expectations. Due to the presence of such diverse groups involved in NPD, it is imperative that they develop a sense of cohesion (i.e., bonding or unity) to make the process successful. Prior research on cohesion in organizations suggests that employees develop a sense of commitment when they are cohesive to achieve a shared goal $[57]$ . An ICTP offers a mechanism to store communications and make them available for later retrieval. In many cases, communications are visible to others, thus creating a team memory and potentially a sense of commitment to the NPD project. Further, an ICTP can bring employees closer to each other through features such as instant messaging, video conferencing, collaboration workspace, and integration with other systems $[11, 70, 98]$ .

Collectively, when employees feel they can communicate effectively, coordinate work, and form interpersonal relationships, they are likely to develop a sense of and appreciation for the benefits received from the ICTP. These feelings will be manifested through their positive, holistic appraisal of collaboration capability. That is, platform features are deemed fit for purpose when they deliver underlying collaboration capabilities (i.e., collaborative process capability, boundary spanning capability, and interaction capability). When use is in line with the essential ways employees create value in the NPD process, employee perceptions will be more positive. We suggest that such favorable perceptions of a collaboration technology's utility are likely to be formed as individuals gain direct experience with the technology, work tasks, other collaborators and the organization context of use. In the context of the NPD process, the acquisition of these knowledge-building experiences, individuals will have increased ability to collaborate effectively in various situational contexts and will thus perceive the technology more positively.

## Hypothesis 1: Collaboration technology use will positively influence IT-enabled collaboration capability in the NPD process.

Collaboration satisfaction is an individual's holistic assessment of an ICTP. We suggest that such an assessment can only be possible when individuals gain experience with the platform by using various features. From a theoretical perspective, Au et al. [4] noted that individuals are more likely to be satisfied with an IT system if they perceive that it fulfills their needs. This is consistent with the consumer behavior literature that suggests customers are more likely to be satisfied with a product or a service if it meets their expectations [33]. We suggest that the extent to which individuals understand whether an ICTP fulfills their needs or meets their expectations can only be assessed when an individual actually uses the platform to accomplish work-related tasks [6, 10]. In particular, when individuals use different features of an ICTP, they are more likely to understand how well the platform serves their purposes and helps them improve performance [10, 14, 98]. In the IS literature, it has been suggested that IT use is an important determinant of satisfaction [4, 25]. In fact, Au et al. [4] noted that individuals need to exploit the capabilities of an IT before they are able to assess whether they are satisfied or not. Therefore, we expect that greater use will likely lead to a higher degree of collaboration satisfaction.

Hypothesis 2: Collaboration technology use will positively influence collaboration satisfaction in the NPD process.

While ICTP use will influence collaboration satisfaction, we suggest that collaboration capability will be a proximal determinant of collaboration satisfaction. If individuals find that the platform provides a high degree of benefits as related to collaboration capability (via collaborative process capability, boundary spanning capability, and interaction capability), it is likely that they will have a favorable affective reaction to the entire collaboration experience enabled by the technology. We suggest that positive perceptions of collaboration capability are an important precursor to the formation of attitudes toward the object that delivers the capability $[4, 8]$ . If individuals find that an ICTP offers value to them by enabling mechanisms, such as collaborative process capability, boundary spanning capability, and interaction capability, for high quality collaboration, coordination, and communication in NPD activities, they are likely to develop a favorable attitude (i.e., collaboration satisfaction) toward collaboration experience enabled by an ICTP. Given the complexity and interdependent nature of NPD activities, we suggest that employees engaged in NPD process are likely to develop favorable affective reactions toward their collaboration experience when they believe that the ICTP indeed enhances their collaboration capability in NPD activities.

Hypothesis 3: IT-enabled collaboration capability will positively influence collaboration satisfaction in an NPD process.

## Moderating Role of NPD Process Orientations

While we suggest that ICTP use will lead to favorable IT-enabled collaboration capability and have a positive influence on collaboration satisfaction, we expect that these relationships will be moderated by NPD process orientations—exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity. The exploration-exploitation framework suggests that the different orientations require different learning and activities $[40, 43, 55]$ . Employees are likely to have different collaboration needs relative to different NPD process orientations and activities. Therefore, we expect that employees will place different degrees of importance on ICTP use while forming perceptions of collaboration capability and satisfaction. For example, employees who have an exploration orientation may need to collaborate more (within and between business units) than employees who have an exploitation orientation because exploration requires idea generation, refinement, and improvement $[40, 47, 49]$ . However, employees who have an ambidexterity orientation will probably need the platform more than the exploration and exploitation groups because these employees try to pursue exploration and exploitation at the same time and they are under pressure to leverage all possible technologies and tools to achieve this goal $[72, 73]$ .

In a typical NPD process, we anticipate that the development phase requires activities that are ambidextrous in nature because it is the bridge between discovery and production. Prior research has suggested that effective communication is critical during the development phase of NPD $[6, 7, 53]$ . Employees who are responsible for the development phase deal with activities such as gathering and finalizing product requirements (based on the initial ideas generated in the discovery phase), conducting design iterations, verifying and testing product designs, and providing final design handoffs to production teams $[1, 6, 69]$ . Employees in this phase often deal with unstable and volatile product design information and must communicate critical parameters to each other. Further, they may face issues, such as missing and/or incomplete data, or incomplete product specifications developed in the discovery phase. They may be given multiple product specifications from the discovery phase and they are responsible for the identification and evaluation of alternative design $[24, 72, 87]$ . These tasks and associated interactions are often less structured. Therefore, with an ICTP and appropriate standards for the exchange of product data and associated information for employees involved in ambidextrous-oriented NPD activities, we expect platform use to have a stronger effect on collaboration capability and satisfaction.

Hypothesis 4: The effect of collaboration technology use on IT-enabled collaboration capability is moderated by process orientation such that the effect is stronger for employees working in ambidexterity-oriented NPD activities.

Hypothesis 5: The effect of collaboration technology use on collaboration satisfaction is moderated by process orientation such that the effect is stronger for employees working in ambidexterity-oriented NPD activities.

We further suggest that the effect of IT-enabled collaboration capability on collaboration satisfaction will be moderated by NPD process orientations. We argued earlier (H3) that if individuals find that an ICTP offers value via enabling mechanisms for high-quality collaboration, it is more likely they will develop a favorable attitude toward collaboration capabilities enabled by the platform. We now suggest that individuals with an ambidexterity orientation in the NPD process will be in an ideal position to develop a more accurate appraisal of the relationship between collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction (than individuals with either an exploration or exploitation orientation). Those involved in ambidextrous learning and adaptation are likely to use various platform features to enhance and solidify their own collaboration capabilities because of their need for simultaneously pursuing exploration and exploitation activities $[24, 72, 87]$ . Thus, they will develop an appreciation for collaboration capabilities that facilitate and enable their dual desire. Consistent with coordination theory, we suggest that these individuals are likely to use their collaboration capabilities to manage task and resource interdependencies and to avoid potential conflicts that will hinder their pursuit of exploration and exploitation activities $[24, 72, 87]$ . They are likely to be able to accurately identify collaboration capabilities (afforded by the ICTP) and place appropriate importance on these capabilities as they form an attitude (i.e., satisfaction) toward the platform.

Hypothesis 6: The effect of IT-enabled collaboration capability on collaboration satisfaction is moderated by process orientation such that the effect is stronger for employees working in ambidexterity-oriented NPD activities.

## Method

We conducted two field studies in a major Fortune 500 pharmaceutical company—“PHARMA”—to test our research model. PHARMA has more than 40,000 employees worldwide and is considered a major leader in pharmaceutical innovations. About 20 percent of its employees, located in 9 countries, are involved in the NPD (i.e., drug discovery and development) process. Given the hypercompetitive nature of the industry, NPD is considered a strategically important business process in pharmaceutical firms because the success and long-term viability of these firms hinges largely on their ability to discover and launch new drugs. NPD is not only important for strategic reasons, it is also a costly process (i.e., it typically takes more than a billion dollars to discover and develop a new drug). Further, the success of NPD in this industry depends on collaboration between employees, worldwide laboratories, and other external stakeholders, such as alliances, vendors, regulatory agencies, and customers.

During the early 2000s, senior executives of PHARMA felt the need to develop a robust and scalable collaboration technology platform to increase collaboration among employees and external stakeholders in its NPD process. In particular, they were concerned that it was difficult to share and/or access information internally among employees working in different business units due to redundant and/or incompatible IT systems and collaboration tools. The IT landscape at PHARMA was not conducive to internal and external collaborations. Due to data security and governance concerns, it was particularly challenging to set up collaboration with external stakeholders. However, given the nature and importance of the drug discovery and development process, it was imperative for PHARMA to develop a robust collaboration platform to ensure high quality of collaboration across the NPD process.

## Collaboration Technology: ATHENA

PHARMA started to develop its ICTP, referred to here as “ATHENA.” The platform was designed and developed by a high-profile IT engineering team (IET) created by the Executive Policy Committee, the highest body that makes PHARMA’s strategic decisions. IET designed ATHENA’s core architecture and defined it as an integrated process and technology framework to enable secure and efficient access to and exchange of information for all stakeholders. In keeping with the overall goal of supporting the NPD process at PHARMA, IET ensured that ATHENA was integrated into the NPD process so that it would help PHARMA achieve its strategic goals.

ATHENA was developed using a modular architecture. IET emphasized both the business process architecture and technical architecture of ATHENA to ensure a synergy between the NPD process and collaboration needs of employees and external partners involved in the process. Further, IET streamlined and standardized collaboration processes for ATHENA to reduce variations and increase efficiency in these processes. ATHENA had three core modules (i.e., identity management, content management, and collaboration workspace) that were built onto three design layers (i.e., presentation, workflow, and functionality). The presentation layer provides a web-based graphical user interface (GUI) for PHARMA employees and external partners. The workflow layer offers a mechanism for employees to view a list of tasks (and associated task dependencies) that they are assigned to perform with respect to their role in business processes. Finally, the functionality layers presents a set of features (e.g., e-mail, document sharing, online chat, scheduling) that employees can access to perform various collaborative tasks. These design layers were used in all three core modules of ATHENA.

The identity management component provided a single sign-on for employees and external partners, and ensured that intellectual assets were protected during the collaboration process. This component used a role-based approach so that employees and external partners got access to appropriate resources in ATHENA. The content management component was built based on a unified strategy of creating and sharing documents and other contents needed for the NPD process. This component was responsible for ensuring a stable, secure, and productive environment for content creation and sharing. Finally, the collaboration workspace component offered a set of tools and applications for PHARMA employees and external collaborators to access shared data, interact, and coordinate work activities.

ATHENA offered the following key features for collaboration purposes: (a) e-mail (i.e., employees were able access their corporate e-mail in addition to using desktop based e-mail clients), (b) scheduling (i.e., employees were able to access their personal calendar as well as shared calendars of team members and managers), (c) instant messaging (e.g., employees were able to send instant messages, enabling synchronous and asynchronous interactions), (d) awareness feature (i.e., in addition to making themselves available through ATHENA, employees were able to see when someone was online and having one-click access to interaction), (e) conferencing (i.e., employees were able to initiate and make audio and video calls for conferencing), (f) documenting (i.e., store and share documents with others), and (g) external sharing (i.e., sharing documents with external partners). These features have been suggested in prior research as standard features in integrated collaboration platforms (see $[6, 10, 98]$ ).

The core components of ATHENA were developed as modules that could be integrated with other applications at PHARMA. These modules were developed as services with plug-and-play capabilities to be discovered and accessed by other applications. Users could customize different functionalities of these modules and deploy different tools within ATHENA for their collaboration needs. This ensured a high degree of interaction between consumers and providers of content as they collaboratively decide and deploy the tools needed for collaboration.

## Procedure and Participants

Our first study, Study 1, was conducted approximately six months after the deployment of ATHENA. This gave enough time for employees to routinize the use of ATHENA in their day-to-day work processes. A request with a link to a web-based survey was sent to ATHENA users involved in PHARMA's NPD process, including all discovery, development, and manufacturing functions. A total of 5,350 employees were targeted to participate in the survey. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. We received a total of 2,103 initial responses (39 percent response rate). We conducted missing data analysis and removed responses that were incomplete. Our effective sample size was 1,749 (33 percent response rate). Of these participants, 448 represented the discovery functions (26 percent), 976 represented the manufacturing functions (55 percent), and 325 represented development functions (19 percent). Employees from different organizational levels participated in the survey (see Table 2): 769 (44 percent) were operational employees (e.g., operators and support services), 732 (42 percent) were knowledge workers (e.g., scientists, analysts, engineers, and technical specialists), and 248 (14 percent) were managerial employees (e.g., supervisors, group and/or team leaders, and managers). About 60 percent of the participants worked for 10 years or less at PHARMA.

Study 2 was conducted approximately one year after Study 1. The purpose of Study 2 was to ensure that findings from Study 1 were robust and generalizable. In addition, PHARMA top management was interested in understanding whether there were any major changes in how employees used and perceived ATHENA over time. In Study 2, we followed the same data collection procedure as in Study 1. A total of 4,905 employees were invited to participate. We received a total of 1,765 initial responses (36 percent response rate). After conducting the missing data analysis, our effective sample size was 1,454 (30 percent response rate). Of these participants, 338 were from the discovery functions (23 percent), 796 were from the manufacturing functions (55 percent), and 320 represented the development functions (22 percent). As in Study 1, employees from different organizational levels participated in the survey (see Table 2): 611 (42 percent) were operational employees (e.g., operators and support services), 623 (43 percent) were knowledge workers (e.g., scientists, analysts, engineers, and technical specialists), and 220 (15 percent) were managerial employees (e.g., supervisors, group and/or team leaders, and managers). We compared the demographic variables (e.g., organizational tenure and membership) of early and late respondents in both studies to test for nonresponse bias and did not find any significant differences. Further, PHARMA senior managers confirmed that our sample accurately represents the profile (e.g., job description, role in the organization, organizational tenure) of average ATHENA users.

## Measures

Table 3 presents the measures that we used. We measured collaboration technology use using seven items that represent the core features of ATHENA (e.g., “I use ATHENA for my e-mail”). Given that platform use was conceptualized as a formative construct, these items essentially form an overall assessment of the extent of ATHENA use by PHARMA employees. These items were created in consultation with the members of IET who assessed the face and content validities and confirmed that these items indeed captured the core use cases of ATHENA from typical users’ perspective. We used a five-point Likert-type scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree) to assess employees’ use of different features for collaboration purposes. A similar approach was taken by Burton-Jones and Straub [14] who operationalized feature-level use of Microsoft Excel. Building on recent works on postadoptive IT use [14, 48, 92], ICTP use is conceptualized as a first-order formative construct composed of different dimensions of feature use. A formative model is appropriate because the use of different features facilitates or forms overall use. Also, these features are complementary to each other, and they cumulatively combine to serve the overall purpose of ICTP use. Finally, because a change in any feature use by an employee does not necessarily cause an equal change in the other feature use by the same employee, we suggest that a reflective model is unlikely [80]. Therefore, a formative model was used to operationalize collaboration technology use.

IT-enabled collaboration capability was operationalized as a formative construct with three dimensions (i.e., collaboration process capability, interaction capability, and boundary spanning capability). Each dimension was operationalized using formative indicators capturing different aspects and challenges of IT-enabled collaboration. The indicators were identified in two ways. First, we interviewed the IET members to unearth different aspects of ATHENA and the design team's views about the expected benefits of using ATHENA. These interviews were semistructured such that we started with a set of questions followed by probing questions based on the responses. Example questions include: How might you describe key aspects of NPD collaboration? What are some challenges that are not being currently met with existing technologies? Where and how can ATHENA's features be used to support different aspects of collaboration and/or overcome these challenges? Based on these interviews, we developed a set of items reflecting these benefits. Second, we interviewed 22 employees and conducted 3 focus groups (8–10 employees/group) to find out various ways employees thought ATHENA might improve their collaboration efforts. The dimensions of collaboration process capability were: activities coordination, information exchange, problem solving, socialization, and real-time communication. The dimensions of interaction capability were: informal interaction, formal interaction, and presence awareness. The dimensions of boundary spanning capability were: local collaboration, remote collaboration, and external collaboration.

We operationalized collaboration capability as a second-order formative construct comprised of the three first-order factors (i.e., collaboration process capability, interaction capability, and boundary spanning capability). We considered a formative model appropriate because these three first-order constructs facilitate or form the second-order collaboration capability. These three first-order constructs are complementary to each other, and they cumulatively combine to serve the overall purpose of collaboration. Also, from a theoretical point of view a change in any of these constructs does not necessarily cause an equal change in the other construct $[80]$ . Therefore, a reflective model was not appropriate in this context. Hence, we used a formative second-order model (see Figure 1).

In addition to the formative construct, collaboration capability was also assessed using two reflective indicators. The correlation between collaboration capability measured as a reflective construct and collaboration capability measured as a second-order formative construct was .88 and .86 in Studies 1 and 2, respectively, indicating that the second-order formative construct was indeed a representation of a latent collaboration capability construct. These correlations were consistent with Pavlou and El Sawy [79] who used a similar approach to validate a formative model. Collaboration satisfaction was assessed using two reflective indicators (including, e.g., “I am very satisfied with the collaboration capabilities provided by ATHENA”).

NPD process orientations were operationalized using Giovannetti and Morrison's $[36, pp. 46–47]$ discussion of NPD process in biotechnology organizations and suggestions from the senior executives in PHARMA. Giovannetti and Morrison $[36]$ suggested that the drug discovery and development process can be broken into distinct orientations related to discovery, development, and manufacturing (or production). Rothaermel and Deeds $[87]$ suggested that exploration is more likely to be pursued in the discovery phase and exploitation is more likely to be focused in the development phase of the NPD process in a pharmaceutical firm. Although they did not include ambidexterity in their study, in light of recent thoughts in the organizational adaptation literature $[40, 47, 72]$ , we suggest that exploitation is more likely to be prevalent in the production stage of NPD and ambidexterity is the dominant process orientation in the development phase of NPD in a pharmaceutical firm.

Our discussion with the senior executives of PHARMA confirmed this view that employees working in the discovery functions were more involved in activities similar to exploration (e.g., idea generation and identification of drug candidates). The senior executives also indicated that the development functions were primarily responsible for activities such as drug candidate development, idea validation, and preclinical testing. These activities tend to be associate with a both an exploration and exploitation process orientation simultaneously, that is, ambidexterity. Finally, the PHARMA senior executives suggested that the production-related activities would be associated with an exploitation orientation because these employees were primarily responsible for exploiting prior manufacturing knowledge as well as the knowledge transferred from development as they undertake production-related activities.

Although we did not directly measure process orientations from PHARMA employees, we suggest that our approach of operationalizing these constructs based on functional affiliation is operationally similar to prior research that operationalized national culture dimensions (e.g., power distance, uncertainty avoidance) at the aggregate level without directly measuring them from individuals (e.g., [91]). While it is possible that individuals vary in their cultural values, national cultural data present information on the aggregated level of beliefs held by members of a society. Similarly, prior research on collective identity suggests that individuals within a defined professional/group boundary share similar values, identifying themselves as members of the defined profession/group (e.g., [9]). We suggest that employees working in specialized functional units are likely to have a high degree of belongingness and exhibit behaviors that follow the norms and expectations of the functional area. We indeed collected data from employees who had professional roles in their respective functional areas (see Table 2).

Finally, our approach of incorporating suggestions from the senior executives at PHARMA is consistent with the key informant-based data collection in organizational research in which a senior member of an organization answers questions about organizational, project, and business unit characteristics (e.g., $[37, 81]$ ). While it is possible that not all units or employees share the same view on these characteristics, we expect that the score provided by a senior member will represent at least an average level of these characteristics shared by respective employees. Given that process orientations were operationalized using senior executives' input, we expect that these measures are likely to represent these orientations fairly accurately in PHARMA.

## Results

We used PLS (partial least squares), a component-based structural equation modeling (SEM) technique, to test our measurement and structural model. PLS has been widely used in IS and marketing literatures to test models that have both formative and reflective constructs such as ours. We used SmartPLS version 3.2.4 [84]. We employed a bootstrapping method (5,000 times) that used randomly selected subsamples to test significance levels of the various PLS models reported in the study.

## Measurement Models

Before conducting any substantive analysis, we first assessed the validity of our measures. We followed the guidelines specified in recent research commentaries on formative constructs (e.g., [16, 80]). It is important to note that the traditional approaches for assessing of reliability (e.g., Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability) and validity (e.g., convergent and discriminant validity) are not applicable to formative measures. Therefore, following recent guidelines, we examined item weights and loadings to assess the relative and absolute importance of each formative measure. We also assessed variance inflation factors (VIFs) to ensure that multicollinearity was not a major issue. VIFs were in the range of 2.1 to 3.7 suggesting that multicollinearity was not a concern in the data. All formative measures had significant weights of various magnitudes indicating their relative importance (see Appendix B). PLS results (not reported in the study for brevity) also indicated significant loadings for all formative measures.

Given that collaboration satisfaction was measured using reflective items, we assessed composite reliability to ensure that the items were reliable (.94 and .95 for Studies 1 and 2, respectively). Following Fornell and Larcker [32], we assessed convergent and discriminant validities of collaboration satisfaction in two ways: (a) item loadings were greater than .707 in both studies and (b) the square root of the average variance extracted ([AVE] .94 in both studies) was greater than collaboration satisfaction's correlations with other constructs in our model (see Table 4). Common method bias is a potential drawback of self-reported data that can inflate the correlations between variables. Given that we collected data using cross-section surveys in both studies, it is possible that common method bias is a potential threat. Following the guidelines from prior research (e.g., [58]), we employed a set of procedural and statistical remedies and found that common method bias was not a serious concern (see Appendix C for details). ## Structural Model Results

Once the adequacy of the measurement models was established in both studies, we proceeded with testing the structural models to examine our hypotheses. We theorized that the use of an ICTP in NPD will lead to positive IT-enabled collaboration capability (H1) and collaboration satisfaction (H2). Results shown in Figure 2 indicate that platform use had significant positive effects on collaboration capability ( $\beta = .51$ , p < .001 and $\beta = .56$ , p < .001 in Studies 1 and 2, respectively) and collaboration satisfaction ( $\beta = .19$ , p < .01 and $\beta = .21$ , p < .01 in Studies 1 and 2, respectively), thus supporting H1 and H2 in both studies. We also theorized that collaboration capability will have a positively influence on collaboration satisfaction (H3). In the presence of a large sample size, p-values tend to reach close to 0. Therefore, it is important to interpret the effect size (e.g., regression coefficients or path coefficients) in addition to focusing on the significance level and $R^{2}$ . For example, the path coefficient of .51 between collaboration technology use and IT-enabled collaboration quality (see Table A1 in online Appendix A for more details) can be interpreted in the following way: if all other variables (e.g., organizational tenure, collocation, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness) remain constant or fixed, then for each change of 1 unit in use, collaboration capability changes .51 unit. In other words, if collaboration technology use increases by 1 unit, collaboration capability will increase by .51 unit (if all other variables are fixed). Similar interpretations can be made for the other relationships in our research model. Further, we calculated Cohen $f^{2}$ (given a value of $R^{2}$ ) to identify the magnitude of the effects [19]. $^{1}$ We found that the range of Cohen $f^{2}$ was between .72 and 1.56, suggesting that we indeed had a large effect size across different analytical models [19]. Overall, our model explained between .44 and .51 percent of the variance in collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction in Studies 1 and 2.

To test the moderating effects of NPD process orientation, we conducted a multi-group analysis for differences across the three process orientations following exemplars from prior research $[50, 94]$ . In particular, we followed the procedure described by Chin et al. $[17]$ to compare the path coefficients across different process orientation groups using t-statistics. Before we calculated the t-statistics, we first checked the measurement models in both studies and found measurement invariance across both studies (e.g., similar factor loadings and factor structures). The specific formula to calculate the t-statistics is shown below. If the t-statistics is significant, it will indicate that the path coefficients between an independent and a dependent variable are significantly different from each other, suggesting that groups placed different levels of importance on the independent variable (see Online Appendix A for the detailed results).

$$
\begin{array}{c} \mathrm {S_ {pooled}} = \operatorname{sqrt} \left\{\left[ (\mathrm{N} _ {1} - 1) ^ {2} / \mathrm{N} _ {1} + \mathrm{N} _ {2} - 2\right) \right] \times \mathrm{SE} _ {1} ^ {2} + \left[ (\mathrm{N} _ {2} - 1) ^ {2} / (\mathrm{N} _ {1} + \mathrm{N} _ {2} - 2) \right] \times \mathrm{SE} _ {2} ^ {2} \Bigg \} \\ t = \left(\mathrm{PC} _ {1} - \mathrm{PC} _ {2}\right) / \left[ \mathrm{S} _ {\text { pooled }} \times \operatorname{sqrt} (1 / \mathrm{N} _ {1} + 1 / \mathrm{N} _ {2}) \right], \end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {where} \\ \mathrm {S _ {pooled}} & = \text {pooled estimator for the variance} \\ \mathrm{t} & = t \text {-statistic with N} _ {1} + \mathrm{N} _ {2} - 2 \text {degrees of freedom} \\ \mathrm {N _ {i}} & = \text {sample size of data set for group i} \\ \mathrm {SE_ {i}} & = \text {standard error of path in structural model for group i} \\ \mathrm {PC_ {i}} & = \text {path coefficient in structural model of group i} \end{array}
$$

We hypothesized that the effect of collaboration technology use on IT-enabled collaboration capability (H4) and collaboration satisfaction (H5) would be stronger for employees engaged in ambidextrous activities. Results shown in Figure 2 (and Table A2 in online Appendix A) support this conjecture (H4) in both studies ( $\beta = .51, p < .001$ and $\beta = .49, p < .001$ in Studies 1 and 2, respectively). As shown in Figure 2, the effect of platform use on collaboration satisfaction was not significantly stronger for any particular group. Thus, H5 was not supported (see the discussion section). We also hypothesized that the effect of IT-enabled collaboration capability on collaboration satisfaction will be stronger for employees engaged in ambidextrous NPD activities (H6). Figure 2 shows that the effect of collaboration capability on collaboration satisfaction was significantly stronger for the ambidextrous group (i.e., development phase employees) than the exploitation group in both studies, but not greater than the exploration group in study 1 ( $\beta = .53, p < .001$ and $\beta = .43, p < .001$ in Studies 1 and 2, respectively), thus partially supporting H6 (see Table A3 in the online Appendix A). Our model explained between .44 and .61 percent of the variance in IT-enabled collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction in Studies 1 and 2 for different NPD process orientation groups.

## Discussion

Our primary goal was to examine the role of an ICTP in the strategically important business process of NPD. We developed a model that theorized that employee use of a collaboration technology would have a positive influence on two collaboration outcomes—collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction. We also posited that these effects would be moderated by NPD process orientations (i.e., exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity) such that the effects would be stronger for employees involved in ambidextrous activities. We conducted two field studies in a major pharmaceutical company and found support for our model in both studies. Our findings have important implications for theory and practice.

## Theoretical Contributions

This work contributes to three streams of research. First, it contributes to the product development literature by exploring the role of ICTP use in different phases of the NPD process. While collaboration has been suggested to be a critical success factor in NPD (e.g., [6, 67, 74, 75, 77]), there has been limited research that examined how the need for collaboration may vary in different process orientations [74]. We theorized that while collaboration is important for all phases of NPD, it is most critical for employees who are involved in activities that are ambidextrous in nature, that is, activities that require a simultaneous pursuit of exploration and exploitation. We posited that these employees need to use an ICTP extensively to maintain collaboration and share knowledge with various stakeholders in order to accomplish their tasks. They were able to extract most benefits and satisfaction from their use of a feature-rich ICTP because of the unique nature of their NPD activities. Thus, our work highlights the importance of ICTP use in the context of product development [53] that involves different organizational adaptation and learning activities, such as exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity [40, 48, 55]. This is indeed an important contribution because our model not only highlights the role of collaboration technologies in NPD but also depicts differential impacts on employees based on different process orientations in NPD.

Second, this work contributes to the collaboration literature in general and the IT-enabled collaboration literature in particular by offering insights on different facets of ICTP use and collaboration capability, and theoretically linking these two important aspects of collaboration. While prior research on IT-enabled collaboration has examined ICTP use and offered insights on various determinants of such use (e.g., $[10, 30]$ ), there has been limited understanding of the outcomes of such use. Further, while prior research has acknowledged that an ICTP has many integrated features $[10, 27]$ , it largely overlooked the extent to which individuals use these features. We extend prior research by conceptualizing various ways individuals use a collaboration platform. Further, we identified three dimensions of collaboration capability (collaborative process capability, boundary spanning capability, and interaction capability) from individual users' perspectives to capture the benefits that individuals expect from using collaboration platforms.

Finally, we contribute to the emerging body of research on IT use in organizations. Much prior research has focused on lean measures of IT use such as duration, frequency, and intensity of use $[95]$ . There have been increasing calls for going beyond these lean measures and developing rich conceptualizations and operationalizations of IT use $[14, 48]$ . We address these calls by developing a rich measure of ICTP use in the context of NPD. We built on prior research that suggested the importance of considering the extent to which individuals use different features of an IT system and we developed a rich measure of use by incorporating various ways that employees use such a platform. We demonstrated that the use of these different features form a higher-order construct. We also contribute to IT use literature by examining the impacts of ICTP use on two outcomes—collaboration capability and collaboration satisfaction. Although IT use has been studied extensively in the IS literature, there has been limited research that examined the impacts of use. We extend prior research through our empirical finding that ICTP use provides sufficient benefits to employees that they perceived a higher quality of collaboration.

Although ICTP use also had a positive influence on collaboration satisfaction in both studies, we did not find support for the differential impacts of use on collaboration satisfaction across different process orientations. We suggest that employees involved in different phases of the NPD process will identify and use a set of platform features over time to accomplish their tasks. Once these features are identified, used, and routinized in their work processes, there will not be differential impacts of collaboration technology use on collaboration satisfaction because employees involved in different phases are likely to place equal (albeit significant) importance on their use while forming their reactions. In other words, although collaboration technology use will still have a significant influence on collaboration satisfaction, it will have equal impacts across different phases of the NPD process over time.

## Limitations and Future Work

Our findings should be interpreted in light of some limitations. First, our data collection was cross-sectional. Even though we collected data from the employees of the same organization twice (a year apart), we were not able to match the responses due to company policy. We acknowledge this as a limitation of our study. The persistence of our results over the course of two studies conducted a year apart suggests that our model and findings are relatively robust. Regardless, there is a need to conduct longitudinal studies to garner further insights on the role of collaboration technologies in strategic business processes and understand changes in use over time. A longitudinal data collection would help us develop a better understanding of how employee use of and satisfaction with a collaboration platform evolves over time. Another fruitful avenue of research would be to conduct a multilevel study incorporating groups within strategic business processes to understand the influence of group level constructs on individual-level outcomes. Future research can leverage state-of-the-art data analysis techniques to study change over time (e.g., latent growth modeling $[5]$ ) and multilevel models (e.g., hierarchical linear modeling).

Second, we operationalized process orientations (i.e., exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity) using prior research on NPD processes and a key informant approach instead of directly measuring them from our participants. It is possible that employees' process orientations were not accurately represented through their functional affiliations. We provided detailed explanation regarding the appropriateness of our measurement approach in the methodology section. Nonetheless, future research should measure these process orientations from employees and test whether individuals with different process orientation propensity have different experience and outcomes from an ICTP regardless of their functional affiliations. Further, we did not find major differences between exploration and exploitation groups with respect to use and outcomes of ICTP. This could be due to the organizational context at PHARMA and our measurement approach. Future research should examine the difference in ICTP use and outcomes between employees who are engaged in exploration and exploitation tasks in NPD. For instance, exploratory activities may require certain collaboration capabilities (e.g., interaction and boundary spanning capabilities), whereas exploitation activities may require different capabilities (e.g., collaboration process capability).

Third, we only examined two outcomes of collaboration technology use. There are potentially other important outcomes such as job performance. Further, there are potentially important determinants of collaboration platform use, such as individual characteristics (e.g., personality traits), technology characteristics (e.g., media capabilities $[27]$ ), and situational factors (e.g., task types and project characteristics), that could be incorporated in our model to develop a holistic understanding. We believe that this will be a fruitful avenue of future research. Fourth, we collected data from a pharmaceutical organization for which NPD is a critical driver and a central business process. While product and service development is critical for many organizations, it is possible that NPD is not as pivotal in other organizations. Therefore, we suggest that future research should include other types of organizations from different sectors (e.g., retail, health care, insurance, financial management) to provide additional insights on how employees use collaboration technologies for strategically important business processes. Finally, we examined a specific type of collaboration technology. We acknowledge that there are potentially many other types of collaboration technologies that organizations may consider implementing in the context of strategic business processes, including enterprise social media platforms and mobile technology-based collaboration platforms. Future research is needed to examine the effects of other collaboration technologies on strategic business processes, and outcomes related to these processes.

## Practical Implications

Our model and findings have several key practical implications. First, we suggest that managers in IT organizations should proactively map IT innovations to the facilitation of strategic business processes. For one such process (i.e., NPD) and technology (i.e., ICTP), our findings indicate a positive influence on individual outcomes. There has been much debate recently about the role of IT in organizations. Some argue that IT is merely an infrastructure that offers little or no strategic benefits. Others argue that IT indeed has operational and strategic implications. Moreover, due to the recent economic crisis and global recession, IT managers find it difficult to justify the value of IT in organizations and top management is increasingly more doubtful about the value of IT in organizations. We believe that when directly connected to strategic business processes, IT innovations are important mechanisms through which IT investment delivers high value through enablement of critical organizational outcomes.

Second, we suggest that IT managers should actively consider embracing a service-oriented approach in designing, developing, and delivering IT systems. In most organizations, IT is a support area that helps the organization perform critical business processes. However, IT systems are primarily designed following the principle of a “tangible” product. There is typically no opportunity for employees to cocreate the value from such a product. A service-oriented approach will offer mutual benefits for IT organizations and other business units by changing the nature of how IT systems should be designed, developed, and deployed. The collaboration technology platform that we studied is an example of an IT system that helps employees cocreate value in the context of NPD through communications and knowledge exchanges. Overall, we believe that such a system will facilitate a better relationship and alignment between IT and business processes with technology “fit for purpose.”

Finally, we suggest that managers responsible for NPD and other strategic business processes in organizations should consider how they can leverage IT innovations to improve employee collaboration and performance in these processes. Our findings clearly show that the use of a collaboration technology has a positive influence on collaboration capability and satisfaction. Although we did not incorporate a direct outcome related to employee performance, we expect that an increasing level of collaboration capability and satisfaction will have a positive influence on employee performance. Therefore, process owners in organizations should actively think about how to encourage the development and use of IT innovations to support critical business processes. With respect to the specific innovation that we examined, we suggest that it is important to adapt the use of the IT innovations to the specific requirements of the process. Given the complexity of many business processes, our results suggest the full spectrum of the process needs to be considered and supported. For example, in our study employees involved in the more exploration-oriented aspects of the NPD process required capabilities that helped them exchange information quickly. In contrast, employees involved in the more exploitation-oriented aspects required features such as scheduling and documenting more often. Finally, employees involved in the more ambidextrous-oriented aspects of NPD needed to use a majority of platform features.

## Conclusion

We examined the role of a collaboration technology in the context of a strategic business process. Building on organizational adaptation and learning literature, we theorized that the relationship between collaboration technology use on outcomes—collaboration capability and satisfaction—will be moderated by NPD process orientations, that is, exploration, exploitation, and ambidexterity. Our findings offer insights on the value of a collaboration platform in strategic business processes and provide a theoretical link between such a technology and strategic business processes, thus addressing the call for research linking IT and NPD. We also contribute to the literatures on collaboration technology use by offering a rich conceptualization and operationalization of use in the context of NPD. As organizations continue to invest in IT, our work offers additional avenues for IT managers to show the value of IT innovations for organizational business processes and justify IT investment decisions.
