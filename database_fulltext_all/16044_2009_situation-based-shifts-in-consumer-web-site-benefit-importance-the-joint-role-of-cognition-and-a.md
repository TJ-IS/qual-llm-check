## A B S T R A C T

We considered the reasons why customers who plan to use a web site require different benefits in different situations and investigated two ways by which the differences can occur. Apparently, relative benefit importance shifts due to changes in cognitive fit between each web site benefit and customers’ situations, and, due to changes in their anticipated affective states in these situations customers’ benefit focus also changes. Furthermore, the number of benefits that customers rated as important differed, depending on their anticipated affective state. Jointly the findings provided insight into how and why consumer benefit importance varied by situation.

## 1. Introduction

Use of the Internet allows firms to provide customers with anytime, anywhere access to their services. Such interactions place new demands on web site designers, who must meet consumer requirements in many different situations. Therefore, we decided to investigate the process by which different benefits become important to e-commerce consumers.

Different situations activate different memory cues that depend on a person’s knowledge and experience. Typically, such variations are explained as a cognitive selection process where, depending on the particular usage situation, some benefits are more accessible in memory than others. These differences might exist, for example, because some benefits had previously been important in deciding among alternatives, or because some carry stronger associations with past experience [7,28]. This process implies that consumers cognitively represent benefit information selectively [30] and that situations help consumers define the benefits they require from the use of a web site [33].

This cognitive view has been challenged, however, by researchers who argue for the importance of consumers’ affective states in explaining situational variation in behavior [19]. For example, the state at the time of buying may affect a person’s benefit focus when engaging in a hedonic versus utilitarian shopping experience [1].

For our research, we integrated these approaches and proposed that consumers planning to use a web site in different situations expected different benefits, both due to changes in cognitive fit and anticipated affective state. In particular, we investigated whether consumers’ anticipated affective states helped explain observed variations in web site benefit importance in different situations. We conceptualized a consumer’s anticipated affective state as his or her expectation of an affective response at the time of using the web site [17]. We empirically tested this dual structure in the context of consumer plans to use recipe web sites.

## 2. The impact of situation on consumer web site benefit importance

In our view of how situation may affect the importance of web site benefits, we build on the substitution-in-use approach, which suggests that the benefits consumers require from a product or service vary according to usage situation. Srivastava [29] argued that consumers rely on prior learning and experiences when they face a situation in which they need to retrieve information, because they have limited information-processing capabilities, including memory restrictions [4,5]. We therefore expected that consumers considered only the most relevant subset of benefits (accessed from stored knowledge because they were triggered by the situation). Thus, we anticipated a direct cognitive route from situation to benefits that became salient in the consumer’s mind through a memory-based process.

Typical benefits that consumers look for in firms’ web sites include perceived relevance, reliability, and usefulness, the level of user control that the web site allows, and its entertainment value [16,23,26]. When confronted with a certain situation, consumers may directly obtain the most important benefits from this set; for example, an Internet user anticipating that he or she will face a time constraint when buying a product online might recall a prior buying experience with time constraints and think of benefits— such as ease of use and speed—that are important in this situation. In contrast, a person who anticipates leisurely surfing web sites to browse for possible products probably has a different set of benefits (e.g., entertainment value and information scope).

We extended this cognitive approach. Knowing that the influence of situation-type stimuli on consumers’ affective states has been found important (e.g., in consumers’ emotional responses to various experiences [34], the impact of retail shopping environments on consumers’ emotional states [6,8,9,12], and consumers’ emotional responses to advertising [35]), we proposed that when a consumer anticipates a certain usage situation, he or she also anticipates an affective response to the situation, and this is reflected in the person’s mental representation of the situation (see Fig. 1). Therefore, we decomposed the (direct) cognitive activation of salient benefits in the consumer’s mind from the (indirect) affect-based activation of benefits that may also occur. We hypothesized:

H1. The relative importance of different web site benefits shift in different situations due to (a) direct cognitive changes in anticipated benefit importance, and (b) changes in consumers’ associated anticipated affective state that also change anticipated benefit importance.

Thus, we connected the two steps of usage situation’s influence on affective state, and affective state on decision making, and argued that they jointly determine the impact of the anticipated situation on benefit importance. In our analysis, we focused particularly on the individuals’ anticipated pleasure in a given use situation. Pleasure represents one of the most important consumer emotional states for explaining affective responses to a wide variety of stimuli [24] and expresses feelings such as happiness, contentment, and satisfaction.

Positive affect amplifies consumers’ decision making and encourages approach behavior [13,25]. Isen reported that positive affect increases consumers’ problem solving capabilities and allows consumers to integrate more information into their decision making. Furthermore, positive affect facilitates creativity, cognitive flexibility, innovative responding, and openness to information. Consumers who experience positive affect also seek more variety in their product choices and generally enhance their elaboration and thinking [15]. In general, people in a positive affective state are more likely to find a task to be enjoyable and consequently they are stimulated to process more information [20].

Based on these considerations we expected that consumers who anticipated a greater positive affect to occur in a certain usage situation were likely to have experienced positive affect when encountering the situation in the past. They were therefore also likely to have been more open and willing to process information then. Thus, we expected that more benefits were important to them in that situation. In particular, we hypothesized that anticipated positive affect for a given situation lead consumers to increase the number of benefits they found to be important. Thus:

H2. The number of web site benefits that consumers find important increases for situations with greater anticipated positive affect.

We also investigated a further link between consumers’ anticipated positive affective state and their decision making. Individuals who are in a positive affective state (i.e., feel happy) have been found to wish to maintain this state and are less willing to take risks; they therefore tend to behave conservatively (focusing on any loss) to maintain their happiness [14].

We proposed that a comparable process takes place in situation-induced affect on benefit importance. Specifically, we expected that consumers who had experienced positive affect in a given situation had learned the relevance of maintaining this positive state by focusing on pleasure-oriented benefits. This cognitive connection among the positive usage situation, affective state, and benefit focus also should be reflected in consumers’ mental representation of a usage situation when they are made to anticipate it. Therefore, we hypothesized:

H3. The relative importance of pleasure-oriented web site benefits increases for situations with greater anticipated positive affect.

## 3. Research method and data

We tested the hypotheses empirically by asking respondents to imagine being in a situation in which they planned to use the Internet to look for a recipe. This type of scenario-based approach has been applied in many other research projects [3] and the ecological validity of consumers’ cognitive and affective processes to scenarios has been demonstrated [2]. The application of recipe web sites, is a relatively common application on the Internet (e.g., http://www.allrecipes.com, http://www.epicurious.com) and it shows the importance of anytime, anywhere access for consumer–firm relationships and reveals how firms could meet varying needs of individual consumers. For example, a consumer searching for a recipe when wanting to prepare a quick meal is likely to look for very different benefits than a consumer looking for a recipe when planning to prepare a special meal for a friend. Possible consumer concerns about health-related factors further emphasize the diversity of this application.

## 3.1. Participants and experimental design

Our survey participants were recruited from volunteer pools at two universities; we announced the study by sending an e-mail to potential participants, who were told that they would be eligible to win a gift voucher for taking part using a link to the study website. The study was run online; a total of 281 people responded within 12 days. Responses from 5 participants were eliminated from analysis because they contained outliers, and another 7 were eliminated because they indicated that they cooked less than once a week, making them unlikely to respond meaningfully to our study. Thus, we possessed a sample of 269 subjects, 21.9% of whom were men; they had a mean age of 24.3 years.

We adopted a 2 - 2 between-subjects experimental design to vary usage situation and assigned respondents randomly to one of four conditions that represented different hypothetical situations in which consumers consider using different web sites to find a suitable recipe for cooking a dinner (see Appendix A). We focused on time and social factors as the two key situational characteristics for manipulation. To create two conditions, we varied the time constraints that respondents had been told that they faced in the hypothetical usage situation. For the strong time constraint condition, respondents were told they would have to look for a web site with recipes to prepare a dinner ‘‘for tomorrow night,’’ whereas for the weak time constraint situation, they were told that the dinner would take place ‘‘in two weeks.’’

To create two different social conditions, we varied the context in which respondents had to look for recipes. Respondents in a high social pressure situation were asked to imagine they recently made a new female friend whom they wished to impress, and that this friend valued a healthy, low calorie dinner. In the low social pressure situation, respondents were told that a dinner was to be prepared for someone living in the same apartment complex. In a pretest, we had tested whether men and women responded differently when inviting male and female friends to dinner. Men were significantly more socially pressured when expecting a female friend, whereas women were equally (though highly) pressured when entertaining friends of either sex. Therefore, we presented both men and women with scenarios in which a female friend was invited.

After respondents had been exposed to the scenarios, we assessed their ratings of their anticipated affective states (i.e., pleasure and arousal) in the specific usage situation and also obtained their anticipated benefit importance measures [31]. Thus, respondents thought about which web sites they might want to consider searching for the anticipated scenarios, but did not actually visit web sites in response to the survey.

We used two approaches to ensure that our scenario manipulations worked as planned. First, we tested whether respondents found the situations realistic. We selected a sevenpoint Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree) and asked respondents to rate two items:

(1) The situation as described was realistic and

(2) I had no difficulty imagining myself in this situation.

The results showed that respondents had no difficulties, with moderately positive mean ratings of 4.68 and 5.08 for the two items, respectively. Second, we used manipulation checks to test if our manipulations were successful in generating anticipation of social pressure and time constraints. We used a seven-point Likert-type scales (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). The manipulation check for social pressure consisted of four items [11], adapted for the context of our study. After reading the description of the usage situation, subjects responded to the following statements:

(1) In this situation I find it necessary to look for information,

(2) My guest expects to receive the right menu from me,

(3) My guest would be disappointed if I had forgotten to follow their needs, and

(4) I absolutely have to look for the information

We compared the average of these items across the high and low social pressure groups and found a significant difference between the means of the low (3.48, n = 126) and high socia pressure group (3.99, n = 143) (t = 3.42, p =< 0.001) (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.79). To measure the perceived impact of the time constraint, respondents answered the following questions:

(1) In this situation I find myself pressed for time,

(2) I am in hurry,

(3) I have only a limited amount of time to look for the information,

(4) I must finish looking for information fast, as I have other things to do, and

(5) I have more than enough time to look for the necessary information [27].

The averages of these items in the weak (Mean = 2.15, n = 132) and strong (Mean = 3.04, n = 137) time constraint groups were significantly different (t = 6.75, p = <0.001) (Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.79). Thus, we were confident that our manipulations worked. These constructs were only used as part of the manipulation checks and not in the analysis, in which we used condition dummies to represent the scenario manipulations.

We also asked respondents to report on their Internet search skills and how often they cooked to screen out respondents who would not find the usage situation meaningful. Respondents were heavy Internet users $( \mathrm { M e a n } = 5 . 3 0 $ for a Likert-type scale from, 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Furthermore, all respondents cooked at least once a week, and the majority (61.3%) cooked four or more times.

## 3.2. Measures and construct validation

## 3.2.1. Affective state

In manipulating respondents’ anticipated affective states in our empirical analysis, we adopted an approach taken in many studies by focusing on pleasure/positive mood (e.g., giving a small gift such as candy). However, we recognize that respondents may also expect to be aroused, which involves an additional component of affective response that shows how excited and stimulated the person feels. Arousal thus could be a correlate of positive affect [32]. Therefore, we included anticipated arousal as a control variable in our model.

To measure responses of these affective dimensions, we used seven-point bipolar adjectival scales. For the pleasure dimension, respondents rated the following items: unhappy/happy, melancholic/contented, annoyed/pleased, unsatisfied/satisfied, despairing/hopeful, and bored/relaxed. Respondents rated their arousal as: sluggish/frenzied, calm/excited, relaxed/stimulated, unaroused/aroused, dull/jittery, and sleepy/wide awake.

## 3.2.2. Benefits

In total, we defined 13 web site benefits: relevance, understandability, reliability, adequacy, scope, usefulness, usability, speed, entertainment, navigation, tailored information, hyperlinks, and decisional control. Taken together, these represent the main themes in today’s marketing and MIS literature. Table 1 provides an overview of these and the source of their scales. We adapted the benefits slightly to the context of our study (Appendix B gives an overview of the scale items). To measure anticipated benefit importance, we asked respondents to show, on a seven-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), whether they found the benefits important in the particular usage situation. We assessed the number of salient benefits by determining how often respondents indicated a score of six or higher for each (composite score) and adding them. We also performed an analysis in which we assessed the number of salient benefits by determining how often respondents indicated a score of five or higher. This did not change our conclusions.

## 3.2.3. Construct validation

We conducted two confirmatory factor analyses to purify our scales. First, we tested for the reliability of the pleasure and arousal constructs; some items were deleted because of their low factor loadings and/or high modification indices: bored/relaxed, unsatisfied/satisfied, calm/excited, sleepy/awake, and sluggish/frenzied. The subsequent results provided good model fit $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 3 0 . 7$ $\mathrm { d . f . } = 1 3 , p < 0 . 0 0 4$ ; comparative fit index [CFI] = 0.98, nonnormed fit index [NNFI] = Tucker-Lewis index $\mathrm { [ T L I ] } = 0 . 9 7 ,$ , root mean square error of approximation $\mathrm { \Delta } [ \mathrm { R M S E A } ] = 0 . 0 7 1$ , and adjusted goodness-of-fit index [AGFI] = 0.93 with a standard RMS residual [SRMSR] of 0.042). All factor loadings were significant (t-values greater than 6), and all completely standardized loadings greater than 0.56 with an average of 0.78. These findings supported the convergent validity of our measures. Cronbach’s Alphas were 0.88 and 0.67 for pleasure and arousal, respectively. To verify the discriminant validity, we checked that the average variance extracted (AVE) of the latent construct was greater than the squared correlations with other latent constructs [10]. The estimates of the AVE were 0.82 and 0.61 for pleasure and arousal, respectively, and exceed their squared correlation. Thus, we averaged the appropriate scale items to obtain a composite score for pleasure and arousal.

We conducted a second confirmatory factor analysis to test the reliability of the 13 benefits and deleted 7 items because of their low factor loadings and/or high modification indices across constructs (see Appendix B). This deletion provided good model fit (x<sup>2</sup> = 1483, d.f. = 599, p < 0.001, CFI = 0.96, NNFI = TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.074, and AGFI = 0.72 with SRMSR = 0.047). All factor loadings were significant (t-values greater than 11), and all completely standardized loadings were greater than 0.70, with an average of 0.86. These findings supported the convergent validity of the measures. The Cronbach’s Alphas of all factors ranged from 0.79 to 0.90 (Appendix B). The estimates of the AVE ranged from 0.74 to 0.90 and were larger than the squared correlations with the other constructs, which provided evidence of discriminant validity. We therefore averaged the appropriate scale items to obtain a composite score for each benefit.

## 3.3. Model specification

When the impact of the usage situation on benefit importance was significantly different for different situations in the model, we supported H1a. Furthermore, if the impact of anticipated pleasure and arousal on consumers’ relative benefit importance was significant, we supported H1b. Finally, and more specifically, if the impact of anticipated pleasure on the relative importance of pleasure-oriented benefits was significant and positive we found support for H3. Finally, we tested the effect of anticipated positive affect on the number of salient benefits and show the results in Table 3. This effect was significant and in the positive direction in support of H2. We controlled for the possibility that the usage situation might have directly affected the number of salient benefits. The results showed no effect of the time constraint. However, social pressure decreased the number of salient benefits, even when we took the effects of anticipated affective states into account. Therefore, it appeared that anticipated affective state was not the only driver of the number of salient benefits in consumers’ mental representations of anticipated situations but that situation may also have directly affected the number of important benefits. Finally, we controlled for the effect of anticipated arousal on benefit importance. As expected, we found some significant effects, but they did not change our conclusions about the impact of positive affect on benefit importance.

## 5. Conclusion and discussion

We demonstrated the importance of situation in explaining the relative importance of anticipated benefits and the number of benefits that are seen as important by consumers. Furthermore, we showed that the mechanism involved is multifaceted and operates through a direct cognitive effect as well as the indirect effect of anticipated affective state. Thus, our results provided support for our model and highlight the importance of accounting for cognitive and affective effects simultaneously to understand the effects of situation on consumers’ web site benefit importance.

The usage situations that we examined triggered importance for some benefits but not others, and importance shifted across manipulation conditions as we expected. In particular, we note that consumers who experienced anticipated positive affect found the entertaining aspect of a web site more important than other benefits and consider a greater number of benefits to be important.

Our results also showed that consumers who experienced stronger anticipated positive affect, experienced more salient benefits; this may have direct managerial implications on web site customization. For instance, knowing the anticipated affective states that are triggered by a specific situation might help provide better web site designs. Being aware of consumers’ usage situations and their impact on anticipated affective states could help a web site designers match the site to the consumer’s mood, promoting desirable behavior (e.g., more time and money spent on the site). Thus, we hope that our findings provide relevant new insights to better understand the impact of situation on consumer web site benefit importance.

## Strong time constraint/low social pressure

You have offered to someone in your apartment complex to cook dinner tomorrow night. You are now going to look on the Internet for a healthy, low calorie recipe.

## Strong time constraint/high social pressure

You recently made a new girlfriend and you want to impress her; therefore, you invited her for dinner at your place tomorrow night. You know that she values a healthy, low calorie dinner; therefore, you are now going to look on the Internet for a healthy, low calorie recipe.

## Weak time constraint/high social pressure

You recently made a new girlfriend and you want to impress her; therefore, you invited her for dinner at your place in two weeks. You know that she values a healthy, low calorie dinner; therefore, you plan to look on the Internet for a recipe.
