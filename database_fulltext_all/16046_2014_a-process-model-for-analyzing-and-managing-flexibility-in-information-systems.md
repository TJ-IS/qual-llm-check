# A process model for analyzing and managing flexibility in information systems

Ram L. Kumar and Antonis C. Stylianou

University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Ram L. Kumar, Department of Business Information Systems & Operations Management, The Belk College of Business Administration, University of North Carolina at Charlotte, Charlotte, NC 28223, U.S.A. Tel.: 704.687.7649: Fax: 704.687.6330

## Abstract

Competitive pressures are forcing organizations to be flexible. Being responsive to changing environmental conditions is an important factor in determining corporate performance. Earlier research, focusing primarily on IT infrastructure, has shown that organizational flexibility is closely related to IT infrastructure flexibility. Using real-world cases, this paper explores flexibility in the broader context of the IS function. An empirically derived framework for better understanding and managing IS flexibility is developed using grounded theory and content analysis. A process model for managing flexibility is presented; it includes steps for understanding contextual factors, recognizing reasons why flexibility is important, evaluating what needs to be flexible, identifying flexibility categories and stakeholders, diagnosing types of flexibility needed, understanding synergies and tradeoffs between them, and prescribing strategies for proactively managing IS flexibility. Three major flexibility categories, flexibility in IS operations, flexibility in IS systems & services development and deployment, and flexibility in IS management, containing 10 IS flexibility types are identified and described.

European Journal of Information Systems (2014) 23, 151–184. doi:10.1057/ejis.2012.53; published online 8 January 2013

Keywords: IS flexibility; IS management; Process model

## Introduction

Current business environments require organizations to be flexible in order to be responsive to changing business requirements. As organizations become increasingly dependent on IS, such organizational agility and flexibility cannot be achieved without flexible Information Systems (IS) (Boynton, 1993; Evans 2002). Lack of IS flexibility constrains the ability of the organization to remain competitive (Lucas & Olson, 1994; DeSouza, 2007). At the same time, the IS area has been the center of great and often turbulent change in both the underlying technologies and business practices. Rapid technology changes have resulted in more frequent system changes as well as greater distribution of IS resources in a network environment. Given the very large investments in IT, organizations have been questioning the business practices of IS departments, resulting in many cases in decisions to outsource IS functions (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Saunders et al, 1997). Outsourcing as well as the more recent Application Service Provider concept (Kern et al, 2002) have increased pressure on the IS organization to be more responsive and flexible. Hence, in order for both the IS department and the organization as a whole to remain agile and competitive, it is important to have an increased understanding of IS flexibility and how to manage it.

Past literature describes flexibility as a desirable attribute of IS systems and infrastructure, linked to organizational flexibility (Golden & Powell, 2000), and contributing positively to organizational performance (Lucas & Olson, 1994; Duncan, 1995; Byrd & Turner, 2000). Careful design and management of IS has been shown to increase organizational flexibility (Lucas & Olson, 1994; Golden & Powell, 2000). At the same time, inflexible IT systems can inhibit organizational flexibility (Lucas & Olson, 1994; Attaran, 2004; Oosterhout et al, 2006). Effective solutions to business situations requiring IS flexibility require a systematic decision making process.

Prior research has recognized the existence of different IS flexibility types (such as technical and human infrastructure flexibility) and dimensions (such as connectivity, compatibility, and modularity) (Knoll & Jarvenpaa, 1994; Byrd & Turner, 2000; and others). The flexibility types offered so far address IS flexibility only partially, with a primary focus on infrastructure and software systems. Furthermore, the issues of relationships and tradeoffs between flexibility types or dimensions as well as strategies for managing flexibility have not been adequately addressed. Although many of the dimensions identified in previous literature could be viewed as strategies, no systematic study of IS flexibility strategies for each type of flexibility has been done. Executives need to consider the context, the need for various kinds of IS flexibility, how to achieve them, and other relevant factors. Better understanding and articulation of these factors can help in determining the proper action, timing, cost, and expected results. A systematic process that views IS flexibility from a broader perspective, analyzes the need for different types of flexibility, identifies strategies for managing each type of flexibility, and recognizes moderating factors would serve these needs.

We develop an empirically derived framework for better understanding and managing different types of IS flexibility. At the center of this framework is a conceptual process for analyzing and managing IS flexibility that includes understanding contextual factors, recognizing reasons why flexibility is important, evaluating what needs to be flexible, identifying relevant flexibility categories and stakeholders, diagnosing types of flexibility needed, understanding synergies and tradeoffs, and prescribing strategies for proactively managing IS flexibility. Also contained in the framework are descriptions of flexibility types and relationships between them, illustrative examples of contextual factors, flexibility needs, and strategies derived from case data.

Finally, unlike earlier studies that have been either conceptual or survey-based, we use a combination of grounded theory methodology and content analysis to analyze secondary data from real-world case studies. We analyze cases in which managers have applied particular strategies to deal with problems that required IS flexibility and through the grounded theory methodology we generate a process model and general descriptions of these conditions that require IS flexibility and strategies for achieving flexibility.

Process models describe a sequence of events or steps in order to achieve an outcome (Mohr, 1982). Such a model is appropriate for our research since our goal is to present a sequence of activities that managers can undertake in order to understand and manage flexibility in the context of IS. In contrast, survey research tends to produce factor models that describe factors that are important in the context of a specific dependent variable of interest (Newman & Robey, 1992). Factor models have also been referred to as variance models since independent variables in these models are used to explain the variance in the dependent variable (Shaw & Jarvenpaa, 1997). Examples of research comparing process and factor models in the context of IS research include Shaw & Jarvenpaa (1997) and Newman & Robey (1992).

We hope that the process model along with the descriptions of conditions requiring IS flexibility and the sample strategies for achieving flexibility will help managers understand and manage IS flexibility.

## Theoretical foundations

The term flexible is defined in Webster’s dictionary as ‘capable of responding or conforming to a changing or new situation’. Eppink (1978) describes flexibility as a strategic response to the unseen. Evans (1991) provides a review of research on the concept of flexibility. It is important to note that multiple terms have been used in the context of flexibility terms (Oosterhout et al, 2007). Some authors view agility as one type of flexibility (e.g., Das & Elango, 1995; Kumar, 1999), others view flexibility as a subset of agility (e.g., Lui & Piccoli, 2007) and others use the terms interchangeably (Galliers, 2007). Flexibility is typically associated with the ability to respond to some kind of uncertainty or change. In some cases, where uncertainty may be positive (an opportunity), and flexibility may be measured by the relative ease (in terms of time, effort, or cost) of proactively seizing this opportunity, terms such as agility are used. In other cases, it is possible to have negative uncertainty (problems or threats), and terms such as robustness and resilience typically refer to dealing with this uncertainty. The term versatility could be used in the context of being able to respond to a range of positive and negative uncertainties. In addition to the ‘responsiveness to change’ aspect of flexibility, Scott (1966) also identified ‘response variability’, that is, diversity of reactions, as a second dimension of flexibility.

Flexibility has been studied from multiple perspectives. These include an overall organizational, a manufacturing, as well as an IS perspective. Studies that discuss how to manage flexibility either discuss ways to achieve flexibility or present flexibility analysis processes in the context of systems development.

The strategic management literature recognizes the importance of organizational flexibility and contains interesting ideas on appropriate organizational strategies under different environmental conditions (Wernerfelt & Karnani, 1987). Several studies (e.g., Aaker & Mascarhenas, 1984; Wernerfelt & Karnani, 1987; Das & Elango, 1995; Kumar, 1999) discuss the need for organizational flexibility. Evans (1991) developed a framework of four types of strategies (maneuvers) towards strategic flexibility: pre-emptive, exploitive, protective, and corrective. Eardley et al (1997) discussed Evans’ framework in an IT context. Golden & Powell (2000) provide a comprehensive survey of the organizational flexibility literature and identify dimensions of flexibility.

Flexibility has been an important topic of interest to researchers in the area of Operations Management (OM) and has been studied extensively in the context of flexible manufacturing systems (Slack, 1987; Sethi & Sethi, 1990; Gerwin, 1993). The OM literature recognizes the fact that manufacturing flexibility is an important part of organizational flexibility and performance (Gerwin, 1993). Also, there is considerable emphasis on the fact that the term flexibility means different things to different people, and that it is important to understand the context in which flexibility is used.

## IS flexibility

Clemons & Weber (1994) and Kumar (1999) argue that IS enable organizations to adopt flexible strategies. Present day IS using distributed IS architectures allow companies to adopt a wider and more finely tuned (flexible) set of strategies than those suggested by Porter (1985). Weill et al (2002) conclude that organizations’ investments in different types of IT infrastructure services should be tailored to their strategic initiatives in order to facilitate agility. Loebbecke et al (1997) find that organizations can proactively manage their business risk by building flexible IS. In his book, Desouza (2007) presents a collection of papers emphasizing that agile IS are fundamental to enterprise agility. Overby et al (2006) define enterprise agility as ‘the ability of firms to sense environmental change and respond readily’. They classify IT investments into knowledge and process-oriented IT and propose a framework for understanding the relationship between IT investment type and the responding and sensing aspects of enterprise agility.

Lucas & Olson (1994) illustrate that, while IS can often enhance organizational flexibility significantly, poorly designed IS can be very inflexible and hence limit organizational flexibility. Attaran (2004) and Oosterhout et al (2006) also describe the role of IS as a barrier to or as a disabler for agility. At the same time, the flexibility of the IS function is dependent on both the technology and the organizational flexibility. Within each process, there are technology, personnel, and organizational/managerial decisions that can impact the flexibility of the process.

Flexibility is a multidimensional concept that may have different meanings depending on the context (Evans, 1991; Knoll & Jarvenpaa, 1994; Kumar & Stylianou, 1995; Suarez et al, 1995). Prior research on IS flexibility, however, has focused mostly on flexibility of the IT infrastructure and flexibility in systems development. A flexible IT infrastructure has been identified as a top concern of IS managers (Evans, 2002). Several researchers explored concepts relating to IT infrastructure flexibility (Boynton, 1993; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1994; Duncan, 1995; Broadbent & Weill, 1997; Broadbent et al, 1999; Byrd & Turner, 2000). Broadbent & Weill (1997) and Henderson & Venkatraman (1994) emphasized the need to differentiate between technical IT infrastructure and human IT infrastructure. Duncan (1995) suggested that one way to characterize technical IT infrastructure flexibility, is based on empirical evidence, and using the constructs of connectivity, compatibility, and modularity. Connectivity refers to the ability of a device in the IT infrastructure to connect to another device within or outside the organization. Compatibility refers to the ability to share information across different technologies in the infrastructure. Modularity refers to the ease with which software and hardware components can be added to or removed from the IT infrastructure. Lee et al (1995) proposed an empirically derived taxonomy for effective human IT infrastructure. They identified (a) technology management knowledge and skills, (b) business functional knowledge and skills, (c) interpersonal and management skills, and (d) technical knowledge and skills as being important for an effective human IT infrastructure. On the basis of the literature, Byrd & Turner (2000) developed and empirically tested an instrument to measure IT infrastructure flexibility. The instrument differentiates between technical and human IT infrastructure flexibility. Technical infrastructure flexibility is conceptualized as consisting of the following four dimensions: IT connectivity, application functionality, IT compatibility, and data transparency. Human infrastructure flexibility dimensions are technology management, business knowledge, management knowledge, and technical skills.

Using Huber & McDaniel’s (1986) definition of organizational flexibility, Nelson et al (1997) define technology flexibility as consisting of structural and process flexibility. Structural flexibility ‘reflects the ability of the design of a technology to be adapted to changes in the business process and is pro-actively designed into the technology’ (Nelson et al). Process flexibility ‘is the ability of people to make changes to the technology using management processes that support business process changes’ (Nelson et al).

Besides IT infrastructure flexibility, IS flexibility has also been studied in the context of systems development. Nelson & Cooprider (2001) used the Nelson et al (1997) two-dimensional model of technology flexibility to study flexibility in software systems. Their findings suggest that ‘organizations seeking flexibility through software technology need to consider both the structural design of the software application and the people and processes used to support the application’. Lee & Xia (2005) developed measurement scales of IS development project team flexibility along two dimensions: Response Extensiveness and Response Efficiency (range vs speed). Their results indicated a trade-off between those two dimensions. On the basis of case study research, Lyytinen & Rose (2006) found that the relative value of process goals to organizations (speed vs other process characteristics such as quality, cost, risk, innovation) varies over time depending on their learning focus. Other researchers have suggested methodologies and tools oriented towards making systems and the systems development process more flexible (Schwan & Jones, 1986; Fitzgerald, 1990; Davis, 1995).

Gebauer & Schober (2006) developed a mathematical model to study the relationship between business process characteristics (uncertainty, variability, and time criticality) and two IS flexibility types (flexibility to use the IS and flexibility to change the IS). They found that flexibility to change the IS is important under high process uncertainty, while flexibility to use is important under conditions of low process uncertainty. They also conclude that the value of an IS depends on process variability and time criticality. The paper did not go into how to achieve these types of flexibility.

Using a broader view of flexibility, Knoll & Jarvenpaa (1994) conceptualize three types of IS flexibility flexibility in functionality, in use, and in modification – and 20 dimensions of these types of flexibility. In spite of the broader view of flexibility, however, the study does not differentiate clearly between flexibility types and strategies for achieving each type of flexibility. In addition, the results are based on limited data from only nine academic articles.

Since flexibility is needed to anticipate, respond, or adapt to change, it is important to understand the possible sources of such change. Land (1982), Longworth (1985), and Fitzgerald (1990) suggested various types of change affecting IS. These changes include: changes in technology (hardware, software, and computing paradigms), laws, the economy, the environment, the organization (structure, policies, and strategies, processes, finances, mergers and acquisitions), the users (attitudes, expectations, tastes, and requirements), interfaces (internal and external systems, customers, suppliers), and IS personnel (skill sets, turnover). Fitzgerald argues that ‘a large proportion of major changes to our IS are potentially predictable'. He suggests the use of a technique he termed ‘Flexibility Analysis’ to analyze the impact of such changes and take necessary action during the systems development process. He emphasizes the need for flexibility analysis despite some practical reasons why such an analysis might be difficult. These reasons, which are in effect challenges to managing IS flexibility, include management commitment, organizational politics, additional time and resource requirements, and lack of analyst/designer experience. Similar to the concept of flexibility analysis, Land (1982) suggests a technique called ‘future analysis’, which aims to identify ‘systems features which are vulnerable to possible changes’ and provides guidance in the development of flexible systems able to accommodate such changes. A risk of not performing such an analysis, according to Land, is having inflexible systems that perform well under the current circumstances but whose performance degrades as those circumstances change. Land also describes the designer’s dilemma in deciding the amount of flexibility to build into a system given the cost and development effort necessary. Characteristically, he gives the example of a real case where ‘the designer is involved in a trade-off between the extra development and operational cost of designing a system which is adaptable and flexible – a very general system – or of designing a very specific system dedicated to the needs existing at the time of implementation, but which may be incapable of modification, and may have to be replaced if requirements change’.

## Research focus

In summary, Tables 1 and 2 together provide a summary of the extant literature on IS flexibility and our approach to studying IS flexibility in an integrated manner that considers different aspects of flexibility. Our focus in this paper is on the analysis and management of different types of flexibilities that are relevant to an IS manager. We do not limit ourselves to the IT infrastructure, or software systems, but explore flexibility in the context of the broader range of decisions encountered in the IS function. A major contribution of this paper is a process model for analyzing and managing flexibility that considers different types of flexibility in the context of a broad range of IS management decisions. Another contribution is identifving different types of flexibilities and providing reasons why they are important, exploring linkages and tradeoffs between them, and identifying strategies and moderating factors for proactively managing flexibility. We provide illustrative examples based on real-world data. The different types of IS flexibilities that we identify are empirically derived from data using the grounded theory methodology. It is important to realize that we do not attempt to develop an exhaustive classification of mutually exclusive flexibility types. Instead, we attempt to ‘partition’ the general concept of IS flexibility into flexibility categories and types while recognizing that their symptoms, treatments, and even the types themselves may overlap. The flexibility categories and types are components of a process model that can help practicing managers diagnose and manage flexibility problems. Unlike previous studies that have been either conceptual or survey-based, this research uses secondary case data. Such case-based research is especially appropriate for researching ‘how and why’ questions about a phenomenon (IS flexibility) (Yin, 1994). This broader perspective provides an enhanced understanding of flexibility in the context of IS, and provides a theory base for ‘flexibility analysis’ in the IS function.

## Methodology

A major objective of this research is to use data to develop a process model for IS flexibility analysis and management, generate descriptions of different kinds of IS flexibility, and understand reasons why they are important, and how to manage them.

Our research is based on the grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Strauss & Corbin, 1990), which has been used in other MIS research to study phenomena such as the adoption of CASE tools (Orlikowski, 1993) and EDI use (Crook & Kumar, 1998). Grounded theory is ‘a general methodology of analysis linked with data collection that uses a systematically applied set of methods to generate an inductive theory about a substantive area’ (Glaser, 1992). A major benefit of the grounded method is that it results in theory derived from data, and ‘is more likely to resemble the “reality” than is theory derived by putting together a series of concepts based on experience or solely through speculation (how one thinks things ought to work)’ (Strauss & Corbin, 1990).

Following the development of a grounded theory-based conceptual model, we further analyze the relationships between the identified IS flexibility categories, types, and strategies using content analysis.

## Data collection

Grounded theory generation may begin with data of any kind, including, for example, interviews and field observations, as well as documents such as trade and academic journal articles, newspaper articles, and other media materials (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). This study uses secondary data in the form of articles from academic and practitioner publications and online sources. Anecdotal descriptions of situations involving IS flexibility are used as ‘cases’ for subsequent grounded theory-based analyses. Research data described as anecdotes have been recognized as important sources of information for generating theories (Selltiz et al, 1976) particularly in applied areas (Mitroff & Kilmann, 1976). Mintzberg (1979) described the importance of using anecdotes for theory building as follows:

For, while systematic data create the foundation for our theories, it is the anecdotal data that enable us to do the building. Theory building seems to require rich description, the richness that comes from anecdote. We uncover all kinds of relationships in our hard data, but it is only through the use of this soft data that we are able to explain them. (p. 587)

This approach to analyzing cases from secondary data is similar to the award-winning ‘retrospective case analysis’ used by Dunn & Swierczek (1977). It is also consistent with research by Locke & Golden-Biddle (1997), which used an analysis of academic articles to develop a grounded theory of how organizational scientists construct opportunities for making research contributions to the field. Our approach is also consistent with Glaser and Strauss’s (1967, pp. 161–184) analysis of the similarities between field research and library research and the need to use library research as a ‘new source of qualitative data’.

The data used in this study were descriptions of the way organizations went about managing IS flexibility published in academic and practitioner articles and online sources in the period from January of 1990 to June of 2009 (see List of data sources). The data sources were selected with the objective of being representative of as broad a range of academic and practitioner perspectives as possible.

On the basis of the terms used in the literature to capture the meaning of flexibility, the following search keywords were used: flexibility, agility, elasticity, resilience, robustness, versatility, adaptability, reusability, modularity, interoperability, compatibility, and connectivity, along with information technology or IS. Using a variety of keywords relating to flexibility and searching multiple sources helped us ensure that IS flexibility is studied from a variety of perspectives, and no single viewpoint dominates the data.

The intent of the search process was not to exhaust the search space of all possible references to flexibility. We do believe, however, that the data sources and search terms used are representative of a broad range of academic and practitioner perspectives. Our data collection methodology was as follows:

1. First, we took steps to ensure the inclusion of relevant cases from leading academic and practitioner publications. Given that these journals, as well as many others, are included in the repositories searched in step 4, this was a redundant step. However, we wanted to make sure that we did not miss important cases reported in these leading publications. We searched the following publications:

1.1. Leading MIS academic journals: MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, European Journal of IS, IS Research, Journal of the Association for Information Systems, Journal of MIS, Harvard Business Review, Sloan Management Review, Information & Management, and DATA BASE.

1.2. Leading MIS practitioner magazines: Information-Week, CIO Magazine, ComputerWorld, and Datamation. This search resulted in 1350 matches.

2. We then read the abstracts from the above matches and screened them for suitability as follows:

2.1. Sources dealing with IS flexibility at a very low level of abstraction, such as for example an article describing how a proxy firewall is more flexible than other types of firewalls, were excluded.

2.2. Sources that did not provide real cases of IS flexibility were excluded.

2.3. Sources dealing superficially with IS flexibility or the other search terms and not providing enough detail for analysis were excluded.

2.4. Sources dealing with flexibility other than in the IS domain were excluded. This screening resulted in 64 cases, which were included in the data set.

3. Following, we performed an online search of three major software developers: Microsoft, IBM, and Oracle. This search resulted in 111,389 hits, the majority from the IBM site (106,637). Given the large number of hits, we took advantage of the relevance ranking features of the search engines to focus our attention to the 10 most relevant cases in each of the search keyword combinations. We then read the abstracts from those cases and screened them using the same rules from step 2 above. This screening resulted in 10 cases, which were included in the data set.

4. Finally, we searched each of the following repositories containing articles from academic and practitioner publications: AIS eLibrary, ACM Digital Library, Proquest ABI Inform, EBSCOHost Academic Search Elite, EBS-COHost Business Source Elite, Science Direct, and two major technology magazine syndicated online sites (Advisor.com and TechWeb.com).

This search resulted in a set of 130,949 matches. Starting with the top of the list of matches, we determined whether a case passed the screening criteria described above and, if it did, we analyzed it. We continued this process (screening and analyzing) with succeeding cases until we became confident that we could not develop the flexibility types any further, that is, reached theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). In all, 21 cases from this group were included in the data set.

In total, we collected 95 cases.

Using such a broad range of sources may introduce in the data differences resulting from the various communication genres and social processes associated with the publication of ‘IT flexibility stories’. However, this broad range of data sources facilitates triangulation of data, reduces bias in the interpretation of IT flexibility due to inclusion of just one type of communication genre, and assures that ‘saturation is based on the widest possible range of data’ (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). We believe that, given the objectives of this study, a wide search is particularly desirable. According to Dunn & Swierczek (1977) ‘Case materials that differ maximally in content and methods of reporting y provide a broad empirical domain with which concepts and hypotheses can be generated, [and]y are likely to reflect more adequately the conditions actually present’ (in this case the conditions present in efforts to achieve flexibility). ‘Multiple sources of information also provide a more adequate basis for interpreting y the content and meaning of concepts relating to flexibility as they are used by both academics and practitioners. In addition, as Glaser & Strauss (1967) point out, diversity in data sources is a critical consideration from a theoretical sampling perspective.

## Coding

The selected data sources were catalogued into cases and a systematic grounded theory process was used to analyze first each case individually and then across cases. Some data sources contained multiple cases. Specifically, each case was read and evaluated independently by multiple researchers. As argued by Eisenhardt (1989), ‘the convergence of observations from multiple investigators enhances confidence in the findings’. Following the independent evaluation, each case was reviewed by all researchers (at least two and in some cases three) together in order to reconcile differences and remove bias.

Our central phenomenon of interest was ‘IS Flexibility’. To help us think systematically about the data and to achieve density and precision in our grounded theory analyses we used the Strauss & Corbin (1990) action/ conditions coding paradigm as a starting conceptual template. On the basis of that and the data analyzed, we used open coding to develop concepts (categories) that best described the data. Development of such a modified coding paradigm based on more general grounded theory coding paradigms has been advocated by other researchers (see Locke, 2001).

Prior research helped to enhance theoretical sensitivity by making us aware of the need to look in the data for different types of flexibility and relationships between them, and by emphasizing the importance of other parts of the coding paradigm, which were under-researched in prior studies (literature gaps; see Table 2). Following the suggestions of how to use prior literature in grounded theory research (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) we were careful not to constrain ourselves by having to adhere to types of flexibilities that were described in prior literature.

## 6. Understand synergies and tradeoffs between flexibility types

Organizations are faced with situations (problems/opportunities) requiring IS flexibility. Addressing such situations is a process of understanding the context and needs, identifying the flexibility category and stakeholders, recognizing the various types of IS flexibility needed, and putting together a set of management actions to address those needs (see IS Flexibility Management Process Model in Figure 1). Effective resolutions require decision makers to be aware of the multidimensional nature of flexibility. While each of the above types of IS flexibility is important in its own right, there are many synergies and even possible conflicts and tradeoffs between them.

Synergies become evident in the data where, for many of the cases analyzed, multiple types of flexibility are desirable in order to address the particular conditions. In Case Ref. 36 for example, the following need is described:

Given the rapid rate of growth in Antelope Valley Health Care System’s customer population, the key criteria [sic] for its new platform was scalability. If, in the future, Antelope Valley Hospital needs to break up applications, or grow an application from 50 to 100 servers, the process should be seamless, manageable, and cost effective.

In response to the above, the company decided to pursue Development, New Technology Deployment Operating, Integration, and Volume flexibility. Further study of this case indicates that New Technology Deployment flexibility (the ability to evaluate and deploy new technology; hereafter referred to as Technology flexibility) helps achieve Volume flexibility. However, Technology flexibility is not always required to achieve Volume flexibility. The above case also illustrates that (the presence or absence of) particular strategies may affect (positively or negatively) more than one type of flexibility. For example, a strategy of having an infrastructure with the ‘ability to create and deploy multiple standards of software’, facilitates both Technology and Integration flexibility.

In another example (Case Ref. 45), inflexible outsourcing contacts not allowing renegotiation or longterm contracts with a single provider (poor Sourcing flexibility) promote unhealthy reliance and do not allow the organization to react quickly to technology changes (poor Technology flexibility). In this case, having contracts that allow renegotiation/termination increases both Sourcing and Technology flexibility.

In another example, an ability to temporarily increase volume for one system or application (Volume flexibility) may also be considered as Operating flexibility for the supersystem, if what is happening is shifting of resources in order to maintain service levels in case of a negative event. Volume flexibility relates to variations in the overall system output. Operating flexibility (as far as quantity of output is concerned) relates to variations in individual system unit output – more like shifting, or redistribution of output between different units.

In some cases, the presence of one type of flexibility can facilitate another type of flexibility. For example, Development flexibility facilitates Technology flexibility, and Sourcing flexibility may be a provider of Operating flexibility.

The above examples indicate the presence of synergistic relationships between some types of IS flexibility. Given these interrelations between flexibility types, when implementing a strategy aimed at gaining a particular type of flexibility, managers should look for synergistic results. In some cases, however, there may be conflicts between different types of flexibility and managers may need to examine tradeoffs between them. Examples of such tradeoffs include:

\- The ability to efficiently store, deliver, access, and use a variety of data and applications (i.e., having systems that are easily integrated) translates to Integration flexibility. Integration enhances multiple types of flexibility, such as Operating, and Development flexibility. However, systems integration could impact negatively other types of flexibility. For example, unnecessary integration could lead to a reduced ability to quickly and easily develop, maintain, and/or redesign systems, that is, lesser Development flexibility. (Case Ref. 14)

While, in general, capacity for increased systems integration was treated as a very important infrastructure concern, executives in a major Texas insurance and investments firm spoke of integration as a source of maintenance misery. They described a case in which business management asked the IS organization to identify the implemented details of a specific business rule. It took IS two weeks to respond.

\- ‘While strict standards about technologies increase the ease with which IS can ensure shareability and reusability of infrastructure components [Integration, Development, Technology flexibility], they reduce business options for IT implementation’ and choices available to systems implementers (Development flexibility). (Case Ref. 13) In addition to conflicts between different types of flexibility, this example demonstrates how a particular strategy (standardization) can have positive as well as negative effects on a single flexibility type (Development). This phenomenon can be explained by referring to the flexibility dimensions/metrics discussed extensively in prior flexibility literature: speed, cost, and range (Avison et al, 1995; Das & Elango, 1995; Golden & Powell, 2000). While standardization can increase Development flexibility, by increasing speed and reducing cost of systems development, it can decrease the same flexibility by limiting the range of choices available for systems developers.

## Prescribe IS flexibility management actions

IS flexibility management actions are actions directed at managing and achieving IS flexibility. As shown in the IS flexibility management process model in Figure 1, these actions are driven by the flexibility needs and contextual factors discussed above and the type of flexibility. For example, in Case Ref. 2 (mentioned above), within the context of a dynamic and complex data environment there was a need to achieve flexible data distribution in order to gain volume and input/output flexibility. Using a ‘broker-based publish and subscribe data architecture’ was the chosen flexibility management action. In Case Ref. 55 (also mentioned above) to satisfy the need for flexible employee assignment (staffing flexibility) the company took the following flexibility management actions: trained employees ‘with broad technical and management skills’ and used broadbanding (a technique allowing employees to work on different roles in different projects).

Surveys can be designed to test the impact of particular strategies on a flexibility type under various contextual conditions or relationships between flexibility types.

In the context of global organizations, centralized management of systems tailored to local needs increases development flexibility.

## 8. Review outcomes

Managing IS flexibility through the application of the process model needs to be done continuously. Hence, it is important for managers to review outcomes and learn from each application of the model. On the basis of our experience, comments we received from practitioners, generally accepted systems theory and quality management principles, questions to reflect on include:

\- Which of the undertaken management actions were successful and to what degree? Which performed less than expected?

\- Were there synergies or conflicts observed among the strategies employed?

\- Were there synergies or conflicts observed between flexibility types?

\- Were there changes in contextual factors that affected flexibility needs or the effectiveness of the undertaken management actions over time?

We should point out that we do not have case data to support the specific questions listed above or even the inclusion of the Review activity in the process model. Rather, we felt the need to include it for purposes of completeness based on generally accepted Systems Theory.

## Conclusion

## Relationship to previous research

The process model in Figure 1 (as described in the sections above) provides an integrated view of IS flexibility management. Some of what have previously been offered as types or dimensions of flexibility are included in our model as strategies that can be used to achieve one or more types of flexibility. For example, dimensions such as IT connectivity, compatibility, data transparency, technology management (Byrd & Turner, 2000; Duncan, 1995), modularity, and reusability (Knoll & Jarvenpaa, 1994) can be considered types of strategies for achieving different types of flexibility.

Previously suggested metrics of flexibility such as speed, cost, amount (Das & Elango, 1995), efficiency, responsiveness, and versatility (Golden & Powell, 2000) can be used to measure different types of flexibility in our model. By measuring the level of various types of IS flexibility, future research could attempt to establish relationships between IS flexibility and organizational performance metrics.

The IS flexibility management process model described above and the related Tables A1–A9 in the Appendix represent a perspective different from those offered in the prior literature on the topic. This perspective is not contradictory to previous literature but broader, integrative, and process-oriented. The unit of analysis of flexibility in this paper (the IS function) is broader than prior research focusing on particular types of flexibility such as software system flexibility (e.g., Nelson & Ghods, 1998) and infrastructure flexibility (e.g., Duncan 1995, Byrd & Turner, 2000). Our model addresses both structural (technology design-oriented) and process (people and management process-oriented) flexibility (Nelson & Ghods, 1998). Some of the strategies to achieve flexibility that we have identified address structural flexibility, some address process flexibility and some address both.

The framework presented here is sufficiently general to be applicable to a range of situations relating to the management of IS flexibility, is easily understandable by practitioners, and should provide useful insight into the actual management of IS flexibility. This research can serve as a basis from which both IS decision makers as well as researchers can understand and manage what is typically a complex but increasingly important aspect of IS-enabled competitive advantage.

The framework proposed here is different from existing frameworks on IS flexibility. Existing frameworks have focused primarily on dimensions of flexibility and distinctions between technical infrastructure, human infrastructure, and process flexibility in limited contexts such as systems development and infrastructure management. This research suggests that, while these distinctions are important, managers may benefit from following a flexibility management process and from conceptualizing flexibility using more granular categories and in the broader context of the IS function.

## Research limitations

This research is primarily qualitative with data from cases reported in academic and professional publications. Such anecdotal data are recognized as important for generating theories (Selltiz et al, 1976; Mintzberg, 1979) particularly in applied areas (Mitroff & Kilmann, 1976). This approach allowed us to generate categories, such as components of the process model, types of flexibility, illustrative strategies and propositions. To better understand and generalize the relative importance of different types of flexibility and to identify the management actions that can best achieve particular types of flexibility, our analysis can be used as the starting point for additional survey-based research.

We recognize that secondary data may suffer from the biases of the creators of these data. However, we believe that using secondary data in the way we have allows us to analyze a broad range of flexibility cases. The effort required to obtain primary data on such a broad range of situations involving IS flexibility would have been prohibitive, in our opinion. Furthermore, secondary case data are recognized in the literature as a legitimate and important source of information as described in the ‘Data collection’ section of the paper.

## Future research ideas

Understanding the concept of IS flexibility in terms of multiple flexibility types and distinguishing between the conditions that make each type of flexibility desirable and the strategies used to accomplish flexibility allows us to answer not only the what (is IS flexibility), but also the why (is IS flexibility important), and how (to manage IS flexibility) questions. Flexible usage and management of resources (people, hardware, software, money, time) can benefit the IS function and the entire organization. It is important to recognize, however, that managing IS flexibility is challenging and no set of strategies could be a prescription for all organizations or even for all times.

The following directions for further research can be proposed:

\- This research has identified contextual factors impacting IS flexibility. As a next step, it may be interesting to examine the relative importance of different types of flexibility in a variety of IS contexts. This may help to differentiate between major and minor types of flexibility and help better manage IS flexibility. One example of such research may be to identify specific types of flexibility that may be more or less important for specific types of projects and for the various stages of a project. In addition, the relationship between the portfolio of IS flexibilities needed given a particular organizational strategy merits further research.
