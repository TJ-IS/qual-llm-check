# Financial performance analysis of US and world telecommunications companies: Importance of Information Technology in the telecommunications industry after the AT&T breakup and the NTT divestiture

Mika Goto

Central Research Institute of Electric Power Industry, 2-11-1, Iwado Kita, Komae-shi, Tokyo 201-8511, Japan

a r t i c l e i n f o

Available online 17 June 2009

Keywords: Financial performance Telecommunications Information technology

## a b s t r a c t

This study investigates the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of the world telecommunications industry by DEA–DA (Data Envelopment Analysis–Discriminant Analysis). The proposed use of DEA–DA has a linkage with Altman's Z score that has long served as a methodological and conceptual basis in <sup>fi</sup>nance. Based upon the Z score of telecommunications companies, we rank them for <sup>fi</sup>nancial assessment. After evaluating their financial performance of the firms. this study pays attention to the financial performance of AT&T (American Telephone & Telegraph) and NTT (Nippon Telegraph and Telephone) after their divestiture. This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that AT&T outperformed NTT because AT&T changed itself to an IT (Information Technology) company that provides wireless communications services and other IT services, but NTT separated IT and wireless services into the other companies after the breakup.

## 1. Introduction

Information Technology (IT) heavily depends upon the development of telecommunications that provides Internet and other various types of information system with global technology infrastructure by connecting computers and other communications systems in the world [1,3,13,18].

A policy linkage between IT and telecommunications could be found in an effort of former Vice President Al Gore. His effort for IT infrastructure development was partly enacted in the form of the High Performance Computing (HPC) Act of 1991 (US Congress, Public Law 102-94; Dec. 9, 1991). The act was established to fully utilize an information network, referred to as ARPANET (Advanced Research Project Agency NETwork), which was originally developed for the US Department of Defense. The network was a large system with digital communication technology that connected universities, <sup>fi</sup>rms, public agencies and other organizations to develop a nationwide time sharing system by connecting computers existing at many research centers. Along with the development of TCP/IP (Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol), the network has been further developed into a very large scale network, now known as “Internet” that becomes a huge global linkage all over the world. See [14] that provided a detailed description on the history of Internet by comparing the information structure development between USA and Japan.

Along with the technology development in IT and telecommunications, the US Department of Justice made a judicial decision on the

AT&T (American Telephone & Telegraph) divestiture that used to provide various communication services including local and longdistance telephone services, as a monopoly entity, all over the United States of America. To examine the validity of methodology used for guiding the AT&T breakup, Professor Cooper and his associates [6] reexamined the empirical study provided by economists [8] who obtained a large research fund from MCI and provided empirical evidence for the side of the US Department of Justice. As a result of their investigation, Professor Cooper and his associates [6] published an article entitled “A Goal Programming/Constrained Regression Review of the Bell System Breakup” in 1988. The article [6, p.1] has mentioned “the court decision to break up Bell (= American Telephone & Telegraph Co.) to accord with US anti-trust laws represents a highly signi<sup>fi</sup>cant policy decision which is proving to be in<sup>fl</sup>uential in other counties as well as the USA. Studies commissioned by the US Justice Department on this topic, utilizing econometric methods, concluded that the evidence failed to support the contention that the Bell system was a natural monopoly”. The research [6] applied a method developed in management science to the same functional form and the same data, but it nevertheless reversed the main <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the economic studies in every one of the 20 years covered.

After the research of [6], many studies investigated the natural monopoly issue of the telecommunications industry. For example, the research [24] extended the research [6] in the manner that it considered the AT&T cost function as a stochastic frontier that consisted of a managerial error (measured by DEA: Data Envelopment Analysis) and an observational error (L1 regression). The research reexamined the AT&T divestiture from the stochastic frontier cost function and found that the AT&T divesture was inconclusive on the natural monopoly issue, but it did not produce the cost reduction bene<sup>fi</sup>t for consumers as expected by [8]. The study [25] fully utilized the approach used for the AT&T divesture to examine the natural monopoly issue of NTT (Nippon Telegraph and Telephone) in Japan. The study found that the NTT consisted of increasing returns to scale in most of observed annual periods but NTT did not consist of natural monopoly.

Following his research spirit of Professor Cooper, this study extends the research [6] by both reviewing the current research and applying a new approach to evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of telecommunications <sup>fi</sup>rms. An important feature of this study is that we focus upon the comparison between the AT&T breakup and the NTT divestiture from the perspective of IT. Furthermore, this study discusses policy implications on the telecommunications industry from the perspective of <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance evaluation and IT-based information infrastructure development. (The importance of this type of research in DSS/IT area can be found in [22].) The methodology used in this study is DEA–DA (Data Envelopment Analysis-Discriminant Analysis: [26]) that is designed to measure the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of <sup>fi</sup>rms. That is the purpose of this study. The research issue has been never investigated in the previous studies.

The remaining structure of this article is organized as follows: Section 2 includes a literature review on DA. The section indicates the methodological position of this research among existing literature on DA. Section 3 describes the formulation of DEA–DA used in this study. Section 4 applies the DEA–DA to examine the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of telecommunications <sup>fi</sup>rms. The last section (Section 5) documents a concluding comment along with future research agendas.

## 2. Literature review

Since Professor Cooper has dedicated his life to the development of various methodologies, this study discusses the position of DEA–DA by comparing it with previous studies on Discriminant Analysis (DA) in Mathematical Programming. DA is a classi<sup>fi</sup>cation method that can predict group membership of a newly sampled observation. In the use of DA, a group of observations whose memberships are already identi<sup>fi</sup>ed are used for the estimation of weights (or parameters) of a discriminant function by some criteria such as the minimization of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cations or the maximization of correct classi<sup>fi</sup>cations. A new sample is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into one of several groups by DA results. The DA has the following research groups from its methodological developments.

## 2.1. Statistics

A group of research is interested in statistical DA methods. The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution may be dating back to [9] and [23]. The conventional statistical DA methods usually maintain underlying assumptions on group distributions. For example, two groups come from normal populations with different means, but a same covariance matrix, all of which should be known to us before the applications. Under the assumptions, the statistical DA methods provide a theoretical basis for conducting various statistical inferences and tests. Furthermore, the ordinary least squares regression is usually used to obtain coef<sup>fi</sup>cient estimates of a linear discriminant function. Thus, there is a computational simplicity in the statistical DA methods. Those are methodological strengths and contributions, indeed. However, it is also true that real data sets do not necessarily satisfy such underlying assumptions.

## 2.2. Econometrics

If independent variables are normally distributed, the statistical DA estimator is a true maximum-likelihood estimator and therefore asymptotically more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient than other DA methods. However, the assumption on normality is not satis<sup>fi</sup>ed in many real data sets. To overcome such a shortcoming related to statistical DA, econometricians developed other several DA methods that had a close linkage with the theory of probabilistic choice discussed by psychologists. The most well known research effort in this area was due to [19,20] who investigated logit and probit models. The two models are solved by maximum-likelihood methods. An important feature of logit and probit models is that the models provide the conditional probability of an observation belonging to a certain class, given its independent variables. Both are based on a cumulative probability function and neither requires that independent variables be multivariate normal or that groups have equal covariance matrices, unlike the requirements of statistical DA. Furthermore, the two models have a close linkage with statistical inferences and various tests.

## 2.3. Mathematical Programming

Mathematical Programming (MP) methods were proposed for solving various DA problems. These methods consist of the third group on DA methodology. The <sup>fi</sup>rst contribution of this group was due to Professor Cooper and his associates [5] who documented how to formulate L1 metric regression by a goal programming model and how to solve the problem by linear programming algorithm. See [4] for a detailed description on goal programming. A popularity of MP-based DA among researchers occurred after the research effort of [10–12]. The studies presented how a DA problem was formulated by goal programming. Based upon the optimization techniques, the third group of DA studies was further classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into (a) linear programming methods (e.g., [2,20]), (b) nonlinear programming methods (e.g., [7]) and (c) mixed integer programming methods (e.g., [21,27]). A comprehensive review on the MP-based DA was found in [29]. A methodological bene<sup>fi</sup>t of the research group is that the MP-based DA methods do not need any assumption on a group distribution. Meanwhile, a shortcoming of the MP-based DA is that statistical inferences and tests are not yet well developed at the level of the statistical and econometric DA approaches.

## 2.4. Position of this research

DEA–DA used in this study belongs to the third research group of DA. The previous DA studies in the third research group have been looking for the development of new DA models and these related computational issues. DEA–DA was developed under such a research direction. Unfortunately, the previous research efforts on DEA–DA have not yet suf<sup>fi</sup>ciently discussed how to use it for real DA applications. Hence, this study describes DEA–DA from a practical perspective of evaluating <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of telecommunications <sup>fi</sup>rms. That is an expected contribution of this study.

Note that this study is fully aware of the existence of previous studies on numerical classi<sup>fi</sup>cation capabilities [e.g., 16,17,28] in computer science. The numerical capabilities focus on weight generation so that it cannot identify which weight is important or not. Hence, this study does not use the numerical approaches.

## 3. Methodology

The research [26] has proposed a new type of nonparametric DA approach that provides a set of weights of a linear discriminant function(s), consequently yielding an evaluation score(s) for the determination of group membership. The new nonparametric DA is referred to as $\ " { \mathrm { D E A - D A } } ^ { \prime }$ because it maintains a discriminant capability by incorporating the nonparametric feature of DEA into DA.

To start our description on DEA–DA, we consider the corporate performance evaluation that contains two groups of <sup>fi</sup>rms $( G _ { 1 }$ and $G _ { 2 } )$ The sum of two groups has n <sup>fi</sup>rms $( j = 1 , . . , n )$ where $n { = } n _ { 1 } { + } n _ { 2 } . G _ { 1 }$ is a group of higher performance <sup>fi</sup>rms $( j = 1 , . . , n _ { 1 } )$ and $G _ { 2 }$ is a group of lower performance <sup>fi</sup>rms $( j = 1 , . . , n _ { 2 } )$ . Each <sup>fi</sup>rm is characterized by h independent <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables $( f { = } 1 , . . , h )$ , where each <sup>fi</sup>nancial variable indicates a <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio such as ROA (return on assets). $\mathsf { A } _ { . }$ discriminant function between the two groups is expressed by $\sum _ { f = 1 } ^ { h } \mathsf { \lambda } _ { h f } z _ { f j }$ . Here, $\lambda _ { f }$ is a weight for the f-th <sup>fi</sup>nancial variable. The f-th <sup>fi</sup>nancial variable of the j-th observation is denoted by $z _ { f j } .$

Mathematically, DEA–DA has two stages for computation. The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage identi<sup>fi</sup>es an existence of an overlap between two groups of observations. Then, the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage classi<sup>fi</sup>es observations not belonging to the overlap. The second stage classi<sup>fi</sup>es observations in the overlap.

Stage 1: The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage has the following formulation:

$$
\min \left\{s \left| \begin{array}{l} \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} \lambda_ {f} z _ {f j} - d + s \geq 0, j \in G _ {1}, \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} \lambda_ {f} z _ {f j} - d - s \leq - \varepsilon , j \in G _ {2}, \\ \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} | \lambda_ {f} | = 1, \lambda_ {f}, d \& s: U R S. \end{array} \right. \right\}.\tag{1}
$$

The objective function of Eq. (1) minimizes an unknown variable (s) that indicates the size of an overlap between $G _ { 1 }$ and $G _ { 2 } .$ The overlap is surrounded by $d { - } s$ (as a lower bound of $G _ { 1 } )$ and $d + s$ (as an upper bound of $G _ { 2 } )$ . The discriminant score for group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is expressed by a scalar value $" d "$ . Both d and s are unrestricted (URS). A very small number (ε) is incorporated into Eq. (1) in order to separate the two groups clearly. When an observation exists on an estimated discriminant function, we have a dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in its classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The small number is incorporated in Eq. (1) to avoid such a case. All the <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables of the j-th observation are connected by the discriminant function $( \sum _ { f = 1 } ^ { h } \mathsf { \lambda } _ { h f } z _ { f j } )$ . Furthermore, the weights are restricted in such a manner that the sum of absolute values of $\lambda _ { f }$ (for all $f { = } 1 , . . , h )$ is unity. Consequently, Eq. (1) estimates each weight by a percentage expression so that we can examine which weight is important or not in terms of group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation. The weight restriction is known as “normalization.” The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage identi<sup>fi</sup>es the existence of an overlap by $s ^ { * } \geq 0$ on optimality of Eq. (1). An opposite case $( s ^ { * } { < } 0 )$ ) indicates no overlap.

Model (1) has the following reformulation because we cannot solve Eq. (1) directly.

$$
\min \left\{s \left| \begin{array}{l} \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} - \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) z _ {f j} - d + s \geq 0, j \in G _ {1}, \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} - \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) z _ {f j} - d - s \leq - \varepsilon , j \in G _ {2}, \\ \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} + \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) = 1, \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} (\text {   for   all   } f), \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} (\text {   for   all   } f), \\ \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} + \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \leq 1 (\text {   for   all   } f), \lambda_ {f} ^ {+} + \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon (\text {   for   all   } f), \\ d \& s: U R S, \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \& \zeta_ {f} ^ {-}: \text {   binary,and   all   other   variables   } \geq 0. \end{array} \right. \right\},\tag{2}
$$

In transforming $\lambda _ { f } ( f = 1 , . . , h )$ into a special ordered set of paired variables $( \lambda _ { f } = \lambda _ { f } ^ { + } - \tilde { \lambda _ { f } } )$ , we de<sup>fi</sup>ne these variables as

$$
\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} = (| \lambda_ {f} | + \lambda_ {f}) / 2 \text { and } \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} = (| \lambda_ {f} | - \lambda_ {f}) / 2,\tag{3}
$$

each representing positive or negative part of $\lambda _ { f }$ respectively. These paired variables maintain the following relationships: $\lambda _ { f } = \lambda _ { f } ^ { + } - \lambda _ { f }$ and ${ | \Lambda _ { f } | = \lambda _ { f } ^ { + } + \lambda _ { f } }$

Such a transformation needs Non-Linear Condition (NLC: $\rangle _ { f } ^ { + } \lambda \cdot$ $\bar { f } ^ { - } = 0 )$ for each $\mathrm { f } \left( = 1 , . . , h \right)$ in order to avoid a simultaneous occurrence of both $\lambda _ { f } ^ { + } > 0$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \lambda _ { f } ^ { - } > 0 . } \end{array}$ . To incorporate NLC, this study uses its MIP (Mixed Integer Programming) equivalence. Here, let $\zeta _ { f } ^ { + } \left( = 0 \mathrm { o r } 1 \right)$ and $\scriptstyle \zeta _ { f } ~ ( = 0 \ \ o { \mathrm { o r } } \ 1 )$ be two binary variables, then NLC is expressed by

$$
N L C: \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \text { and } \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} (f = 1,.., h)\tag{4}
$$

$$
\zeta_ {f} ^ {+} + \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \leq 1 \quad (f = 1,.., h)\tag{5}
$$

Here, Eq. (4) implies the upper and lower bounds of $\lambda _ { f } ^ { + }$ and those bounds of $\overline { { \Lambda _ { f } ^ { - } } }$ , respectively. Furthermore, Eq. (5) implies that the sum of these binary variables is less than or equal to unity. If both $\lambda _ { f } ^ { + } \ge \varepsilon > 0$ and $\lambda _ { f } ^ { - } \ge \varepsilon > 0$ occur simultaneously in Eq. (4), then Eq. (5) becomes $\zeta _ { f } ^ { + } +$ $\begin{array} { r } { \zeta _ { f } = 2 . } \end{array}$ . The result is infeasible in Eq. (5). Hence, the simultaneous occurrence of $\lambda _ { f } ^ { + } { > } 0$ and $\bar { \Lambda _ { f } } { > } 0$ is excluded from the computational process of Eq. (2). All the other $( \lambda _ { f } ^ { + }$ and $\bar { \lambda _ { f } } )$ combinations become feasible in both Eqs. (4) and (5), so being feasible in Eq. (2).

At the end of the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, we need to separate all observations into subsets. To explain the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, let $\lambda _ { f } ^ { * } ( = \lambda _ { f } ^ { + * } - \lambda _ { f } ^ { - * } ) ,$ , d\* and $s ^ { * }$ be an optimal solution of Eq. (2). Then, the original data set (G) is classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into the following subsets $G { = } G _ { 1 } \cup G _ { 2 } { = } C _ { 1 } \cup D _ { 1 } \cup C _ { 2 } \cup D _ { 2 }$ where $\mathsf C _ { 1 } = \left\{ j \in G _ { 1 } \bigg | \sum _ { f = 1 } ^ { h } \lambda _ { f } ^ { * } z _ { f j } > d ^ { * } + s ^ { * } \right\} , \mathsf C _ { 2 } = \left\{ j \in G _ { 2 } \bigg | \sum _ { f = 1 } ^ { h } \lambda _ { f } ^ { * } z _ { f j } < d ^ { * } - s ^ { * } \right\} ,$ $D _ { 1 } { = } G _ { 1 } - C _ { 1 }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { D _ { 2 } = G _ { 2 } - C _ { 2 } . } \end{array}$ Based upon the classi<sup>fi</sup>cation, we determine that observations in $C _ { 1 }$ belong to $G _ { 1 }$ and those of $C _ { 2 }$ belong to $G _ { 2 }$ because those observations locate clearly above or below an overlap identi<sup>fi</sup>ed from Eq. (2). The two subsets $( D _ { 1 } \cup D _ { 2 } )$ consist of the overlap whose observations are not yet classi<sup>fi</sup>ed at the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage.

Stage 2: In a case when two groups have an overlap, we need to reclassify all the observations in the overlap $( D _ { 1 } \cup D _ { 2 } )$ , because the group membership of these observations is still unknown and needs to be determined. Mathematically, the second stage has the following formulation:

$$
\min \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \left| \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} - \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) z _ {f f} - c + M y _ {j} \geq 0, j \in D _ {1}, \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} - \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) z _ {f f} - c - M y _ {j} \leq - \varepsilon , j \in D _ {2}, \right. \\ \left. \sum_ {f = 1} ^ {h} (\lambda_ {f} ^ {+} + \lambda_ {f} ^ {-}) = 1, \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} (\text {   for   all   } f), \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} \geq \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} (\text {   for   all   } f), \zeta_ {f} ^ {+} + \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \leq 1 (\text {   for   all   } f), \right. \\ \left. \lambda_ {f} ^ {+} + \lambda_ {f} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon (\text {   for   some   } f), c: U R S, \zeta_ {f} ^ {+}, \zeta_ {f} ^ {-} \& y _ {j}: \text {   binary,   and   all   other   variables   } \geq 0. \right. \end{array} \right\}\tag{6}
$$

where the binary variable $( y _ { j } )$ counts the number of observations classi<sup>fi</sup>ed incorrectly. The objective function minimizes the number of misclassi<sup>fi</sup>cations. We need to prescribe a large number (M) and a small number (ε) in Eq. (6). A discriminant score (c) is newly incorporated into Eq. (6) as an unknown unrestricted variable. The new discriminant score for the second stage substitutes for the previous discriminant score (d) in Eq. (2) for the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage.

Non-Zero Condition (NZC): A possibility, to which we need to pay attention, is a simultaneous occurrence of $\lambda _ { f } ^ { + } = 0$ and $\bar { \Lambda _ { f } } = 0$ in Eq. (6). The occurrence of zero in the paired variables does not become a mathematical problem as the computational result of Eq. (6). However, to control the number of positive $\lambda _ { f }$ estimates, we need to incorporate the following Non-Zero Condition (NZC) in Eq. (6):

$$
\mathrm{NZC}: \lambda_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {+} + \lambda_ {\mathrm{f}} ^ {-} \geq \varepsilon (\text { for   some } f)\tag{7}
$$

Here, Eq. (7) avoids a simultaneous occurrence o $\therefore \lambda _ { f } ^ { + } = 0$ and $\bar { \Lambda _ { f } } = 0$ regarding the f-th <sup>fi</sup>nancial variable. It is acceptable that all <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables have NZC Eq. (7), as incorporated in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage Eq. (2). However, in the second stage Eq. (6), the incorporation of Eq. (7) depends upon the degree of freedom in the observations within an overlap.

After obtaining an optimal solution on ${ \lambda } _ { f } ^ { * }$ and $c ^ { * }$ from Eq. (6), the second stage classi<sup>fi</sup>es observations in the overlap as follows: if $\sum _ { . } ^ { \cdot . . } \lambda _ { f } ^ { * } z _ { f j } \ge \stackrel { \cdot } { c }$ , then the j-th observation belongs to $G _ { 1 }$ or if $\sum _ { f = 1 } ^ { f \# } \lambda _ { f } ^ { * } z _ { f j } \leq c ^ { * } - \varepsilon ,$ , then it belongs to $G _ { 2 } .$ Thus, all the observations in G are classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as either $G _ { 1 }$ or $G _ { 2 }$ at the end of DEA–DA.

## 4. An application to the world telecommunications companies

## 4.1. Data description

Table 1 summarizes 44 telecommunications companies used in this study. We identify the companies from Telecom Services-Domestic (USA) and Telecom Services-Foreign in the investment section of YAHOO FINANCE (http://<sup>fi</sup>nance.yahoo.com/). Then, we obtain a <sup>fi</sup>nancial data set regarding the 44 <sup>fi</sup>rms from Compustat (Standard & Poor's). The sample period of the data set is from 1997 to 2007. The data is structured by an unbalanced panel data that contains missing data. The total number of samples is 298.

This study classi<sup>fi</sup>es the data set based upon Price Earnings Ratio (market value per share/earnings per share). The ratio is often referred to as P/E ratio. The <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio stands for a company's current share price compared to its per-share earnings. In general, a high P/E suggests that investors are expecting higher earnings growth in the future compared to companies with a lower P/E. It is usually useful to compare the P/E ratios of one company to other companies in the same industry, to the market in general or against the company's own historical P/E.

Based upon the P/E ratio, the whole data set with 298 samples is separated into three groups. The <sup>fi</sup>rst group consists of G1 in DEA–DA. The G1 group contains 99 samples that belong to the top 33% in the P/E ratio. The second group consists of G2 in DEA–DA. The G2 group contains 99 samples that belong to the bottom 33% in the P/E ratio. In addition, there are 100 samples which do not belong to the top 33% and the bottom 33% in the P/E ratio.

All the <sup>fi</sup>rms are characterized by the following <sup>fi</sup>nancial factors:

(a) Return on Assets (ROA: Net Income/Total Assets): The ROA (%) is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial indicator concerning how pro<sup>fi</sup>table a company is relative to its total assets. ROA gives a measure as to how ef<sup>fi</sup>cient management is at using its assets to generate earnings. Calculated by dividing a company's annual earnings by its total assets, ROA is displayed as a percentage. Sometimes this is referred to as “return on investment”.

(b) EBITDA Margin (Total EBITDA/Total Revenue): The <sup>fi</sup>nancial factor (%) assesses a company's pro<sup>fi</sup>tability by comparing its revenue with earnings. EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. Since EBITDA is derived from revenue, this <sup>fi</sup>nancial factor would indicate the percentage of a company is remaining after operating expenses.

(c) EBITDA Interest Coverage (EBITDA/Interest Payments): This is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial ratio (%) that is used to assess a company's <sup>fi</sup>nancial durability by examining whether it is at least pro<sup>fi</sup>tably enough to pay off its interest expenses. A ratio greater than 1 indicates that the company has more than enough interest coverage to pay off its interest expenses.

(d) Total Debt to Capital (Debt/(Shareholders' Equity+Debt)): This is a measure (%) of a company's <sup>fi</sup>nancial leverage, calculated as the company's debt divided by its total capital. Debt includes all short-term and long-term obligations. Total capital includes the company's debt and shareholders' equity.

(e) Quick Ratio ((Current Assets − Inventories)/(Current Liabilities)): This is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial indicator (expressed by ratio) of a company's short-term liquidity. The quick ratio measures a company's ability to meet its short-term obligations with its most liquid assets. The high quick ratio indicates the better position of the company.

(f) Total Asset Turnover (Total Revenue/Total Assets): This ratio indicates the amount of sales generated for every dollar's worth of assets. It is calculated by dividing sales by tota assets.

(g) Current Assets to Sales (Current Assets/Total Revenue): This ratio indicates the amount of current assets to produce one unit of revenue. The smaller ratio implies the more ef<sup>fi</sup>cient production because of fewer inventories.

(h) Free Cash Flow to Debt (Free Cash Flow/Total Debt): This ratio (%) compares a company's free cash <sup>fl</sup>ow to its total debt, which, for purposes of this ratio, is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the sum of short-term borrowings, the current portion of long-term debt and long-term debt. The ratio provides an indication of a company's ability to cover total debt with its yearly free cash <sup>fl</sup>ow.

(i) Free Cash Flow Interest Coverage: The ratio is measured by (Free Cash Flow/Interest Payments).

(j) Assets 3 Year Growth: The growth rate (%) is measured by (Total Assets/Total Assets in 3 Year Ago−1)×100.

(k) Sales 3 Year Growth: The growth rate (%) is measured by (Revenue/Revenue in 3 Year Ago 1)×100.

(l) BETA: The beta is an elasticity measure of the volatility, or systematic risk, of a security or a portfolio in comparison to the market as a whole. In other words, this is a measurement of the sensitivity of a company's stock price to the overall <sup>fl</sup>uctuation in the preselected market index. For example, a beta of 1.5 indicates that a company's stock price tends to rise (or fall) 1.5%, with a 1% rise (or fall) in the index value, The beta is a key parameter in the capital asset pricing model (CAPM).

(m) Dividend Payout Ratio (Dividends/Net Income): This ratio is the percentage of earnings paid to shareholders in dividends. The payout ratio provides an idea of how well earnings support the dividend payments. More mature companies tend to have a higher payout ratio.

## 4.2. Importance of Altman's Z score (Eq. (8))

The score provides an aggregated <sup>fi</sup>nancial measure for assessing the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of each <sup>fi</sup>rm. As discussed previously, we classify all <sup>fi</sup>rms into two groups: high P/E ratio group and low P/E ratio group. DEA–DA estimates weights of a separation function for classifying the two groups. The weight indicates the importance of each <sup>fi</sup>nancial variable. As formulated in Eq. (8), the $\bar { Z }$ score is a <sup>fi</sup>nancial measure that aggregates all <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables weighted by their importance. Hence, the higher Z score implies the better <sup>fi</sup>nancial performer in terms of the P/E ratio. Thus, this study can evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of all telecommunication <sup>fi</sup>rms. Moreover, corporate leaders can easily identify the position of his/her company among telecommunications <sup>fi</sup>rms in the world. The top executives do not pay attention to the Z score. However, they do care the position (rank) of their <sup>fi</sup>rms within the industry. No research provides a <sup>fi</sup>nancial method for ranking <sup>fi</sup>rms by aggregating <sup>fi</sup>nancial variables.

## 4.3. Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test

To examine the null hypothesis (a group distributes randomly among multiple periods), this study uses the Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test. The entire observed period is separated into T periods. To compute the Kruskal–Wallis statistic (H), this study combines all <sup>fi</sup>rms $( n = \sum _ { t = 1 } ^ { T } n _ { t } )$ in T periods where $n _ { t }$ stands for the number of observations at the t-th period. Then, we rank them from the greatest to the least by these Altman's Z scores. Let $R _ { j t }$ denote the rank of the j-th <sup>fi</sup>rm in the t-th period. The rank sum of all <sup>fi</sup>rms in the t-th period is $\begin{array} { r } { R _ { t } = \sum _ { j = 1 } ^ { n _ { t } } R _ { j t } } \end{array}$ . Then, the Kruskal–Wallis statistic (H) is determined by

$$
H = \frac {1 2}{n (n + 1)} \sum_ {t = 1} ^ {T} \frac {R _ {t} ^ {2}}{n _ {t}} - 3 (n + 1).\tag{11}
$$

The statistic (H) follows the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ distribution with a degree of freedom $( d f { = } T { - } 1 )$ ). See Hollander and Wolfe [15] for both a detailed description on the H statistic and a description regarding how to deal with many <sup>fi</sup>rms on a same rank.

To describe the use of the Z score more illustratively, this study pays attention to the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of AT&T and NTT as important examples. The two <sup>fi</sup>rms experienced their divestiture that provided a large impact to the telecommunications industry.

Fig. 1 visually describes an annual change of deviation regarding AT&T and NTT. It is clearly identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the <sup>fi</sup>gure that AT&T has outperformed NTT in term of the deviation of Altman's Z score. The two telecommunications companies decreased their <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance until 2001/2002 and increased it after 2001/2002.

To explore the empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding on the two telecommunications carriers further, this study discusses differences between their divestiture and current industrial structures. First, we return to the breakup of AT&T initiated by the <sup>fi</sup>ling in 1974 by the U.S. Department of Justice. AT&T was, at the time, a single common carrier in the United States. The case, United States vs. AT&T, led to a settlement <sup>fi</sup>nalized on January 8, 1982, under which “Ma Bell” agreed to divest its local exchange service operating companies, in return for a chance to go into the computer business, AT&T Computer Systems. Effective January 1, 1984, AT&T's local operations were split into seven independent Regional Holding Companies, also known as Regional Bell Operating Companies (RBOCs), or “Baby Bells”.

In 2005, SBC Communications purchased AT&T Corp., thus reuniting the venerable phone company with three of its spinoffs (SBC was composed of Southwestern Bell, Paci<sup>fi</sup>c Telesis, and Ameritech). The merger was completed on November 18, 2005.

The most important feature of the Bell system breakup is that the “Ma Bell” agreed to divest between local and toll telephone services for a chance to enter into IT business. The business aspect of AT&T is very different from the NTT divestiture in Japan.

Next, Fig. 3 visually describes the structure of NTT before and after its divestiture. After the breakup of NTT, the carrier consists of a holding company that controls NTT East (a local telephone company covering the east side of Japan), NTT West (a local telephone company covering the west side of Japan) and NTT Communications (a toll and international telephone company). The NTT group is referred to as NTT in this study. In addition to these companies in the NTT group, NTT DoCoMo, whose 63.4% equity share is owned by the NTT holding company as of March 2007, provides wireless communication services. NTT Data, whose 54.2% equity share is owned by the NTT holding company as of March 2007, provides IT-based system integration and communication services for large users.

An important feature of the NTT divestiture is that current NTT does not have the whole share of companies that provide wireless communications services and computer services. Furthermore, NTT cannot enter the IT business as AT&T does. Moreover, the approximately one third of the equity share of NTT is still owned by the Japanese government. Thus, NTT does not have business freedom as AT&T has and is still controlled by the Japanese government. Consequently, investors see more growth potential in AT&T than NTT in the global telecommunications market. ## 5. Conclusion and future extensions

Professor Cooper invented DEA that measured the operational performance of organizations in public and private sectors. Following the research direction suggested by Professor Cooper, this study investigated the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of the world telecommunica tions industry by DEA–DA. The DEA–DA is an extended model of DEA.

The proposed use of DEA–DA has a linkage with Altman's Z score that has long served as a methodological and conceptual basis in <sup>fi</sup>nance. Based upon the Z score of telecommunications companies, we ranked them for their <sup>fi</sup>nancial assessment. The information on corporate rank is very important for investors, corporate leaders and other individuals who are interested in the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of telecommunications <sup>fi</sup>rms. After evaluating their <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of the telecommunications companies, this study compared the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of AT&T with that of NTT after their divestiture. This study <sup>fi</sup>nds that AT&T outperformed NTT because AT&T changed itself to an IT company after the divestiture, but NTT dropped both IT and wireless communications services after its breakup. The empirical <sup>fi</sup>nding indicates that the success of telecommunications companies depends upon how effectively they make a business linkage between telecommunications and IT businesses (including wireless services).

As a future extension of this study, we need to apply the proposed methodology to investigate the corporate strategy of other industries such as electric power industry and other high-tech industries such as electric equipment industry and auto-industry. Furthermore, the proposed approach can be used to evaluate the <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance of IT products. Such are important future extensions of this study.

Finally, I hope that this study makes a contribution in the performance evaluation of the telecommunications industry.
