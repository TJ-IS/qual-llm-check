## a b s t r a c t

Capacity investment and capacity allocation have always been critical management decisions, especially in the presence of agency issue for capital-intensive and congestion-prone service organizations. Prior research has often modeled only one aspect of the issue, such as proposing internal pricing scheme for capacity allocation ignoring demand uncertainty and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the manager, optimizing capacity alone ignoring the agency issue, or incentive contract design ignoring capacity limit and service delay. We show that simply employing a traditional incentive contract (which often ignores service delays) for the manager responsible for promoting a center's services will provide incorrect incentives and lead to a more congested and less pro<sup>fi</sup>table system. When <sup>fi</sup>rms focus on optimizing operational capacity alone, ignoring the impact of managers on service demand, they are able to maintain the optimal utilization and service quality by balancing capacity and delay costs. However, they forgo pro<sup>fi</sup>t-increasing opportunities, as they ignore the impact of the optimal incentive contract and do not motivate the managers enough to boost demand. To tackle the management challenges faced by modern service centers, we take an integrated capacitycontracting approach by incorporating operational delays and capacity decisions within the incentive contract design. Embedding a queuing model in a general incentive contracting framework, we present a novel approach to deriving the optimal compensation contract and operational capacity for a service center. We illustrate in numerical examples that a Pareto improvement can be achieved with our integrated contracting approach because every party, from the <sup>fi</sup>rm to the manager, to customers, to equipment and software vendors, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

## 1. Introduction and overview

Effectively managing service systems, such as data centers, ecommerce sites, computerized diagnostic imaging centers, telecommunication networks, SaaS, and the like has always been a challenging task. First, they are often capital intensive and require large up-front investments in hardware, software, and labor. Second, they often operate under capacity constraints. As a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant portion of their costs are <sup>fi</sup>xed, high utilization is required for them to stay pro<sup>fi</sup>table. Moreover, limited capacity plus random variations in service time and stochastic arrivals of service demand can easily lead to queuing delays [12]. As a result, the owners and users of the centers incur delay-related costs, which may come in the form of lost sales, delayed decision making, increased operational costs, penalties, users' opportunity costs, etc.

For instance, typical costs for modern MRI or CT units range from one to three million dollars [7,12] and the costs for a new proton therapy center are usually above one hundred million dollars [23]. Managing such service centers is challenging, however, because <sup>fi</sup>rms not only need to maximize capacity utilization, but at the same time must maintain an acceptable service level—i.e., control user delays—in order to compete. Because consumer delays are very common in service industries, <sup>fi</sup>rms often commit to certain service levels, measured by the expected waiting time [1]. For example, a service standard commonly used in call centers is the percentage of calls answered within a certain period. Both <sup>fi</sup>rms and consumers can use this standard to evaluate a center's service quality. Consider a computerized diagnostic imaging service. The average wait for non-emergency patients for MRI scans is seven and a half weeks in the UK [21]. It is thirteen weeks for VIHA (Vancouver Island Health Authority) residents, although VIHA's longterm target is <sup>fi</sup>ve weeks [22], and twenty-one weeks at the St. Michael's Hospital in Toronto, although the provincial target for Ontario is four weeks [19]. Clearly, such long wait times for medical procedures and services are a major cause of public dissatisfaction. Thus, controlling customer delays is the top priority for many <sup>fi</sup>rms.

Prior researchers [5,6,9,11,17,18,29] have often modeled IT service centers as queuing systems with exogenous demand. Recognizing the impact of queuing delay costs, they mostly focus on capacity allocation within a <sup>fi</sup>rm through an internal pricing scheme. According to Mendelson [17], the proper transfer price should be equal to the externality component of the marginal delay cost, and a net-value maximizing <sup>fi</sup>rm should treat its IT service center as a cost center because of the delay-cost effect.

However, many centers that provide services to external clients experience random demand, which cannot be controlled by the internal transfer pricing scheme proposed in prior literature. Moreover, demand for these centers' services can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by market conditions as well as their managers' efforts. For example, managers are often contracted to run such facilities as pro<sup>fi</sup>t centers. As such, they are usually responsible for managing daily activities and for generating demand through additional marketing or service quality efforts.

The open research question that we want to address here is how to effectively manage service systems in the presence of delay costs when the service demand is unknown and can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by managers' efforts. Typically, <sup>fi</sup>rms can optimize capacity in order to control delay and provide an acceptable service level, and they can also offer incentive contracts to the managers running the centers in order to boost demand and revenue. We show that the traditional contracting approach often focuses on marketing alone (salesforce compensation) and ignores the impact of compensation on operational delay costs. As a result, <sup>fi</sup>rms often provide inaccurate incentives to their managers and therefore motivate them to invest too much in demand-generating efforts, resulting in more congested and less pro<sup>fi</sup>table service centers. When <sup>fi</sup>rms focus on optimizing operational delay and capacity decisions, they often ignore agency issues or contract design. One common scenario is that <sup>fi</sup>rms optimize capacity by balancing delay and capacity costs. The managers are hired to direct the centers' daily activities only and are not motivated to invest in additional managerial efforts to increase demand. Thus <sup>fi</sup>rms forgo pro<sup>fi</sup>t-generating opportunities.

To tackle the management challenges faced by service centers, we take an integrated approach by incorporating operational delay and capacity decisions in the design of an incentive contract. We show that when <sup>fi</sup>rms optimize their capacity and incentive contracts jointly, the managers are motivated to exert the correct effort to increase demand. Incorporating the impact of increased volume due to their managers' efforts and balancing the delay-cost effect, <sup>fi</sup>rms will install higher capacity to control service delays. Compared with the unilateral capacity optimization case, even though the capacity costs are higher and so are the compensations to the managers, <sup>fi</sup>rms in the end realize higher revenues and higher expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. We demonstrate the results in two numerical examples with the expected market base demand at 20 (low) and 30 (high), respectively. In particular, our proposed integrated capacity-contracting approach leads to a higher capacity installation (57.7% and 39.9% more, respectively) and a higher processing volume (66.7% and 45% more) than the unilateral capacity optimization approach. While the two approaches lead to similar capacity utilization because capacity is optimized in both cases, the average time in the system is shorter (22.5% and 17% shorter) and most impressively, the <sup>fi</sup>rm realizes a much higher expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t level (310% and 46% more) under our integrated contracting approach. Thus, the proposed integrated contracting approach leads to a more pro<sup>fi</sup>table system with improved service quality.

In addition, as illustrated in a numerical example, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's pro<sup>fi</sup>t is more sensitive to deviations from the optimal capacity than to deviations from the optimal contract for the same percentage-wide deviations from the optimal term. Furthermore, the impact of a deviation from the optimal capacity or contract is asymmetric, with under-investment in capacity having a greater negative impact on pro<sup>fi</sup>t than over-investment because of the congestion effect and overcompensation leading to a deeper pro<sup>fi</sup>t decrease. In the case of capacity deviation, this asymmetry becomes more pronounced with an increase in delay costs.

We believe that our study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to address the issue of incentive contract design within a service environment. We unite a <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision regarding the optimal incentive contract with its decision regarding operational delays and capacity. Our work extends existing economics literature on compensation contract problems to a new setting and contributes to current research on incentive-contract design and service resource management. The analysis sheds light on how to balance agency, capacity, and delay costs when managing capital-intensive service facilities.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We review the relevant literature in Section 2. In Section 3, we present our basic model, in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm considers the impact of operational delays when designing the optimal contract at a given capacity level. We also compare our optimal contract—integrating delays—with an alternative contract—the traditional incentive contract that ignores delays. In Section 4, we extend the basic model to the situation in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm jointly determines the optimal capacity and the optimal incentive contract. We conduct comparative statics analysis and demonstrate the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of integrating contracting and operational decisions with some numerical examples in Section 5 and conclude the paper in Section 6. The appendix includes the proofs.

## 2. Literature review

Traditional incentive contracting (for example, [3,13,14,16]) focuses on motivating the manager and managing risk-sharing and often ignores operations-related decisions such as capacity. A common approach is to examine the optimal incentive contract between a riskneutral principal and a risk-averse agent. A number of empirical studies have attempted to validate theoretical predictions regarding the structure of an optimal compensation plan; see [8,10,15], among others. Misra et al. [20] offer new insights into the compensation design process by modeling the case of a risk-averse principal and a risk-averse agent. These studies assume that the principal faces no capacity limit and incurs no delay-related costs. For many service and manufacturing facilities, however, delay costs do exist due to capacity limits, demand <sup>fl</sup>uctuations, random customer (or order) arrivals, and service-time (or production-time) variations. For example, Banker et al. [2] study the relevant costs of capacity in a stochastic manufacturing environment. Using an M/G/1 queuing model, they evaluate and estimate the expected delay costs, speci<sup>fi</sup>cally WIP inventory costs, which are shown to be enormous. Thus, simply applying a traditional incentive contract to modern service and manufacturing centers will provide incorrect incentives to the manager.

While recognizing the impact of delay costs, studies that focus on computing-resource management issues mostly target capacity allocation with regard to <sup>fi</sup>rms' internal IT resources and propose an optimal internal transfer pricing scheme [9,11,17,18]. Cheng [5,6] extends these papers by examining the impact of computer breakdown on <sup>fi</sup>rms' pricing and capacity decision. However, they all assume exogenous demand and mostly apply to the allocation of internal resources. In addition, they ignore the agency issues which are quite common in organizations. An exception is [28] which studies internal IS department resource management with agency issues. In particular, the incentive con<sup>fl</sup>icts between IS department and central management since the IS manager intends to maximize her department's “slack”, either in terms of seeking excess allocated resources, “professional syndrome”, or consuming perquisites [30]. Wang and Barron [28] focus on the case in which IS manager has specialized IS knowledge, for example, the true costs of operating, that may not be fully transmitted to central management due to limited communication. While they extend the traditional principal–agent literature to model mechanism design in the context of limited communication between the agent and principal, same as [5,6,9,11,17,18], they ignore the fact that managers can in<sup>fl</sup>uence demand by investing in demand-generating effort, and thus their results are unlikely to be relevant for <sup>fi</sup>rms that face uncertain external demand. For example, outsourcing service contracts is usually negotiated in advance of service. The service providers then have to deal with uncertain demand while satisfying the service agreements at the contracted prices.

In another related line of research (see [1,4,24,25]), <sup>fi</sup>rms jointly evaluate their marketing decisions (such as advertising, price, or delivery time guarantees), which affect market demand, and their operational decisions (capacity), which affect service levels, to maximize pro<sup>fi</sup>t. Tarakci et al. [26] apply a two-tier call center queuing model to provide a decision framework on managing the adoption and implementation of telemedicine. In their model, the patient arrival rate depends on the technology level established. The main focus is to determine the optimal level of investment in telemedicine technology and staf<sup>fi</sup>ng policy, considering staf<sup>fi</sup>ng, technology, incorrect treatment, and waiting costs. While recognizing the importance of integrating (and coordinating) marketing and operations decisions, they do not consider the impact of agency issues.

Overall, previous studies focus on internal IT resource management with <sup>fi</sup>xed demand and no agency issues; on labor incentives, assuming unlimited capacity/supply; or on marketing and operational delay/capacity, ignoring agency issues. None has looked at optimizing operational delay/capacity in the presence of agency issues for a service environment. Because of the unique characteristics of modern service facilities, to effectively tackle the service center contracting and resource management challenge, we take an integrated capacitycontracting approach by incorporating operational delays and capacity decisions within the incentive contract framework.

## 3. The model

Consider a data center, which could be a service provider that provides data services to other <sup>fi</sup>rms. Its manager is responsible for directing the center's daily activities and marketing its data services to clients. The owner of the center, i.e., the <sup>fi</sup>rm, is responsible for investing in capacity as well as monitoring and compensating the manager. We model the data center as a queuing system where the arrivals of data service requests follow a random process. Fig. 1 describes the service system of the data center. Clearly, this <sup>fi</sup>gure can also be used to describe many service systems in general. Here, the capacity of the system is <sup>fi</sup>xed and is determined by the <sup>fi</sup>rm's investment in software, hardware, and IT personnel. The demand of the system, however, is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by the manager's effort. At a given capacity level, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem is to determine the optimal incentive contract for the manager well before observing the volume of data service requests to be processed at the center. We assume, for now, that the center's capacity is predetermined. In Section 4, we study the case in which the <sup>fi</sup>rm endogenously determines the optimal capacity and incentive contract.

## 3.1. Model setting

Let N, a random variable, be the demand (or the number of service requests). The arrivals of service requests follow a stochastic process with a rate of λ requests per unit time. Suppose the data center's capacity investment decision has been made such that the center has a capacity level $\mu ,$ measured by the expected number of service requests that can be processed per unit time. Both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the manager can observe θ, which is the expected base demand without the manager's demand-generating input. The level of the expected base demand θ is affected by factors such as pricing, general advertising, and other decisions made by the <sup>fi</sup>rm. When the manager invests in service or marketing effort $\alpha , \alpha \in \mathsf { A } \subseteq \mathsf { R } ^ { + }$ , the expected demand will increase by ϕ(α), with $\phi ^ { \prime } ( \alpha ) { > } 0$ and $\phi ^ { \prime \prime } ( \alpha ) { \leq } 0$ . That is, the expected demand increases with the manager's managerial effort, but at a decreasing rate. Given the expected base demand θ and the manager's effort $\alpha ,$ the expected demand λ is characterized by $\lambda { = } \theta + \phi ( \alpha )$ . We assume that both the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the manager know the effort response function $\phi ( \alpha )$ as well as the demand distribution function, and both parties can observe the realized demand (N). However, the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot observe and has no means of verifying the exact level of the manager's effort, which is known only to the manager.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm would like the manager to invest in effort to maximize the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net bene<sup>fi</sup>t. The manager's interest differs because given the same compensation he always prefers to exert less effort. If the manager's effort level was observable and could be veri<sup>fi</sup>ed, then the <sup>fi</sup>rm could offer an incentive plan that only compensated the manager when he exerted effort that maximized the <sup>fi</sup>rm's net bene<sup>fi</sup>t. However, since the manager's effort level is NOT observable and the realized demand N is a random variable, the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot infer the value of α even after observing the expected base demand θ and the realized value of N. It therefore cannot effectively force the manager to behave in its best interest. Arguably, for a given market condition the <sup>fi</sup>rm may form a close estimate of α after observing N for many periods. But ex ante the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot verify the manager's effort level after observing a realized demand $N ,$ and ex post it cannot tell whether the manager exerted the estimated effort in a subsequent period. Additionally, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's estimation of α most likely will not be perfect. Thus it cannot effectively enforce a contract based on the estimation of α to induce the manager to exert the same effort level it can when the effort is observable. Because the effort level is not observable and the <sup>fi</sup>rm cannot effectively prevent the manager from shirking, and the solution is to design a compensation contract based on observable variables, such as the realized number of service requests, to encourage the manager to put forth a desired level of effort.

Following general agency theory assumptions [3,13,27], the <sup>fi</sup>rm is risk-neutral, and the manager is both risk- and effort-averse. The manager realizes utility $U ( s )$ from compensation $s ( \cdot )$ , and this utility function is concave and increasing in s $( U ^ { \prime } ( s ) { > } 0$ and $U ^ { \prime \prime } ( s ) { < } 0 ) ;$ i.e., the manager has diminishing marginal utility for income. At an effort level α, the manager incurs disutility $V ( \alpha )$ , which is convex and increasing in effort α $( V ^ { \prime } ( \alpha ) { > } 0 ,$ , and $V ^ { \prime \prime } ( \alpha ) { > } 0 ) ;$ i.e., the manager's marginal disutility from effort increases with effort. Lastly, the manager's net utility function for compensation and effort is additively separable and can be expressed as $U ( s ) - V ( \alpha )$

Because of demand uncertainty and the stochastic arrival of service requests, given a <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity level $\mu ,$ there will be a service queue at the data center. For a service request, let W be the expected time in the system, which includes waiting time in queue and service time (see Fig. 1). Let τ be the <sup>fi</sup>rm's per request, per unit time delay cost, which represents the cost of a delayed data service, the clients' opportunity cost, etc. Because waiting time and waiting costs directly affect clients' satisfaction, the center's operating costs, its perceived service quality, and therefore its reputation and future demand, τW times the expected number of requests in the system captures the <sup>fi</sup>rm's total expected delay costs.

The <sup>fi</sup>rm will decide the optimal compensation contract $s ( N )$ to maximize its expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t, which is given by revenue minus delay costs and compensation to the manager described in Eq. (1). The <sup>fi</sup>rst term in Eq. The third term is the expected payment to the manager. At a given capacity level, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will decide the optimal compensation contract by balancing delay costs and agency costs.

Here we model the <sup>fi</sup>rm's decision in the steady state because a client (in contract), in the short run, will tend to ignore the waiting time when sending a service request. In the long run, however, excessive waiting times will reduce the reputation of the data center and limit its ability to accommodate a demand surge. Excessive delay will also lead to additional costs for the <sup>fi</sup>rm, such as penalty costs, lost sales, increased service costs, and the costs incurred in dealing with unhappy clients and controlling the effect of negative word of mouth. The <sup>fi</sup>rm thus needs to endogenize the negative impact of the waiting time externality [17]. The cost effect of a long wait time and the negative externality created by a request joining the queue are modeled as delay-related costs incurred by the <sup>fi</sup>rm in our model. The firm thus endogenously determines the optimal expected demand through incentive contract design and taking into consideration the impact of service delays.

Next, consider the constraints. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is the manager's individual rationality (IR) constraint. The manager will accept the compensation contract s(N) only if his expected net bene<sup>fi</sup>t, given by the difference between his expected utility from compensation ${ \cal E } _ { N } [ U ( s ( N ) ) ]$ and his disutility of effort $V ( \alpha )$ , is at least as much as M, his gain from an outside alternative. If the <sup>fi</sup>rm chooses to compensate the manager s such that $U ( s ) = M ,$ then it is only paying the manager for managing the daily operations of the center, without seeking any demandgenerating effort. The second constraint is the manager's incentive compatibility (IC) constraint. That is, given a compensation contract s (N), the manager will choose an optimal effort α to maximize his expected net bene<sup>fi</sup>t. Here $p , \tau ,$ , and M are exogenous in this model, and α belongs to the set of the manager's optimal effort choices.

We model the operation at the facility as an M/M/1 queue. That is, the request arrivals are independent and follow a Poisson process with rate $\lambda { = } \theta + \phi ( \alpha )$ , and the service time has an exponential distribution with service rate μ. We choose the M/M/1 queuing model for its simplicity and analytic tractability. Here the capacity constraint $\mu { > } \lambda$ will always be satis<sup>fi</sup>ed because for an M/M/1 queue in steady state the expected time in the system is given by $W = { 1 } / { \vphantom { 1 } } / { \mu } { - \theta } { - \phi } ( \alpha ) ) ,$ which will explode as λ approaches $\mu$ <sup>ð Þð Þ</sup>Thus when making the incentive contract decision, the <sup>fi</sup>rm must factor in the impact of service delays. Our model differs from the normal moral hazard setting in that we include an operational delay cost term in the model. Since we integrate both contracting (marketing) and operations (service quality) decisions, we refer to our model as an integrated contract design model, and our optimal contract as the integrated optimal contract.

## 3.3. Alternative contract

Traditional contracting models ([3,13], among others) often ignore operational delay costs. They usually assume an unlimited capacity/ supply and do not consider delay-related costs when designing the optimal compensation contract. In such cases, the <sup>fi</sup>rm's problem is similar to Problem (1) but without the delay cost term in the objective function. Applying traditional contract design to service facilities will result in the wrong incentives for the manager, however, since the <sup>fi</sup>rm ignores delay costs.

Proposition 2. A traditional optimal contract that ignores operational delay costs will induce more marketing effort from the manager than our integrated optimal contract. In particular, the traditional incentive contract offers a lower salary parameter and a higher commission rate parameter, and as a result the firm realizes a lower profit due to the increasing delay costs.

As indicated in the proof, the traditional optimal contract and our integrated optimal contract have the same form. Proposition 2 shows that because traditional optimal contract design ignores the impact of delays, a <sup>fi</sup>rm using such a contract would like the manager to invest more in demand-generating effort. To do so, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will offer a higher commission rate parameter and a lower salary parameter.

When the agency cost is not too expensive, a contract ignoring delay costs will often lead to a fully utilized system, i.e., $\rho = 1$ . When the manager's effort becomes costly, however, the utilization rate will be less than 1, even though the <sup>fi</sup>rm does not recognize delay costs when designing the compensation contract. In many service and manufacturing facilities, capacity levels are usually <sup>fi</sup>xed, and the facilities do incur delay-related costs. Thus, a high capacity utilization rate often means extensive delays and high delay costs. By ignoring delay costs, traditional contract design will lead to a less pro<sup>fi</sup>table and more congested system because the manager is over-compensated and will over-invest in demand-generating effort.

## 5. Comparative statics and numerical examples

In this section, we <sup>fi</sup>rst study how the optimal contract and effort level change for different states of expected base demand at a given capacity level. In particular, we demonstrate the advantage of the integrated contracting approach considering operational delays vs. the traditional contracting approach and the advantage of further integrating the contract design with the operational capacity decision vs. a few unilateral optimization approaches.

## 5.1. Comparative statics of contracting at a given capacity level

Proposition 4 compares the optimal effort level, expected demand, incentive contract, and expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t, respectively, for different states of base demand.

Proposition 4. At a given capacity level $\mu$ and under the optimal solution for contract and effort, for any two states of expected base demand $\theta _ { l }$ and $\theta _ { h } ,$ with $\theta _ { h } { > } \theta _ { l } \colon ( i )$ the firm would like the manager to invest less in effort when the expected base demand is high; i.e., α b α ; (ii) the expected demand for $\theta _ { h }$ is higher than that for $\theta _ { l } ; \mathrm { i } . \mathbf { e } . , \lambda _ { h } { > } \lambda _ { l } ;$ (iii) the firm will offer a higher commission rate parameter in the optimal incentive contract and have a higher expected payment to the manager when the expected base demand is $\theta _ { l } ;$ and (iv) the firm has a higher expected profit level when the base demand is $\theta _ { h } .$

Consider Eq. (2). The LHS is the marginal bene<sup>fi</sup>t of effort, which is the difference between the marginal increase in revenue and the marginal increase in delay costs. The RHS represents the marginal cost of effort, which is related to the marginal change in compensation and the manager's utility-maximizing term. At the optimal effort level, the marginal bene<sup>fi</sup>t equals the marginal cost. Proposition 4(i) shows that because the <sup>fi</sup>rm faces a capacity constraint and incurs delay costs when the base demand is high, the <sup>fi</sup>rm would like the manager to invest less in effort so that the system does not become overcongested. On the other hand, the <sup>fi</sup>rm would like the manager to work harder to improve capacity utilization when the base demand is low.

Because the manager's effort is costly, Proposition 4(ii) states that even though the <sup>fi</sup>rm induces a higher level of marketing effort from the manager when the expected base demand is low, the difference in expected demand attributable to the manager's effort is less than the difference attributable to market conditions; i.e., $\theta _ { h } - \theta _ { l } { > } k ( \alpha _ { l } - \alpha _ { h } )$ When the base demand is $\theta _ { h } ,$ the expected demand is higher, a service request's expected time in the system is longer, and the <sup>fi</sup>rm incurs a higher expected delay cost. On the other hand, when the base demand is $\theta _ { h } ,$ the <sup>fi</sup>rm enjoys a higher expected revenue level and a higher expected capacity utilization rate.

Recall that we refer to the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient term $h _ { i } \phi ^ { \prime } ( \alpha _ { i } ) / \lambda _ { i }$ of the optimal contract as the commission rate parameter and the constant term $g _ { i } - h _ { i } \phi ^ { \prime } ( \alpha _ { i } )$ as the salary parameter $( i = l , ~ h )$ . Although the manager's effort is not directly observable, the <sup>fi</sup>rm induces a desired level of effort through a contract adjustment. Proposition 4(iii) shows that when the expected base demand is low, the <sup>fi</sup>rm elicits greater effort from the manager to increase capacity utilization by offering a higher commission rate parameter and higher expected payment.

Because a higher expected base demand increases both the mean and the variance of the demand, the risk-averse manager will demand a higher payment for the same level of effort. As the manager's effort becomes more costly, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will demand less effort. In our model, a higher expected base demand increases not only the mean and the variance of the demand, but also the expected delay costs for a given level of capacity and effort. We can see from Eq. (2) that when the base demand is high, the increased delay costs also lead the <sup>fi</sup>rm to induce less effort from the manager. To do so, the <sup>fi</sup>rm will adjust the contract terms. Because the commission rate parameter is increasing in effort, the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a lower commission rate parameter and provides less incentive to the manager when the base demand is high. As for the salary parameter, it could be higher or lower than it would be in a market with low base demand.

Proposition 4(iv) shows that despite incurring higher delay costs when the base demand is high, the <sup>fi</sup>rm has a greater expected pro<sup>fi</sup>t in the end due to higher expected revenue and a lower expected payment to the manager. To summarize, although we do not have a closed-form solution on the optimal effort, we are able to derive the effect of base demand on the commission rate parameter in the contract and the expected compensation as well as on the <sup>fi</sup>rm's expected demand and pro<sup>fi</sup>t level.

## 6. Conclusions

Modern service systems are both capital and labor intensive. Signi<sup>fi</sup>cant up-front capital investment is required, and this investment affects not only a service center's future revenue but also its service level, which could in turn affect its pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. As a result, owners of such a center must focus not only on maximizing capacity utilization by effectively motivating the managers running the centers, but also on controlling delay costs and on making correct capacity investment decision.

In a common contract design model, since the manager's exact level of demand-generating effort is not directly observable the <sup>fi</sup>rm offers a performance-dependent contract that rewards the manager based on the realized demand. In many cases, <sup>fi</sup>rms have to make capacity decisions before observing the demand for their services. To make their jobs more challenging, demand may <sup>fl</sup>uctuate substantially, service times are often random, and delays are quite common due to capacity limits. The traditional approach to contracting ignores the impact of the manager's contract on delays, and unilateral operations capacity optimization ignores the manager's marketing. Thus, both approaches lead to suboptimal outcomes.

In contrast, our integrated approach—contract design incorporating operational queuing delays and capacity decisions—balances capacity, delay, and agency costs and provides the correct incentive to the manager, who then invests in the proper level of demandgenerating effort for the market condition. Thus, the service quality of the center is improved and its pro<sup>fi</sup>tability increased. As illustrated in the examples, a Pareto improvement is achieved with our integrated contracting approach because every party, from the <sup>fi</sup>rm to the manager, to clients, to equipment and software vendors, bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Moreover, our comparative analysis and numerical examples reveal the sensitivity of the center's pro<sup>fi</sup>t to both the contract and capacity.

Prior research on computing-resource management mostly has focused on controlling service demand through a proper transfer price to effectively allocate limited and costly IT resources. Our study extends that approach by allowing managers to in<sup>fl</sup>uence demand through investing in demand-generating effort. Our model applies to a <sup>fi</sup>rm's internal IT service center when the manager promotes the service to internal user groups, and it also applies to a third-party managed service center ful<sup>fi</sup>lling contractual agreements with outside clients. In the latter case, prior models do not apply directly. In addition, our study is not restricted to data centers and can be applied to similar IT services, Web hosting, radiation oncology centers, and even environments such as manufacturing centers, which incur workin-process inventory costs. Overall, our model generally applies to a <sup>fi</sup>rm that faces demand uncertainty, has a <sup>fi</sup>xed capacity, incurs delay costs or degradation of service, and has to deal with agency issues.

One natural extension of our work is to consider a manager with several responsibilities. When a center's manager also invests in operational effort to reduce service times, the facility can process more requests without further investment in capacity. How the <sup>fi</sup>rm should compensate such a multi-tasking manager in an environment with constrained capacity is an interesting research question. One could also introduce information asymmetry into the model by allowing the manager to possess private information about the state of base demand.

Our work shares the common limitations of agency-theory research. As [27] points out, “[G]eneral results for the moral-hazard problem are rare….Getting more speci<sup>fi</sup>c results requires stronger assumptions on the distribution and the utility functions.” Many speci<sup>fi</sup>c assumptions have to be made (see Section 3.2.1) in order to derive our results. Nevertheless, our analysis extends current research on service resource management and incentive-contract design and sheds light on the complex interplay of agency, capacity, and delay costs for a capital-intensive service facility. Our results clearly demonstrate the best way for service facilities to deal with market uncertainty more effectively, improve capacity utilization, and enhance expected pro<sup>fi</sup>ts. While the integrated capacity and contracting approach always leads to the highest pro<sup>fi</sup>t and improved service quality, sometimes <sup>fi</sup>rms may be constrained to either capacity or contract optimization. We show in numerical examples that when the base demand deviates from a <sup>fi</sup>rm's original expectation and the <sup>fi</sup>rm is limited to either capacity or contract adjustment due to administrational or operational constraints the latter adjustment produces a higher pro<sup>fi</sup>t level. When capacity or contract deviates from the optimal level, over-investment in capacity reduces pro<sup>fi</sup>t less than under-investment and overcompensation through a higher commission rate parameter in the contract reduces pro<sup>fi</sup>t more than under-compensation with a lower commission rate parameter. These insights may help <sup>fi</sup>rms to better deal with various complex situations involved in managing agency issue and operational capacity and delays for capital-intensive and congestion-prone service facilities.
