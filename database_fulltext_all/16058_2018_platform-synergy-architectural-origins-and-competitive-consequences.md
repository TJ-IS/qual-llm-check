# Platform Synergy: Architectural Origins and Competitive Consequences

Amrit Tiwana<sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup> University of Georgia, Athens, Georgia 30602

Contact: tiwana@uga.edu, http://orcid.org/0000-0003-1750-2528 (AT)

Abstract. The internal architecture of apps has yet to receive attention in platform studies, which predominantly study apps’ platform-facing, external architecture. We develop a theory of how the interplay between an app’s internal and external architecture shape its ability to leverage the platform to outcompete rival apps. Primary and archival data spanning four years from 618 BlackberryOS apps show how internal architecture’s complementarity with external architecture influences an app’s competitive lead.

History: Saby Mitra, Senior Editor; Sean Xu, Associate Editor.

Keywords: near decomposability • architecture • platform synergy • modularity • monolithic • apps • innovation race

## 1. Introduction

The platforms literature—historically focused on why one platform (e.g., iOS) outperforms rival platforms (e.g., Blackberry)—has just begun asking why one app outperforms rival apps. The few such studies examine app pricing (Lin et al. 2011), governance (Wareham et al. 2014), screening (Tiwana 2015), and their architecture (McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017). Together, they portray competition among apps as a cutthroat innovation race (Lerner 1997, Lin et al. 2011, Doraszelski 2003). For example, while the top 0.001% iOS apps hog most revenues, most never break even (Canalys 2015).

Prior studies—focused solely on the architecture of app <sup>↔</sup> platform dependencies—have not yet paid attention to the internal structure of dependencies within an app. Even though the innards of apps—their internal architecture—can be vastly heterogeneous (Baldwin 2008, Subramanyam et al. 2012), it remains completely unrecognized in the platforms literature (McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017). Put diferently, they have studied an app’s “external” but not “internal” architecture.

This gap is theoretically important for two reasons. First, architecture is an information technology (IT) artifact’s virtually irreversible DNA that imprints its evolution (Agarwal and Tiwana 2015). Second, firms create value by synthesizing their internal resources with external resources into a market ofering (Sirmon et al. 2007). In platform markets, app developers synthesize into an app their own proprietary code with platform resources also available to rival app developers (The Economist 2014). Therefore, how well an app synthesizes its own code with platform resources influences how well it competitively fares. Three well-known systems anecdotally illustrate how their understudied internal architecture plausibly shapes this.

• Facebook is a single 20-million line application.

• Google’s Search has a tight-knit internal structure with 2 billion lines of code spanning 85 terabytes (Metz 2015).

• BlackberryOS’ snappy performance, security, and unrivaled battery life was attributed to its highly “monolithic” internal architecture (McNish and Silcof 2016, p. 172).

Facebook’s, Google’s, and Blackberry’s vast collections of modularly connected third-party extensions belie how monolithic their internal architecture is. Focusing exclusively on apps’ external architecture masks diferences in their internal architecture. This study addresses the question of how the interplay of an app’s internal architecture with its external architecture influences its performance in intra-platform competition.

Our theorizing uses two building blocks. First, we conceptualize as our model’s conceptual glue, platform synergy—the degree to which an app leverages an evolving platform’s capabilities. Second, we resurrect Simon’s (1962) notion of architectural near-decomposability to conceptualize an app’s architecture as two-sided: its platform-facing external architecture and its internal architecture. We develop the idea that the more modular an app’s external architecture and monolithic its internal architecture, the more it competitively outperforms rival apps. (The former is the degree to which an app is loosely coupled to a platform through platform-specific interfaces; the latter is tightness of coupling among an app’s internal elements.)

Using primary and archival data spanning four years from 618 apps in the Blackberry ecosystem, we show that combining modularity in an app’s external architecture with monolithicity in its internal architecture propels it ahead of rival apps because it enhances its platform synergy. Our original contribution is a platform synergy-centric explanation for how such neardecomposability in an app’s architecture spawns a competitive lead. To the platforms literature, we contribute novel insights linking the architectural origins of platform synergy to its competitive consequences. To the modularity literature, where apps’ internal architecture is overlooked, we add new insights into how it interacts with their external architecture to shape competitive performance. Subsequent sections develop the hypotheses, methodology, and analyses and discuss the contributions and implications.

## 2. Theory Development

Figure 1 previews our research model. It develops an explanation for how near-decomposability in an app’s architecture—by enabling an app to better leverage the focal platform—competitively propels it ahead. We conceptualize an app’s near-decomposability as simultaneous modularity in its external architecture and monolithicity in its internal architecture. Our unit of analysis is an app. (Construct definitions appear in Table 1.)

We first describe the notion of architecture in our context, then introduce our theoretical foundation, and then conceptualize platform synergy, which both drives an app’s competitive lead over rival apps and is shaped by an app’s architecture.

## 2.1. Context: App Architecture in Software Platforms

The architecture of any system refers to the structure of interactions among subsystems that constitute it (Simon 1962, Ulrich 1995). Broadly, it is an artifact’s design structure that describes what depends on what (Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 21). Any system can consist of many interacting smaller systems, which, in turn, might be composed of their own constituent systems and so on. This nested parts-within-parts structure is a system’s design hierarchy (Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 75; Bresnahan and Greenstein 2014; Clark 1985).<sup>1</sup> The focal “system” in question depends on the vantage point—the conceptual zoom-level—chosen in this hierarchy (Agarwal and Tiwana 2015).

Figure 2 illustrates this idea in our context of a software platform’s ecosystem, defined as a platform and its collection of apps (Bresnahan and Greenstein 2014). A platform refers to an extensible technological foundation (e.g., BlackberryOS or iOS) used by complementary, functionality-augmenting apps (McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017, Tiwana 2015).

At the top of the hierarchy in Figure 2 is the entire platform ecosystem, such as Blackberry’s ecosystem. All studies of inter-platform competition use this vantage point (<sup></sup> in Figure 2), consistent with an ecosystem as their unit of analysis (e.g., Cennamo and Santalo 2013). As we traverse downward in Figure 2, we zoom in to the level of individual apps (<sup></sup> in Figure 2). Architecture at this level focuses on an app’s relationship with the platform. Recent studies on intra-platform competition use this vantage point, consistent with an app as their unit of analysis (e.g., Huang et al. 2013; Tiwana 2015). Although an app is a lower-level subsystem of the higher-level platform, an app is the toplevel system of its own hierarchy. Zooming one level further down (<sup></sup>) reveals the structure of interdependencies within an individual app. No previous studies have zoomed in to this level. Baldwin and Clark (2000, p. 21) call this level the artifact’s microstructure. The small body of prior research on intra-platform competition has focused on the architecture of an app’s relationship to a platform but has yet to explore the architecture within an app. Put diferently, it has explored apps’ external architecture (level <sup></sup>) but not their internal architecture (level <sup></sup>).

## 2.2. Theoretical Foundation:

## Near-Decomposability of Architecture

Our theoretical foundation is Simon’s (1962, p. 197) notion of architectural near-decomposability—a twopronged idea about independence among and interdependence within systems. The crux of the idea is that the greater the degree to which any system comprises smaller systems that are internally interdependent but relatively independent from each other, the better it will adapt to its environment. This theoretical perspective was developed for generic systems (technological, social, and biological) and predates platform ecosystems by 50 years. Therefore, we must first translate these ideas to our context.

Our focal system—the vantage point <sup></sup> in Figure 2— is an app. An app’s boundary—shown as the double line in the inset in Figure 2—demarcates its exterior from its interior (Baldwin 2008, p. 187). This boundary is both the separation and junction of an app (Smith 1981, p. 369). The structure of the app’s interdependencies with the platform at this boundary is an app’s external architecture. The structure of interdependencies inside this boundary is an app’s internal architecture.

An app’s architectural near-decomposability is then the degree to which an app’s external architecture has weak interdependencies vis-à-vis the platform and its internal architecture has strong interdependencies. A nearly decomposable app architecture is therefore characterized by weak app <sup>↔</sup> platform interactions and strong within-app interactions.<sup>2</sup>

Diferent apps for the same platform can vary in their degree of near-decomposability because of heterogeneity in their external and internal architectures. We expect this for four theoretical reasons. First, the platform with which an app must interoperate constrains its external architecture, yet an app’s developer can mix and match potentially thousands of APIs and platform services to implement an app (Tiwana 2015). Equifinal realization of the same functionality in numerous possible ways can result in diferences in an app’s external architecture. Second, an app’s external architecture can vary depending on when an app was first created. New functionality is typically introduced into software platforms as platform services accessible to apps through new platform APIs (The Economist 2014). Introduction of new APIs and platform services alters the pool available to app developers at diferent points in time. For example, iOS’ API counts have grown by 1,400 per year since its introduction in 2008, exceeding 14,000 by 2017. The possible app <sup>↔</sup> platform relationships are therefore immense and not deterministic. Third, app developers may use platform-agnostic software-development tools (e.g., Cordova, Sencha, and Phonegap) that allow them to port their app code to multiple competing platforms (e.g., iOS, Android, and Blackberry). This writeonce-run-everywhere approach necessitates genericenough code to work with diferent platforms, further increasing heterogeneity in apps’ architectures. Finally, an app’s designer alone chooses whether and how to internally partition an app into smaller subsystems (Clark 1985, p. 237; Subramanyam et al. 2012, p. 792). Since an app—like any technological system—can be partitioned by its designer in many diferent ways, its internal architecture can be considerably heterogeneous as well (Baldwin 2008, p. 188). Apps for the same platform can therefore exhibit diferent degrees of near-decomposability in their architecture.

## 2.3. Competitive Lead Through Platform Synergy

We define an app’s competitive lead as the extent to which an app competitively outperforms its cohort of rival apps. Our conceptualization builds on the idea that market performance is best understood relative to a comparable cohort of coevolving rivals (Barnett 2008, p. 55). Simon (2005) similarly emphasized that to flourish, a technological artifact (here, an app) must outdo others with which it competes. We focus on market— as opposed to technical—performance because the market mediates competitive rivalries (Barnett 2008, p. 135). Competitive lead therefore represents an app’s comparative performance in a competitive race with other specialized apps in its niche.

A central explanatory pathway in our model is platform synergy, which we define as the degree to which an app’s implementation leverages a platform’s evolving capabilities. This property of an app implies that it derives a greater proportion of its own functionality by assimilating the focal platform’s distinctive, evolving capabilities. Its opposite is genericness, with which an app works on many platforms but is not fine-tuned to leverage a particular platform.

Our conceptualization builds on Schilling’s (2000) notion of synergistic specificity, which she defined as the degree to which a system achieves greater functionality by being fine-tuned specifically to another. She developed it as a general systems theory concept, not specific to software or platforms. This concept is entirely absent in both the subsequent theoretical and empirical literature.<sup>3</sup> Our conceptualization of platform synergy difers from Schilling in one way to make it applicable to software apps. It is an app’s unilateral synergy with one platform, unlike Schilling’s industrylevel conceptualization with which the synergistic artifacts were undefined. (Apps depend on the platform, but the platform rarely depends on an app.)

2.3.1. Illustrations of an App’s Platform Synergy. Consider two examples that contrast apps with higher and lower levels of platform synergy. The iOS version of Twitter builds on iOS’ unique APIs to achieve voice recognition, fingerprint recognition, push notifications, and location awareness. It therefore has higher synergy with the iOS platform. By contrast, Google Voice, which uses platform-agnostic HTML5 rather than deep integration with iOS APIs, exhibits lower synergy with the iOS platform, but it invokes rival platform Android’s APIs to exhibit higher synergy with AndroidOS. App developers’ choices in how they implement app features can also result in diferent levels of platform synergy. For example, an app can realize lower versus higher platform synergy, respectively, using keyboard input versus voice recognition APIs; HTML5/Flash interfaces versus platform-specific notification service APIs; passwords versus fingerprinting APIs; and local file storage versus iCloud storage APIs. The more an app’s own implementation exploits a particular platform’s idiosyncratic capabilities, the more synergistic it is to that platform.

Greater platform synergy matters competitively for an app for two interrelated reasons. First, firms create value by synthesizing a firm’s own internal and external resources into a market ofering (Sirmon et al. 2007). Here, internal resources are the app developer’s proprietary code, and external resources are the platform capabilities and services available to app developers through a platform’s APIs. App developers produce apps by combining their own code with platform resources, such as APIs, platform services, and cloudcomputing infrastructure (The Economist 2014). A competitive lead depends on an app developer’s ability to build on its distinctive strengths while simultaneously capitalizing on opportunities that arise as the focal platform’s capabilities evolve. Every platform advance that an app exploits to enrich the functionality delivered to its end users can potentially produce a temporary competitive advantage over rival apps.

Second, greater platform synergy conserves the effort an app’s developer expends implementing generic functionality from scratch, which can instead be sourced as platform services (e.g., notifications, voice recognition, fingerprinting, and cloud storage in mobile platforms). This specializes division of labor, with the platform contributing to the app undiferentiating functionality that it shares in common with other apps and the app developer contributing functionality unique to the app. This allows an app’s developer to devote more attention to refining an app’s unique functionality, which is what diferentiates it from rival apps. We therefore expect an app’s platform synergy to enhance its competitive lead.

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Greater platform synergy enhances an app’s competitive lead.

## 2.4. External App Architecture

An app’s external architecture describes the “independence among” part of its architectural near-decomposability, that is, an app’s relationship with the focal platform (zoom level <sup></sup> in Figure 2). Such app <sup>↔</sup> platform independence is the modularity of an app’s external architecture, defined as the degree to which an app is loosely coupled to the platform through platformspecific interfaces (Sanchez and Mahoney 1996). Loose coupling is the degree to which ripple efects from changes within an app do not jeopardize its interoperability with the platform. Interface conformance is the degree to which the app conforms to interface specifications explicitly prespecified by the platform owner (e.g., platform-specific APIs and protocols). Both are a matter of degree rather than all-or-nothing choices. Therefore, reducing its coupling with the platform and conforming more closely to the platform’s interface specifications increases the modularity of an app’s external architecture. The polar opposite end of the continuum with perfectly modular on one end is a perfectly monolithic system, which is inseparable into smaller, independent subsystems. An app’s location on this monolithic–modular continuum describes its independence from the focal platform.<sup>4</sup>

2.4.1. Illustrations of Variation in the Modularity of External Architecture Among Apps. The Dropbox iOS app is an example of low modularity in external architecture. It does not use platform interface standards for file change notifications and instead uses a proprietary implementation. This has often caused Dropbox notifications to stop working after major iOS platform version changes. By contrast, the Google Calendar app extensively uses iOS interface standards for interacting with the iOS platform. This allows it to interoperate robustly after iOS upgrades and to talk to new apps by other developers that follow the same standards.

Greater modularity in an app’s external architecture fosters greater platform synergy because it increases an app’s autonomy to evolve independently of the platform without compromising interoperability. The more modular an app’s external architecture is, the less the app’s developer needs to understand the internal details of the platform. Conformance to the platform’s interface specifications alone ensures interoperability and minimizes ripple efects from changes within the app (Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 77; Colfer and Baldwin 2016). This increases the app developer’s autonomy to internally modify the app because the weaker ripple efects from such changes demand lesser coordination with the platform owner (Parnas 1972, p. 260). Consequentially, the greater the modularity of an app’s external architecture, the more rapidly it can assimilate new and evolving platform capabilities as they become available. For example, the externally modular American Express app was able to replace passwords with fingerprint recognition technology shortly after Apple introduced a new iOS fingerprinting API. Similarly, the externally modular Skype app was able to incorporate Siri capabilities within a week after Apple introduced the Siri API in 2016. Therefore, increasing modularity in an app’s external architecture accelerates the incorporation of platform capabilities into the app’s implementation.

Hypothesis 2A (H2A). Greater modularity in an app’s external architecture enhances its platform synergy.

## 2.5. Internal App Architecture

An app’s internal architecture describes the “interdependence within” part of its architectural near-decomposability, that is, interdependencies among the elements within an app (zoom level <sup></sup> in Figure 2). Internal architecture is the lesser-studied facet of neardecomposability—unlike external architecture that has been more widely studied. An app’s internal architecture is visible to the app developer but not to the platform owner.

High interdependence within an app translates into monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture (Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 52). We define monolithicity of internal architecture as the tightness of coupling among the functional elements within an app (Brusoni and Prencipe 2006). Envision it as the degree of absence of internal modularity (Frenken and Mendritzki 2012, p. 942). The more monolithic an app’s internal architecture, the less divisible its functional elements are into discrete modules. Its functionally distinguishable elements that handle input and output, application logic, and its user interface (UI) are tightly interwoven (Page-Jones 1988). They are designed as a single unit that cannot be decomposed into smaller components. By contrast, a less monolithic architecture would organize them as a number of loosely coupled smaller subsystems. Synonyms, such as integral architecture (Ulrich 1995), nonhierarchical design (Mihm et al. 2010), integrated architecture (Schilling 2000, p. 329), and coarse-grained design (Subramanyam et al. 2012), in the literature all refer to this architectural property.

The internal architectures of the three illustrative apps in Figure 2 have increasing degrees of monolithicity as we move from left to right $( a p p _ { 1 }  a p p _ { n } )$ . The ones on the left can be decomposed into more smaller subsystems than the ones on the right. Such internal design choices are independent of an app’s external architecture.

2.5.1. Illustrations of Monolithicity of Internal Architecture in Apps. Consider some examples illustrating monolithicity in apps’ internal architecture (besides the illustrations in Section 1). In native Java apps that BlackberryOS uses, an app with a single directory hierarchy of NodeJS code or a single Java web application resource (WAR) file is more monolithic than one with a multi-directory hierarchy or a package of multiple WAR files. (A WAR file is used to distribute a collection of Java servlets, Java classes, XML files, and tag libraries that collectively constitute an app.) The more the app logic and UI are fused together and not developed as separate classes, the more monolithic the app’s internal architecture is. Similarly, in iOS, the calculator app and the Etsy app have more monolithic internal architectures because their internal code is indivisible into discrete smaller subsystems (High Scalability 2015). Their internals are designed as a single module optimized for overall performance. (Calculator also does not use any platform services; all functionality is self-contained internally.) By contrast, a lesser internally monolithic app, such as Apple News or Gmail, is comprised of smaller subsystems that can individually be improved but cannot be optimized as a whole to perform as well.

Greater monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture enhances an app’s platform synergy for two reasons: (a) it focuses on improving overall app performance, and (b) it circumvents irreversible constraints that can straitjacket an app’s internal tweakability. First, an app’s internal architecture alters its developer’s behavior in the search for improvements over successive versions; its design and development are intertwined (Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 30). The more intertwined the design decisions of an app’s internal elements, the more individuals responsible for them must work in close contact (Schilling 2000). Novel ideas that bring an app closer to the frontier of functionality and help diferentiate it emerge from unstructured technical dialog within the app development team (Monteverde 1995). Greater monolithicity necessitates such dialog whereas greater modularity reduces it. Internally monolithic apps therefore encourage an integrated development process to improve the app as a whole. Google Search’s 2 billion–line monolithic core, for example, requires 25,000 engineers to work in close collaboration, which enables optimizing its performance experienced by end users (Metz 2015). (It uses bots and a proprietary code management tool called Piper to coordinate their work.) An internally monolithic architecture therefore shifts the focus from individually improving discrete components in an app to optimizing an app’s internal elements to perform well with each other.<sup>5</sup> This is more likely to yield faster overall app-wide improvements as well as better technical performance (for example, by minimizing latency within an app). By contrast, a less monolithic internal architecture is more conducive to component-bycomponent improvements that subsequently require time-consuming iteration within the app development team to integrate, slowing the app’s evolvability. Empirical evidence indeed shows that more internally monolithic systems have higher solution quality and performance, are easier to test, and less buggy (Mihm et al. 2010, Subramanyam et al. 2012). This accelerates the time to market for each new revision of an app attempting to experiment with evolving platform functionality in its own implementation.

Second, an IT artifact’s initial architecture can stylize how it can and cannot be tweaked. Changing the initial architecture can be so prohibitively costly that it is virtually an irreversible choice (Agarwal and Tiwana 2015; Baldwin and Clark 2000, p. 79). Initial app designs often begin with incomplete knowledge of end-user needs, which demand exploratory experimentation to discover (Bresnahan and Greenstein 2014). Early decisions about an app’s internal architecture are also often based on incomplete knowledge of interactions among design choices within the app (Colfer and Baldwin 2016), which can cause rouge dependencies (Lindberg et al. 2016). Reconciling such dependencies can require timeconsuming iterations after individual tweaks are made in the app’s internal elements. Internal monolithicity therefore avoids prematurely introducing design rigidities that can unduly constrain an app’s experimentation with new platform capabilities. Greater monolithicity in internal architecture therefore imposes fewer constraints on permissible improvements and is less susceptible to iteration slowdowns. This leads us to expect that monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture will enhance its platform synergy.

Hypothesis 2B (H2B). Greater monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture enhances its platform synergy.

## 2.6. Complementarity Between an App’s Internal Architecture and External Architecture

Near-decomposability of an app’s architecture encompasses both independence in its external architecture and interdependence in its internal architecture. The former is the modularity of its external architecture;

the latter is the monolithicity of its internal architecture. Their interaction—which represents the neardecomposability of an app’s architecture—undergirds their complementarity. The rationale for their complementarity is that their combination reallocates the app developer’s attention, which is otherwise devoted to ensuring each app revision’s interoperability with the platform to innovating within the app. Increasing modularity in the app’s external architecture increases its internal changeability because interface conformance guarantees each revision’s interoperability with the platform. This conserves scarce cognitive resources otherwise expended on integration by the app’s developer (Colfer and Baldwin 2016). Efort conserved integrating the app with the platform is efort available to refine the app itself.

The attention freed is more valuable as the monolithicity of the app’s internal architecture simultaneously increases because the app’s evolvability is less constrained by internal decomposition into smaller parts. The efort originally devoted to ensuring integration with the platform can instead turn to how the app can leverage advances in platform functionality to better address trends, preferences, and shifts in the app’s end users’ needs. Therefore, the more monolithic an app’s internal architecture, the more modularity in its external architecture enables leveraging platform capabilities.

Hypothesis 2C (H2C). Greater monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture strengthens the extent to which modularity in its external architecture enhances the app’s platform synergy.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Research Context

We collected primary and archival data in five phases from 2013 to 2016 from 629 independent app developers in the Blackberry platform ecosystem. Blackberry was a pioneering smartphone platform with 80 million users in 30 languages on 500 carriers in 170 countries (McNish and Silcof 2016, p. 170). It was once a cult brand with a 50% market share, which Apple’s iOS and Google’s Android subsequently eroded (McNish and Silcof 2016, p. 75).

An ideal setting to observe a competitive race is a crowded environment, in which competitive advantage is fickle and imitation is rife (D’Aveni et al. 2010, Lerner 1997). Mobile apps are an appropriate petri dish to test our model for three reasons that reflect this context. First, mobile apps compete with potentially millions of other apps in a platform market. By contrast, add-ons for enterprise systems number in the hundreds, making the sampling frame unviably small. Focusing on a single platform mitigates crossplatform diferences (e.g., diferences in programming languages) from confounding our app-level model of intra-platform competition. Second, such apps are small programs with limited functionality. This smallness is an advantage because it does not demand modularizing their internal architecture as larger programs necessitate (Parnas 1972). Third, their narrow scope permits demarcating their rival cohorts unlike enterprise system (ERP) add-ons that typically have broader functionality and might compete in multiple market segments.

The Blackberry platform has three particular advantages as our research setting. First, the Blackberry platform itself is internally monolithic (McNish and Silcof 2016, p. 172). This internal monolithicity mitigates the platform’s internal architecture from confounding our tests. Second, it categorizes each app exclusively in one market category, permitting objective identification of cohorts of apps to compute our dependent variable. Third, as Table 2 illustrates, this setting exhibits fierce competition among apps as well as variance in apps’ internal $( \sigma ^ { 2 } = 1 . 4 4 )$ and external $( \sigma ^ { 2 } = 1 )$ architectures.

3.1.1. Data Collection. Our sampling frame was a random sample of about 10,000 apps spanning all categories except games, which resulted in 3,800 independent Blackberry app developers.<sup>6</sup> To ensure independence among observations, we included only one app per developer. Our key informant was the lead developer of each app identified through Blackberry’s records. This ensured that the respondents were knowledgeable and representative of the team responsible for each app. We anchored each of the 3,800 surveys to an explicitly named app. We received 618 usable responses after two reminders for a 16.8% response rate. (We discarded 11 responses because of substantial missing data; 96 opted out.) T tests comparing the early (first 30%) and late (last 30%) respondents provided no evidence of nonresponse bias in the dependent variable (<sup>−</sup>1.51, n.s.) or in the predictors (external modularity (0.46, n.s.); internal monolithicity (0.43, n.s.)).

Figure 3 summarizes the timeline of our data collection. Predictor data were collected using a survey in October 2013 $( t _ { 1 } )$ and archival dependent variable data in April 2014 $( t _ { 3 } ;$ six-month lag); robustness checks used secondary data collected immediately after primary data collection (February $2 0 1 4 ; t _ { 2 } )$ and with 24 and 36 month lags $( t _ { 4 }$ October 2015; $t _ { 5 }$ October 2016). The six-month $t _ { 1 }  t _ { 3 }$ lag provided a temporal window long enough for competitive consequences to be visible but short enough to disallow confounds.

## 3.2. Measures and Scale Development

We used seven-point multi-item Likert scales for all predictors and time-lagged archival data from Blackberry’s records for the dependent variable (see Appendix B). An app’s competitive lead was the extent to which it outperforms its cohort of rivals in the marketplace, building on Barnett’s (2008) and Lerner’s (1997) work as well as the work of Lin et al. (2011) on competitive races. We used assessments from the market—here an app’s actual end users’ ratings on a 10-point scale— as its source of data. We had an average of 154 independent end-user raters for each app at t (190 at $t _ { 4 } )$ Following Cennamo and Santalo’s (2013, p. 1336) conceptualization of market segments, we define an app’s cohort using its exclusive category. We measured competitive lead as the diference between an app’s lifetime rating and the mean rating of all apps in its category.<sup>7</sup> A net positive diference indicates a competitive lead vis-à-vis its cohort; a net negative diference indicates a competitive lag.

We developed new app-level scales for monolithicity of internal architecture, platform synergy, and some controls; we adapted the rest to the Blackberry context from the sources shown in Appendix B. The proprietary, closed-source nature of apps in the Blackberry platform precluded us from objectively estimating properties of app architecture from source code. We instead relied on perceptual measures for our predictors. We generated preliminary item pools for monolithicity of internal architecture and platform synergy based on a review of the literature and then iteratively refined the items using a convenience sample of seven Blackberry developers and four academic experts.

Our item pool for monolithicity of an app’s internal architecture (a) built conceptually on Simon’s (1962) notion of high interdependence within a system and (b) the reverse of loose coupling in prior modularity scales (e.g., Tiwana 2015) but anchored on an app’s internal architecture. The key diference between independence among systems in Simon’s neardecomposability and modularity in external architecture is the latter’s stipulation of interface standards (Frenken and Mendritzki 2012). The items for monolithicity of internal architecture therefore tapped into internal interdependence without any reference to interface standards within an app. Our new scale used four items tapping into the degree to which the major internal features and functions implemented within the app were highly integrated, tightly coupled, monolithic, and internally cohesive.

Our item pool for platform synergy used Schilling (2000) solely as its conceptual starting point. Following our refined definition, we anchored the preliminary items explicitly on an app’s synergy specific to the Blackberry platform. We then built on descriptions of proximate constructs explicitly grounded in software systems (see Appendix A), such as synergistic integration (Sarker et al. 2012, p. 327), modulespecificity (Baldwin 2008, p. 170), and platform leverage (McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017) to generate a preliminary item pool. The scale used five items to assess the degree to which the app leveraged the Blackberry platform’s native capabilities, APIs, and services and built on and complemented its functionality. As the app and the platform can both evolve, the scale anchors tap into increases in platform synergy since the app’s initial release, building on Schilling’s (2000) emphasis that it can change over time. After extensive iteration, both new scales exhibited psychometric adequacy $( \alpha \ge 0 . 8 3 ;$ and high loadings and low cross-loadings; see Appendix C).

## 3.3. Descriptive Statistics

The apps in our study span a variety of market categories, such as social (21%), reference (14.7%), business (10.2%), utilities (45.5%), and multimedia apps (8.6%). 23.8% of their developers were Canadian firms (Blackberry’s home country), 21.8% were from the United States, and the remainder were from 50 other countries. 81% of the apps were in English. On average, at $t _ { 3 } ,$ each app had existed for about 19 months (σ 12.7), had 154 independent end-user reviews (σ 1,727), was 2,934 kilobytes in size, and had an average rating of 6.9 (σ 2.4) on a 10-point rating scale. 24.3% of the apps in the sample were free; the average paid app cost \$2.28 (σ \$2.26). The majority of apps targeted an unrestricted audience as opposed to a restricted (adult, mature, or teen) audience; very few used in-app advertising to generate revenue. Some apps also multihomed on rival platforms, such as Android, iOS, and Windows. On average, their developers had 6.4 other Blackberry apps (σ 7.8), of which 43% (σ 38%) were free.

## 4. Analysis and Results

If developers exercise their latitude in choosing an app’s internal and external architecture, overlooking their potential endogeneity can bias estimates. We used Garen’s (1984) econometric procedure to assess endogeneity in an app’s internal and external architecture. The results—summarized in Appendix D—failed to show endogeneity, suggesting that an ordinary least squares (OLS) model was appropriate.

## 4.2. Control Variables

To account for rival explanations for competitive lead, we used four sets of control variables: (a) app characteristics, (b) app developer characteristics, (c) app market characteristics, and (d) evolutionary dynamics.

(a) App characteristics. We controlled for app complexity (measured as its file size in kilobytes) premising that more complex apps were more likely to deliver greater functionality to end users; app age to account for the liability of aging of software (Subramanyam et al. 2012); a dummy for whether the app was free (premising that paid apps are more likely to signal quality in competition with free apps; Lin et al. 2011); whether the app was advertising supported, premising that it subsidizes development costs; its lifetime review count to account for the efects of accumulated reputation; its language, premising that English language apps would reach the broadest audience; whether the app multihomed on rival iOS, Android, and Windows Phone platforms, premising that it broadens insights into end user needs and also controls simply for an app’s presence in multiple markets with diferent logics of competition (Barnett 2008, p. 223); and the degree to which it depended on services outside the Blackberry platform (extra-ecosystem dependence).

(b) App developer characteristics. We controlled for developer tenure on the Blackberry platform, premising that more experienced developers’ apps will outperform less experienced ones; the total number of other Blackberry apps by the app’s developer (platform experience); the pursuit of a freemium strategy (measured as the percentage of the developer’s apps that were free), premising that a free version promotes paid apps; and three dummies for the app developer’s country to account for nationality efects. We also controlled for the degree of organizational modularity that characterized the developer’s relationship with the platform owner, based on the idea that organizational architecture has a still-debated relationship with product architecture (Brusoni and Prencipe 2006, Colfer and Baldwin 2016).<sup>10</sup>

(c) App market characteristics. We used four dummies for the app’s primary market category with the rationale that competitive dynamics can vary across markets (Cennamo and Santalo 2013). We also controlled for technological uncertainty in the app’s category, premising that it difers across diferent types of apps.

(d) Evolutionary dynamics. Finally, we controlled for two evolutionary dynamics: Changes in the app’s price (∆price) and in its mean rating (∆rating) from $t _ { 1 } \to t _ { 3 } ;$ intended to account for dynamics during the observation period even though they are not part of the theorizing (see D’Aveni et al. 2010). The patterns in Step 1.1 in Table 3 show that apps that perform better are more complex, target an unrestricted audience, are in English, have seen recent spikes in ratings, and are by developers with longer platform tenure and pursuing a freemium strategy. By contrast, apps that fare more poorly are older and free, contend with greater technological uncertainty, and are by developers that have produced more apps.

## 4.4. Limitations

Three limitations warrant attention. First, objective measures of the architectural properties of the app estimated directly from the source code would have been ideal; we used perceptual measures because we did not have access to the proprietary, closed-source code of the commercial apps in our study. Open-source apps would be ideal to do this, but they might not be representative of commercial apps that must survive conventional market competition. Second, although a mere 16%–26.5% variance explained is typical in embryonic empirical work, it signals fertile opportunities for nomological expansion. Third, our findings cannot readily be generalized to third-party ERP add-ons built around commercial ERP systems often used as the context for earlier information systems (IS) platform studies (e.g., Huang et al. 2013, Sarker et al. 2012) because they (a) are costlier and larger systems with broader functionality (unlike our small \$2 apps) and (b) often compete in multiple markets (unlike our niche-specific apps). To assess how the small apps in our study limit generalizability to larger ERP modules, we created an interaction with app size in our focal platform synergy <sup>→</sup> competitive lead relationship. The interaction was nonsignificant $( \beta = 0 . 0 2 3$ T value <sup></sup> 0.25). We also failed to observe a relationship between app size and modularity of external architecture (T value <sup> −</sup>0.3; n.s.) or monolithicity of internal architecture (T value<sup> −</sup>0.4; n.s.). We therefore failed to find evidence that the smaller app size in our context systematically constrains generalizability. Nevertheless, we urge caution in extrapolating to other types of software systems.

## 5. Discussion

We studied how the interplay of apps’ understudied internal architecture with their more-studied external architecture influences their performance in intraplatform competition. They together give a system its distinctive character (Smith 1981; pp. 359, 363), particularly to compete by synthesizing its own code with platform services and APIs also available to rival apps.

Using Simon’s early work as a springboard, we conceptualized the near-decomposability of an app’s architecture as modularity in its external architecture and monolithicity in its internal architecture. Our overarching idea was about how such near-decomposability fosters a competitive lead over rival apps. Platform synergy—introduced here—plays a central explanatory role in our theory.

Our analyses of archival and survey data from 618 apps in the Blackberry ecosystem support these ideas. The novel insight is into how the competitive consequences of an app’s platform synergy originates from its architecture. Our original theoretical contribution is how—by enhancing platform synergy— complementarity between an app’s internal architecture and its external architecture competitively propels it ahead of rival apps. These findings make a handful of original contributions to research on platforms, modularity, and broader organization theory.

## 5.1. Contributions and Theoretical Implications

5.1.1. Contributions to Platforms Research. Unlike prior research fixated on inter-platform competition (level <sup></sup> in Figure 2), our study makes two novel contributions to intra-platform competition (levels <sup></sup> and <sup></sup>). First, it develops the notion of platform synergy to show that heterogeneity in apps’ assimilation of a fluid platform’s capabilities helps explain why one app outperforms rival apps. This idea directly addresses Mcintyre and Srinivasan’s (2017) recent lament that no study of platforms has attempted to reconcile that there even is heterogeneity in complement attributes and hence in their ability to leverage the resources provided by the platform. Platform synergy—the conceptual hook of our contribution to platforms research—underscores that technology races in platform markets are continuous (Lin et al. 2011), where a competitive lead is fragile. The efect of an app’s platform synergy on its competitive performance complements the scant literature on intra-platform competition focused on app governance (Wareham et al. 2014), pricing (Lin et al. 2011), and screening (Tiwana 2015).

Subsequent interviews revealed that app developers’ use of third-party platform-agnostic development tools to multihome hampers platform-specific tweaks that undergird an app’s platform synergy.

Second, an app’s architectural near-decomposability bolsters an app’s lead over its cohort. This implies that the more interdependent an app’s internal architecture, the more valuable is its independence from the platform encoded in its external architecture. Our new conceptualization of internal architecture zoomed in to the within-app level <sup></sup> in Figure 2, which neither intra-platform nor inter-platform studies have previously studied. This theoretically bridges level <sup></sup> to the understudied level <sup></sup> in a platform ecosystem’s design hierarchy in Figure 2. As the interaction plot in Figure 5 illustrates, the influence of modularity in external architecture is stronger when monolithicity in internal architecture is higher (the dotted line) than when it is lower (the solid line).

A competitively ideal app architecture is a modular monolith. Such a design allows an app to better synthesize commonplace platform resources with its proprietary code into an app diferentiated enough to command a competitive lead. Greater monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture, therefore, amplifies a more modular external app architecture’s conduciveness to assimilating platform capabilities. This complementarity translates into an app’s competitive lead because it bolsters leveraging platform capabilities to enrich the functionality that the app delivers to its end users. These findings about the consequences of heterogeneity in app architecture complement prior economics of IS studies on heterogeneity in app quality, variety, and network efects.

A significant direct efect from monolithicity in an app’s internal architecture suggests that it enhances its competitive performance through two plausible pathways besides bolstering the app’s platform synergy. First, by circumventing the nontrivial up-front costs of modularization (Subramanyam et al. 2012), it allows an app’s developer to devote more resources to better grasping user needs in the app’s niche. Second, it might thwart imitation by rival apps because code monolithicity obfuscates comprehensibility (Parnas 1972). The numerous intertwined design choices in a monolithic design are harder for rivals to decipher, making imitation more dificult for rivals. Collectively, these findings ofer a starting point for linking technology architecture to competitiveness in innovation races.

5.1.2. Contributions to Modular Systems Theory. The notion of modularity that underpins object-oriented programming, service-oriented architectures, and micro-services pervasive in modern IT systems originated from Simon’s work on architectural near-decomposability. However, the modularity literature has focused only on one system’s interactions with others without consideration of interactions within a system. We returned to modularity’s roots to bring into the conversation the overlooked facet, a system’s internal architecture. Our results have three theoretical implications for designing software systems that simultaneously compete with each other yet depend on others higher up in their design hierarchy.

First, greater monolithicity in an application’s internal architecture competitively propels it ahead, in part, because it fosters greater synergy with systems higher up in its design hierarchy in Figure 2. Advocating lesser modularity internally might appear counterintuitive, but Simon (2002, p. 593) suspected inasmuch in his foresight that individual apps (“modules”) need not be decomposable. Diferences in apps’ internal architectures can be envisioned as baked-in traits favoring or opposing greater platform synergy. Modularization that is advantageous in an IT artifact’s external architecture is counterproductive in its internal architecture. This espouses opposite design philosophies inside and outside an app’s boundary, implying that the pursuit of modularity on both sides can be foolhardy.

Advocacy against (Ethiraj and Levinthal 2004) and for (Frenken and Mendritzki 2012) greater modularity in the modularity literature is a straw man argument without the internal versus external architecture distinction elaborated here. One stance has merit for internal architecture and the other has merit for external architecture. Monolithicity and modularity can harmoniously coexist in the same artifact. An internally monolithic app that is a modular part of a platform’s ecosystem preserves diferentiation without forsaking the platform as its springboard.

Our results also underscore the importance of disentangling platform synergy from its causes. Schilling (2000, p. 317) associated synergistic specificity with monolithicity in architecture, implicitly, but erroneously, confounding in our model’s language platform synergy with monolithicity of external architecture. The low, nonsignificant correlation of platform synergy and external architecture in Table 2 contradicts her asserted association. An app with high platform synergy can be highly modular in its external architecture (e.g., an app that heavily invokes platform services need not be tightly coupled to the platform). Similarly, an app that is modularly connected to the platform need not necessarily derive a large proportion of its functionality from the platform.

The second, more distinctive, implication stems from monolithicity in internal architecture increasing the marginal returns from modularity in external architecture. Their complementarity implies that although both internal architecture and external architecture matter independently, they matter more together. They are distinct but intertwined sides of the same coin.

The 2 <sup>×</sup> 2 matrix in Figure 6 uses median-split, dichotomized subgroups to illustrate our findings. It shows how the combination of internal architecture and external architecture influences an app’s competitive lead (<sup>→</sup>) or competitive lag (<sup>c</sup>). (The data are plotted to scale; the vertical line in each cell is the cohort mean.) The only combination associated with a competitive lead is a nearly decomposable—externally modular and internally monolithic—app architecture (cell a). Reversing these architectural choices (cell c)—an internally modular but externally monolithic design—creates the severest competitive lag. An app that does not diferentiate internal from external architecture and assumes modularity to be unequivocally advantageous (cell d) performs worse than a nearly decomposable app (cell a) but also pays the least penalty compared with other cells. This implies that making everything more modular is the least terrible of all design errors. Making everything monolithic (cell b), which every IS undergraduate is discouraged from, is still only the second worst in its competitive penalties (i.e., better than cell c). An app’s architecture accounts for almost half a rating point diference between cells a and d. Such small diferences in ratings can have large consequences because relative positions matter in a competitive race (Doraszelski 2003).

Third, the central role of platform synergy conceptualized here ofers a new explanation for how a software artifact’s overall architecture generates cohort-leading performance. It explains why some apps can better assimilate platform innovations to create a competitive advantage manifested as competitive lead.

The implication for broader organization theory is using technological architecture as a design intervention to balance the perennial integration–diferentiation tension; external architecture facilitates integration, and internal architecture facilitates diferentiation.

5.1.3. Implications for Practice. For app developers. Our findings direct app developers’ attention to the historically-overlooked internal side of app architectures.

A market advantage accrues from practicing a design philosophy in the internal design of their apps contrary to conventional wisdom. Deliberately choosing a more monolithic internal architecture amplifies how extensively app developers can leverage the platform by modularizing their app’s connections to it. The mantra is modular on the outside and monolithic on the inside. This modular–monolith design strategy can create a competitive edge and retard imitation by rival apps.

For platform owners. Platform owners can tilt interplatform competition in their favor by attending to apps’ internal architecture in their platform’s ecosystem. Creating APIs, platform services, and interface standards to support app development addresses only one piece of the puzzle in making their platform indispensible to apps in their ecosystem. If they simultaneously encourage app developers to make their apps more tightly coupled apps internally, the greater the benefits of modular platform APIs to app developers and the more indispensible the platform will become to them. Greater platform synergy of apps also reduces code reusability, discouraging them from multihoming on rival platforms. This is consequential for how a platform fares against its rivals. Permeation of software platforms into gaming, corporate IT, healthcare, and retail is widening this strategy’s broader leveragability.

5.1.4. Ideas for Future Research. These findings lead to six fertile questions for future research. First, how can an app’s external architecture expand beyond the platform as the sole referent to encompass mash-ups and micro-services outside the platform that apps increasingly exploit? (We merely controlled for their use by an app.) Second, what insights can platforms ofer into the dynamics of IT outsourcing, which has historically used a transaction–cost lens? Shifts over time in the services that an app invokes from the platform are akin to a temporal morphing of the boundary between two cooperating firms. Third, does degradation of modularity as an app ages imply that the mere passage of time weakens the advantages of modularity in an app’s external architecture? Fourth, how does power asymmetry between a platform owner and app developers interplay with app architecture? Does platform synergy make an app more vulnerable to opportunism by the platform owner? Fifth, can network externalities prevalent in platform markets substitute for platform synergy? Sixth, does internal monolithicity sow the seeds of later competitive destruction of a platform as well as apps? For example, Windows XP’s internal monolithicity eventually forced Microsoft to scrap and rebuild subsequent version of Windows. Similarly, the scrapped-and-rebuilt Blackberry 10 platform incredulously demanded all existing apps be redeveloped. (This proved fatal to Blackberry by the time this study was published.)

In conclusion, the competitive consequences of an IT artifact’s internal architecture are inseparably intertwined with its external architecture. More broadly, we advocate more nuanced attention to technological architecture as a competitive instrument.

## Appendix D. Endogeneity Assessment and Econometric Diagnostics

We used Garen’s (1984) two-step econometric procedure to assess endogeneity in both the external architecture and internal architecture of an app—specifically, selection bias as a result of omitted variables. Baldwin (2008, p. 188) implicitly alludes to such endogeneity in saying that designers have great latitude in determining their designs’ internal structure and external linkages. For example, an app’s developer chooses which interface standards, such as APIs, that provide app developers hooks into the platform to use. Large numbers of interface standards in platforms (e.g., more than 10,000 sets of APIs and protocols exist in Apple’s iOS, each with hundreds of specifications) can make it daunting for app developers even to be cognizant of all relevant platform interface standards.

In Step 1, we used a reduced form model with instrumental variables to estimate endogeneity-correcting Garen η’s for the two potentially endogenous predictors. Given scant empirical work to guide instrument selection, conceptual logic guided our choice of instruments. We used (a) organizational modularity between the app developer and the platform owner (Sanchez and Mahoney 1996), premising that how app decision rights are divvied between a platform owner and app developer for a specific app will influence an app’s architectural choices by the app developer; (b) app age, based on the idea that an app’s architecture might be afected by the number of APIs existing at the time of an app’s inception, and (c) how long the app developer had developed for the platform, premising that market tenure afects app developers’ behaviors. The results appear in Table D.1. Noteworthy here are (a) the significant link between organizational modularity and internal monolithicity (implying that inter-firm organizational modularity leads to internally monolithic apps) and (b) that app age and developer tenure on the platform push monolithicity of internal architecture and modularity of external architecture in opposite directions.

Step 2 (Table D.2) included the two η’s from Step 1, the predicted values (y<sup>ˆ</sup>) of monolithicity in internal architecture and modularity in external architecture from Step 1, and their product terms to predict platform synergy. The statistical significance of the η’s (the highlighted rows) in Step 2 indicates endogeneity. The nonsignificant η<sub>external</sub> <sub>modularity</sub> (1.39; n.s.) and η<sub>internal</sub> <sub>monolithicity</sub> (0.97; n.s.) in Table D.2 suggest the absence of endogeneity. The nonsignificant η <sup>∗</sup> y<sup>ˆ</sup> product terms further suggest no systematic endogeneity bias over the range of internal and external architectural choices at the app level. 2SLS and 3SLS estimates using the same instruments produced consistent results. We triangulated this using the Durbin–Wu–Hausman endogeneity test, which also failed to show endogeneity in external (Hausman f <sup></sup> 0.048, p <sup></sup> 0.83; n.s.) or internal architecture (Hausman $f = 3 . 4 9 , p = 0 . 0 6 2 ; \mathrm { n . s . } )$ . Since we found no evidence of endogeneity, we subsequently repurposed the three instruments as controls in our OLS model.

## Additional econometric diagnostic tests for instrument suficiency and model identification

We conducted a battery of additional econometric tests to assess instrument suficiency and model identification.

a. Instrument suficiency using the Anderson–Rubin test. This test’s null is that the excluded instruments are uncorrelated with the error term, and the set of instruments included are suficient. The Anderson–Rubin $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was 3.85 $( p = 0 . 1 4 , \mathrm { n } . s . )$ suggesting that the instruments are suficient and valid. Further as desirable, none of the instruments had high correlations (<sup>≤</sup> 0.19) with platform synergy. Caution is nevertheless warranted as we cannot infer instruments’ theoretical appropriateness from empirical sufficiency.

b. Model identification using the Sargan and Bassman tests. The null hypotheses for both the Sargan test and the Bassman test are that the model’s over-identifying restrictions are valid. The Sargan Chi $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was 4.99 $( p = 0 . 0 8 2 , \mathrm { n } . s . )$ and the Bassman F was 1.91 $( p = 0 . 1 4 ; \mathrm { n } . s . )$ indicating that the model was appropriately over-identified.

Collectively, these analyses fail to show that endogeneity is a concern in our model.
