# e-Service enhancement priority matrix: The case of an IC foundry company

Chun-Cho Chen <sup>a</sup>, Ching-Sung Wu <sup>b,\*</sup>, Rebecca Chung-Fern Wu <sup>c</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Corporate Development, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, Hsin Chu 300, Taiwan <sup>b</sup> Department of International Business, College of Management, National Taiwan University, Taipei 106, Taiwan <sup>c</sup> Department of Accounting, College of Management, National Taiwan University, Taipei 106, Taiwan Received 8 June 2003; received in revised form 20 December 2005; accepted 19 January 2006 Available online 19 June 2006

## Abstract

This is a case study of the use of electronic service (e-Service) at the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company. We discuss the approach used in evolving an e-Service business model and propose a conceptual framework for identifying its key service attributes. In order to formulate an enhancement method, we conducted a customer survey; customer segmentation was identified and an e-Service enhancement priority matrix for defining the management of customer relations was developed as a way of prioritizing improvements.

The e-Service enhancement priority matrix included service attributes identified as leveragable strength, high-priority improvement, low-priority improvement, and maintenance of customer relations. In analyzing the priority matrix, the correlation of system performance with overall e-Service satisfaction was relatively low, whereas the accuracy of logistics information, platform functionality, and e-Service scope had a much higher correlation. Based on this analysis, enhancement priorities were identified.

While both integrated device manufacturers and design houses recognize the importance of e-Service, the firms showed a lower awareness of these new services. This indicated that such types of e-Service should be promoted. <sup>#</sup> 2006 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.

Keywords: Customer satisfaction; Electronic commerce; e-Service; Service quality; Virtual factory; Web-based survey

## 1. Introduction

The rapid expansion of e-commerce in the past few years has convinced people that a new economy has emerged. Using Senn’s [36] categorization of ebusiness applications: electronic markets or e-marketplaces, inter-organizational systems, and customer services, we present a methodology for customer e-Service enhancement, based on successful applications at the Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), one of the leading integrated circuit (IC) foundry companies in the world.

Information technology is now a strategic part of most businesses, enabling the redefining of markets and industries and the shaping of business strategies [1,12,23]. Today’s executives are faced with many organizational and market driven choices. Porter [34] argued that because of the Internet, strategy has become more important and that the most successful new business models are those adopting a ‘click and brick strategy, which integrates web technology, creating an enterprise-wide value chain.

Chang et al.[9] proposed that e-commerce initiatives were important strategic initiatives and that firms with a stronger EC market orientation would be more successful. Phan [32] presented a study of e-business competitive advantage strategies using the success at Intel. He concluded that good e-business strategies had contributed to Intel’s long lasting and solid financial success. By using e-business to enhance its distinctive positioning, operational efficiency, and competitiveness, Intel had continued to strengthen its strategic position in the global market.

## 1.1. Business model for an IC foundry industry

Recent research has studied success stories of foundry business models and their impact on the IC industry. Before the 1990s, integrated device manufacturers (IDMs) dominated the IC value chain, running the gamut of design, tape-out, mask making, manufacturing, assembling, testing, and product marketing. Motivated by economies of scale, TSMC successfully broke their dominance in the IC industry. Today, as IC manufacturing has entered the 12-in.-wafer era and processing has evolved into deep sub-micron technologies, capital expense for equipment and research has dramatically increased. The risk of the increased capital investment in running IDMs, has resulted in the disintegration of the traditional ‘‘vertical integration’’ mode; the development of diverse companies, dedicated to one or more nodes of the value chain has arisen, as shown in Fig. 1.

Fabless firms now cooperate with foundry companies, providing advanced technology and production capacity and competing with traditional IDMs. On the other hand, IDMs have reduced their investment risk through outsourcing, forming a competitive but complementary relationship with foundry companies. The result has been that the foundry segment has continued to expand its worldwide market share in IC manufacturing as more companies compete in this booming industry.

## 1.2. Importance of e-Service in the IC foundry industry

While facing increasing competition, customer service, in addition to manufacturing excellence and technology superiority, has emerged as a third factor. Through the Internet, e-Service has become one of the key components of satisfactory customer service. The ‘‘Virtual factory’’ vision, describing the service that customers would demand if they owned and operated their own manufacturing facilities, could not be realized without the implementation of superior e-Service. Seddon [35] pointed out that the Internet capacity to provide customized service, flexible operation, and dynamic capacity allocation had become a key competitive barrier. Utilizing TSMC as an example, Hsieh et al. [18] concluded that the Internet service improved operational flexibility and customer relations in the dynamic IC market environment, resulting in competitive advantage. E-Service or ‘Virtual factory’ services could help foundry companies strengthen customer relations, form a sustainable competitive advantage, and offer operational flexibility to deal with the increasing uncertainty of the environment. The influence of these services on the management of customer relations could be characterized as accelerated information exchange, a high degree of interaction and flexibility, and removal of time and geographical barriers [5,6].

e-Service has allowed enterprises to strengthen customer relationships by providing information with unprecedented width and depth, collecting customers requests or complaints in real time. Several features of the foundry industry have defined e-Service applications. The number of active customer accounts is usually in the order of several hundred, while mainly categorized into IDMs or Fabless segments, customers vary in size, flexibility, resources, and e-Service experience. Throughout their business life cycle relationship with foundry companies, customers may request large volumes of information in various stages. The information flow can be bi-directional, forming a collaborative relationship, especially in the IC design stage. To gather all the information, foundry companies must liaise between partners, such as Silicon Intellectual Property vendors, Electronic Design Automation (EDA) vendors, forwarders, and assembling/testing houses.

As shown in Fig. 2, the e-Service received by the foundry industry has been aimed at fulfilling three modes of collaborating with customers by designing a user interface to ensure ease of use and location of information:

1. Design collaboration supports customer access to complete and accurate information at each step of the design and tape-out process.

2. Engineering collaboration provides customer access to engineering information, including pilot runs, risk runs and yield analyses, as well as quality and reliability data.

3. Logistics collaboration provides customer access to the status of wafer fabrication, assembling, testing and shipping.

To serve customers through the Internet, foundry companies generally provide two channels. As the primary channel, a web-based customer portal provides a secure online platform to support customer activities throughout the foundry service cycle; through this, the foundry company can improve information transparency, raise transaction efficiency, and give the customers the ability to adjust their production schedules as necessary due to a swing in market demand, etc. The customer is granted passwordprotected access to technical documents, design service data, quality and assurance documents, and production status information.

System-to-system integration uses third-party software to link mission-critical processes, such as the order, the wafer production status, etc., between foundry companies, their partners, and their customers. The software transforms the data, from the foundry companies, into the system compatible format that the customer has requested. In this way, the internal systems of the suppliers and buyers are effectively linked, enabling collaborative production planning, engineering data sharing, real-time order placement, and confirmation, etc. The participants are thus integrated into a value chain spanning the whole enterprise, reducing cycle time, inventory levels, and cost. One key factor facilitating the progress of this integration was the introduction of the RosettaNet standard, which was produced by a consortium of more than 400 of the world’s leading IC companies to create, implement, and promote open IC industry e-Commerce standards, such as a data dictionary, an implementation framework, message schemas, and process specifications. It provides a frame of reference prior to building system-to-system integration and results in significant savings.

This e-Service has improved efficiency and productivity. For example, the time to load purchase orders has been reduced from days to minutes. Documentation is now easily accessible, updateable in real time, and fully traceable. Increased client customization has been achieved through the use of ‘privilege settings’. Upon login, customers can download documents that address their specific needs and access only the data they require, while restricting access to other data. With privilege being set in advance, time and error are reduced, while data accuracy and efficiency are improved.

By providing customers with greater accessibility, the time previously consumed in responding or reacting to customer inquiries is reduced. Online service also instantly delivers documents for downloading, upon customers’ instructions, a task that previously would have taken several weeks. Lead time has been reduced in several instances by more than 25%. Time delays in dealing new customers have also been reduced by implementing a list of frequently asked questions.

More transparency in foundry operations has resulted in productivity improvements. For example, in the past, improvement of an internal data channel might have been slow, but because this is now visible to the customer, it must be addressed quickly, with significant impact on both operations and future development. Now production teams react rapidly to customer requests and inquiries: the customer knows the stage of the order and can ask why it seems to be stalled. Putting the customer in control allows production teams to focus on the important issues and look at ways of reducing time delays. Productivity and yields have improved and are achieved in shorter time-frame.

## 2. e-Service application development and enhancement at TSMC

## 2.1. History of e-Service: phase 1 (1996–1999)— Virtual factory vision

For TSMC e-Services, 1996–1999 was a burgeoning phase: the company attempted to apply emerging web technologies to customer service. As a pioneer, they focused on positioning the Internet service and aligning it with traditional service channels; some information was made available through different web sites, prepared by different customer interfacing departments, such as the customer engineering and production planning departments. The resulting user interface was not friendly; besides poor art design, the users had some difficulty in locating the technical information and documents they required. Information was not integrated; users often had to look in several places to collect the information they needed. Information availability throughout the product life cycle had not been introduced. Users often found the information incomplete and needed to use a service engineer to complete their requirements. Although TSMC provided

Internet services and a certain level of convenience, the service was inadequate and immature.

Besides improving the web site set-up, Direct-System was launched to construct system-to-system integration between TSMC and key customers. With this service, information and data could be transferred between enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. Also, in 1998, the YES-System, a yield enhancement tool allowing customers to analyze and measure the outcome of wafers manufactured by TSMC was introduced.

## 2.2. History of e-Service: phase 2 (1999–2001)— channels and applications

In 1999, TSMC entered a new phase of Internet services: a series of Internet services were launched and enhanced. Online-System 1.0, introduced in 1999, unified all the web sites services as a single customer portal with addition of key applications. In 2000, the system was upgraded to Version 2.0, with a completely new user interface, web organization, and personalization capability. After many years of trial-and-error, the architecture finally emerged; it included two channels or platforms. One was an system to deliver important applications, such as the YES-System, and the other, a Direct-System, providing for collaboration between TSMC and customers on design, engineering and logistic domains.

The e-Service model allowed for the flow of customer/ foundry bi-directional information, 24-h a day 7 days a week, from anywhere in the world via a web-enabled PC or workstation. The program has been continuously expanding and will cover all interactive activity between the foundry and its customers (see Fig. 2). To achieve a complete system, activities such as document and information sharing, the collection of customer feedback, etc., will be achieved by following the product life cycle. Four stages of activity were identified: evaluation and initiation; design implementation; pilot and risk runs; mass production and shipping. After leaving the production facility, the wafers are shipped to customer-assigned assembly and testing sub-contractors for further processing. The finished goods are then shipped to the customer, ready for sale.

Categorized by three areas of collaboration – design, engineering and logistics – the four interactive stages are:

1. Evaluation and initiation—Entering the basic technical information, customers can evaluate whether TSMC fits their need as a foundry manufacturer. After choosing TSMC, more technical information and business documents, such as a non-disclosure agreement (NDA) and contract, must be executed.

2. Design implementation—From TMSC technical information (or that of associated IP and EDA vendors), customers can start designing their products and developing the mask file.

3. Pilot and Risk runs—To verify whether the design is appropriate, customers can run a few experimental lots. After functionality has been verified, some customers initiate a small production run, usually of a few hundred wafers, to ensure the design.

4. Mass production and shipping—The customer launches a full-scale mass production run, with the finished product being shipped to the customerassigned destination for assembly and testing.

Design collaboration is the sharing and integration of IC design related information and knowledge. Through the Internet, TSMC helps customers resolve IC design problems, improve channels of communication, and reduce cost.

Engineering collaboration integrates engineering knowledge with information sharing in wafer manufacturing; it includes the circuit probe and the wafer acceptance tests, etc. Services that focus on engineering collaboration include the YES-System, etc.

Logistics collaboration seamlessly builds the business and logistics flow of information sharing, integrating and smoothing the exchange. Related services are cargo tracking, supply chain management, etc.

## 2.3. Challenges for e-Service enhancement

With first mover advantage, TSMC experienced high growth and profits, which inevitably attracted followers into the marketplace. Facing increasing competition, a third competitive barrier, in addition to manufacturing excellence and technological superiority became apparent: customer service, with e-Service being one of its key components. TSMC continued to implement a strategy of becoming an integrated, essential part of their customers’ business, rather than simply providing alternative short-term outsourcing.

Since 1996 TSMC e-Service has gradually evolved into a foundry-orientated total solution. Although their early development gave them the leading edge, competitors are catching up fast; sustaining the lead becomes more difficult. As technology advances, customers become more discerning in the scope and quality of service demanded, while the semiconductor industry has experienced its worst down cycle ever from the middle of 2001.

## 3. Development of an enhancement conceptual famework

## 3.1. Identification of key attributes of e-Service

Two key characteristics of the Internet are its interactivity and connectivity. Sullivan [37] defined interactivity as the degree to which a dialogue can be generated between service providers and users. By having the capacity to interact, users are able to influence the format and content of information exchanged, accelerate negotiations, and achieve effective communication [33]. Deighton [10] proposed that interactivity has two attributes: addressability, the ability to communicate directly, and responsiveness, the ability to gather and document the response. A highly interactive web site is helpful in collecting customer opinions and complaints [16], delivering personalized services [31], and creating a high termination cost for customers [14].

Connectivity makes the implementation of e-Service feasible in many business sectors because of the cost savings, the reduced cycle time, and improved information flow. Web owners can build content navigation paths that are intelligible and interesting to users [19,27].

From various studies, three important factors have been identified in measuring the success of a Web site: information quality, system quality (e.g., response time), and system accessibility [24,29]. Huizingh broke down a Web site into two parts: content, the information, features, or services; and design, the way this content is made availableto users [30]. Aladwani and Palvia[2]developed a new instrument that captured key characteristics of web site quality from the user’s perspective. This measured four dimensions of web quality: specific content, content quality, appearance, and technical adequacy.

## 3.1.1. Scope of e-Service

Depending upon the activity phases of the marketing cycle, the scope of e-Service has been categorized as pre-sale, online, and after sale [26]. Kotler [22] suggested there are at least seven ways that companies use the Internet to: do research, provide information, provide a discussion forum, provide training, provide online buying and selling opportunities, provide online auctioning or exchanging, and deliver online ‘‘bits’’. Through customer portals and system-to-system integration, e-Service in IC foundry companies have mainly focused on providing information, collecting customer feedback, providing online training, and taking online orders. As information technology advances, and customers continue to demand higher interactive efficiency [7,8], it will be important to discover how emerging technology could be used to extend the scope of the service; this, therefore, as shown in Fig. 3, has become an important attribute in measuring the success of the e-Service of IC foundry companies.

## 3.1.2. Content of e-Service

The main advantage of e-Service is to provide an inexpensive way of searching, organizing, disseminating, and storing large amounts of information [11]. This capacity is being used by a growing number of companies to strengthen their ties to their customers [3,28,41]. Consequently, the quality of information on a successful web site, in terms of availability and accuracy, becomes important [25]. Studies have shown that this can directly impact the perceived usefulness of a web site. Huizingh defined a web site’s information quality as its completeness and accuracy. When a web site offers complete and accurate information, user categories become broader and customers are retained [17,40].

## 3.1.3. Design of e-Service

There are three key attributes associated with the design of a customer support Web site provided by IC foundry firms: system functionality, ease in locating information, and system performance. System functionality includes Web security and learning capability, such as personalization, help functions, etc. The site should only allow entry to those holding a valid password and having a specific job function. For example, production control engineers may only be allowed to view logisticsrelated information, such as wafer status reports. Personalization or help functions are examples of the platform functionality especially useful for Web sites having information width and depth. To save time, customers can personalize their home pages, etc.

A hypertext environment allows for deep, nonlinear searches initiated by the customer. Web designers can influence this, by designing a navigational structure with links between pages. Huizingh distinguished four types of navigational structures: a tree; a tree with a return-to-home page button; a tree with a few horizontal links; an extensive network. The greater the amount of information, the more difficult it becomes to search through hyperlinks. The customer information portals of IC foundry companies usually include large content and a hyperlink navigational structure; these rapidly becomes ineffective to users in locating the information they require: a search function is needed.

Because of the burgeoning growth of users and application complexity, on-line traffic congestion has become important. Lin and Lu suggested that web site owners design a fast-response web page by excluding any unnecessary graphics when they are concerned with system performance. A shorter response time results in smoother man-machine interaction with a higher perceived ease of use [4,13]. Functionality, system performance and ease of locating and finding information were the key attributes identified as desirable.

Several major e-Service attributes can improve customer satisfaction: service scope, content availability and accuracy, web site functionality, system performance, and ease in locating information; other factors are: playfulness, multimedia capability, and system accessibility.

Due to the unique features of the IC foundry industry, many attributes were not included in our survey. For example, entertainment definitely was not an important attribute in a professional industrial product setting and enhancing the public Internet infrastructure was not under TSMC’s control. We did, however, indirectly incorporate several attributes based on studies from Negash and Liu, such as Learning capability. TSMC has already built up a complete set of platform-level functions, such as a help function, key word search, etc. Therefore, this attribute is indirectly incorporated in Platform Functionality.

Judging from the characteristics of the e-Service provided by IC foundry companies, the quality of content and web site design, and the scope of the service were the most important attributes.

## 3.2. E-Service enhancement priority matrix

Kearns [21] established a framework for aligning EC and business strategies based on the Miles and Snow typology. He argued that applying proven business approaches and achieving consistency has the potential to increase returns on EC investments. Wan [39] described a framework of analysis that categorized features of website design in a matrix of business functions versus customer values. The framework supported a development plan for a commercial website that could involve all aspects of a transaction. It also included examples of Web features that could be evaluated from the visitor or customer perspective. The framework chose Web features that were directly related to customer values: those that might bring only strategic advantages to the company but were intangible to customers were deliberately ignored.

Harvey et al. [15] stuggested that the priority matrix was a simple but effective tool for deciding which tasks should receive immediate attention and which could wait.

Johnson and Gustafsson [20] as well as a number of others suggested using an importance-performance analysis. Such an analysis mapped the attributes that drive performance into a simple matrix that helped to prioritize the improvement opportunities.

We used a two-dimensional e-Service enhancement priority matrix to provide guidelines for identifying the critical e-Service areas that should be improved. The matrix (Fig. 4) was designed to prioritize the service attributes based on their satisfaction rating and correlation with the overall satisfaction.

On one axis, the level of satisfaction is the indicator of performance, while on the other, Pearson’s correlation with overall satisfaction reflects the degree of importance. The higher the correlation, the more important the service attribute to the customer satisfaction. After plotting the correlation factor with the customer satisfaction rating, four quadrants in the e-Service enhancement priority matrix can be identified. The priority matrix displays the degree of performance across the degree of importance to reveal areas of high e-Service improvement requirement. The priority for enhancement is based on the quadrant in which the data point is located.

## 1. Quadrant I: Leverageable Strength

Service attributes here have a relatively strong correlation with overall customer satisfaction and also received a high satisfaction rating. These are the factors identified for building and maintaining a long-term customer relationship. The service attributes in this quadrant can be regarded as leverage in building customer relationships.

## 2. Quadrant II: High-priority Improvement

Attributes here have a strong correlation with overall e-Service, suggesting that improving these attributes could significantly raise the overall satisfaction. On the other hand, customers not satisfied with them gave low ratings. Thus these service attributes should be considered high-priority improvement items.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Data collection

Foundry chip manufacturing accounted for about 20.6% of all chip manufacturing in 2005. Pure-play foundry sales are expected to display a 21% CAGR from 2005 through 2010, almost double the 11% CAGR of total IC industry as more companies depend on outsourcing. TSMC held 46% of the global foundry market in 2004, and 49% market share for 2005.

The customer survey was designed to measure the aspects of e-Service provided by TSMC. The process ran from mid-March to late-April, 2001. The respondent was expected to have an overall understanding of the buyer–supplier relationship.

## 4.2. The survey instrument

In addition to the general company information, respondents were asked to rate their awareness of new e-Services, and the perceived importance them to business success. The survey contained two categories of questions dealing with overall e-Service satisfaction levels and e-Service attributes. Overall satisfaction ratings for e-Service were based on the respondent’s evaluation of TSMC’s performance. This measure could have been an absolute evaluation of TSMC or a comparisone with its nearest competitors: generally other professional foundry companies, such as United Microelectronics Corp. or IDMs firms, such as IBM, which provide manufacturing services in addition to producing their own products.

The companies were asked to rate their satisfaction level on each of the following items for both TSMC and its nearest competitors:

1. Accuracy of logistics information (Logistic accuracy)—the accuracy and consistency of information in the area of logistics, such as wafer status report, shipping report, etc.

2. Accuracy of design and engineering information (Design/Engineering Accuracy)—the information accuracy and consistency in the area of design and engineering, such as design rule checks, command files, in-line monitoring of data, etc.

3. Ease of locating information (Ease of locating)—the ease in finding information through navigational structure and search functions.

4. Information availability—the scope of information provided in design, engineering, and logistics.

5. Platform functionality (Functionality)—availability and usefulness of platform capability levels, such as personalization, help functions, search engines, etc.

6. System performance—the response time to achieve interaction with the portal, in functions such as navigation, searching, and document downloading.

7. Scope of services—the scope of present applications, such as technical document downloading, online order taking, etc.

Service attributes 1 and 2 both deal with ‘‘Accuracy’’. The reasons for inaccuracies in design/engineering information (mainly technical papers), and logistics information (mainly wafer processing status data generated by an automatic system) are different. It was thus necessary to make this distinction prior to formulating the proper approach for improvement.

Responses to both sets of questions were assessed using 7-point Likert-type scales, ranging from a 1 for ‘Low level of satisfaction’ to a 7 for ‘High level of satisfaction’. Based upon the seven key attributes, identified in the literature review and a prior customer survey, Questions 1–7 were designed to incorporate IC foundry practice and knowledge. The correlations between overall e-Service satisfaction and the seven service attributes were identified in order to build up the e-Service enhancement priority matrix.

## 4.3. Sample description

The survey instrument was initially field tested with a few TSMC customers. After this, modifications were made and the questionnaires were posted on TSMC’s web site. One or more persons in each company could use the company password to log in to fill out the questionnaire. After a period of time, e-mails were sent to those customers who had not responded. For some key companies, follow-up phone interviews were placed to solicit feedback. All responses from the same company were consolidated to produce one overall result.

## 4.4. Testing for non-response bias

One major issue was the extent to which the respondents differed from non-respondents. Chi squared tests were conducted to determine if it was likely that nonrespondents differed from respondents in any systematic manner. The non-response bias testing compared the demographics of respondents and non-respondents using the nature of the customers (IDMs, Fabless, or System Houses) and their country of origin. No significant differences were found between the samples.

The survey respondents were either technical (design engineers, production planners) or managerial (foundry service, procurement). The number of engineering respondents was small (8 persons). T-tests for equality of means were performed to determine if the two types of respondents were sufficiently similar to be pooled for overall analysis. The test results on overall e-Service satisfaction were insignificant, both toward TSMC itself and to its major competitors.

The channels used for collecting data were mainly from the web (168 respondents), and 33 personal interviews were conducted for those who did not respond. An investigation about possible differences between the responses in these two channels was also conducted. The results on overall e-Service satisfaction toward TSMC itself were significantly different between the two channels. The sample mean from the web survey was significantly higher than that of personal interviewees (average mean 5.6 versus 5.0). To avoid this source of possible bias, we excluded the personal interviewees from our analaysis.

## 4.5. Segmentation analysis

Segmentation of responding companies can be based on company location, company size (revenue), or type. IDMs/Fabless segmentation, regarded as the most significant segmentation in the foundry industry, was used for this survey. This method has been used frequently due to the distinctive characteristics of each segment. IC foundry companies must adopt different strategies for these two categories of customer. IDMs firms (usually large-scale IC companies) cover all of the activities in the IC supply chain, whereas Fabless firms (usually smaller) focus solely on IC design, relying heavily on the manufacturing capability of foundry companies. Although IDMs firms still have around 80% of the worldwide wafer capacity, outsourcing to foundry companies has become a trend.

For foundry companies, it has become important to discover how to accelerate this trend and secure large outsourcing orders from IDMs companies. Because of their highly flexible, focused and innovative characteristics, Fabless companies have gradually increased their IC market share, resulting in a reduction of the share of IDMs companies. ## 5. Results and discussion

## 5.1. Rating the awareness and importance of e-Service

Table 3 shows that the awareness of new e-Service technology by Fabless firms is significantly lower than that of IDMs companies. This may be due to promotion resource allocation and other difficulties. Since outsourcing has become a trend in reducing the high risk of capital investment, IDMs firms have usually received more attention and resources from foundry companies because of the larger wafer capacity ordered from them than from Fabless firms. Thus more promotional activities, such as road shows, workshops, or customer visits are more likely for IDMs customers.

It is easier to promote new e-Service to IDMs firms because of their limited accounts than it is to the large number of geographically dispersed Fabless companies. Developing Internet-based promotional activities, such as e-mail marketing, online banners, or Fabless community forums can overcome the geography, time zone, and account number barriers.

When asked if e-Service is important to the success of their businesses, all respondents gave high scores: apparently they recognize that e-Service will play an increasingly important role in building a successful business relationship with IC foundry companies.

## 5.3. Rating service attributes for IDMs and Fabless respondents

Fig. 6 shows the outcome of the IDMs/Fabless segmentation analysis. Also, IDMs respondents may have had more difficulty in adopting the format and service interface from TSMC. On the other hand, due to their smaller size, Fabless firms usually had only a simple or basic IT system, thus bearing a lesser burden or difficulty in linking with the foundry systems. For TSMC alone, the satisfaction rating on service attributes indicated that the largest gaps between IDMs and Fabless firms were in system performance and platform functionality. Obviously, most customers, especially IDMs ones, were dissatisfied with poor system performance.

Coincidentally, the smallest gaps between TSMC and their nearest competitors was in system performance and logistical information accuracy. The reasons can be categorized mainly as ‘‘behind-firewall’’ and ‘‘beyond-firewall’’ ones. The first are related to Internet infrastructure such as traffic, routing, or bandwidth and were outside the service providers’ control. The second were caused by TSMC’s continuously adding new information and applications without making adjustments to memory capacity, calculation power, or architecture.

The inaccuracy of logistics information, such as the wafer status report, was caused by poor integration of back-office data source systems, usually computer integrated manufacturing (CIM) systems. When companies increased or upgraded their CIM systems within one of their factories, lack of standardization on input/ output frequency or format and data extraction criteria, etc., could lead to data inaccuracies or inconsistencies. Thus the existing systems had to be integrated or a centralized data center was needed.

## 5.6. Suggestions for improvement priorities

While system performance and logistics information accuracy are identified in the satisfaction rating as the primary targets for improvement, the e-Service enhancement priority matrix indicated that accuracy of logistical information had a higher correlation with overall e-Service satisfaction. Therefore the top priority in the improvement list should be enhancing the accuracy of logistics information, such as wafer status and shipping reports. Raising the system performance becomes the second priority. For Fabless firms, more online promotional campaigns, such as e-mail marketing and banner advertisements, should be deployed to raise the awareness of new e-Service features. For IDMs firms, the findings from the e-Service enhancement priority matrix strongly suggested the need for more platform functionality, such as personalization, for ease of use.

## 6. Conclusions

Management of customer relations through e-Service has created a situation involving complex dynamics, requiring a better understanding of the relations between the parts. With a large gap between a theoretical framework and practical realities, greater empirical research is needed to support models that help define factors of success in the enhancement of e-Service.

We have investigated the effect of type of e-Service in the IC foundry industry and developed a methodological approach for refining the service. Two main channels have been adopted by IC foundry companies: a web-based customer portal and direct system-to-system integration. Services required by customers throughout the life cycle are needed to help in building a collaborative relationship. To further improve the service, key service attributes in content, web site design and overall scope, have been found to measure the performance of e-Service. The service attributes were included in a customer satisfaction survey and investigated to discover their correlation with overall e-Service satisfaction.

Both IDMs and Fabless companies recognize the importance of e-Service in the success of their business. Fabless firms have a lower awareness of the new e-Service of TSMC than IDMs firms. Although all the ratings for TSMC are higher than their nearest competitors, their lowest score was in system performance, with the closest gaps being between system performance and accuracy of logistical information.

Our data showed that content quality, system design, and the scope of the service were the key attributes affecting successful implementation of e-Service. However, e-Service is only part of overall customer services in the IC foundry industry; the rest is carried out by company personnel, such as technical and logistical support teams. How to position the two complementary services for overall customer satisfaction can be the next research topics.

In order to provide customers with a total solution, e-Service along the IC supply chain – design support, mask making, foundry manufacturing, assembly, test, and transportation – needs to be integrated together. The consolidation of information exchange through Internet in engineering and logistics can significantly reduce the interaction cost. How to link IT systems and solutions together among various companies, based upon customers’ needs, is also a complicated but important topic for further study.
