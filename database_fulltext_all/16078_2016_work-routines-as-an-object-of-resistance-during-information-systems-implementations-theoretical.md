# Work routines as an object of resistance during information systems implementations: theoretical foundation and empirical evidence

Sven Laumer<sup>1</sup>, Christian Maier<sup>1</sup>, Andreas Eckhardt<sup>2</sup> and Tim Weitzel<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Otto-Friedrich University of Bamberg, Department for Information Systems and Services, Centre of Human Resources Information Systems, Bamberg, Germany; <sup>2</sup>German Graduate School of Management and Law (GGS), Heilbronn, Germany

Correspondence: Sven Laumer, Otto-Friedrich University of Bamberg, Department for Information Systems and Services, Centre of Human Resources Information Systems, An der Weberei 5, 96047 Bamberg, Germany. Tel: +0951 863 2873; Fax: +0951 863 2872

## Abstract

When implementing new information systems, organizations often face resistance behavior from employees who avoid or underutilize the system. We analyze the extent to which such user resistance behavior is explained by users’ perceptions of the technology compared with their perceptions of work routines. We developed a research model based on work system theory and evaluated it using a study (N = 106) of a human resources information system implementation in one organization. The results show that work routines are an object of resistance during IS implementations. We identify perceived usefulness and perceived ease of executing work routines as perceptions of work routines during an IS implementation that have a strong influence on user resistance behavior. In addition, our results show that the perceived ease of executing the work routines mediates the impact of perceived ease of use on user resistance behavior. In practice, our findings imply that interventions during IT implementations should focus on both the new technology and changing work routines. ## Introduction

Newly implemented information systems (IS) in organizations are often not used to their full potential (Melville et al, 2004) with employees resisting the new IS (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). This resistance behavior is frequently observed with ‘comprehensive commercial software packages with embedded industry best practice business processes’ (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013, p. 1113). One common example of such a comprehensive package are enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). While organizations continue to implement these systems to support effective and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient work routines and to gain strategic advantages (Seddon et al, 2010; Strong & Volkoff, 2010), their inability to understand and manage employees’ perceptions during IS implementations has been recognized as a key reason for implementation failure (Markus, 2004; Cohen, 2005; Jasperson et al, 2005).

User resistance research focuses on explaining why and how individuals resist IS implementations (Williams et al, 2009; Laumer and Eckhardt, 2012; Polites & Karahanna, 2012) and aims at guiding organizations on how to manage employees’ perceptions and behaviors. Yet, there are still substantial knowledge gaps and related challenges for organizations (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) that struggle to realize the full potential of a new IS (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). A key insight of extant resistance research is that user resistance behavior directed toward IS implementation is caused by perceptions of different objects of resistance (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). These include perceptions of the new IS itself (e.g., Venkatesh et al, 2003; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010) but also of how work routines are affected by the new IS. A promising avenue for future user resistance research Venkatesh (2006) proposes differentiating between individuals’ perceptions of technology and their perceptions of work routines. From a theoretical perspective, this ought to give us a deeper understanding of why and how employees resist IS implementations to then derive better managerial suggestions to manage employees’ perceptions and behaviors during IS implementation.

Following this strand, the goal of this research is to better understand employee resistance to IS implementation and how it is related to perceptions of the new technology – a traditional core focus of user resistance analyses – vs perceptions of work routines. On the basis of work system theory (Alter, 2013), we developed a research model that enabled us to differentiate users’ perceptions of technology from their perceptions of work routines as determinants of user resistance behavior. Figure 1 shows the nomological network used to investigate the impact of employees’ perceptions of work routines on user resistance during IS implementations and to provide an answer to our research question:

How do IS users’ perceptions of technology and work routines in<sup>fl</sup>uence their resistance to a new IS implemented in an organization?

In the following, we <sup>fi</sup>rst discuss the theoretical background for our arguments about user resistance and work routines. We then develop a model of user resistance to technology and to work routines. Next, we present the methodology and the results of our empirical study of user resistance to the implementation of a new human resources information system (HRIS) as an example of an IS inducing changes in employees’ work routines. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for future research and practice.

## IS implementation

An IS implementation is regarded as an organizational change ‘directed toward diffusing appropriate information technology within a user community’ (Cooper & Zmud, 1990, p. 124). IS research provides a rich body of knowledge about IS implementation, which, according to Bala & Venkatesh (2013), can be grouped into three categories.

First, some research focuses on the implementation process itself (e.g., Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Yetton et al, 1999; Soh et al, 2000; Hong & Kirn, 2002; Robey et al, 2002; Liang et al, 2007). Research in this area theorizes and studies the process of IS implementation, interventions used to implement an IS successfully, and organizational change processes associated with IS implementation (e.g., Shanks, 2000; Al-Mudimigh et al, 2001; Akkermans & van Helden, 2002; Markus & Mao, 2004; Scheepers, 2006; Wagner & Newell, 2007; Goh et al, 2011; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). This research generally distinguishes between the pre-implementation phase of IS implementation, which is the period before the new IS is available for use in the organization (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010; Schwarz et al, 2014), the implementation phase, which is the period when the new IS has been implemented and employees are starting to use it (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005; Bruque et al, 2008; Schwarz et al, 2014), and the post-implementation phase, which is the period when the new IS is becoming or has become part of the organization (Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Rodon et al, 2011; Schwarz et al, 2014).

A second research strand examines the impact of IS implementation on an organization (e.g., Shang & Seddon, 2002; Gattiker & Goodhue, 2005; Cotteleer & Bendoly, 2006). For example, such research has demonstrated how IS implementation positively affects <sup>fi</sup>rm performance (Grant, 2003; Melville et al, 2004; Chang, 2006; Seddon et al, 2010; Sykes et al, 2014). In this context, it has been shown how different characteristics of individuals, groups, technologies, and the organizational context in<sup>fl</sup>uence employee performance (e.g., Tesluk & Mathieu, 1999; Carson et al, 2007; Davidson & Chismar, 2007; Quigley et al, 2007; Sykes et al, 2014). Furthermore, it has also been demonstrated that IS implementation only provides value to an organization when complementary investments in organizational resources and the human IT resource are undertaken (Melville et al, 2004). Hence, the positive impact of an IS implementation does not only rely on the IT itself, but also on the characteristics of complementary organizational resources such as employees, groups of employees, work routines, or other organizational elements (Strong & Volkoff, 2010).

A third area of research investigates employees’ reactions to IS implementation (e.g., Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Volkoff et al, 2007; Goh et al, 2011) to explain why and how employees accept or resist IS implementation (Williams et al, 2009; Schwarz et al, 2014) and how organizations can address and in<sup>fl</sup>uence employees’ reactions (Sharma & Yetton, 2003, 2007; Venkatesh & Bala, 2008; Rodon et al, 2011; Ngwenyama & Nielsen, 2014).

As we are interested in employees’ reactions to IS implementation and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in user resistance to the IS, our study draws on research from this third category that explains why employees resist IS implementation.

## User resistance to IS implementations

Since the technology acceptance model (TAM; Davis, 1989) was introduced in the late 1980s, a tremendous body of subsequent research has focused on extending, modifying, replicating, and developing competing models of technology acceptance (Venkatesh et al, 2003; Williams et al, 2009) in order to explain the degree to which individuals accept a new IS implemented in an organization. Some approaches also focus on user resistance, arguing that user resistance is more than simply the opposite of acceptance (Cenfetelli, 2004, Turel, 2015).

In general, research theorizes user resistance as a subjective process based psychologically at the individual level ( Jermier et al, 1994), which is also referred to as negative psychology inertia (Besson & Rowe, 2012). This subjective process starts with an interaction between initial individual conditions and an object related to the work system into which a new IS is introduced. Initial individual conditions include personality (e.g., Devaraj et al, 2008), skills (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), capabilities (e.g., Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), and experiences (e.g., Taylor & Todd, 1995). When faced with imminent change, an individual develops perceptions of relevant objects of resistance that determine the extent of his or her resistance behavior (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). Table 1 provides an overview of objects of resistance focused on by prior user resistance research, illustrating that the object of resistance may be the new IS itself (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007). Typically, if a user perceives characteristics of these objects as rather negative or as a threat, he or she will demonstrate resistance behaviors (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), that is, an active or passive, overt or covert negative behavioral response (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009).

User resistance has mainly been studied in relation to IS implementation in organizations, but some approaches have also focused on user resistance in different contexts, such as social networking sites in a private setting (see Table 1, research context column). Approaches focusing on IS implementation in organizations provide insights into the pre- and post-implementation phases of IS implementation and identify several perceptions of objects of resistance that tend to induce user resistance behavior.

Most approaches focus on the technology itself as an object of resistance, identifying perceived usefulness (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Maier et al, 2013), relative advantage (Polites & Karahanna, 2012), value (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), compatibility (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007), ease of use (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Maier et al, 2013), complexity (Klaus & Blanton, 2010), and threats (Selander & Henfridsson, 2012) as important determinants of user resistance behavior to a newly implemented IS.

In addition, several approaches study the change from the status quo as an object of resistance, identifying resistance to change (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007), perceived threats (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007), switching bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009), and switching costs (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009) as causes of user resistance to IS implementations. Such research indicates that perceptions of the change in<sup>fl</sup>uence perceptions of the technology (e.g., Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Additional objects of resistance investigated by prior research are summarized in Table 1.

While most approaches focus on the technology, work routines have rarely been considered as an object of resistance so far. Klaus & Blanton (2010) indicate that perceived workload can lead to a rather negative perception of work routines and to user resistance behavior. Meissonier & Houzé (2010) discuss potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts related to work routines and Marakas & Hornik (1996) identify stress and fear related to work routines as drivers of user resistance. Generally speaking, these approaches have used qualitative research to support the notion that individual perceptions of work routines can in<sup>fl</sup>uence user resistance behavior. However, prior user resistance research has not yet investigated the relationship between work routines and any other object of resistance.

The following provides a theoretical basis for studying the impact of work routines as an object of resistance on user resistance behavior, by summarizing research on work routines and developing a research model to explain the speci<sup>fi</sup>c in<sup>fl</sup>uence of individual perceptions of work routines on user resistance.

## Work routines as object of resistance

Unlike most research focusing on technology as an object of resistance, this study focuses on work routines as an object of resistance. This section discusses research related to work routines and introduces the theories we build on to differentiate perceptions of technology from perceptions of work routines.

Work routines are de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘temporal structures that are often used as a way of accomplishing organizational work’ (Feldman, 2000, p. 611). They are ‘repeated patterns of behavior that are bound by rules and customs and that do not change very much from one iteration to another’ (Feldman, 2000, p. 611). From an individual perspective, employees perform work by following work routines and by using information, technology, and other resources to produce products or services for customers (Alter, 2013). Hence, work routines are important in organizations because a lot of the work in organizations is performed through routines and employees are used to working with routines to perform their tasks (Feldman, 2000).

One theory that explicitly distinguishes between technologies and work routines as different components is Alter’s (2013) work system theory. This theory proposes that employees working in speci<sup>fi</sup>c work systems perform work routines using technologies and information to produce products and services for customers (Alter, 2013). As a work system must contain at least one work routine to accomplish anything, work routines are a core concept of work systems (Alter, 2013). The underlying assumption is that work routines need to be aligned with participants, information, and technologies. Hence, work system theory links participants and speci<sup>fi</sup>c technologies that they use to perform work routines within a work system. In summary, work system theory (Alter, 2013, Figure 2) provides the basis for theorizing about perceptions of work routines because it considers technology and work routines as two different and related components of a work system.

When IT-induced organizational changes caused by IS implementation are considered using work system theory, it becomes clear that while the technology component of a work system changes, the IS implementation also impacts work routines (Alter, 2013). For example, when a comprehensive commercial software packages is introduced, new work routines are embedded in the technology (Volkoff et al, 2007) and the new IS provides the material aspect for the work routines. In this case, the IS implementation requires changes to existing or the deployment of new work routines (Markus & Tanis, 2000; Morris & Venkatesh, 2010). Some research has focused on the role of job characteristics (Morris & Venkatesh, 2010; Venkatesh et al, 2010) or on employees’ perceptions of changes at various levels of work conditions following an IS implementation (Davis & Hufnagel, 2007). It has also been demonstrated that employees often resist IS implementation because they are afraid that their jobs will be radically different after the implementation (Boudreau & Robey, 2005; Morris & Venkatesh, 2010; Sykes et al, 2014). Furthermore, qualitative research approaches provide evidence that individuals name work routines when asked about con<sup>fl</sup>icts or challenges associated with IS implementation (Marakas & Hornik, 1996; Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010). It has also been shown that functional affordances of an IS such as new, missing, incompatible, or advanced features shape the adaption of new work routines as well as the manifestation of workarounds (Goh et al, 2011).

The central determinants of user resistance to new IS are, on the one hand, perceptions of the new technology, which in the context of a comprehensive commercial software package to some degree determines work routines (Volkoff et al, 2007), and perceptions of work routines on the other. As Venkatesh (2006, p. 501) notes, ‘[although] research has examined various aspects of [work routine] change, little research has focused on the individual employee and studied the drivers of [work routine] adoption by employees, the factors in<sup>fl</sup>uencing resistance, the impacts of [work routine] change on employees and potential interventions to ease the transition’.

Venkatesh (2006) proposes two possible explanations for why perceptions of technology and work routines have not yet been considered separately in individual-level technology adoption or user resistance research. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, research looked at fairly simple software systems that were designed to support, not change existing routines. Even in cases that included work routine change, researchers typically treated the entire technology solution as a single entity and did not explicitly model technology or work routine characteristics (e.g., Venkatesh & Davis, 2000; Venkatesh et al, 2003). To address this gap, our research draws on work system theory (Alter, 2013) to understand the impact of perceptions of technology and work routines on individual resistance to newly implemented IS. The following section develops our research model in more detail.

## Research model

On the basis of work system theory (Alter, 2013), we posit that user resistance to IS implementation will be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by perceptions of the technology and the work routine (see Figure 3). Consistent with prior work, we postulate that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, as perceptions of the technology as an object of resistance (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007), are two major technology perceptions (see Table 1). Extending prior work, we further postulate that perceptions of work routines affected by IS implementation will also explain why users resist a newly implemented IS in an organization. Table 2 summarizes the core constructs of our research model that we will use for hypotheses development.

## Technology perceptions as determinant of user resistance behavior

Several research approaches have focused on enablers, inhibitors, and consequences of an individual’s technology usage (Williams et al, 2009). Most of these approaches build on the TAM (Davis et al, 1989; Davis, 1989), which suggests that an individual’s behavior can be explained by perceived usefulness and by perceived ease of use (Davis et al, 1989). The TAM led to a tremendous volume of research resulting in various extensions, modi<sup>fi</sup>cations, replications, competing (Venkatesh et al, 2007), and unifying models (Venkatesh et al, 2003). As the TAM has been used to predict user resistance behavior (Martinko et al, 1996; Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Maier et al, 2013, see Table 1 for a detailed overview), we will also adopt the two TAM variables that re<sup>fl</sup>ect perceptions of the technology for our research model.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst TAM variable is called perceived ease of use. It explains user acceptance, which has also been utilized in user resistance research to explain user resistance behavior (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007). It has been further speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in the UTAUT as ‘the degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her to attain gains in job performance’ (Venkatesh et al, 2003, p. 447). Both models suggest that perceived ease of use and usefulness are two salient cognitive determinants of user behavior. These two variables are also used to measure the perceptions of the technology as an object of resistance (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007) and to explain user resistance behavior (Maier et al, 2013; Laumer et al, 2015).

When employees perceive that the new IS reduces their effectiveness, the negative perception may lead to user resistance behavior in terms of a lower intention to use (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007) and opposing to or not agreeing with the new way of working with the new IS (Laumer et al, 2015). Applying the model of resistance to IS implementations (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), we argue that employees may feel threatened in their interactions with certain characteristics of the new IS (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). If the employees perceive that the new IS will not provide the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and will not be useful for their work (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007), the employees’ evaluations of the new IS will become perceptions of a threat, as they do not expect the system to support their work, but rather to hinder them from performing their work effectively (Alter, 2013). This may put additional cognitive burden on employees to accomplish their work in the expected way. Consequently, when low usefulness is anticipated user resistance behavior will occur (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). Thus, based on prior research, we derive a <sup>fi</sup>rst hypothesis about technology as an object of resistance:

## <sup>H1:</sup> The lower the perceived usefulness of a new technology, the higher the user resistance behavior.

Moreover, when employees perceive that a new IS is not easy to use, requires extra efforts, or is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to learn, they will resist using the new IS (Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007) or refuse to comply with the planned change (Laumer et al, 2015). In addition, if employees have a negative perception of an IS’s ease of use, their overall job satisfaction can decrease and turnover intentions may increase (Maier et al, 2013). Discussing this phenomenon through the lens of the model of resistance to IS implementations (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), we argue that employees may feel threatened in their interactions with certain characteristics of the new IS (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) because it takes them extra effort to learn how to use the new technology. Employees who perceive the new IS as not easy to use and perceive that it will require a great effort to learn how to use, it will consider the usability of the new IS as a threat requiring extra effort to use the IS and taking time away from other tasks. This will hinder them from working ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently, which puts additional cognitive burden on them to accomplish their work in the expected way. Hence, with low perceived ease of use, employees will show user resistance behavior (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005). We assume:

## <sup>H2:</sup> The lower the perceived ease of use of a new technology, the higher the user resistance behavior.

In line with other scholars investigating the perceived usefulness and ease of use of a new IS, we argue that when employees feel that they do not have the ability to use a new IS, they do also not believe that the new IS will bene<sup>fi</sup>t them (Davis, 1989; Davis et al, 1989). Hence, following prior research we assume:

<sup>H3:</sup> The higher the perceived ease of use of a new technology, the higher the perceived usefulness.

## Work routine perceptions as determinants of user resistance behavior

Implementing modern IS in organizations often requires the adoption of signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly new work routines (Orlikowski, 2000; Venkatesh, 2006; Goh et al, 2011). Work routines are the major component of a work system in which employees uses technologies and information to produce products or services for customers (Alter, 2013). As employees’ perceptions of work routines vary (Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010), negative perceptions of work routines can determine user resistance behavior.

Consequently, understanding work routines better may help organizations increase the acceptance and effectiveness of an IS (Venkatesh, 2006; Goh et al, 2011) by establishing new work routines or changing existing work routines effectively (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). It may be argued that the intent of the IS implementation is to establish new work routines that will lower labor costs, accelerate transactions, improve ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency, and enable users to provide better service to customers and other stakeholders (Strong & Volkoff, 2010). Nonetheless, as outlined in work system theory (Alter, 2013), work routines are related but distinct from the technology itself. By nature, work routines and employees’ perceptions about them affect the organization’s ability to achieve the goals associated with IS implementation (Volkoff et al, 2007; Alter, 2013). It is the transformation of existing work routines and disruption induced by new routines that trigger symbolic expressions of users (Goh et al, 2011). As outlined above, this study applies work system theory to explain user resistance to a newly implemented IS, treating work routines and technologies as two distinct components of a work system (Alter, 2013). We thus consider perceptions of the work routines (Klaus & Blanton, 2010) and argue that these perceptions will have an impact on user resistance, such as when employees feel threatened by work routines and develop negative beliefs about these routines which lead to user resistance behavior (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005).

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne our <sup>fi</sup>rst work routine variable, perceived ease of executing work routines, as the degree to which a person believes that the new routines associated with a new IS will be easy to learn and execute effortlessly. When a new IS is implemented, employees have to follow new work routines and make the effort to learn how to execute the new tasks (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Employees may perceive it as dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to handle new and emerging tasks, eliminated tasks, a new sequence of tasks, new access to information or new products and services produced by these routines (Alter, 2013). Therefore, during an IS implementation employees may <sup>fi</sup>nd it more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to execute new work routines than work routines they had followed before the IS implementation. When employees have to put extra effort into learning and executing new work routines, they have to work harder and have to spend more time on the new work routines (Marakas & Hornik, 1996; Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013).

Considering this phenomenon using the model of resistance to IS implementations (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), employees may feel threatened in their interactions with certain characteristics of the new work routines (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) because it requires more effort to execute the new work routines. This hinders employees from working ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently and that puts additional cognitive burden on employees in order to accomplish work in the expected way. Consequently, these potentially negative perceptions of the ease of executing work routines are perceived as a threat and lead to user resistance behavior (Marakas & Hornik, 1996; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Klaus & Blanton, 2010). We thus hypothesize:

## <sup>H4:</sup> The lower the perceived ease of executing work routines, the higher the user resistance behavior.

We de<sup>fi</sup>ne our second work routine variable, perceived usefulness of work routines, as the degree to which a person believes that working with the new routines will enhance her job performance.

Consistent with our arguments for H4, when employees feel that they do not have the ability and/or resources to execute the new work routines, they do not believe that they have capabilities to perform their tasks effectively (Bandura, 1997; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). New work routines can make employees fear that they may not have the ability to perform their jobs effectively (Marakas & Hornik, 1996). For example, they may feel pressure to accomplish tasks by deadlines determined by their supervisors or deliver products or services in a timely manner to an internal or external customer (Alter, 2013). However, when organizations implement a new IS, work routines are often standardized, which can make employees’ work processes rigid (Volkoff et al, 2007; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Hence, if employees believe that the new work routines do not support the overall objective of the work system and are obstacles to reaching their overall goals (Alter, 2013), they may have an unfavorable opinion about the new work routines (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013).

Considering this phenomenon and using the model of resistance to IS implementations (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), we argue employees may feel threatened in their interactions with certain characteristics of the new work routines (Volkoff et al, 2007) if they perceive that the new work routines reduce their effectiveness. Hence, employees may perceive the new work routines as less useful for performing work in the respective work system (Alter, 2013). As the new work routines hinder employees from working effectively, this puts additional cognitive burden on employees to accomplish work in the expected way. Consequently, these rather negative perceptions of the usefulness of new work routines are perceived as a threat and lead to user resistance behavior (Klaus & Blanton, 2010). We, therefore, hypothesize:

## <sup>H5:</sup> The lower the perceived usefulness of work routines, the higher the user resistance behavior.

In our arguments for H5 we focused on the relation between the rather negative perception of the ease of executing new work routines and the perceived usefulness of new work routines. When employees have to spend more time learning how to execute their new work routines, they might perceive that the new work routines are preventing them from doing their work effectively (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Hence, the rather negative perceptions of the ease of executing work routines also induce rather negative perceptions of the usefulness of work routines. Consequently, we assume:

<sup>H6:</sup> The lower the perceived ease of executing work routines, the lower the perceived usefulness of work routines.

## Technology perceptions as determinants of work routine perceptions

According to work system theory, work routines are not only generally important to consider when implementing an IS, but they also interrelate with the technologies used in executing the work routines (Alter, 2013). When an organization installs a comprehensive commercial software package, it is the role of technology to embed organizational elements. This material aspect affects how employees are able to interact with those elements embedded in a technology (Volkoff et al, 2007). As it is the technology that embeds work routines and gives them a material aspect that employees can interact with (Volkoff et al, 2007) and as functional affordances such as removing or adding technological features transform existing routines (Goh et al, 2011), we argue that perceptions of the technology are a determinant of how employees perceive work routines.

In this context, Robey et al (2002) and Hakkinen & Hilmola (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nd that employees develop perceptions of technology characteristics when they <sup>fi</sup>rst start using a new IS and Bala & Venkatesh (2013) <sup>fi</sup>nd that when employees start using an IS to execute their work routines, they will take their perceptions of technology characteristics into consideration. For instance, if employees <sup>fi</sup>nd the new IS to be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to understand and use, they are more likely to <sup>fi</sup>nd it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to execute their new work routines (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013) if these work routines are embedded in the new IS (Volkoff et al, 2007). Moreover, Goh et al (2011) indicate that new, missing, incompatible, or advanced features in<sup>fl</sup>uence routines as new actions, new sequences, or workarounds occur. Hence, functional affordances of the IS in<sup>fl</sup>uences the symbolic expression of routines as the functional affordances shape the adaption of work routines (Goh et al, 2011). Thus, working effectively and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently using a new IS requires an understanding on the various aspects of work routines embedded in the IS (Volkoff et al, 2007; Alter, 2013; Bala & Venkatesh, 2013), such as how to access and navigate different screens and the information needed on each of these screens and for various options. This may put an enormous cognitive burden on an employee performing a work routine to produce products or services for internal or external customers.

Hence, when employees <sup>fi</sup>rst start using an IS, they discover the complexity of the system and that the complexity is also related to the embedded work routine (Volkoff et al, 2007). Therefore, when employees believe that using the new IS is complex, they also perceive that performing a work routine embedded in the IS is complex, because the IS dictates the material aspect for interacting with the work routine (Volkoff et al, 2007). Consequently, employees feel that because of the complexity of the IS, they now have more tasks to do in their work processes, as they are not only learning the new IS, but also using the IS to perform their work routines (Bala & Venkatesh, 2013). Hence, we assume:

## Research methodology

To evaluate the proposed research model, we accompanied one of the world’s leading automotive suppliers throughout the implementation of a new HRIS. The main objective of adopting the new HRIS was to enhance technology support for the recruiting process and to introduce work routines that would make it possible to manage tasks faster and improve the perception of the organization in the job market. The system was designed to integrate the recruiting activities at <sup>fi</sup>ve different plants in Germany, whose 150 HR managers all have access to the new e-recruiting system. The project began at the start of 2008, when the company wished to replace its legacy system and optimize and standardize the company-speci-<sup>fi</sup>c recruiting process.

The holistic recruiting process starts with identifying hiring needs. On the basis of the de<sup>fi</sup>ned job description, a job advertisement is published either in traditional or online media. In response, job seekers submit their applications which are then evaluated by recruiters. Recruiters also communicate with applicants to further manage the selection process. Finally, a job is offered and a contract is signed (Lee, 2007). The new recruiting process follows these six steps and induces new and re-designed work routines compared to the old recruiting process, which are now completely embedded in the new e-recruiting system (see Table 4 for a comparison).

The process is initiated when there is a vacancy in any of the organization’s departments in the <sup>fi</sup>ve plants in Germany. Before the implementation of the new e-recruiting system the organization’s departments reported their vacancies via email or letters. With the new system, they have to enter vacancies into SAP E-Recruiting directly, allowing the HR department can access and approve hiring needs directly in the new e-recruiting system.

The next step is posting a job ad. With the new e-recruiting system, job ads are designed by the responsible recruiter at the respective plant using standard templates and approved by the corporate competence center. Only approved job ads can be published. The work routines of publishing job ads thus changed in two ways: using the old system, recruiters were allowed to use their own templates and publish job ads without any approval from any other party.

After the ad is published on the company’s website, at online job portals such as Monster.com, and/or in print media, applicants have to submit their CV portfolio via the company’s website using an email application or mail a paper application. The new system encourages applicants to use the CV portfolio on the company’s website, whereas the old work system only allowed applicants to submit paper-based and e-mail applications, which were printed for further manual processing. The new system stores digital applications automatically in the HRIS database and paper applications have to be entered manually.

In the old recruiting work system, the HR department sent printed applications to the organization’s departments and also communicated with applicants using printed letters based on individual templates. Within the new e-recruiting work system, all communication is initiated in the SAP E-Recruiting system using standard templates and guidelines, and sent via e-mail. Moreover, the organization’s departments have access to applications in SAP E-Recruiting, which are related to their respective vacancy, such that no application has to be sent anymore.

For selecting and evaluating appropriate applications recruiters had to manually prescreen applications and make a shortlist of quali<sup>fi</sup>ed candidates. In the new e-recruiting work system, applications are prescreened and a shortlist of applications is generated automatically. In both cases recruiters and managers of the organization’s department are responsible for conducting job interviews or assessing applicants.

If an applicant is hired, his or her data has to be transferred to the general SAP HR system, which is done automatically in both the old and the new work system. With the new e-recruiting system, applications of quali<sup>fi</sup>ed not hired candidates are transferred to the new central organization-wide talent pool, which every recruiter can access and search when a vacancy arises. Within the old recruiting system each recruiter could, but did not have to, generate such a talent pool by herself.

In summary, the company introduced several changes to work routines. A core aspect of the project was to eliminate misunderstandings caused by old routines that gave the organization a bad image in the job market as an unstructured and inconsistent employer. This was achieved by standardizing the recruiting process so that recruiting followed the same processes in all <sup>fi</sup>ve German plants. As a result, individuals work routines changed as media disruptions were eliminated by embedding all work routines in the new e-recruiting system.

We gathered employees’ opinions about the new e-recruiting system and the changes described above before the implementation and ‘go-live’ phases of the project in June and July 2010, ensuring a pre-implementation focus on user resistance (Meissonier & Houzé, 2010). We applied a mixed method research approach (Venkatesh et al, 2013), using both qualitative and quantitative data. The qualitative data was collected in a pre-test, which focused on the distinction between work routines and the technology when developing perceptions. Using interviews we are able to conclude whether employees have different perceptions of the technology and work routines. The quantitative data was collected in an empirical study used to test the assumed effects of perception of the technology and work routines on user resistance behavior. Hence, we applied a developmental mixed method approach as we used the qualitative study to develop constructs and the quantitative data to test our hypotheses (Venkatesh et al, 2013).

## Research results

This section presents the results of our research. We used structural equation modeling and partial least squares (PLS) (Chin, 1998a) for validation. We used SmartPLS 3.1.9 (Ringle et al, 2015) to test our structural equation model.

## Measurement model

As all perceptual beliefs about the technology and work routines, the alternative theoretical explanations and control variables, as well as the resulting user resistance are measured by re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators, content validity, indicator reliability, construct reliability, and discriminant validity need to be ensured to validate the measurement model (Bagozzi & Yi, 1988).

Content validity We utilize items of constructs that have proven to be suitable measurement items in prior research, modifying these items where necessary to <sup>fi</sup>t the HR context. For the perceptions of work routines, we developed new items based on existing ones. Our measurement model and the development of the new items is described below. To ensure content validity, we discussed the items with the project managers of the implementation project we observed. An empirical pre-test was conducted with <sup>fi</sup>ve of the organization’s HR employees.

To capture an employee’s beliefs about the ease of use and usefulness of the implemented e-recruiting system, we used measurement items introduced by Davis et al (1989) and speci<sup>fi</sup>ed by Venkatesh et al (2003), modifying the scales to <sup>fi</sup>t the HR domain context. Resistance to change was measured using items proposed by Oreg (2006). Following Polites & Karahanna (2012), we designed resistance to change as a second-order formative, <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective, multidimensional construct using an affective, a behavioral, and a cognitive dimension (Polites & Karahanna, 2012). User resistance was measured as recommended by Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) using items to capture an individual’s intention to resist HRIS-induced changes both to the embedding technology and the embedded work routines. For dispositional resistance, we used the scale proposed and evaluated by Oreg (2003) and Oreg et al (2008), and designed dispositional resistance to change as a second-order formative, <sup>fi</sup>rst-order re<sup>fl</sup>ective, and multidimensional construct following Polites & Karahanna (2012).

To measure the perceptions of the work routines, we had to develop new variables and corresponding measurement items. This approach is in line with methods that have been used in prior research developing new scales (e.g., Chin et al, 1997; Salisbury et al, 2002; Ragu-Nathan et al, 2008). In a <sup>fi</sup>rst step, we searched for literature involving perceptions of either work routines or technologies in relation to user resistance research (see Table 1). In this step we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the work by Klaus & Blanton (2010) and Marakas & Hornik (1996); however, these approaches only provide evidence for work routines as an object of resistance but do not provide speci<sup>fi</sup>c variables for capturing perceptions of work routines. To identify such variables, we relied on the interviews in our pre-test to identify perceived ease of executing and perceived usefulness of work routines as the two most important perceptions mentioned by our interviewees. We then developed new items for these variables by using the measurement model of TAM (Davis, 1989) and UTAUT (Venkatesh et al, 2003), changing the objective of the items to perceptions of work routines. We then assessed the reliability and construct validity of these new items using Q-sorting (Landis & Koch, 1977; Nahm et al, 2002, N = 64, see Appendix Table A1). In addition, we also evaluated construct reliability and discriminant validity using the survey conducted to perform factor analyses and to calculate Cronbach’s α (CA) (Nunnally, 1967; Hair, 2010) for the perceptions of the technology and the perceptions of work routines (see Table A5 in the Appendix). This approach revealed three items for each variable, which we used in validating the proposed research model. Table A3 in the Appendix summarizes the measurement model used.

Moreover, using the project setting of the e-recruiting implementation enabled us to distinguish clearly in the survey between the technology and the work routines to ensure content validity. Project management created two different project teams: one responsible for technology issues, and one for the new work routines. ## Common method bias

In line with Podsakoff and colleagues (Podsakoff, 1986; Podsakoff et al, 2003; Mackenzie et al, 2011), we account for the fact that individuals’ social desirability in selfreported data (Hufnagel & Conca, 1994; Harrison et al, 1996) could imply common method biases (CMBs). We conducted a statistical analysis to identify the extent of CMB by including an additional CMB factor in the PLS model (Podsakoff et al, 2003; Williams et al, 2003) that contains every indicator of the origin model. The remaining origin factors are transformed into single item constructs and we compare the ratio of R<sup>2</sup> (96:1) as well as the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient (1116:1) with the CMB factor to those without the CMB factor. Acknowledging the critique of this technique by Chin et al (2012), we <sup>fi</sup>nd no sign of CMB in<sup>fl</sup>uence, although there is still a remaining possibility for a CMB in<sup>fl</sup>uence. In addition, we used Harman’s single factor test that indicates the presence of CMB, if a single factor emerges using an exploratory factor analysis (Podsakoff et al, 2003). As this test reveals a non-single factor structure, additional evidence can be provided that our data might not be affected by CMB.

## Limitations

As with all empirical research, our results are limited by several issues. For example, the results represent only one example of one particular change in technology and work routines in one particular organization in one particular cultural setting. In our study we focused on an e-recruiting system. Therefore, the impact of an individual’s evaluation of the technology and work routines may differ in other departments such as marketing, <sup>fi</sup>nance, or accounting. Furthermore, there may be differences in other organizations with different organizational cultures. In addition, there may be country-speci<sup>fi</sup>c characteristics that lead to different results for organizations based in different countries. The results may also differ in a setting where employees are free to decide whether they want to use the system or the new process.

The results are further limited by the timing of the study. We conducted the study during the implementation of the new system when participating employees were aware of the new system and the work routine changes, and when some were involved in different project teams. Moreover, there had been training and initial steps in working with the new system had been conducted. Therefore, our results only evaluated the impact of technology and work routine perceptions at a relatively early stage of IS implementation. The results may change over the system’s lifecycle.

## Discussion and implications

The results presented in this paper provide empirical evidence that resistance to the implementation of an IS is caused not only by the characteristics of the technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003) but also by perceptions of work routines. On the basis of our observation of one particular IS implementation, we can answer the research question by showing that resistance to IS implementations is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced even more strongly by the perceptions of work routines than the technology. The HR personnel we observed indicate that their resistance toward the new e-recruiting system is driven more by perceived ease of executing the new recruiting work routines than by perceived ease of use and usefulness of the new e-recruiting system. Therefore, the results extend research on user resistance by theorizing and providing empirical evidence of work routines as an important object of resistance (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005), in addition to the proven in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the new technology (Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Klaus et al, 2010; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010) and the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of the incumbent system (Polites & Karahanna, 2012). The results and their implications for theory and practice are summarized in Table 6 and will be discussed in the following in more detail.

## Implications for theory

On the basis of our theorizing on work routines as an object of resistance and our empirical study, we can derive the following implications and contribution to theory.

Work routines as an object of resistance The results provide empirical evidence for work routines as an object of resistance when implementing a new IS (Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Klaus et al, 2010). This research identi<sup>fi</sup>es major issues related to user resistance and categorizes user resistance determinants. On the basis of a three-phase multimethod qualitative approach, they assumed that process issues are important user resistance determinants. In addition, Goh et al (2011) outlined in their qualitative work focusing on healthcare IS that through users’ symbolic expression the IS interacts with work routines and that the functional affordances transform existing routines and shapes the adaption of new routines.

Our approach allows us to validate empirically that the perceptions of work routine changes are a strong predictor of user resistance. These results extend general research on user resistance, as Lapointe & Rivard (2005) theorize in general that user resistance is based on an object of resistance and most user resistance research focus either on the new IS (e.g., Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Meissonier & Houzé, 2010; Kim, 2011), the incumbent system (e.g., Polites & Karahanna, 2012), or the change from the status quo (e.g., Bhattacherjee & Hikmet, 2007; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009). Hence, we extend prior research, summarized in Table 1, by adding new empirical evidence that perceptions of work routines as an object of resistance lead to user resistance behavior. In summary, we can con<sup>fi</sup>rm the effects proposed by prior more qualitative research approaches (e.g. Klaus & Blanton, 2010; Klaus et al, 2010; Goh et al, 2011).

Our proposed model is a <sup>fi</sup>rst step toward integrating work system theory with technology acceptance or user resistance research. To differentiate clearly between technology and work routines as objects of resistance, we apply work system theory (Alter, 2013). This research is in line with the call of Orlikowski & Barley (2001), who state that IS research must ‘make much more use of more recent developments in organization theory’ (Orlikowski & Barley, 2001, p. 153). Using work system theory makes it possible to distinguish between work routine and technology perceptions as described in this paper. Therefore, it provides the theoretical base for the ‘understanding and isolating of change related to technology vs process characteristics’ called for by Venkatesh (2006, p. 502). However, work routines are just one example of a work system component (Alter, 2013) and future research might evaluate other components in relation to user resistance. Work system theory identi<sup>fi</sup>es not only the well-researched technology and task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t, but also the information, the task-information <sup>fi</sup>t, products and services, and the relationship to customers as relevant components of a work system that might be changed by implementing a new IS. Hence, future research might focus on different components of a work system to further theorize employees’ reactions when implementing an IS in an organization and how these reactions are related to IS-induced changes to these elements of a work system. Such an approach would require a more sophisticated approach using a combination of different research methods. Our research takes a <sup>fi</sup>rst step in this direction as it provides initial evidence for the need to examine a broader range of perceptions related to work systems or broader socialtechnical systems to better explain user acceptance or resistance.

Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of executing work routines as perceptions of work routines This study identi<sup>fi</sup>es perceived ease of executing and perceived usefulness of work routines as perceptions employees have when a new IS is implemented. The results respond to Venkatesh’s (2006) call for research on individual acceptance of work routines. He calls for research to identify relevant work routine-related constructs that could predict adoption or resistance and thus help predict the success of an IS implementation. Taking a multi-method approach, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed two variables related to work routines, developed a measurement model for these variables and demonstrated that these variables are predictors of user resistance behavior. Hence, we extend prior research summarized in Table 1 by adding perceived ease of executing and perceived usefulness of work routines as perceptions of work routines that are important when researching user resistance behavior. To date, research focusing on work routines as an object of resistance has identi<sup>fi</sup>ed perceived workload (Klaus & Blanton, 2010), fear, and stress (Martinko et al, 1996) as perceptions of work routines; however, no other research has provided variables that can be used in an empirical study. Hence, our study contributes to user resistance research to the degree that future research can use the new variables to capture perceptions of work routines when IS are implemented in an organization to be able to control for their effect on relevant dependent variables such as user resistance.

Perceptions of work routines have stronger impact on user resistance behavior than technology perceptions Our study provides evidence that employees’ perceptions of work routines are even stronger predictors of user resistance than perceptions of the new IS. Perceived ease of executing work routines, in particular, is a strong predictor of user resistance and even stronger than technology perceptions. From the point of view of Venkatesh (2006), these results can be explained by the freedom associated with the use of technologies compared to the adoption of work routines. Venkatesh (2006) states that ‘unlike technologies, where it may indeed be possible for there to be freedom associated with their use, the same freedom may not be formally afforded to employees with regard to business processes’ (p. 502). Therefore, employees may perceive work routine changes as more threatening and develop a stronger intention to resist. We advise future research to control for the impact of perceptions of work routines on user resistance when researching user resistance to IS implementation.

Technology perceptions as determinants of work routine perceptions Another interesting result is that perceptions of the technology are important predictors of employees’ perception of work routines. We provide evidence that it is also important to consider the interplay of the technology and work routines to the degree that technologies are used in work routines when employees produce products or services for customers (Alter, 2013). Comprehensive commercial software packages often embed work routines into the technology and this material aspect affects how employees are able to interact with those elements embedded in a technology (Volkoff et al, 2007). Goh et al (2011) indicates that it is the functional affordances of an IS that shape the adaption of new work routines. In this context, our results reveal that employees consider technology and work routines two distinct objects when an IS is implemented; however, the results also indicate that users’ perceptions of the embedding technology are strong determinants of their perceptions of the embedded work routines. Hence, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings support the distinction made by work system theory (Alter, 2013) and the indication that functional affordances shape the adaption of new work routines (Goh et al, 2011), as employees perceive the embedding technology and the embedded work routines differently. When work routines are embedded in a technology, technology provides the material basis for the work routines that enable employees to interact with work routines (Volkoff et al, 2007). Negative perceptions of the embedding technology can lead to negative perceptions of the embedded work routines. Hence, we contribute to user resistance research that perceptions of the technology, as an object of resistance in<sup>fl</sup>uence the perceptions of work routines, as an object of resistance. Consequently, we extend research on user resistance by providing evidence for an additional relation between two different objects of resistance. Moreover, based on our results, future research might further theorize about different work systems components embedded in a technology to determine whether users’ perceptions of these components in<sup>fl</sup>uence resistance behavior during IS implementation.

## Implications for practice

The results have several implications for IS implementation projects in organizations. Primarily, the results indicate the importance of designing change management activities to focus both on the new technology and the work routines. For example, training activities during the implementation phase should target both technology and work routine issues. If organizations are able to communicate the necessity of a change in technology infrastructure and in the related work routines, the likelihood of achieving desired goals increases. Thus, organizations might implement platforms for managers and employees to discuss the changes and where arguments for and against implementing the IS could be exchanged. It is important to take negative beliefs seriously if employees are to be convinced about the usefulness of a change.

The implication from this study is that, it is unlikely that ‘one size <sup>fi</sup>ts all’. There are two major drivers of user resistance that are perceived differently by the HR employees we observed. If management is knowledgeable about and aware of the various types of objects of resistance discussed in this research, they can design better change management strategies overall.

## Conclusion

In response to calls by IS researchers, the paper applies work system theory and <sup>fi</sup>nds that employees’ perceptions of work routine are a major predictor of resistance to IS implementation in organizations, and an even stronger determinant than their perceptions of the technology itself.

## Measurement model development

## Q-sorting

To assess the reliability and validity of the newly developed items and to evaluate discriminant validity we used Q-sorting (Landis & Koch, 1977; Nahm et al, 2002; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). We recruited 64 students from our department and asked them to perform the Q-sorting routine. We used the newly developed items for perceived ease of executing work routines and perceived usefulness of work routines and the well-established items for perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of the technology (Venkatesh et al, 2003). We asked each individual to assign all items to one of these four variables. We de<sup>fi</sup>ned each variable <sup>fi</sup>rst and provided two examples how to assign the items. On the basis of the assignments of the 64 individuals, we calculated ratios of correctly assigned items. As suggested in prior research (Landis & Koch, 1977; Nahm et al, 2002), we rejected all items with at least 40% incorrect assignments. As two items were not assigned correctly by more than 61% we removed them from our <sup>fi</sup>nal analysis (PEOU-4, PU-4).

## Factor analysis

To further elaborate reliability and discriminant validity, we ran factor analysis for both perceptions of the technology and perceptions of work routines. For this step, we used the survey conducted, which included the <sup>fi</sup>nal pool of items revealed from the Q-sporting approach. Our factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure for both perceptions of the technology and perceptions of work routines. As all item loadings are above the recommended threshold of 0.7, we included all items in the validation of our research model.

In summary, the measurement development process reveals three items, which are used for the newly proposed variable and in validating the proposed research model. The measurement items for each construct of our research model are summarized in Table A3.
