# Evaluation of information system investments: a value dials approach to closing the theory-practice gap

Jane Elisabeth Frisk<sup>1</sup>, Frank Bannister<sup>2</sup>, Rikard Lindgren<sup>3,4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Business Administration, School of Business, Economics and Law at University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, Sweden; <sup>2</sup>School of Computer Science and Statistics, Trinity College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland;

<sup>3</sup>Swedish Center for Digital Innovation, Department of Applied Information Technology, University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, Sweden;

<sup>4</sup>School of Business and IT, University of Borås, Borås, Sweden

## Correspondence:

J Elisabeth Frisk, Department of Business Administration, School of Business, Economics and Law at University of Gothenburg, Gothenburg, Sweden.

## Abstract

Failed or partially failed information systems (IS) investments continue to be a problem in both public and private sectors. Although there are many causes of such failure, lack of clarity about the goals and bene<sup>fi</sup>ts expected and how they are to be realized is one of the major contributors. This is particularly so in the public sector where the purpose of IS investment is not to increase pro<sup>fi</sup>ts, but to accomplish social and political goals. One way of reducing failure and wastage rates is to have ex-ante evaluation. Over the past three decades IS research has proposed a plethora of new theoretical approaches to improving ex-ante evaluation, but with a few exceptions, these have had little impact on practice. This paper presents a way to address this gap between theory and practice. It proposes a way in which managers can improve IS investment evaluation by changing perspective from a focus on traditional analytic tools towards a design attitude that seeks to develop multicriteria IS evaluation approach based on contextual experience and prior knowledge. The proposed approach enables organizations to design and develop a value case using value dials as a common language that de<sup>fi</sup>nes what constitutes the value of particular IS investments for a speci<sup>fi</sup>c context. To assess its effectiveness, this approach was applied using a multilevel dialogical action research project within a Swedish public organization. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings show that managers in this organization signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly improved the effectiveness of their evaluation of IS investments.

## Introduction

<sup>even</sup> <sup>years</sup> <sup>ago</sup> <sup>Nordea,</sup> <sup>a</sup> <sup>part</sup> <sup>private,</sup> <sup>part</sup> <sup>public</sup>organization and one of the largest banks in Sweden, organization and one of the largest banks in Sweden, embarked on a major ICT investment project which was eventually to cost it 5 billion SEK (€570 million). Recently Sit was decided to close this system down leaving the bank with almost nothing of value from its original investment (Danielsson, 2013). As this and other examples show, problems with ill-judged information systems (IS) acquisition continue to exist in both the private and the public sectors (Purao and Desouza, 2011). It is not, perhaps, surprising that in a 2009 survey undertaken by the British Computer Society (2009) obtaining value and return from IS investments was ranked as the second most important issue for Information Technology (IT) management (alignment was ranked number one). In fact the evaluation of IS investments has consistently been one of the top IT management issues for a long time (Luftman, 1996; Luftman and McLean, 2004; Luftman, 2005). The history of evaluation of IS acquisition, both ex-ante and ex-post, is one of a continually expanding range of ideas and proposals as to how both of these should be done. This has resulted in a steady accretion of IS evaluation and assessment methods (Strassman, 1990; Symons, 1991; DeLone and

McLean, 1992, 2003; Belcher and Watson, 1993; Cronk and Fitzgerald, 1999; Seddon et al., 2002; Stockdale and Standing, 2006; Ward and Daniel, 2006; Grimsley and Meehan, 2007; Kohli and Grover, 2008). Recently the rate of contributions to this topic seems to have been declining – at least in top ranked journals – and there has been a shift in the literature to the topic of business value realization (Schryen, 2013). This decline is discussed in more detail in the next section where it will be argued that, after a highly fertile period up to the mid-1990s, there have been relatively few new ideas on evaluation methods put forward in the past two decades and almost none in recent years.

The intense debate on the problem of IS evaluation that took place from the mid-1980s onwards had two implicit dimensions, namely content and process (Symons, 1991). Content was concerned with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c features/technical aspects of evaluation metrics or combinations of metrics used in evaluation. Process was concerned with how evaluation, as a business process, was or should be carried out. IS investment was increasingly conceived of as requiring a multi-objective, multi-criteria and multi-stakeholder evaluation and decision process (see, e.g., Walsham, 1993, 1999; Jones and Hughes, 2001; Stockdale and Standing, 2006). To this was added the dimension of multiple forms of measurement, which in turn raised the problem of metric selection. In the literature most of the attention was given to content, while process was often present only inasmuch as it dealt with the mechanics of the method in question. For multi-metric evaluation, the question of how one arrived at the right mix of such methods was handled, if it was discussed at all, only at a broad conceptual level. Consequently, in adopting what Symons (1991) labeled multiple perspectives, many organizations often made ad hoc decisions about which techniques to use without any formal methodology to drive this process.

In a review of the state of the art in 2001, Bannister and Remenyi (2001) categorized IS evaluation as taking one of three forms: Partisan, based on a single metric (such as ROI); Composite, based on a predetermined set of metrics (as in weighted ranking); and Meta filter, based on a two-step process to <sup>fi</sup>rst determine the appropriate metrics and then to apply them. Although Bannister and Remenyi describe the concept, they do not discuss the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs or mechanics of the last of these approaches. This paper describes the development of such a meta <sup>fi</sup>lter approach, that is, the development of a process to address the problem of metric selection by means of an extended research project, which set out to develop a better, more effective and sustainable method of IS ex-ante evaluation. The process is based on the concepts of value dials, a technique originally developed by Curley (2005) and Sward (2006). Value dials are context-speci<sup>fi</sup>c indicators that provide a common language for articulating the value of a particular IS investment, or expressed differently, its value case. Brenda and Kai (2009) described value dials as ‘… form[ing] a framework for measurement activities associated with assessing the value of IT projects….’ At one level, the value case is similar to the balanced score card in that it uses a mix of measures to evaluate an investment. However there is a fundamental difference between the two approaches. Values dials are dynamically determined as part of a case-speci<sup>fi</sup>c evaluation design process as opposed to being determined from a predetermined set of phases. The design of each value dial is based on managers’ (who may be at different levels in the organization and/or who may have different roles) contextual experience and prior theoretical knowledge. The value case framework is the outcome of a design process involving three iterative phases: interviewing, developing and presenting.

Such a design process can be particularly valuable in the public sector where pro<sup>fi</sup>t is rarely a factor (and where it is almost never the primary factor). Public IS investments are about achieving or supporting the achievement of social and political goals. Moore (2000: 186) discussed value in public sector as: ‘…the achievement of the politically mandated mission of the organization and the ful<sup>fi</sup>llment of the citizen aspiration that were more or less reliable re<sup>fl</sup>ected in that mandate.’ Moore’s development of the concept of public value (Moore, 1995) opens a further frontier to the discussion of IS evaluation in the public sector at least. To date the idea of public value has received little attention in the IS evaluation literature. This issue is discussed in more detail in the next section.

The research described in this paper was one part of a larger project carried out in the Swedish <sup>fi</sup>re and rescue services over the period 2005–2008 with a follow-up evaluation review undertaken in July 2012. The research started from an acknowledged problem, namely that many services were spending large amounts of money on IS and seemed to be getting little in return. This included signi<sup>fi</sup>cant expenditures on systems which were subsequently little used, possibly a manifestation of the type of project optimism bias discussed by Flyvberg (2008). At the outset of this research, management had been aware of this problem for some time and part of the rationale for the research was to <sup>fi</sup>nd a way to remedy it. The research approach used in this study was a form of action research that sought to establish an innovative process for IS evaluation in the partner organization. While the prior research was concerned with mostly the content, this paper focuses on the process aspect of ex-ante evaluation of IS investments and some of the lessons that emerged from it.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews a number of relevant and representative articles from the IS evaluation literature. The subsequent section describes the action research approach. The penultimate section presents the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the action research effort. Finally, the last section discusses these <sup>fi</sup>ndings and offers theoretical re<sup>fl</sup>ections.

## Theoretical background

## A predominance of conceptual studies

The available IS literature on IT evaluation is extensive and any review of it is necessarily selective. Over the past 40 years there has been a steady accumulation of proposed evaluation and assessment methods ranging from return on management (Strassmann, 1990) to strategic alignment (Ballantine and Stray, 1998). In the mid-1990s Farbey et al. (1993) listed 11 such methods while Powell (1992) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 19 though both lists are far from comprehensive (nor do they overlap much). A few years later Renkema and Berghout (1997) identi<sup>fi</sup>ed 50. As ever more methods were proposed, scholars and theorists started looking at ways in which these multiple perspectives might be combined (Symons, 1991; Farbey et al., 1999; Bannister, 2001). Some approached the problem from what might be called a traditional multi-objective, multi-criteria perspective using tools such as SMART (Goodwin and Wright, 2000), data envelopment analysis (DEA) (Baker and Talluri, 1997) or the analytical hierarchy process (Saaty, 1986). Others proposed more radical ways of integrating or combining diverse metrics. Cronk (1999) adapted the concept of holistic construal to IT evaluation. Irani and Love (2001, 2002) proposed a ‘frame of reference’ for evaluation and examined use of investment techniques. Bannister and Remenyi (2001) suggested the related concept of informed instinct. Hallikainen and Chen (2006), revisiting the work of Irani and Love, proposed another form of holistic framework.

During this period a number of polarities emerged. One was between what were described as hard and soft methods. These are ill-de<sup>fi</sup>ned terms, but broadly speaking hard methods are based on or grounded in not just numbers, but so-called ‘hard’ numbers such as projected capital outlays and forecast savings. Soft methods do not necessarily eschew numbers, but are often based on fuzzier types of data such as subjective assessments or opinions, for example, customer or user satisfaction surveys or <sup>fi</sup>nancial estimates of intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Remenyi et al., 2007). Some techniques, notably cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis, embraced both soft and hard numbers employing techniques such as shadow pricing to monetize non-<sup>fi</sup>nancial bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs. A feature of the extensive literature on multi-stakeholder, multi-criteria IS evaluation is that much of it is conceptual in the sense that it is not well grounded in empirical research. A good example is Information Economics (Parker and Benson, 1988), which is discussed in the context of an imaginary company. Parker et al.’s approach essentially evaluates, scores, and ranks IT investments by the potential positive and negative impacts of a series of prede<sup>fi</sup>ned criteria classi<sup>fi</sup>ed into business (e.g., ROI, strategic match) and technical (e.g., technical uncertainty, infrastructure risk) domains (thus, it might be noted, anticipating the balanced scorecard (BSC)). Although this approach in effect is a form of weighted ranking (Kepner and Tregoe, 1976), the authors do not provide an example of putting their model into practice in a real organization. Ward (1994) proposed a portfolio approach to evaluating IT investments by dividing potential investments into strategic, high potential, key operational, and support. As Berghout and Remenyi (2005) note, there have been a number of other variations on the portfolio and options approach proposed. An obvious criticism of these and similar abstract exercises, however, is that there are often no empirical data or multiple case studies to help determine whether such techniques/models are either easy to implement or are effective and sustainable in practice.

## A need for empirical research

There are some researchers who have implemented multimetric systems in real organizations. One example of such is the Prudential IT Investment Appraisal Method (Coleman and Jamieson, 1994), which is based on using a spider diagram segmented into customer, pro<sup>fi</sup>t, product, and decision culture. This stream of research has tended to comprise one off experiments and, as far as can be judged from the literature, none of these methods have been widely adopted for ex-ante IT investment evaluation purposes. The latter is true even of the otherwise widely used BSC for which there is an extensive literature (Györy et al., 2012). Like information economics, the Prudential and portfolio methods, and other methods not discussed here, the BSC uses a framework of pre-determined domains. In Kaplan and Norton’s (1992) original version these were <sup>fi</sup>nancial, internal business processes, customer and learning, and growth, but there have been many subsequent variations on this (Walter and Spitta, 2011) such as Martinsons et al. (1999) and Milis and Mercken (2004). From an investment evaluation perspective a problem with the BSC is that is essentially an ex post performance evaluation tool. This is not to say that it cannot be adapted to ex-ante evaluation and IT investment decision-making, but like all of the other methods discussed, it usually starts from a predetermined framework rather than emerging organically from the needs and nature of the organization itself. There remains a gap therefore not just in knowledge of how these approaches are used or should be used in ex-ante IT investment decision-making, but in how to build a model and a process that will be meaningful to users as opposed to solely being derived from some predetermined n dimensional categorization or framework (cf. Jones and Hughes, 2001; Stockdale and Standing, 2006).

The problems of multi-perspective, multi-criteria, multi stakeholder ex-ante IT evaluation and decision-making can be particularly problematic in the public sector (Bannister, 2001). Public sector decision-making is often more complicated than that in the private sector not least because there are many more stakeholders involved and the evaluation criteria are both more diverse and more diffuse. A further complication is the question of ‘public value’ (see next section) which can be hard to assess in a tractable manner. Soft and/or intangible bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, which frequently include political considerations, typically play a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant role in decisions. Public sector decision-making has to take into account values (e.g., equity, fairness, and accountably) which either do not apply at all or only apply in more limited ways in the private sector. Walsham (1999) has pointed to the dif<sup>fi</sup>culties of involving different stakeholders in such efforts due to the political issues that are often involved in such decision-making. Developing a robust, IT evaluation and decision-making process for the public sector that delivers systems capable of creating real public value is an important challenge, which, despite many good ideas, has to date never been satisfactorily addressed. While not the only cause, this has been one factor in a great deal of taxpayer money wasted on IT investments (Cross, 2005; Gold<sup>fi</sup>nch, 2007; Purao and Desouza, 2011). The vacuum left by the absence of a solid evaluation methodology for complex public sector IT projects is unfortunately often <sup>fi</sup>lled with that old stand-by cost bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis. Despite its virtues, this method has many <sup>fl</sup>aws and drawbacks not the least of which is that it uses what can be arbitrary and poorly thought through <sup>fi</sup>nancial values to represent highly complex and subtle impacts. There have been some efforts to address this problem. For example, a tentative (the authors’ word) framework for e-government evaluation is proposed by Jones et al. (2007). This is quite a complex model built around decision-making, evaluation methods, performance assessment and practitioner concerns. The evaluation methods are considered as quantitative (metrics coverage and change management) and qualitative (non-economic justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and soft issues). Based on a study of e-government evaluation in three UK local authorities, they authors draw a series of lessons, most of which are unsurprising (e.g., ‘Organization should de<sup>fi</sup>ne e-Government ownership to clarify responsibility’ (p. 6), but they do not offer a procedure or methodology that can be adapted to different e-government evaluation problems.

One reason for the lack of good cases of multi-methodology IT decision-making in the literature may be the practical dif<sup>fi</sup>culties in implementing such an evaluation approach. Multi-objective, multi-criteria decision-making is inherently complicated even before one adds multiple methods and multiple stakeholders; indeed it is the subject of a large literature (see Chou et al. (2006) or Ho et al. (2010) for examples from just two related <sup>fi</sup>elds). The challenge is therefore to devise an evaluation and decision approach that is coherent, meaningful, workable, which avoids oversimplifying the problem and which has the support of all parties to the decision. The value dials and evaluation framework method presented in this paper has parallels with the n-way categorization approaches outlined above, but is based on combining a dialogical action research methodology with the elements of the Delphi method and the BSC in order to develop an evaluation framework for a given organization, in a given environment looking at a given project.

A recent special issue of the European Journal of Information Systems was dedicated to the question of IT funding. Reviewing research opportunities in IT funding, Peffers and Dos Santos (2013) discuss the complexities involved in ICT evaluation today. They propose the concepts of ‘justi<sup>fi</sup>cation dynamism which moves from static to continuous justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and ‘justi<sup>fi</sup> cation evidence’ which ranges from qualitative to quantitative. This is a variation on a long-standing idea dating back at least to the 1990s (Hirschheim and Smithson, 1998; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998). Writing of the business value construct Peffers and Dos Santos (2013: 136) say that:

It is generally the business value construct in some form that is estimated ex-ante in the IT funding decision. In this article we have outlined a number of permutations of the IT funding context, in terms of justi<sup>fi</sup>cation dynamism and the quality of the evidence. If IT funding practice is to be able to adapt to the many permutations possible, new concepts and techniques are required to incorporate dynamic justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and qualitative evidence of many types into the business value construct.

The approach used in this research is a form of justi<sup>fi</sup>cation dynamism, though not quite in the sense that Peffers and Dos Santos mean it. It is a form of dynamism that enables the contextual construction of the business value constructs (in the terminology of this paper, value dials) by which the implicit construct will be measured. It is, in effect, an adaptation and operationalization of Peffers’ and Dos Santos’ ideas.

Finally, returning to the comments in the opening section, a feature of IS evaluation continues to be that, dynamic justi<sup>fi</sup>cation and evaluation as design apart, there has been a paucity of new thinking on multi-criteria ex-ante evaluation in recent years. In an extensive literature review, Schryen (2013) notes that there has been a general decline in the number of articles in the <sup>fi</sup>eld of IS evaluation and IS business value since 2000. Although Schryen’s article is focused on ex post rather than ex-ante evaluation much of what he <sup>fi</sup>nds bears out the assertions made above. Looking at leading IS journals, he notes that only 10 articles on IS Business Value have been published since 2005. He suggests a ‘synthesized IS business value model’ which suggest that performance measures be determined by contextual/environmental factors and lag effects. Shyren cites Kohli and Grover (2008) as observing that IS value manifests itself in many ways (though this is hardly a new insight, even in 2008). Furthermore, he suggests that as a result of:

…de<sup>fi</sup>ciencies in the research on the ambiguity and fuzziness of the ‘IS business value’ construct, we argue that our future efforts to develop a consistent and comprehensive understanding of the complex ‘IS business value construct should account for (a) linkages between different types of performance, (b) market-oriented capabilities that go beyond hard indicators, (c) various types of internal capabilities, (d) an environmentally oriented understanding, and (e) perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts that are dependent on the respective stakeholders.

(p. 148)

In this paper it is argued that, adjusting for the fact that the FRS is a public body, the value dials approach meets these criteria and in particular it meets criteria (a) and (e). Schryen further proposes six research thrusts. The <sup>fi</sup>rst of these is: ‘How can we yield a comprehensive, consistent and precise understanding of the multifaceted construct “IS business value?” ’ (p. 151). This research addresses precisely this question and proposes one answer to it.

## Public value

A feature of the IT evaluation literature is that it is largely oriented towards the private sector and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally towards forpro<sup>fi</sup>t <sup>fi</sup>rms/organizations which market and sell products or services. In addition to the differences noted above, a further distinction arises from the concept of public value. Much of the current conceptualization of (and debate about) public value dates back to Moore (1995). Moore’s work in the 1990s was intended as a counter to the emerging dominance of the New Public Management (NPM) model and neo-liberalism (Hood, 1991; Bennington and Moore, 2011). Moore argued that the role of government is more than just that of a referee and safety net provider in a world where public services were delivered by private concerns subject to market forces. He argued that governments create or co-create tangible public value, namely things that the public values and wants. Public servants, and in particular senior public managers, are not just ‘stewards of public assets’ but can be innovators and orchestrators of public discourse, working with multiple stakeholders in the development of better policies, services and governance. In a similar vein Wastell (2011), while not speci<sup>fi</sup>cally addressing the question of value, proposes that public managers need to be designers.

Moore proposes a ‘strategic triangle’ comprised of an authorizing environment, an operational capacity and public value outcomes (the <sup>fi</sup>rst two parts of bearing a strong resemblance to Hood’s tools of government (Hood, 1983)). Perhaps the most important aspect of public value from the perspective of an organization like the FRS is the question of placing value, in Bennington’s (2011) words: ‘…into an alternative framework which starts with the public and the collective as the primary units of analysis, rather than with the private and the individual’ (p. 31). While, in certain circumstances (particularly in the case of an ambulance call-out), the services provided by the FRS is a service to the individual, it is just as often a collective service as no one individual know when she will need that service and, in the case of <sup>fi</sup>re or a large accident, it is more than likely that many people and the wider community will be affected. Bennington differentiates between the traditional public administration concept of public goods, the NPM concept of public choice and the networked community governance concept of public value. He presents the <sup>fi</sup>rst of these as being the old-fashioned way of doing things, the second as being the currently fashionable way of doing things and the third as being an emergent way of doing things.

Bringing the concepts of public value to bear on decisionmaking about individual IT investments is not a straightforward process. Much of the discussion of public value tends to be at a broad level (such as education policy or policing). How its ideas can be directly applied to a decision whether or not to upgrade a computer network or acquire a new call response system is not self-evident and more work is required to explore this. Highly technical IT decisions are hardly good candidates for deliberative democracy or co-creation. Nonetheless, if there is to be public value delivered by any investment by a government agency, it needs to be measurable in terms of outputs at least and outcomes by preference. In the words of Horner and Hutton (2011: 125):

Public value must somehow articular the distinctive type of value produced by a public oriented service – one that reconnects public bodies with the public they are there to serve.

To try to achieve this aim, an obvious thing to measure is outcomes. The problem is that in ex-ante evaluations, the projection of improved outcomes often requires many assumptions and the number of unknowns can be large. Projected outputs are easier to forecast. One promising line of investigation is the concept of public value through innovation and improvement (Hartley, 2011). Hartley points out that innovations which are primarily technical can have real public value outcomes (e.g., faster response to call outs or lower levels of injury), but she also notes the risks of unanticipated consequences of such innovations. Examining the approach in New Zealand, Norman (2011) suggests: ‘Managing for outcomes while accounting for outputs’ (p. 202) which might be useful in ex post evaluation and business value management, but is of little relevant in front end decision-making.

Evaluating the societal impacts of ICT is challenging (Bannister and Remenyi, 2001) and the concept of public value itself is still fairly <sup>fl</sup>uid (Bennington and Moore, 2011). In practice public value may indirectly in<sup>fl</sup>uence IS decisionmaking via internal stakeholders’ perceptions of that value. In the case of the FRS, as in other evaluations of public sector IS investments, the implicit assumption is made that the many managers involved in the decision have an awareness of public value and factor this into their thinking. This is an assumption that perhaps needs to be rigorously tested, but that is a task for another piece of research.

## Method

## Action research

The objective with the development of a value case was to help managers improve IT investment evaluation. Action research was therefore selected as the mode of inquiry. This research approach combines theory generation with researcher intervention to solve immediate organizational problems (Baskerville and Wood-Harper, 1998). It is directed towards collaboration and changes (Checkland and Holwell, 1998), and thus puts researchers in a helping role with practitioners (Baskerville and Wood-Harper, 1996). Because studying a real-world problem without facilitating its resolution is seen as unhelpful action, researchers should take on the responsibility to collaborate with practitioners to develop new and apply existing knowledge (Mathiassen, 2002). Given that the role of intervention conveys a pragmatist philosophy in which practical outcome embodies truth (Baskerville and Myers, 2004), action research aims to link theory and practice as well as thinking and doing (Susman, 1983).

Action research has been seen as an accepted research approach for several decades in disciplines like organization development and education (Baskerville and Myers, 2004). However, Baskerville and Wood-Harper (1996: 235) portray it as ‘a post-positivist social scienti<sup>fi</sup>c research method, ideally suited to the study of technology in its human context.’ IS researchers have recently used different action research approaches such as canonical action research (Lindgren et al., 2004), collaborative practice research (Iversen et al., 2004), networks of action (Braa et al., 2004), and participatory action research (Street and Meister, 2004). Our study resembles the dialogical action research approach as formulated by Mårtensson and Lee (2004).

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues

Dialogical action research advances scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theory while simultaneously tackling real-world problems. This approach addresses heterogeneity in knowledge interests in that it balances the ‘the scienti<sup>fi</sup>c attitude’ taken by the researcher and ‘the natural attitude of everyday life’ taken by the practitioner. The researcher and the practitioner are members of different ethnic groups, each of which has its own culture and language. Whereas these groups rely on different knowledge types, the knowledge held by one group is not necessarily better or worse than the knowledge held by the other group. In dialogical action research, the researcher carries the burden of acquiring an understanding of the social context of the client organization. Mårtensson and Lee (2004), however, argue that communication across the respective cultures and languages is a problem that requires its own intervention.

Re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues that resolve the problem of communication of scienti<sup>fi</sup>c knowledge and practitioner knowledge are at the heart of dialogical action research. The intervention thus takes the form of dialogues (sometimes taking place outside the practitioner’s organization) that encourage the practitioner to think re<sup>fl</sup>ectively about day-to-day activities. The researcher also tries to speak the practitioner’s language when proposing actions informed by theories. That is, the researcher suggests actions to a real-world problem in a way that makes sense in the practitioner’s own context as a professional. Indeed, there is no need for the practitioner to consume ‘raw’ scienti<sup>fi</sup>c theories directly. Still these actions should serve as an experimental stimulus or treatment for testing and advancing theory. The subsequent reaction or response of the real-world problem to the actions taken therefore conveys not only success or failure in solving or remedying the problem, but also evidence conforming or discon<sup>fi</sup>rming the theory on which the action was based (Mårtensson and Lee, 2004).

To date most IS articles reporting on action research draw on a single level of analysis. Indeed, Mårtensson and Lee (2004) who reconstruct one-on-one re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues between an IS researcher and a managing director at a European company is a good example of this tradition. Our study, in contrast, incorporates multilevel thinking (e.g., Hitt et al., 2007) to propose the value dials approach as a means to help the partner organization to improve its evaluation of IT investments (cf. Walsham, 1993). The simple idea is that re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues at the individual, group, and organizational levels must be organized to leverage perspectives, experiences, and skills across different roles and levels. This allows, for example, lower-level in<sup>fl</sup>uences to shape investment decisions and their subsequent organization-level outcomes. In addition, our study is design-oriented in that it not only produces valuable knowledge, but also a tangible artifact in the form of a value case framework.

## Evaluation criteria

As Baskerville and Wood-Harper (1998) note, it is important to specify the criteria by which a research project should be evaluated. Despite the adaptations made, the approach in this project relies on the evaluation criteria proposed by Mårtensson and Lee (2004) because these were developed speci<sup>fi</sup>cally for dialogical action research. This evaluation framework identi<sup>fi</sup>es three criteria, each helping to ensure both relevance and rigor. The three criteria are:

● Criterion #1 seeks to ensure that the practitioner considers the real-world problem to be solved or remedied satisfactorily.

● Criterion #2 highlights that there needs to be an improvement in the practitioner’s expertise.

● Criterion # 3 stipulates that there should be an improvement in the researcher’s expertise.

To assess the quality of this study, the dialogical action research approach pursued will later be compared with these criteria.

## Project setting

This research was carried out from 2005 to 2008 as an action research study between the Swedish Fire Rescue Agency (SFRA) and the Viktoria Institute with a follow-up evaluation review under 2012. The SFRA is a government authority at the national level pursuing emergency operations such as <sup>fi</sup>re protection, disaster prevention and response, and accident and incident management. Viktoria Institute is a non-pro<sup>fi</sup>t research institute specializing in applied research for value creating IT usage. The project was managed by the Viktoria Institute and funded by the SFRA research program. A researcher-client agreement specifying the responsibilities of the participating organizations was signed to avoid any potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts over each party’s contribution to and role in the project (Rapoport, 1970). This agreement addressed the allocation of resources such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial support, labor, and equipment, as well as rights and responsibilities for the research results. The project had a reference group comprising experienced practitioners from SFRA and senior researchers from Viktoria Institute and University of Gothenburg. They met twice a year to discuss the project’s progress and its immediate outcomes.

The project focused on IS evaluation and sought to transform IT investment decision-making in three <sup>fi</sup>re rescue services (FRSs). This paper describes the research undertaken together with the Gothenburg FRS (hereafter simply referred to as the FRS). With responsibility for <sup>fi</sup>ve municipalities, it contained nine <sup>fi</sup>re stations employing approximately 1000 employees. The FRS is responsible for several services including <sup>fi</sup>re prevention, preparation (e.g., advising organizations and homeowners on <sup>fi</sup>re alarms and extinguisher systems), and emergency response. Financial and budgetary control over the FRS is exercised by a confederation comprised of politicians drawn from the <sup>fi</sup>ve municipalities.

Like the classic genesis of action research (see Avison et al., 2001), the project was established when the FRS sought assistance from knowledgeable researchers at the Viktoria Institute. The organization faced a serious problem in that it had a history of repeated failures and malfunctioning enterprise systems. The focus of this study was on managers with experience of evaluating IT investments and in particular enterprise systems including the decision support tools used when dealing with incidents, prevention, and team location and positioning. The study included two operational managers, <sup>fi</sup>ve functional managers, and two strategic managers. In addition, 22 people serving at the operational level took part.

A feature of the FRS was that IT investments could be initiated from both inside and outside the organization. An external initiative might arise in a number of ways, for example, a new law or regulation from SFRA. Inside initiatives could come from anyone and anywhere. Requests for major IT expenditures could also occur at any time of year even though such expenditures had not been agreed during preparation of the annual budget. Decisions on such investments were decentralized and were often taken at departmental level. If the expenditure exceeded a speci<sup>fi</sup>c amount, however, the Chief Manager of the FRS became responsible and had to sign off on the request. There were other controls on major outlays. Expenditure above a certain level had to go before the organization’s board for approval. In situations where a proposed IT investment exceeded normal budget restrictions, it became a political decision and had to be referred to a committee appointed by the local municipality.

Prior to the start of the project, ex-ante evaluations of IT investments were based on a number of factors including the expected bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, costs, and the technology itself, but there were several weaknesses in the way this was done more notably in the absence of an adequate business case. While management was aware that there were problems, the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage of the research sought to identify, agree, and document these problems (see the research process presentation below). A contributing factor to this laxity in the IT evaluation process was that for many years the FRS had had ample funding. That is, there was little incentive to spend time and effort on detailed analysis of the impact of such investments (or on bene<sup>fi</sup>t/value realization). By 2005, however, the economic climate had changed leading to a much greater focus on cost and value for money. The change provided the impetus both for the research and for managers’ cooperation in the research.

## Research process

The action research study involved <sup>fi</sup>ve stages: a preliminary stage, three core stages, and a post-evaluation stage (see Figure 1). The value dials approach is primarily about the three core stages, but all <sup>fi</sup>ve stages are reported in this paper. These stages are brie<sup>fl</sup>y outlined here.

Overview of the process stages.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage, Diagnostic interviews, was an investigation into the problems that effectively hampered adequate evaluation in IT investment decision-making. This investigation included diagnostic interviews with managers occupying different roles at multiple levels. The goal of these interviews was to detect, illuminate, and represent the evaluation problems that the study participants felt had a detrimental effect on IT investment decisions.

To verify the accuracy of the problems identi<sup>fi</sup>ed, the second stage consisted of discursive interviews with individual members of the client organization. These re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues created not only a shared understanding of the problems, but also an awareness of proposed ways of overcoming them. The net effect was an emerging change agenda that helped prepare the client organization for the subsequent process stages.

In parallel with the second stage and the two workshops which comprised stage three (involving group re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues) a review was undertaken of the extant IS evaluation literature. This was done to support the process of solving the problems identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in stages one and two and two development workshops. The analysis of the literature as performed by the action researchers resulted in the development of a multi-criteria model for evaluation tailored to the needs and synthesized value aspects that were deemed critical to resolve or ameliorate the problems identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the <sup>fi</sup>rst stage. Following the design orientation adopted, these theoretical understandings were then used in the two following workshops. A value case was developed based on design dialogues with the practitioners.

The fourth stage, presentation workshops, was comprised of a workshop that catered for review dialogues between researchers and practitioners in order to accomplish an organizational re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogue. The question put at the workshop was whether the emergent value case and the knowledge inscribed into it could actually resolve the problems they faced. Given its preliminary design, the ambition was to create understanding for how the value case could be adapted to the speci<sup>fi</sup>cs of the implementing organization.

The <sup>fi</sup>fth and <sup>fi</sup>nal process stage consisted of a postimplementation review in which the organizational perspective was once more re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon. A number of outcome interviews with some of the stakeholders involved were undertaken to enable all parties to re<sup>fl</sup>ect on the process and its degree of success. Two key questions were: (1) whether, through such joint assessments, the organization as a whole advanced its evaluation capacity, and (2) whether the process had created effects that would persist and remain sustainable after the project was complete. ## Research practice

## Diagnostic interviews

The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage in this process was to de<sup>fi</sup>ne the scope of the problem faced by the FRS. A series of interviews was undertaken with the speci<sup>fi</sup>c objective of gaining a good understanding of the current state of IT evaluation in the organization. A number of problems were identi<sup>fi</sup>ed including those listed in the Introduction. These problems included:

● IT systems being acquired to meet departmental needs without regard to organizational impacts,

● uncoordinated purchasing of IT, ● as a consequence of the above, there was siliozation of IT into non-communicating islands of processing,

● an excessive focus on technology rather than business needs,

● failure to invest adequately in post-acquisition implementation,

● absence of any post-implementation review or evaluation process and

● short-term thinking

all resulting in a continuing failure to realize value from IT investments. One informant described the problems thus:

The FRS in Gothenburg has too much focus on technology rather than on the organization’s needs. It should be the needs of the organization that govern. For instance, the Technical Department<sup>1</sup> has initiated too many different technical solutions and this has meant that the IT department ends up having to support hundreds of different solutions. This is hard to operate and maintain whilst delivering a high quality service.

The relevance of this remark becomes clearer when it is understood that the FRS is an organization in which, at the tactical level, many managers have a technical focus and have a tendency to see IT investments purely from the perspective of what it can do for them and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally in helping them to carry out the narrow range of tasks for which they are responsible. Furthermore, for managers, the concept of cost was limited to hardware and software. Other direct and indirect costs were not only not understood, managers did not even seem to be aware that they existed. As a result, evaluation was <sup>fi</sup>tful and erratic. The Chief Information Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CIO) made a similar point:

## Discursive interviews

The second stage, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst of the three core stages, involved developing an agreed understanding of managers needs and worldviews and getting them to discuss their value dials. A common obstacle that needs to be overcome in building a multi-criteria evaluation process of this nature is the varying perspectives, mental equipment and/or skill sets which different stakeholders bring to the table. During this research, this problem, and the need to resolve it, was expressed succinctly by one business manager:

Today we have in the organization different views of IT. I have a vision; the Department of Prevention has a vision the managers at the department level have a vision (and they want it all); the IT department has a different view and they do not want to pay anything. It is important that we develop a common platform where we learn to crawl, then walk and then run.

These varying views re<sup>fl</sup>ect the stakeholders’ positions in the organization, position here encompassing their level as well as their role, responsibilities, function and professional background. In any organization managers’ value dials will re<sup>fl</sup>ect these factors. Thus, for example, in a manufacturing organization senior <sup>fi</sup>nance people typically think in terms of <sup>fi</sup>nancial metrics such as return on investment, payback or pro<sup>fi</sup>tability indices. Marketing may think in terms of market share or customer churn. Production may measure the potential impact of IT in terms of greater capacity utilization, higher yields or reduced levels of re-work. In the public sector such measures are typically even more diverse and varied and in extreme cases stakeholders can have diametrically opposed interests. For this reason, public sector evaluation can be particularly complex which is one reason why broad-brush tools such as cost-bene<sup>fi</sup>t analysis or value for money analysis are so often used in public sector evaluation.

In the FRS one common thread that was visible from the outset of the process was that the various worldviews of senior managers did not embody much understanding of the role and importance of evaluating IT investments. IT evaluation was a mix of the technical and the parochial and this led to many downstream dif<sup>fi</sup>culties for the IT department (see below). For the Chief Executive Of<sup>fi</sup>cer (CEO), IT was perceived primarily as an expense, something on which considerable money had been spent with seemingly little return. Understandably the CEO’s focus was on the business, not the technology, and his primary concerns related to the justi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the expenditures involved. The CEO re<sup>fl</sup>ected this when he acknowledged that:

When deciding upon IT investments it is important to consider if the solution is in accordance with new law proposals after which then it is an economic matter. Revenues are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to calculate; costs are easier. However, we do not take into account the cost of employees. We just consider whether we have suf<sup>fi</sup>cient resources for the project or not. It would be great if we could evaluate an IT investment in more correct manner. The manager of the IT department does a better job at evaluating and he does take into account staff costs.

Unsurprisingly, the metrics that the CEO used at the outset of the study were those related to cost and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally payments to external suppliers though he was aware of the bigger picture.

A consequence of this cost focus was that at senior management level short-term indicators, such the performance of IT expenditure against budget, were paramount. While the importance of the value of IT was acknowledged, to senior managers the value of IT remained a rather vague concept; they had dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in de<sup>fi</sup>ning precisely what it meant. The fact, noted above, that coordination of IT systems was almost nonexistent was aggravated by this phenomenon. This failure to understand the true nature of IT costs created a problem for the IT department which understood the nature of IT costs all too well and speci<sup>fi</sup>cally that current IT investments tended to lead to large downstream future costs in support, maintenance and enhancement. The CEO, while he was more aware of the wider cost implications of IT investment, remained primarily concerned with the short term and this dominated his thinking about IT evaluation. Longer-term measurements of both cost and value, while acknowledged, were deemed to be of secondary importance and this thinking in<sup>fi</sup>ltrated the senior management ranks. The problem was put thus by the CIO:

Operational costs for ICT are a ticking bomb as the costs for ICT will increase enormously in the future as no one has control over the costs that will emerge. Today only the costs for hardware and software are considered when evaluating and deciding upon new ICT investments.

None of this is intended as a criticism of the managers concerned. All of the managers work within a business and cultural environment that had evolved over many years. Unless they have been exposed to different forms of measuring value before (say in a training course or in another context), it can be dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for managers and line staff, particularly those at more senior levels and in more specialized functions, to understand evaluation techniques and measurements which are outside their experience or knowledge base. There are two principal reasons for this. The <sup>fi</sup>rst is unfamiliarity with the technique itself; the second, and probably the more important, is a lack of understanding of the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the metrics concerned.

To illustrate this with a simple example, an IT manager considering a system acquisition may place great emphasis on the system’s agility. A <sup>fi</sup>nance executive looking at the same system may focus primarily on its cost. A dispatch manager may be interested in it solving the <sup>fi</sup>re engine deployment problem he has now. While at a vague level the <sup>fi</sup>nance executive and the dispatch manager might understand that agility is useful or even important, in both cases their ability to actually evaluate such an abstract feature is likely to be limited. The IT executive, while he may have a good understanding of the value of agility, may have a poor understanding of the cost implications of providing such a feature. The dispatch manager many not be interested in either; he has a problem that needs to be solved now and his only question is can this system do the job? Given situations like this, one of the critical steps in developing a coherent multidimensional view of value and evaluation is to create a common language or an understanding of other people's language and, in this context, other people’s value dials.

This problem was exacerbated in the FRS by the silo mentality already discussed. Business and line managers’ attitude to IT was ‘what can it do for me/my department?’ What mattered to managers was the local impact of IT; value was not a concept that they normally considered. In contrast, in the FRS theCIO’s view was that the IT should deliver value from an organizational perspective and not from an individual perspective.

In this second stage a series of interviews with managers was carried out. Some managers were interviewed more than once. Normally, the purpose of such interviews is to elicit information from the interviewee. In this study the objectives were different. First it was critical not just to understand each stakeholder’s requirements, but to understand how the each interviewee understood value. However, there was a further objective in each interview, which was to introduce and explain to the interviewee a number of new concepts and evaluation metrics and tools. Consequently, the interviews were designed to be more than just the researcher asking questions and the informant answering them. It was designed to be a form of mutual learning between the researcher and the interviewee. Once the stakeholder/ interviewee’s understanding of value was established, various potential ways of evaluating these would then be discussed (and researched if necessary).

For this reason different value dials were discussed and these included not only the dials that the informant himself would normally use in such circumstances, but also new or different ones with which he or she might not be familiar. Third, the conversation extended beyond the interviewees world view and discussed how other managers might see the problem(s) and what tools and metrics they might use. There were two rationales for this. The <sup>fi</sup>rst was to ensure that when it came to the fourth stage, the building workshops, the participants would be well prepared and would come to the table with a wide vocabulary about evaluation, a broader understanding of the possibilities and a better understanding of how other stakeholders were likely to view the evaluation problem. The second was to enable the researcher identify the appropriate set of tools which would eventually comprise the evaluation framework.

Sometimes interviewees/managers understood these concepts without dif<sup>fi</sup>culty. However, in many cases discussions were extended as ideas put forward by the researchers were discussed and accepted or in some cases discarded. Invariably there were dif<sup>fi</sup>culties as stakeholders tried to understand other viewpoints, but the very process of trying to understand other viewpoints enriched the interviews. These discussions not only opened up the possibility for discussing how other stakeholders’ view of value and methods for evaluating value might differ, but also opened the informant’s awareness of the possibility that there might be numerous value dials applicable to the evaluation of a given IT project.

A number of interesting aspects of thinking in the organization emerged in this phase. As has been noted there was considerable divergence about what constituted value. The importance of getting individual managers to think outside their own area can be seen in the following observation from the CIO:

Unfortunately, we often miss the total picture because we tend to focus way too much on individual desires. For example, when it comes to digital calendars [as an add-on feature to the enterprise system] we’ve failed to agree on a single brand for the entire organization. This has led to a costly ad-hoc approach to IT investments, and therefore we must improve the coordination within the organization.

A particularly striking aspect of the culture was the perception that decision-making should be fast. This re<sup>fl</sup>ected the emergency response role of the organization itself, one where fast, situated decision-making was at the core of the service it provided. This was referred to by one informant as ‘the 90 second perspective.’ Unfortunately this culture, essential at the front line, permeated back up the organization into levels of decision-making where it was not helpful. Another feature that emerged in the interviews was the dominance of two foci in thinking about evaluation: technology and cost. The concept of IT as a strategic resource was simply not understood. In the words of one project manager:

When deciding upon IT investments there is too much focus on the technology, technical platform and the system. The focus should instead be on the business and how we need to develop our business, what we want to achieve and how to go about it. Based on this, it can be clari<sup>fi</sup>ed how IT can assist in achieving this.

## The CIO concurred with this assessment:

One of the greatest shortcomings is that we have not realized that IT is a strategic resource and that there should be a forum where such issues are discussed. Today, initiatives come from different parts of the organization and there is no clear structure for how to purchase IT; purchases are isolated within each department.

## Development workshops

The third stage of the project was to hold workshops for all participants. Two such workshops were organized. Because the ground had been well prepared in the preceding phase, it was not anticipated that the workshops would prove contentious. Instead it was expected that they would serve to improve understanding and communication between different stakeholders. In the workshops, the various ideas that emerged during the interview phase were summarized on <sup>fl</sup>ipcharts and whiteboards.

The <sup>fi</sup>rst workshop started by setting out the problems that had been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in the interviews. Next the theoretical framework involving factors and criteria identi<sup>fi</sup>ed in prior research were presented, explained, and discussed. In general, the managers considered the presented factors and criteria relevant to use when evaluating IT investments. It was notable that during the discussions, managers frequently gave visible signs of understanding and agreement often nodding at idea and saying ‘yes’ as an indication of their understanding of agreement with the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from prior research. The participants then discussed what should be involved in a value case in order to be able to evaluate value of IT investments properly at an organizational level. As the managers from different levels and with different roles have different views what constitutes value, they focused on selecting different components from the theoretical framework. The value case was then further developed based on various managers’ experience and contextual understanding. Again because of the preparatory work in the interview stage, there was little disagreement in the workshops and the workshops became, in the words of one participant, a ‘learning arena’ that incorporated knowledge from prior research and from different stakeholders perspectives and one in which the different managers, each from their own perspective, added components to the value case.

The output from this <sup>fi</sup>rst workshop was a rough consensus on how IT should be evaluated and the shape of what was now called the value case document. All participants emerged with a deeper understanding of what was involved in IT investment and how it should be evaluated. Speaking of costs, one manager who heretofore had only considered costs to be hardware and software remarked:

[I am now] aware of costs now such as operating costs, licenses development costs…

At this point, the researcher took the material from the workshop and consolidated it into a draft proposal for the second workshop. The technique here was adapted from the Delphi technique where expert opinion is solicited, consolidated and fed back to the experts. In this case, the outcome sought was not an expert consensus, but a stakeholder consensus. One manager summed the value of this process and outcome up neatly:

## The FRS needs another type of competence.

The second workshop in this stage took the form of presenting the draft of the value case, which was drawn out from the discussion and agreements at the previous workshop. In this workshop, the output of the work so far was presented to the stakeholder group to discuss and sign off. At the conclusion of this stage, the participants agreed upon the factors and criteria involved in the value case, but as the participants maturity level with respect to and understanding of IT evaluation had increased at this stage, a number of new dimensions to the evaluation structure were discussed for possible inclusion in the <sup>fi</sup>nal framework. Criteria that emerged in this second workshop included those related to strategic alignment, IT perspective and IT organization. These outputs were taken by the researchers and used to prepare for the <sup>fi</sup>fth stage, the <sup>fi</sup>nal presentation workshop.

## Presentation workshop

The purpose of the fourth stage, a presentation workshop, was to present the <sup>fi</sup>nal outcome to all of the managers. The framework that emerged is shown in Table 2. From a long list of possibilities, nine value dials were selected for the <sup>fi</sup>nal value case.

There was some brief discussion of this, but because of the nature of the process all participants had, by this point, a full understanding of this framework and they had acceptance was, if not quite a formality, relatively straightforward.

During this last workshop participants re<sup>fl</sup>ected also on the processes. The reaction of all present was unambiguously positive. Managers felt that aspects from prior research and the managers’ own contextual and practical knowledge had added valuable input to the discussions and were re<sup>fl</sup>ected in the <sup>fi</sup>nal outcome. During the discussions the managers has combined their practical knowledge as to what to include in the value case with theoretical ideas and tools provided by the research. As one manager put it, bridges were built between not just between theory and practice, but also between different department, different roles and different perspectives. Some of the value of the workshop can be seen from comments made by participants. On the value of the learning process:

It is the learning in the process which cause me to re<sup>fl</sup>ect more.

On the value of openness and feedback:

It is important to create an environment that opens up the discussion and allows for negative feedback.

On understanding the longer-term implications of IT investment:

In the process, I became aware that I need to look more at the process after implementation. Previously, I thought that when IT was implemented in the <sup>fi</sup>re engines that was it – full stop.

On the broadening of understanding:

Corporate culture plays a big role and the requirements of top managers. Because we had our IT manager as Rescue Of<sup>fi</sup>cer, this has been very apparent in the organization and greater demands have been made. On the other hand, we have lost the other sides of the business.

## Outcome interviews

The FRS subsequently implemented the agreed value case and value dials for all ex-ante project evaluation and decision-making about IS investments. In July of 2012 a number of outcome interviews were carried out with managers in the FRS. These interviews con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the earlier research. Managers expressed satisfaction with the value case, which was considered to have been successful and the value case had been integrated into the FRS IT evaluation and decision-making. For example, the value case was applied to evaluate investments in emergency support systems and seven out of the nine factors were used to identify values of a mobile system acquisition and a stationary system upgrade. The involved managers argued that the conversations based on the value case created alternative views of the IT investment, of which some came as a surprise. ## Discussion and conclusion

## A three-stage learning process

The use of dialogical action research in this study is an operationalization of the process described by Bannister and Remenyi (2001) as a meta <sup>fi</sup>lter approach, that is, where the organization selects a set of measures, in this case value dials, for a particular context and/or a speci<sup>fi</sup>c set of circumstances. In this research, this concept takes the form of a dynamic evolution of such a <sup>fi</sup>lter using a process of re<sup>fl</sup>ective dialogues designed to increase not only cross-functional, but also multilevel awareness and understanding. This research demonstrates how, using dialogic action research, a dynamic multi-criteria, multi-methodology and multi-stakeholder approach can support and enhance the ex-ante evaluation of IT investments. Previously such approaches tended to be based either on pre-determined criteria and/or pre-determined categorizations/domains or frameworks. The study shows how the evolutionary approach results in better decision-making and better decision outcomes.

The core of the process developed during this research can be described as a form of three-stage loop learning. It is important to make it clear that this is meant in a quite different sense from the conception of single and double loop learning as set out by Argyris and Schön (1978). In this case the three-stage learning process is as follows:

● The <sup>fi</sup>rst stage is individual manager or user learning. In this stage the manager articulates his or her own information and system needs with guidance from the researcher. He or she builds mental models of how to evaluate and develops a deeper understanding of his or her existing evaluation problems and available knowledge about how to address them. The researcher also learns as (s) he gains an understanding of the manager/user’s perspectives.

● The second stage is organizational learning. In this stage the different stakeholders as a group learn about and gain and understanding of each other’s’ perspectives. This gives them an organizational as opposed to a merely local/departmental perspective on both IT value and cost. Thus, the governing variables of evaluation are explored from an organizational perspective.

● The third stage is also organizational learning when managers in different functional areas and at different organizational levels interact amongst themselves to consolidate their value dials into a value case and proceeded to develop a process how to implement it.

This three-stage process of organizational learning results not just in a better understanding of the individual and organizational experience of making IT investment decisions, but also involves the knowledge provided, in this case, by a researcher, it leads to a more coherent and agreed framework for making those decisions, improved management communication and better decisions. An important deliverable of this process is that it creates a common language for IS evaluation which in turn facilitates understanding and clear communication. The different dimensions of learning during this process are important. For instance, it bridges the gap between managers understanding of each other’s roles and perspectives in two ways. First it does so horizontally, that is, between departments and functions. Secondly it does so vertically, that is, between the different levels of management. One notable bene<sup>fi</sup>t of this in the FRS case was the development of a better understanding of IT life cycle by non-IT managers. Heretofore, lack of awareness and transparency in what was going on in IT was a source of considerable frustration and resentment. Some users were required to supply (and even key in) large amounts of data without having any understanding of why these data were needed or for what they were used. Often the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of this work were realized solely by other departments. Stakeholders resented the fact that, without any consultation, other departments created additional work for them from which they themselves gained no obvious bene<sup>fi</sup>t.

By creating a wider internal understanding, the new process reduced this source of friction and misunderstanding. Interestingly, the IT managers were more enthusiastic about developing a formative formal process than were the business managers. The CEO observed that, prior to this process, he was not sure he wanted to know the outcome from an IT project or even whether it was successful or a failure. Five years on, the value case has improved the evaluation of IT investments in several ways.

## Value dials and public value

The integration of evaluation theory and manager engagement via a process of dialogic action research offers an approach to ex-ante IT evaluation which, by deeply engaging stakeholders, leads to better decision-making as the probability of the decision delivering public value is increased. One of Moore’s (1995) core ideas about public value is that the public sector does not just administer processes and set rules, it creates value; public managers should, in Moore’s words, have ‘restless value-seeking imaginations.’ This approach facilitates and encourages the exercise of such imagination. The manifestation of the drive for public value in the FRS is the commitment of managers to delivering on their core mission which is ensuring the safety of citizens. The approach used in this research provides a mechanism for delivering two of the three components of Moore’s strategic triangle namely de<sup>fi</sup>ning public value and building operational capacity. It achieves the former by encouraging managers to articulate value as they understand it and to identify tools with which to measure it. It achieves the latter by clear identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of IT solutions which are appropriate for purpose and which have the commitment of all stakeholders.

One problem with public value is that because value is expressed in terms of subtle political and social goals rather than simple metrics such as return on investment, it can be hard to measure and because public value can sometimes be problematic to de<sup>fi</sup>ne and measure, it is sometimes easier to hide behind cost budgets. This also makes it even more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to expose the issue of optimism bias and strategic misrepresentation as discussed by Flyvbjerg (2008). Flyvbjerg’s work is grounded in that of by psychologist Daniel Kahneman who won Noble prize in economics in 2002 and others who have demonstrated that human judgment is generally optimistic as a result of overcon<sup>fi</sup>dence and insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient with regard to or understanding of probability and risk. Flyvbjerg (2008) also draws attention on the fact that bene<sup>fi</sup>t-cost ratios are often wrong, not only by a few percent, but by orders of magnitude. A formal evaluation approach, focusing on a range of forms of organizational value can, as was evident in this case, moderate the problem of over optimistic judgment.

## Re<sup>fl</sup>ections on the research approach and lessons learned

‘As noted above, Mårtensson and Lee (2004) offer three criteria to ensure both relevance and rigor in action research. The first is the practitioners consider the problem solvable. In this case the FRS managers initially were interested to involve in the research project in order to be able to improve ex-ante evaluation of IS investments in the organization. The second is that the practitioners have learned from the exercise. This was expressed continuously during the project that they learned during the project and also in the outcome interviews (section ‘Outcome interviews’) several managers put attention on the fact they have learned both when it comes to the costs of IS investments, what constitutes value of IS and also changed focus from the technical artifact towards the expected effects of IS investments. The third criterion is that there is an improvement in the researchers’ expertise. In this regard a number of useful insights and lessons emerged from this research:

● The <sup>fi</sup>rst is that in multi-dimensional evaluation and decision-making on IS, preparing the ground is important. Engaging in a mutual learning process with managers and users lays the groundwork for consensus and makes it much easier to develop a multi-criteria, multi-method, and multi stakeholder evaluation model of this nature.

● Second, the value case framework emerged as a valuable and supportive tool from which to draw inspiration as to how to express factors/value dials and criteria and to creating a shared understanding of how, from a holistic perspective, a given IS investment is likely to affect the organization internally and externally.

Third, when managers and line users talk about value of IS investments they often think in terms of effects rather than bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. While in the literature value dials, as they are called here, are typically described in terms of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, users perceptions of them was as positive effects and they tended to relate to this outcome of IS investment rather than to the word ‘bene<sup>fi</sup>t.’ The word ‘effect’ may have greater resonance in public value (what impact does this have on the public?). This needs further research.

● Fourth, the true cost of IS investments was signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly larger than most managers perceived it to be and came as a considerable surprise to many participants. Once their attention had been drawn to this, however, all participants agreed that knowledge of full costs was essential to proper decision-making.

● Fifth, it was clear that in order to be able to understand the discussions and learn from them the participants in the workshops needed experience of evaluating IS investments. In the absence of this, discussions were perceived to be too abstract, in particular when the theoretical framework developed by the researcher was discussed. Managers who had experience of evaluation appeared to value the workshops more highly.

A number of the <sup>fi</sup>ndings were speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the FRS and, while not generalizable, may apply in similar organizations:

● One notable <sup>fi</sup>nding was that the nature of the organizations operations, emergency response, was re<sup>fl</sup>ected in its approach to decision-making – that is, rapid and short term. It required a considerable cultural shift for managers to adapt to an extended decision-making process as they worked within an operational environment where rapid decision-making was usually essential.

● A further <sup>fi</sup>nding was that while all participants thought that the organization needed explicit strategies for both the business and for IT, they were rather frustrated they did not have any. Prior to this study there was no mechanism for creating such a strategy. A consequence of the lack of both business and IT strategies was that the managers perceived that the risks of not achieving organizational value of the IS investments were higher than they were in practice.

● The unions were considered to be important actors in the evaluation of IS investments, particularly when considering changes in work practices. This demonstrated the importance of different stakeholders’ considerations when evalu ating IS.

● The creation of a common language for evaluating IS investments was a key component of the success of the value dials framework. During the workshops the managers acquired an increased understanding not just of other managers’ concerns, but of the terms in which those concerns were expressed and the metrics by which they measured performance.

One of the acknowledged limitations of the single case study is that it is hard to generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Notwithstanding this, the success of the change in decision-making in the FRS strongly suggests that the approach used can be applied in other type of organizations and environments and in particular with the types of problems the FRS faced. The process used in this study requires much commitment by management and researchers, but the effort involved is far outweighed by the better decisions and organizational outcomes achieved.
