# An exploratory study into factors of service quality for application service providers

Qingxiong Ma<sup>a</sup>, J. Michael Pearson<sup>b,\*</sup>, Suresh Tadisina<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Central Missouri State University, Warrensburg, MO 64093,USA <sup>b</sup>Southern Illinois University, Carbondale, IL 62901-4627, USA

Received 28 March 2004; received in revised form 28 August 2004; accepted 25 November 2004 Available online 19 January 2005

## Abstract

An examination of the services offered by application service providers (ASPs) indicates that their current service quality needs to be improved to satisfy current and future clients. This study attempted to fulfill this need by identifying the dimensions of service quality for the ASP industry through both qualitative and quantitative approaches. As a result, seven dimensions were identified (features, availability, reliability, assurance, empathy, conformance, and security). In addition, an initial checklist was developed to help ASPs assess and diagnose their service quality performance. Some insights and implications to the ASP practitioners were also discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The Internet and ever-increasing bandwidth have spawned a new method of delivering software functionality—the application service provider (ASP). Currently, more than 1000 companies provide ASP-type services to a diversified collection of businesses (see www.ASPstreet.com) and appear, as an industry, poised for explosive growth. Industry reports predict that the market for such services could hit US\$ 25 billion by 2005 [19].

Unfortunately, there is no universally accepted definition for ASP: one that has been suggested and utilized here is ‘‘any third party organization whose main business is providing software-based services to customers over a wide area network in return for payment’’ [47]. These services can include enterprise (CRM and ERP), horizontal (functionally focused), and vertical (industry-specific) applications [46]. Other services include activities such as application upgrades, technical support, virus protection, data security, consulting, integration and staff training [32]. As a result, the types of ASPs are complex and no one definition fits all of them. This study concentrated on start-up ASPs. Generally, they are new, small businesses described as ‘‘pure-plays’’. They usually deliver services through the Internet and coordinate with network providers, hosting services, software vendors, and consultants. The key motivation to utilize ASP services is to reduce the cost of purchasing, installing, and maintaining software and related hardware [20,29].

However, the ASP industry appears to be vendordriven, with relatively few customers actually adopting ASP solutions. Seltsikas and Currie, in a study of managers/owners from small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs), found that 70% of the respondents were not familiar with the term and that only 6% indicated they use the services of an ASP. The factors contributing to this low utilization may include narrow domains of service, lack of successful experiences, and a risk-avoidance propensity among SMEs. Some studies have found that ASPs lack the tools and standards to ensure consistent performance, service uptime, interoperability, and security [5]. As a result, many early customers experienced difficulties, including long deployment schedules, non-customizable applications, and inadequate network connectivity; many have seen their ASPs go out of business [49].

Obviously, ASPs have many challenges to overcome. The cost-effective feature of ASP itself cannot ensure business success. How, then, can an ASP survive and succeed in this competitive market? As Evans and Lindsay [12] stated, the focal point of any successful company is the customer, because the satisfied customer will spend more and refer new customers. Other studies have also shown that customer satisfaction is a strong predictor of corporate success in terms of market share, return on investment, and cost reduction [1,38]. One strategy used by successful businesses to achieve high levels of customer satisfaction is to deliver high levels of service quality [16]. In fact, in a study of ASP adopters, Peterson and Fairchild [41] found that quality was a critical success factor.

The importance of service quality for ASPs calls for special attention. First, the level of service is so important that it is frequently contracted in a service level agreement (SLA), which details the contractual obligations, including applications, training, support, updates, termination, and other important service and business issues. From an ASPs’ perspective, it is a promise. Thus, it is necessary to understand the content of ASP service quality; this helps ASPs make realistic commitments to their customers. Second, in order to monitor and improve its performance proactively, ASPs need a quality tool to benchmark, diagnose, and evaluate their services so that they can improve it for their customers.

Given the potential economic impact of ASPs and their role in delivering applications to end-users, it is necessary and helpful to conduct research on ASP services. However, due to its emerging nature, only a few studies have been published. Recent studies have mainly concentrated on exploring the ASP business model using descriptive case study methods, theorybased ASP adoption, and issues such as ASP customer relationship management and financial problems [54]. Very few of these studies are empirical and directly target the service quality aspect of ASPs. The objectives of our study were therefore to

 define the dimensions of service quality in the ASP industry;

 develop a checklist for ASPs to assess and diagnose their service quality performance;

 provide insight and possible courses of action to ASP practitioners.

## 2. Background

There are several different perspectives on how ASP quality is conceptualized and operationalized. Reeves and Bednar [44] stated that ‘‘no universal, parsimonious, or all-encompassing definition or model of quality exists’’; the quality construct space is so broad and includes so many components that any model that tried to encompass them all would be of little utility. Official definitions of quality were standardized in 1978 by the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) and the American Society for Quality (ASQ). They defined quality as the totality of features and characteristics of a product or service that impact its ability to satisfy given needs. By the 1980s, researchers generally agreed that quality was meeting or exceeding customer expectations.

 intangibility: service cannot be counted, measured, inventoried, tested, and verified in advance of sale;

 heterogeneity: it is difficult to assure consistency of behavior from service personnel;

 inseparability: one cannot separate consumption from production.

With the development of the service sector, the notion of service quality has become increasingly important. Parasuraman et al. have been leaders in service quality research [39,40,55]. They conducted an exploratory investigation, which revealed 10 ‘‘potentially overlapping dimensions’’ that consumers use in forming expectations about and perceptions of service quality. They analyzed executive responses and obtained the following insight:

‘‘A set of key discrepancies or gaps exists regarding executive perceptions of service quality and the tasks associated with service delivery to consumers. These gaps can be major hurdles in attempting to deliver a service which consumers would perceive as being of high quality’’.

They derived and operationalized their model of service quality—SERVQUAL. After two stages of verification, the number of dimensions was reduced from 10 to 8, and then to the following dimensions:

tangibles, reliability, responsiveness, assurance, and empathy. The five dimensions consisted of 22 items; each was phrased into two statements—one to measure expectations about a firm’s quality in general and one to measure perceptions about the quality of the particular firm. According to Parasuraman et al., these dimensions and their corresponding items represent core evaluation criteria that transcendspecific companies and industries. They stated that SERVQUAL was most valuable when it was used periodically to track service quality trends in conjunction with other service quality measurements.

Cronin and Taylor [6], in a study with a sample size of 660 across four industries, developed and tested a performance-based measure—SERVPERF. They argued that SERVQUAL was too simplistic for complex cognitive evaluation processes. They contended that the perception of service quality already entails an expected service. Rather than using ‘‘gap scores’’, Cronin and Taylor said that a direct measure of perceptions was preferable.

However, Pitt et al. [43] argued that although SERVPERF had superior predictive power, it had inferior diagnostic power, because it provided less information. In an effort to reconcile the debate, Zeithaml, Berry, and Parasuraman later introduced the notion of a ‘‘zone of tolerance’’ into their model. They suggested that service expectations exist at two levels which customers use as standards in assessing service quality:

(1) desired: the level representing a blend of what customers believe ‘‘should be’’ provided;

(2) adequate: the minimum level that customers were ‘‘willing to accept’’.

Between these two levels is a ‘‘zone of tolerance’’ that represents the range of service performance that a customer considers satisfactory. This revised model was referred to as SERVQUAL+ by Kettinger and Lee [26].

## 2.2. Related quality research in IS

Service quality studies in IS typically fall into two stages. In the earlier stage, a number of studies focused on IS effectiveness. Techniques such as system usage, cost/benefit analysis, information economics, and critical success factors were all used to assess the contribution of information service. DeLone and McLean [7] proposed that the existing measures of IS quality could be classified into six major dimensions: system quality, information quality, system use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. However, the basis of this classification was product-oriented because they were mainly associated with ‘‘engineering-oriented performance characteristics’’. Many researchers realized that the typical IS department is also a service provider and tried to involve user satisfaction as a proxy for the quality of the IS department services. As a result, several standardized instruments were developed and tested [3,4,9]. However, such measures provided little direct information about strategic direction and continuous improvement, because quality was treated as a single variable [45].

Recently, recognizing the need for more comprehensive IS quality measures, several researchers [21,22,24,25,27] attempted to adapt SERVQUAL and its quality dimensions to an IS context. These SERVQUAL adopters were seriously challenged by other IS researchers [42]. For example, Van Dyke et al. [51,52] argued that IS-SERVQUAL is ‘‘neither a valid nor reliable measure of perceived service quality in IS’’.

The modified version of SERVQUAL+ also was tested in the IS context. The advantages were: (1) it overcomes many of the complaints concerning the ambiguity of the IS-adapted SERVQUAL; (2) it has stronger service diagnostic appeal for practitioners since it provides richer information; (3) it facilitates strategic decisions by managing tolerance zones.

## 2.3. IT/IS outsourcing and related ASP research

Research on IT/IS outsourcing has typically focused on requirements for outsourcing and client– vendor relationships [2,10,18,23,30,31,33,34,36,53]. It has addressed fundamental questions such as:

 What are the drivers for an organization to contractout or sell its IT/IS assets, employees, and activities to a third party vendor?

 How to select vendors and make IT/IS outsourcing decisions?

 How to assess the risk of IT/IS outsourcing?

 What are the outsourcing relationships?

Most of these studies have been conducted from the clients perspective. Although studies on the service quality of IT/IS outsourcing providers are limited, this does not imply that service quality in IS outsourcing is unimportant. Not surprisingly, commitment to quality was found to be the most important factor when selecting vendors [28]. One of the important studies on IT/IS outsourcing that directly addressed vendors’ service quality was conducted by Grover et al. [15]. They explained that outsourcing involves the quality expectations of both the service provider and the service receiver. Because of the imprecise nature of services and the difficulty in assuring consistent quality, service receivers often form ongoing relationships (partnerships) with service providers. These help clarify service requirements and desired quality levels. Grover et al. asserted that as more and more vendors enter the outsourcing business, and organizations battle to remain competitive and technologically current, service quality and the nature of the client– vendor relationship will be of fundamental importance. In their study, service quality was measured with tangibles and reliabilities.

Due to its emerging status, ASP research has generally focused on exploring the ASP business model [8], the relationships among partners, financial analysis [50], and ASP adoption. Most of the studies did not directly address ASP service quality issues. Jayatilaka, Schwarz, and Hirschheim presented an integrated, theoretical model and identified eight factors that can explain the ASP selection: four of the major factors were task environment (which includes characteristics such as credibility, experience, security, application quality) and functionality, flexibility and support.

In an exploratory study, Peterson and Fairchild found that one of the four major factors influencing SMEs to adopt the ASP model was maturity, which described the level of readiness and expertise in providing application services. The elements included in maturity were quality of service, level of security, level of application integration, and basic operational requirements. They also suggested that credibility and market reputation were important to increase clients’ confidence that the ASP would provide the service according to agreed upon specifications and found that ASP security was the most important, ASP service quality was moderately important, and ASP power was relatively unimportant.

When compared to conventional IT/IS outsourcing, the ASP model shared many features, but remained unique. Last, both ASPs and outsourcing vendors needed to maintain a good relationship with clients. However, the ASP model differed in six aspects: target markets, vendor characteristics, available functions, extent of customization, resource ownership, and contract types.

## 2.4. Limitations of existing quality research in ASP context

Literature in marketing and IS suggested that none of the existing quality measures are immune to validity problems or dimensionality issues. When applied to a particular industry, quality measures can produce misleading or incomplete information.

These problems can occur because the existing measures were developed for measuring the quality of products or services but seldom both. As Pitt et al. suggested, goods and services ‘‘exist along a spectrum of tangibility, ranging from relatively pure products, to relatively pure services, with a hybrid somewhere near the center point’’. Virtually, all tangible products have intangible attributes, and all services possess tangible properties. Thus, certain dimensions are not suitable; for example, ‘‘durability’’ measures a product’s life and physical facilities and appearance of personnel are less important. ASP products/services are an example of such ‘‘duality’’. Because of this, ASPs need an instrument that can measure both applications and services to support quality improvement initiatives.

The differences between traditional IT/IS outsourcing and ASP have some important implications in terms of framing research inquires. For example, service quality can be critical to ASP adoption, since ASP activities directly affect how end users perform their daily business functions. Also unfavorable events such as disasters and security breaches can have a much larger impact when the data of multiple competitors is stored at the same site. Hence, quality factors such as network uptime, reliability and security are ongoing concerns in the ASP context. Therefore, simply applying the findings from IT/IS outsourcing research to the ASP setting is unrealistic.

Although existing quality measures are problematic and not directly appropriate for ASPs, previous studies on quality measures are still useful. First, the procedures used to develop SERVQUAL are considered to be reliable. Second, Kettinger et al. suggested two approaches to improve the definitions of the dimensions of IS service quality: careful reexamination of content validity, such as the review of underlying theory and the operationalization of items, and a parsimonious refinement using specification search techniques of confirmatory factor analysis of the items. Thus, the dimensions of Garvin and Parasuraman et al. were used as check lists when we categorized dimensions in our study.

## 3. Research methodology

Our study sought insights by collecting observations about ASP service quality from ASPs; thus, it was exploratory.

## 3.1. Qualitative approach

The first phase was qualitative: used to identify relevant dimensions of ASP quality. This is Mintzberg’s [35] ‘‘direct research’’, where observations are collected in two stages—a review of literature and an electronic survey of ASPs.

## 3.1.1. Content analysis of literature

The selected literature spanned academic and professional journals and books in IS and other disciplines. Because of its ‘‘renting’’ feature, the ASP model has frequently been regarded as a return to IT/

IS outsourcing. Thus, the ASP model and IT/IS outsourcing might share quality characteristics. Articles and books were reviewed if the terms ‘‘ASP service’’, ‘‘Application Service Provider’’, or ‘‘IT/IS outsourcing’’ were in the title or key words. The period covered was from 1975 to 2003.

Industry reports of ASP and IT/IS outsourcing were replete with examples of successful service contracts. Although most reports did not categorize service quality into dimensions, they were included as a source. Apparently, ASP quality needs were considered more complex than those for traditional products or services; quality included applications and tele-service, networking, contract, and service monitoring/auditing tools. Some related to the ASP itself such as age and certification, while others were related to the service activities. Also, services varied from provider to provider.

## 3.1.2. Content validation

A pilot survey was conducted to validate the items identified in the literature review. The sample source came from www.Internet.com. On this website, contact information for over 600 ASPs was provided. From this list, the first 20 ASPs starting with letter ‘‘A’’ were selected and contacted. The respondents were asked to evaluate items, based on the definition provided, completeness, and clarity of wording. Responses were reviewed and any new items identified were added to the quality characteristics list derived from the literature review. Using the same method, 20 ASPs starting with letter ‘‘B’’ were selected and contacted. This procedure was repeated until no new items resulted. Open-ended questions were utilized to solicit the opinion of the respondents. This results in face validity for exploring the range of perceptions about the service quality of ASPs, as open-ended questions generate data in the participants’ own words.

The major questions were:

(a) What are the key characteristics of service quality?

(b) What problems and tasks are involved in providing high quality service?

(c) If you could make one change that would improve service quality, what would it be?

Based on the feedback, four items were removed from the list because they were unclear or inappropriate; two compound items were split into four single items; and three new items were added. As a result, 49 items were identified (see Appendix A).

## 3.1.3. Items sorting and grouping

The ASP quality-related items collected in the earlier stages (49 items) were copied to individual cards by the authors. These cards were then randomly ordered before being distributed to a group of five individuals (two IS faculty and three IS Ph.D. students). This procedure invoked the following procedure:

1) Item reduction: All items with the same or very similar meaning were regarded as the same; thus redundancy was eliminated.

2) Initial grouping: Each individual was asked to group the items by looking for similarities.

3) Regrouping: Each individual was asked to regroup the items by looking for subtle similarities and differences between groups. This forced comparison strategy had been suggested by Eisenhardt [11] for across-case analysis. It could lead to new categories and concepts that the investigators had not anticipated.

4) Synthesizing: All five individuals worked together in trying to achieve consistency in the groupings. Each member had a copy of the dimensions identified by Garvin and Parasuraman et al. They were encouraged to use these labels, but were asked to be careful about overlaps that can occur within the dimensions.

As a result of this process, 40 items were identified, representing nine different dimensions. Table 3 gives a summary of these.

## 3.2. Quantitative approach

The second phase utilized a quantitative approach as now discussed.

## 3.2.1. Instrument development and sampling

The selected items were included in the survey instrument. Each was worded as a statement and measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging from ‘‘not important at all’’ to ‘‘most important’’. Stevens [48] suggested a case-to-variable ratio of 5:1 to guarantee a reliable principal component analysis (PCA) procedure; however, some researchers have worked with ratios as low as 2:1. In an effort to achieve an acceptable case-to-variable result, we utilized all 1380 ASPs’ email addresses available from the major websites. As an incentive to participate, we promised each respondent a pre-paid calling card. Of the 1380 ASPs, 220 e-mail addresses were invalid and undeliverable. We obtained 123 completed responses for a response rate of 10.5%.

## 3.2.2. Verification of dimensionality

Because this was an exploratory study, we utilized a principal component analysis to identify the critical factors. The analysis was conducted using SPSS (11.01). We applied two criteria to decide the number of factors that can be identified: eigenvalues greater than 1.0 [17] and factor loadings greater than 0.50. Item analysis was also used to ensure that each item was assigned to its proper factor.

In order to maximize the correlations of each item on a factor, we used an oblique rotation, as we assumed that there were correlations among the underlying components. Each of the extracted factors was present in the component pattern matrix. According to the guidelines by Hair et al., to obtain a power level of 80% at a 0.05 significance level with 120 responses, a factor loading of 0.50 is required.

After dropping low-loading items, we derived nine factors. Surprisingly, only one item loaded on factors 8 and 9. The resultant factor matrix indicated that these items could not be forced onto other factors and that they were indeed unique. After examining the contents of these two factors, we found that they addressed price and data ownership. Data are regarded as an information asset and typically owned by the customer. When using an ASP, data are stored remotely on the ASP server. If the service contract with the ASP is discontinued, ownership of the data becomes questionable. This might be an important legal or ethical issue, but not a quality characteristic of an application or service. Consequently, we dropped this item. After this, the loading for ‘‘price’’ became very small and it was therefore also dropped. After several steps of factor refining, we derived the results displayed in Table 4.

The sampling adequacy index, Kaiser–Meyer– Olkin (KMO) of the factor analysis of the remaining 33 items was 0.82. This index ranges from 0 to 1 with 0.80 or above being considered meritorious; 0.70 or above, middling; 0.60 or above, mediocre; 0.50 or above, miserable; and below 0.05 unacceptable. We also used Bartlett’s test of Sphericity—an indicator of the overall significance of the correlation matrix, which tests for the presence of correlations among the variables. The approximate Chi-square for Bartlett’s test of Sphericity was 1623.7 with 528 d.f. The Bartlett’s test indicated that non-zero correlations existed at the significance level of 0.0001. Therefore, the validity of the factors selected in this study was statistically established.

Most of factor loading in Table 4 were significant and items loaded cleanly to the underlying factor. However, for some factors, the item loadings are lower than the 0.50 threshold. As this is an exploratory study, we decided to lower the loading cut point to 0.40 and include the items that met this criteria.

## 3.2.3. Instrument reliability and validity assessment

Internal reliability was measured using Cronbach’s alpha (Table 5); it varied between 0.60 and 0.77. According to Hair et al., 0.60 is satisfactory for exploratory studies. An additional test to check the robustness of the factor structure would be a comparison of the obliquely rotated factors with orthogonally rotated factors. Under an orthogonal rotation, the same factors were extracted: this confirmed the stability and robustness in the initial factor structure.

Validity was assessed in terms of content and discriminant validity. Content validity was evaluated qualitatively by examining the data collection and processing method. This ensures that the instrument does measure the concept of service quality of ASPs. Discriminant validity was assessed by the varianceextracted test [13]. The variance-extracted test assesses the amount of variance captured by an underlying factor in relation to the amount of variance due to measurement error. The total variance extracted was approximately 60%. In social science research, the acceptable percentage of variance is generally considered to be about this level.

## 4. Implications

## 4.1. Implications for IS researchers

Our study provides the theoretical dimensions of service quality for the ASP industry. From an IS researchers’ perspective, it provides evidence that though similarities exists between the dimensions here and in previous studies discrepancies still exist. In order to provide insight to the service quality of ASPs, a sideby-side comparison of the factor model with the models from Garvin and from Parasuraman et al. is useful. The three different models are presented in Table 6.

When comparing the quality factors, one can see that people expect digital products (applications) to meet certain industry standards in data processing, storage, and protection. However, for a physical product, durability is important to the customer, while for digital product like software, the purchased product can be used forever (or until the next version is announced).

Because ASPs provide both products (applications) and services, the expectations and requirements are also greater and more complex. For instance, most people expect applications to be ‘‘reliable’’ and ‘‘secure’’. Unlike physical products where quality may be ‘‘perceived’’, the performance of small applications (digital products) can generally be ‘‘assured’’ and assessed by tools. Since the risk of discontinuity is critical to businesses, ‘‘availability’’ is perceived to be important also.

However, ‘‘reliability’’ involves slightly different concepts: for a physical product, people expect the product ‘‘does not malfunction during the specified period’’, but for applications, users expect that ‘‘both the services and the applications work properly’’. For physical products, people expect repair or replacement quickly and easily but for applications, they expect to be understood by the service provider and be rapidly working again. For both service and ASP models, ‘‘reliability’’ is also important; however, for general service, people expect the service providers to ‘‘perform the promised service dependably and accurately’’ [37].

The ‘‘tangibility’’ dimension is not important in the ASP model. For general service, customers can communicate with the service providers and receive the service face-to-face. Customers can see the physical equipment used by service providers. In the ASP model, the communications are mostly conducted through electronic media.

Definitely, general service does not involve ‘‘features/functions’’. However, the ‘‘features/functions’’ of an application are the basic requirements for an ASP. Besides, other dimensions such as ‘‘conformance’’ are important for software (application), but not for general services.

In summary, the quality dimensions of ASP service identified here cover both characteristics of product and service. Thus, when the conventional singlepurpose quality models are applied in ASP service, they can only assess partial quality characteristics. Therefore, IS researchers need to develop new measures for assessing ASPs.

## 4.2. Implications for ASPs

Factor analysis extracted seven factors from the perceptions of ASPs: ‘‘features’’, ‘‘availability’’, ‘‘reliability’’, ‘‘assurance’’, ‘‘empathy’’, ‘‘conformance’’, and ‘‘security’’. Although these labels may be disputable in terms of representation, most are consistent with those identified in previous studies.

Knowledge of these factors and the underlying items can provide practitioners with potentially useful diagnostics. ASPs can help their customers by training and educating them. ASPs should share work practices and problem solving approaches with their clients and deploy more staff to improving the functions of the system rather than dedicating more staff to improve technical support.

The items and factors can also be developed into an initial checklist by ASPs in evaluating the levels of product/service quality they are delivering to clients. As an example, we developed a quality instrument based on expectation–perception theory (Appendix B). This checklist aids in evaluation of ASPs. For management purposes, the difference between what was expected and perception is the indicator of potential problems or strengths. Management must always pay attention to items with large gaps. When the difference between expected and perceived is large, resources must be applied in order to improve the product/service quality.

## 5. Conclusion

One way to improve the ASP’s customer relationship is to offer a higher quality of service and thus improve customer satisfaction. Our study explored the quality factors of ‘‘pure-plays’’ ASP services and developed a initial quality checklist to track and benchmark the service quality delivered to customers. The checklist was derived from a quality-dimension identification process from the ASPs’ perspective through qualitative and quantitative approaches. Pragmatically, our study contributes to the ASP industry by providing a tool that aids ASPs in making better management decisions.

However, some cautions must to be taken; there are some limitations. First, the dimensions of ASP quality are not finalized and the effectiveness of the quality measurement instrument is still to be proved. Second, using ASP practitioners rather than academics in the initial focus group would provide better internal validity to the dimensions identified by the qualitative approach. Similarly, using ASPs exclusively as the only source of data in the quantitative approach may have biased the objectivity of the results. Third, although the results are an initial/preliminary checklist, it is necessary to develop a valid quality instrument for ASPs continuously to ascertain the quality of services/products they offer. To be able to develop such an instrument, a larger sample size should have been used.
