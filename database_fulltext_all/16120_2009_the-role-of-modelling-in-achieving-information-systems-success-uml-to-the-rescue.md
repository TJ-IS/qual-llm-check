# The role of modelling in achieving information systems success: UML to the rescue?

Tor J. Larsen,\* Fred Niederman,<sup>†</sup> Moez Limayem<sup>‡</sup> & Joyce Chan<sup>§</sup>

\*Department of Leadership and Organizational Management, Norwegian School of Management, Nydalsveien 37, 0442 Oslo, Norway, email: Tor.J.Larsen@BI.NO, <sup>†</sup>Decision Sciences and MIS, Cook School of Business, Saint Louis University, 3674 Lindell Blvd., St. Louis, Missouri, USA 63108, email: Niederfa@slu.edu, <sup>‡</sup>Information Systems Department, Sam M. Walton College of Business, University of Arkansas, 204 Business Building, Fayetteville, Arkansas, 72701, email: mlimayem@walton.uark.edu and <sup>§</sup>Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Tat Chee Avenue, Kowloon Tong, Kowloon, Hong Kong, email: isjoyce@cityu.edu.hk

Abstract. Much computer science literature addresses the mechanics of the Unified Modelling Language (UML) and requirements modelling, but little research has addressed the role of UML in the broader organizational and project development context. This study uses a socio-technical approach to consider UML as a technology embedded in a social environment. In this study, project developers were interviewed in detail about their use of UML along with influences on their decisions to use this approach and the results of using it. Data were analyzed using causal mapping. Major findings included: (1) that definitions of success may differ by unit of analysis (e.g., developer, project, organization) and that the relationship among these definitions are complex; (2) a very large number of variables impacting project success were identified; (3) a number of important variables exist in complex (non-linear) relationships with project success; and (4) the majority of interviewees linked the use of UML to project success.

Keywords: representation, object modelling, systems development, unified mod elling language, information system success

## INTRODUCTION

Productivity in computing hardware for decades has roughly followed Moore’s Law in producing doubled power at a lower cost every 1.5 years or so. During the last decade, new applications with radical novel functionality (when compared with previous generations of software) have been taken into use and existing application portfolios have been extensively redesigned – for example in the areas of enterprise resource planning, business to consumer, business to business, portal and customer relationship management. ISO 9000 standards and the capability maturity model are examples of an overarching approach to increased software quality (Ashrafi, 2003). Yet software development productivity seems to be progressing slowly – if at all (Chatzoglou, 1997; Sauer, 1999; Cline & Guynes, 2001).

One direction of effort has been the search for better ways in which conceptual modelling can be used to build a representation of selected project-relevant phenomena (Wand & Weber, 2002). For example, data flow diagrams (De Marco, 1978), data modelling (Chen, 1976) and the object-oriented (OO) modelling (Brown, 2002) have all been added to the repertoire of system designers. The underlying concept is that visual representation, accuracy and consistent nomenclature in modelling system characteristics can serve to help bridge the understanding between system users and developers (Larsen & Naumann, 1992). Such an understanding should provide clarity for technical designers and coders to more efficiently translate requirements into artefacts while reducing the number of technically valid systems that do not effectively resolve business problems.

Despite the best of efforts, the methods used in developing representations are no guarantee of success. In fact, methods may distract developers from the real understanding of user needs, the political aspect of the systems development process, or the pure technical challenges of the effort (Wastell, 1996; Fitzgerald, 1997). Recently, the OO method has been hailed as a prerequisite in systems development for business success (Watson et al., 2004). The most advocated approach within OO is the Unified Modelling Language (UML).

We are predominantly interested in UML to develop an understanding of its use in context. In particular, this study considers the influences on the use of UML and the results from the use of UML at the organizational level. We believe that this approach is consistent with a sociotechnical perspective. UML and Computer Assisted Software Engineering (CASE) tools support the development process and present a new ‘technology.’ The various social contexts interact with the tools to produce the ultimate results (Mumford & Ward, 1968). This is consistent with the discussion on principles of the socio-technical theory in viewing the domain as a set of human and technical subsystems that combine towards achieving the overall goals of the whole system (Lyytinen, 1987; Hirschheim et al., 1995). It is also consistent with other research looking at the alignment of software development processes with organizationa strategy (Slaughter et al., 2006).

The primary purpose of this paper is to examine the precursors to and the effects of UML in the organizational environment; the secondary purpose is to understand the interplay of additional factors influencing successful software development practices. Our study uses an external view on research into building representations for information systems (IS) (Wand & Weber, 1995). We investigate the prerequisites for using a specific modelling approach, aspects of its use, and stakeholders’ views of success relative to the employment of the modelling approach in its own right, the project effort, and the information system.

In the remainder of this paper, we first review recent studies about UML and discuss where UML may fit into the existing literature on Management Information Systems (MIS) project success. Next, we discuss the research method which involves extensive interviews and causal analysis of the response. Then, we begin with a description of our observations regarding the nature of success, the dependent variables in development projects and con tinue with a discussion of the results obtained through interviews and causal analysis regarding the research questions of this study. After that, we discuss implications of these findings for practice and for research. Finally, we conclude with observations about limits of the study and directions for future research.

## LITERATURE REVIEW

Requirements mapping and analysis are key issues within the domain of representation. UML is held to be a powerful framework and notation for modelling business processes and objects (Vidgen, 2003a; 2003b). The use of approaches such as semantic modelling (Domínguez et al., 2002; Joosten & Purao, 2002) or ethnographic analysis (Viller & Sommerville, 1999), when combined with UML, leads to a more robust representation. Research reports also document that UML contributes to improved project risk control (Tah & Carr, 2000) and more precise modelling of the entire life cycle within technology management (Chandra & Kumar, 2001).

UML in its totality might be looked upon as a formal or idealized information systems development (ISD) method (Parnas & Clements, 1986) and each of its diagram types as an example of an ISD technique (Iivari et al., 2004). This is consistent with Fitzgerald’s (1998) distinction between formal methodology as proposed and as practised as well as Parnas & Clements’s (1986) exposition of the value of ‘faked’ rationality in the development process. Yet relationships between organizational matters and ISD realities may coincide with or expand beyond well-established views on ISD paradigms (Iivari et al., 1998; 2000–2001) or system atization of ISD issues into groupings such as organization, project, or supplier.

More precisely, UML is a language for the specification, visualization and documentation of software systems (OMG, 2004). It also supports business modelling, including structure and design (Booch et al., 1999; Jacobson et al., 1999; Rumbaugh et al., 1999). UML consists of a set of diagrams for representation of system models in diagrammatic notation. These diagrams fall into three categories: structural diagrams (including class diagram, object diagram, component diagram and deployment diagram), behaviour diagrams (including use case diagram, sequence diagram, activity diagram, collaboration diagram and state chart diagram) and model management diagrams (including packages, subsystems and models) (OMG, 2004). In a sense, the UML provides a set of rules that standardize creation, display and interpretation of system requirements. Business process rules may be implicitly or explicitly represented within the UML approach, thus providing both surface and deep structures (Truex & Baskerville, 1998). Although UML is relatively new, it is the most complete approach to capture different aspects of a system from user-information processes to implementation concerns. It is also widely held to be the future approach to modelling information systems (Edwards, 2003).

Research and practice reports on UML are diverse and scattered (Sim & Wright, 2001; Cho & Kim, 2002; Johnson, 2002). A study of UML in the organizational context must take as its baseline that UML is a highly complex phenomenon (Siau & Cao, 2001; 2003). This complexity shows itself in many ways. For example, content is important as well as formatting – elaborate and nice diagrams do not, in their own right, represent good design (Kim et al., 2000; Eichelberger, 2003). On the other hand, without clear formatting, the content may not emerge accurately and clearly. When moving from the perspective of individuals to commonl expressed representation, such as UML, the end result may be coloured by preferences among those making the representation as well as by observable aspects of business processes (Kjellman, 2002). Moreover, the many rules in UML governing each of the several mapping techniques applied to different views of an application is generally not easy to learn (Sim & Wright, 2001). In the spirit of requisite variety (Ashby, 1957; Van de Ven, 1986), a ‘technology’ that has a significant internal complexity, as we see from the computer science literature, should require some complex capabilities for assimilation in its organizationa environment.

A database survey documents hundreds of publications about UML and OO modelling published during the last 3 years (2003–2006). About 95% of these publications address one or a few aspects of modelling or propose further refinements such as conducting a comparison of coarse-grained concepts in UML, Object-oriented Process, Environment, and Notation (OPEN) Modelling Language (OML), and Taxonomic Object System (TOS) (Zendler et al., 2001), supporting agent-oriented modelling with UML (Bergenti & Poggi, 2002), presenting Extensible Stylesheet Language Transformation (XSLT) from UML models to Layered Queuing Network (LQN) performance models (Gu & Petriu, 2002), investigating the consistency of composite data using an OO approach (Ndiaye et al., 2002), introducing UML profile to support requirements engineering with Keep All Objects Satisfied (KAOS) (Heaven & Finkelstein, 2004) and expanding UML to support the modelling of mobile agent-based software systems (Saleh & El-Morr, 2004). Development efforts related to UML are vigorous and ongoing (Kobryn, 2002).

While we find a great deal of literature on the role of UML within the technical umbrella of system development and on the complexity of various versions of UML (Akehurst & Waters, 1999; Glinz, 2000; Halpern, 2001; Siau & Cao, 2001; 2003; Dori, 2002; Medvidovic et al., 2002), we find little regarding its application and contribution in the organizationa context.

We find documentation of negative as well as positive effects of UML deployment. Typically, a small subset of UML-related issues is addressed within a very narrow area of the project effort (such as Chabrol & Sarramia, 2000; Rástocˇný et al., 2004; Saleh & El-Morr, 2004). However, reflecting upon how UML is used in the organizational context, it quickly becomes apparent that decisions and implementation interacting at multiple levels must be involved. For example, managers may or may not purchase or lease CASE tools supporting UML modelling. If such tools are not made available through the organization, individual designers or design teams may extract some of the UML concepts, but are unlikely to use the full range of modelling approaches that UML makes available. If the organization does make such tools available to workers, workers may or may not be interested in using the tools or in using them faithfully to their initial design. Even where the use of such tools is mandatory in an organization, token use may be sufficient to show compliance but genuine work may be performed outside the standard guidelines. As Parnas & Clements (1986) indicate, the robotic following of a particular development method may be both impossible and undesirable. Rather, as they argue, the effort to approximate such a methodology leads to better coordination and insurance against employee turnover as well as creating documentation that, if imperfect, can be useful.

In practice, we anticipated that some development teams use methods influenced by but significantly divergent from strict adherence to UML formalities. At high levels of UML use, we expect the existence of organizational transformation, where change is looked upon as endemic to the practice of organizing (Orlikowski, 1996; Iivari & Huisman, 2001). The adoption and use of UML most likely involves a process of organizational change over time (Orlikowski, 1993; Fichman & Kemerer, 1997). Such an organizational change can be expected for at least two constituencies – the developers who are most directly affected by changes in their work processes and the end-users who are affected by changes in communication patterns fo describing the existing and future work processes as well as evaluating information technology (IT) development plans.

One purpose of this study, therefore, is to shift focus from the intricacies of UML rules and its role in technical development to additionally consider how its use or non-use is manifest within the organizational environment. To this end we pose the following general research questions:

RQ1: Within the organizational context, what is the relationship between the use of UML and system development success?

RQ2: What organizational factors influence the use of UML in the system development?

Although developers and users of UML debate its merits as a tool in system design, construction and implementation, consideration of UML has not been central to past exami nation of IT project management. For example, Schwalbe (2006), in the fourth edition of her textbook on IT project management, has no listing of UML in its index and alludes to CASE tools in one sentence on one page of more than 500 pages of text. To be fair, this book does describe various project management software tools. In fact, it is difficult to find project management studies that explicitly address the nature of success in the organizationa systems development context. For example, given the same project, management and technical staff may differ in their assessment of the nature of success and its prerequisites (Linberg, 1999; Glass, 2003).

Nevertheless, there are numerous studies regarding IT project management success (Yetton et al., 2000). In a study of key issues related to determining IS success, developers perceive that management and user input in the development process are the most critical factors, followed by characteristics of the team members and project management with issues related to the project manager and methodology viewed as less important (Kim & Peterson, 2000). In a study specifically addressing the development of e-commerce software, Hevne et al. (2003) find key success factors to be: (1) inter-unit communications; (2) the ability to create multi-user products (not develop each product as a custom application); (3) find the appropriate organizational location for managing development; (4) managing the customer expectations; (5) highlighting the specification of system requirements; (6) effective selection and development of system architectures and use of component-based development; (7) strategic selection of standards and staff training; and (8) use of appropriate metrics. In the case of UML, this modelling tool would appear to potentially influence inter-unit communications by providing a common format and language over time and varied stakeholders. It would also potentially help clarify and define user expectations, help to enforce the use of standards once selected, and provide documentation to which metrics can be applied.

The second purpose of this study is to look more broadly at the constellation of factors tha may influence project success. To this purpose we pose the following broad research question:

RQ3: What other factors influence development efforts and how do these interact to create the prerequisites for project success?

## METHOD

To address these research questions, a qualitative study was designed and conducted with the aim of extracting concepts from the experience and observations of those engaged in the relevant activities. Similar to the works by Kirsch (2004) and Levina & Ross (2003), this study takes a hybrid approach blending aspects of positivist investigation. such as the view that pre-existing phenomena and relationships among them under investigation are stable and objectively exist, with aspects of interpretivist or grounded theorists who intend to move beyond pre-identified constructs to surface additional observations. This hybrid approach is consistent with Eisenhardt’s (1989) method and conducted in the spirit of Grounded Theory as espoused by Glaser & Strauss (1967) and enriched by MIS researchers such as Bryant (2002), O’Connor et al. (2003), Galal (2001) and Hughes & Jones (2002). As such, it emphasizes the practical contribution of insights and experiences provided by practitioners with a thorough understanding of the phenomena they encounter everyday (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). We wanted to get close to UML-related phenomena as the stakeholders themselves would perceive them but simultaneously hoping to avoid drowning in data (Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997). We also sought to avoid creating a feedback loop where our premises become mirrored back to us (Bohman, 2000).

In this study we sought to consider a broad range of relationships between individua developers and their organizational contexts; therefore, we chose to collect data from interviews with individuals within a diverse array of organizations and positions within those organizations. Although Grounded Theory has been associated with case studies, we see a potential contribution of this paper in showing that such a method can also be successfully applied where data is gathered using interviews from a wider variety of contextual settings. As Eisenhardt (1989) points out, a case study can generate data that is ‘rich in detail’ but is idiosyncratic to the particular case. The method of interviewing across sites shares the advantage of generating a rich data set but militates against overemphasis on a particular element idiosyncratic to a particular setting. This data collection method also shares the merits of examining phenomena in a real-life setting, examining the complexity of the phenomena, not isolating the phenomena from its context, and observing an uncontrolled and unmanipulated environment which is consistent with case study method (Benbasat et al., 1987; Dubé & Paré, 2003). For this study we are interested in the full range of influences that may be important, even if infrequent, rather than in establishing central tendencies.

## Data collection

We conducted in-depth interviews with individuals concerned with IT development who logi cally would be involved in the selection of development approaches and tools or users of these approaches and tools. We defined research participants as those working on system devel opment projects for 3 years or more in at least one project involving at minimum three full-time project members and lasting for at least 6 months.

Participation was solicited in two ways. First, individuals of personal acquaintance to the researchers meeting the qualifications above were invited to participate in this study. Second, organizations likely to have individuals meeting the qualifications below were invited to identify staff members to participate. Although we originally only sought systems developers, we discovered individuals playing a wide array of roles pertaining to system development – including corporate strategic information systems planners, project managers, user security officers, information systems consultants and about 40% of current systems developers. In all cases, each interviewee has current or prior experience as a member of system development project teams. In retrospect, this turned out to enrich the range of perspectives represented in our sample (see Appendix A for interviewee demographics).

All interviews were conducted in North America. Two authors, one from North America the other from Europe, conducted each interview. This approach allowed targeting of the research to issues of interest as suggested by the literature without predetermining the content as viewed by the interviewees. Therefore, our data collection method avoided predefining a set of constructs/variables and causal relationships, as in Fahey & Narayanan (1989). Our goal is to observe variables, constructs and relationships as practitioners view them in the real world. Pre-specification of these can lead to observation of practitioner reactions to the researchers specifications rather than to their independent observations of the ‘real world’ as they experi ence it. Such a practice might appear to generate clean results while obscuring nuances of categorization by interviewees or hiding missing elements (Kjellman, 2002).

Most interview questions were ‘semi-structured’ in design to suggest a general topic of interest while allowing interviewees to interpret the questions in terms of their own experience. An example of such a question is: ‘Thinking back over the last 5 years or so, what are the most drastic changes with regard to how you work on projects?’ (The complete interview protocol is available from the authors upon request.) Research based on semi-structured interviews represents an intermediate level of engagement between the researchers and the research ‘subjects’ and is reported to be used in 8% of ‘positivist’ research and in 32% of ‘interpretivist research (Nandhakumar & Jones, 1997). By working from a consistent core of questions posed to each interviewee, a threshold of consistency in discussion topics is achieved. However, by varying from a rigid recitation of the interview protocol, the opportunity for an unexpected discovery is increased. This approach contrasts markedly with the use of closed interview questions in stimulating wide-ranging views including those not pre-identified by the researchers. Although all interview questions were posed to individuals, many of the questions pertained to their observations regarding organizational practice and history.

Following the logic of Morgeson & Hofmann (1999), it is noted that in one sense, interviewees are limited to statements regarding their individual observations and perspectives. However, these observations may pertain to multiple levels of organizational hierarchy including themselves, their projects, their groups and their organization as a whole. Thus, we can collect data from individual agents but from whom the object of this data crosses organizationa levels. This is consistent with ‘guideline 10’ on the model for multilevel research proposed by Morgeson & Hofmann (1999), saying that constructs are conceptual entities and can co-exist together without difficulty across hierarchical levels.

## Data analysis

We selected ‘causal mapping’ as the analysis technique for observing relationships and providing insights into UML’s role and its use in the organizational context (Larsen & Niederman, 2005; Larsen et al., 2006). Causal mapping provides a method for organization of qualitative data emphasizing the range of observations and the relationships among them. Causal mapping strongly emphasizes a clear definition of the two elements of construct variable and causal relationships between pairs of constructs/variables. The causal mapping technique has been observed to be complex, especially as the number of concepts grows (Lee & Truex, 2000). For this study we largely followed the method used by Fahey & Narayanan (1989) and Nelson et al. (2000). Their method provides a straightforward approach to deriving visual representation from taped, transcribed and pre-processed interviewee comments. In order to insure that the discovery is based on the contents of the interviewees’ remarks, this study employed a method different from those used in prior causal mapping research by no predefining categories but by letting them emerge from elemental-level analysis. Alternative approaches to causal mapping, particularly in the many works of Eden and Ackermann and colleagues (e.g., Eden & Ackermann, 1998; Ackermann et al., 2007), include numerous steps towards developing common understanding and action plans which were outside the scope of this research study.

## Causal analysis coding

Given the absence of prior research on UML as it is embedded in the organizational context, we used open coding (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). This coding approach was based on the contents of the interviews themselves rather than on predefined coding schemes. As a result, rather than each researcher coding items separately then comparing outcomes quantitatively as one does with most communication research coding, we coded individually then discussed the differences to reach a consensus using the following steps (an example is provided in Appendix B). First we transferred comments from the transcript into separate rows within the first column in a table. Second, we derived or inferred causal statements, examining transcripts line by line. and added notations regarding cause' and ‘effects' into corresponding table columns. These ‘notations’ were subject to change in the mapping phase for either of two reasons: (a) in the context of the entire interview, the meaning of a particular statement sometimes showed a slightly different orientation from its literal meaning or (b) slight differences in phrasing might be collapsed into a single descriptive term. In the end, this process yielded a set of elemental cause–effect pairs that serve as the basis of causal analysis mapping for this study.

## Causal analysis mapping

Following from the verbal description of the content of the statements, visual aids were prepared to integrate the individual causal segments. This occurred in two stages. First, we transformed each page of the table into a set of causal segments – each statement generated a pair of (occasionally multiple) causal relations which were delineated in a graphical presentation tool. Even at this stage, references to a single entity in multiple states would be represented as multiple arrows to or from that entity. Second, segments from each page of an interview were consolidated by the target or dependent variable into a holistic view of that interview’s relationships. The initial consolidation looked for occurrences of UML or CASE tools supporting UML as both the cause of organizational effects and as the recipient of organizational influences. As an example, the final consolidated causal map for interview 5 is shown in Figure 1.

We see from this sample consolidated causal map (Figure 1) that even a single interview can yield a large number of variables that impact project success. The causal mapping process reveals indirect relationships. For example, ‘Standardization’ (at the right of Figure 1) impacts ‘Use of OO/UML’, which in turn has an impact on ‘Project success’. The indirect relationship between standardization and product success is therefore ‘discovered through this integration. The set of variables impacting project success and indirect relationships varied to a large degree among our 11 interviews. This is illustrated by contrasting the sample shown in Figure 1 with the consolidated causal map for interview number 6 in Figure 2. As an exploratory study, we did not take the position that these different relationships represent conflicting theories where some must be correct and others incorrect, but rather that each represents a possible valid relationship, at least under some conditions, that merits further consideration.

Axial coding is the final step of the causal analysis mapping process (Strauss, 1987). By contrasting the consolidated causal map with the interview transcripts and the observation memos, the data were checked and revised. Finally, we cluster the wide range of variables potentially affecting project success by grouping them under a more abstract heading called category (Corbin & Strauss, 1990). This grouping is done by developing a categorization of these variables (see Appendix C). As discussed in the next section, these variables are o different types and occur at different levels of analysis in the overall picture of project success.

## RESULTS

The observations from the transcripts and the various mappings have led to conclusions regarding each of the three research questions derived from our literature search. These findings will be discussed below. Before presenting these results, however, it is necessary to consider an unexpected finding based on the observations from the interviews. The conceptualization of ‘success’ was observed to be richer and more varied than originally anticipated. This discussion is presented prior to the presentation of the expected categories of results because it is important to understand the interviewees’ meaning of the idea of IT success before understanding how this relate to UML.

It is not assumed that a given circumstance or action will always cause a particular result, although particular circumstances or actions may be correlated with particular out comes. However, unlike DeLone & McLean (2003), we do not define success as the benefits above cost, but rather as a variable that can be judged as highly positive, highly negative, or anywhere in between. In fact, the same outcome may be judged differently by different stakeholders. We chose to use the term ‘success’ rather than outcome as this term implies more than just accidental results of circumstance and action, but also a ‘goal’ that organiza tions may strive for with differing levels of achievement.

Emergent from the data were four distinct views of success of interest to participants. These can be categorized as project management success, project success, application success and documentation success. Project management success refers to the ap proaches and the activities that an organization implements to improve its design methods across projects and, ultimately, to improve its IT portfolio. Project success refers to the outcomes generated for each project. Application success refers to the technical outcomes for a particular development project. Finally, documentation success refers to the outcomes for creating, storing, retrieving, and using the meta-knowledge pertaining to the requirements, design and implementation of new systems. The views held on these issues are no singular or one-dimensional. Project stakeholders’ evaluation of each success category (and how important each category is held to be) may vary considerably (Klein & Jiang, 2001; Glass, 2003).

It is worth focusing some additional attention on documentation success because it was discussed in various ways by the study interviewees and because it relates closely to the use of UML. The documentation success variables include documentation quality, consistency, utility, standardization, thoroughness, requirements/technical models linkage and use of a document management tool. These variables describe aspects of successful project documentation in the sense that they are goals and measures for the documentation itself. Interviewees mentioned quality of documentation as a cause of more successful projects, as an end in itself, and also observed that the lack of quality documentation can cause project failure or increase project cost or time to completion. Two comments illustrate the complexity of this variable:

You know, you can document everything in the world and if you’ve got people that either (a) don’t read it or (b) read it and don’t care about it – it doesn’t matter. You’ve just wasted a lot of time. So, you know, I’ve been on teams where they’ve spent a great deal of time doing everything by the book and being very formal about it. And doing a great job at it. It’s not like they didn’t do a good job at it but the problem was that the team didn’t just follow it (software engineering manager, small biotech firm).

Uh . . . yes, we had both of them [object and class diagrams] . . . those were very advantageous because you could clearly see, you know, at a glance, what all was involved with an object and what the procedures and methods were (contract programmer/analyst, smal biotech firm).

Documentation success, in its various aspects, appears to be influential relative to project success, but probably not in a linear relationship at all levels for all projects. As the creation of documentation has inherent costs, producing more than needed or used will decrease project success by increasing costs that are not recoverable, particularly if those who could potentially profit from their use choose not to do so. Producing too little will force maintenance personne to ‘reinvent the wheel’ to understand programs enough to repair them.

What are the relationships among the four different types of success? Documentation success should generally influence application success. Application success should influence, although not determine, project success. Clearly, an application that will not compile is not going to be of much practical value; however, one that performs flawlessly a task that no one needs is also not going to be highly valued. Each of these, however, focuses on measures for individual application or project. Project management success, in contrast, considers portfolios of applications and approaches to development that can influence documentation, applications and project success project by project. This presents a potential feedback loop in that project management success is rather hollow if individual projects are not successful. It is possible that an organization has a strong normative commitment to generally successful project management approaches yet fails to implement or execute these methods in some or all particular projects. It is also possible that an organization applies methods skilfully on a project-by-project basis with little or no self-conscious project management organization. It would be expected that in most cases, project success and project management success should move together, yet these scenarios show circumstances where they might move in opposing directions (e.g., where projects are highly suc cessful yet no explicit project management approach is in place or used). Therefore, these variables require separate conceptualization and measurement.

Independent success measures may be attributable to differing perspectives among varied stakeholders. However, success may also be attributable to differences in emergent properties of different levels of analysis (Klein et al., 1994). We see this in the tension between project success and project management success. That is, to some extent, new attributes beyond those of individual projects emerge when considering projects as a portfolio of capabilities. Enforcing standard documentation may decrease the efficiency of production project by project but create net benefits for the IS portfolio as a whole.

## RQ1: within the organizational context, what is the relationship between the use of UML and system development success?

Most interviewees in the study clearly linked the use of UML with overall project success. Of the 11 interviewees, 8 indicated a direct link of one sort or another. Commonly the source of that benefit came from providing a common understanding from which multiple staff members could work independently yet remain coordinated.

I think it [UML or OO analysis CASE tool] contributed significantly on the project we’ve been talking about. I’ve been kind of drifting into other projects but that particular one . . . (pause) . . . it was beneficial because there were programmers, contract programmers, who had certain expectations. They expected to be given a specification from which to work, not, you know, go on figure out how to do this cart blanche. So there was ‘here’s a use case’, ‘here’s a sequence diagram’, these are the classes, methods you need to implement so creating a specification that can be given to someone is the benefit . . . in a format that they’re going to familiar with (lead developer, medium-sized financial firm).

However, the interviewees also mentioned several variables linking UML with its influences and effects. These variables are presented by the interviewees in Appendix D. More than one interviewee mentioned that UML leads to documentation success but is independent of application or project success. They also mentioned UML having an impact on successfu testing and creation of more reusability. Each of these comments indicates more specifically how UML can be expected to influence overall project success.

One of our developers has done some UML training and he purchased a tool called Rational Rose to help us try to write out all the documentation. He unfortunately had to go back to India and was out for almost two full months and we kind of lost sight of the UML types of concepts. But we were trying to use that approach where you do your activity diagrams and all that. And then it kind of stopped. And we kind of went off on our merry way of developing various text types of requirement diagrams. Uh, not really diagrams, just the text. And we really really hurt ourselves especially when we sent some of the work over to India. They . . . it was not clear . . . I think if we would have thought ahead and did the whole site that we were developing with a standard set of diagrams and not just text, I think it would have been a lot better (application systems manager, medium-sized manufacturing firm).

Based on the interaction of UML and other organizational and development variables, it is not clear that UML by itself will create successful outcomes. For example, project size was mentioned by two interviewees who indicated that the use of UML does not provide sufficien benefit for its cost of use among the smallest projects. The same logic should apply if projects are low in complexity and therefore do not require the full development of specifications implied by the use of UML. The relationship between UML and the various success measures does not appear to occur in a straightforward linear fashion such that increasing the use of UML would result in an equivalent increase in successful outcomes. Based on the various comments about UML found across the transcripts, it would appear that some guidance from UML can prevent organizations from creating too little or too non-standardized documentation; however, full implementation of UML may be costly relative to project benefits. Further, we did not observe comments directly linking UML to project success; rather, it would tend to work through other variables such as increasing the quality of coordination, standardization, documentation, or staff capabilities.

## RQ2: what organizational factors influence the use of UML in the system development?

The use of UML is influenced by a number of key factors including staff attributes such as developer acceptance, developer preference, and staff skills and knowledge. The influence o staff attitudes on the use of UML is exemplified by comments of one interviewee:

Actually we are trying to decide what makes sense and sequence diagrams were something that we haven’t really decided if we are going to use them overall or not. One developer thinks that they’re wonderful. The other ones think they’re a waste of time – so what we’ve decided right now is we’re starting with high level stuff and we’re coming down to what really supports the high level design, detailed designs, and we’re going to basically come to some conclusion here shortly whether we’re going to use them or not (application systems manager, medium-sized manufacturing firm).

Numerous references to various staffing characteristics were noted as affecting both whether UML is used at all and the degree to which it is used effectively. Overall skills and knowledge were said to influence whether UML methods and CASE tools could be implemented in terms of the cost of training, especially if all of such costs were to be allocated to a particular project. Emphasis was placed on the extra communication requirements and on the potential of UML to improve this when project teams are blended from personnel of multiple countries and when there is a mixture of consultants, contractors, and full-time employees.

Because we couldn’t have that face-to-face interactions walking through code reviews like you would [with domestic workers exclusively], seeing where things were not quite fitting, what we were trying to do in our structures and it was very stressful. We came to the conclusion that you can’t over communicate, you can’t over document and we learned a lot about how we develop with that type of collaboration that we did with India (application systems manager, medium-sized manufacturing firm).

Staffing was also important in terms of the prior experience of developers and the ease with which they took to UML specifically and to OO approach more generally. In one case, assigning individuals to work with both object oriented and traditional systems may have negatively affected the rate of learning and, hence, effectively using OO – whereas those assigned only to the object oriented systems experienced a much higher learning and use effect.

Some of the people that didn’t grasp onto the new concepts as much were still somewhat tied to other applications that they were supporting (application systems manager, medium sized manufacturing firm).

Environmental factors, organizational culture, and project management practices were also mentioned as playing a role in the amount and success of using UML. The implementation of UML may require organizational commitment beyond supporting a particular individual project. Without such an investment (and follow-through in implementing such an approach), UML use tends to be inconsistent and may contribute to diverse documentation that is difficult to integrate and serve as a unifying force. Organizations that emphasize and sustain requirements gathering from users and other stakeholders are more likely to recoup their investment when choosing UML as a representational mechanism. Contrarily, where organizations do not specifically assign staff to take time and effort to gather and document requirements and reward them for doing so, the amount of use of UML is likely to be lessened and its potential benefits limited.

Although most of the interviewees noted the potential of UML to influence project success, our data suggest that UML is partially used, at best. It would be simplistic to expect that UML alone would lead to positive results. Thus, we cannot link one specific culture, rather than alternatives, to increased use of UML or to higher levels of project success (Iivari & Huisman, 2001).

## RQ3: what other factors influence development efforts and how do these interact to create the prerequisites for project success?

Interviewees commented on many variables besides UML as influencing success variables. We consider how this wide range of variables may affect the different views of success first by developing a categorization of these variables (see Appendix C). The eight categories derived from these variables are: environmental factors, organizational factors, staffing issues, coordination, methods/process, OO and CASE tool use, specific modelling tools, and mixed direction factors (see Figure 3). We have represented these relationships with bidirectiona arrows as we anticipate that in the short run, factors difficult for individual managers to influence, such as the environment or staffing, will affect success variables but that in the longer run, variance in success outcomes will influence the culture, the staffing and the selection of tools (by reinforcing those used when success is high and by provoking conditions for change when success is low or negative).

The environment category of variables includes package use/customization, user contract, task complexity/project difficulty, physical arrangement, and user characteristics, including their level of skills and knowledge, each of which is derived from interviewees comments. Ideally, decisions such as whether to go with installation of a package or a self-built system as well as negotiation regarding the user contract are initial elements of the project itself. The task complexity or project difficulty may be fixed given the nature of the desired functionality, or may be amenable to phasing, scope management, decomposition or other tactics for managing complexity. Physical arrangement pertained to working space limitations which led in one instance to significant communication and coordination difficulties.

The organizational factors category of variables includes organizational culture, change management, matrix organization and leadership. This category has fewer variables than we expected, but three of them would seem quite important in many circumstances and probably broad enough to include more detailed elements. Organizational culture and leadership (as suggested by Westerveld, 2003) would seem both important and closely related as expressed by one interviewee:

. . . because the real issue is not technical, it’s cultural. And what I keep . . . at least in our environment, I’m finding, it is a bottom up trying to get this stuff in. And I’m becoming more and more convinced that bottom up culture changes don’t work. Unless you have a top down commitment to doing it, the bottom up . . . there [are] just too many people. People are trying to advocate one way are actually adding to the chaos rather than actually producing a solution (senior technical data consultant, medium-sized financial company).

Change management was mentioned by four interviewees three times in the sense of managing behavioural change of developers and system users to take advantage of the new capabilities, but also in terms of managing changes or revisions in the project requirements. The idea of a matrix organization was mentioned once by an individual in a larger company, suggesting that IT managers need clout in dealing with business managers from some IT organizations that cut across various business departments and can therefore influence adherence to standards, even when not optimizing individual project outcomes.

The staffing variables include skills and knowledge, multinational teams, developer skills and staffing, staff learning curve, project staffing, staffing patterns, and internal/external staffing. Project success is viewed as negatively related to employee turnover and vendor turnover. Developing new systems must be the responsibility of employees (or consultants) and the quality of their work should be reflected in the outcomes of the project. Overall skills and knowledge was observed in how UML methods and CASE tools could be implemented particularly in terms of the cost of training/learning these methods and tools for a particular project. Extra communication is required when project teams are blended from personnel of multiple countries or from a mixture of consultants, contractors and full-time employees. Staffing patterns were viewed as important. Not only are the skills of the staff over the lifetime of the project important, but also the way that staff enter and exit from their portion of work on a project. Where turnover was mentioned, it was viewed as having a significant negative affect on the project in terms of requiring rework, venturing into new directions and changing the culture of the group. One interviewee focused on the appropriate levels of technical and business-related skills of the project manager, which was viewed as critical for development team leadership and, ultimately, for project success.

The coordination variables are communication coordination and coordination between phases. Six of the interviewees mentioned coordination and communication. Communication among developers and communication between developers and users were both mentioned. Communication involves an open-ended exchange of ideas viewed as a key to project success by Belout & Gauvreau (2004). In examining the flow of multiple causal statements, coordination seemed to moderate some of the environment, project management, and organizational factors as they in turn influenced UML and tool use as well as ultimate project success.

The methods/process category of variables includes early testing, task accounting, metrics, master scheduling, quality assurance, OO project management, analysis/requirement specification/requirements gathering, phased development and scope creep. These variables share the commonality of pertaining to the approach and methods used in project management that shaped the activities of the particular project. This category includes some specific activities and the inclusion of which contributed to project success. These are early testing, task accounting, metrics (as suggested by Avritzer & Weyuker, 1999), master scheduling and quality assurance. OO project management refers to the decision to move to UML for the documentation of the project and is closely related to the use of OO per se. Analysis, requirements specification and requirements gathering are grouped into one factor as they all represent aspects of documenting the desired functionality of the new application. These variables relate to project success, but they would affect project management success in a more general application across projects. This topic area is a primary message of systems analysis courses since they have been offered but at the same time remained a source of frustration as its absence or insufficient use of it is a cause of lack of project success according to our interviewees.

The OO and CASE Tool Use variables include quality of use and standardization of use. The amount of use of either the OO approach, UML specifically, or CASE tools was not observed as a critical factor in interviewee comments. Rather, how well the approach and tools were used was viewed as impacting the ultimate project outcomes. In a sense, the standardization of use might be viewed as a subset of quality of use; however, quality of use referred to how well the tools provided a mapping between user requirements and technical specification, whereas standardization referred to how consistently the various development team members applied the tools. Several interviewees mentioning the use of the OO approach noted that such an approach was only feasible when supported by appropriate use of CASE tools. In other cases, interviewees performed UML modelling with standard office tools such as MS Word for describing use case scenarios and PowerPoint or Visio for graphically showing relationships. Even when used, a number of issues with CASE tools were raised, including cost and difficulty with the size of documents relative to viewing screen size. On the other hand, multiple interviewees indicated that significant benefits from the use of the OO approach with CASE tools was largely because of improved communication, allowing for a smoother transition from analysis/design to coding and implementation. In the words of one interviewee:

It was beneficial because there were programmers, contract programmers, who had certain expectations. They expected to be given a specification from which to work, not, you know, go on figure out how to do this cart blanche. So there was ‘here’s a use case’, ‘here’s a sequence diagram’, ‘these are the classes/methods you need to implement’. So creating a specification that can be given to someone is the benefit . . . in a format that they’re going to familiar with (lead developer, medium-sized financial firm).

Specific modelling tools are tools interviewee reference to in the OO/UML category, including sequence diagrams, activity diagrams, use cases and class/object diagrams, as well as some more traditional tools including prototyping, entity-relationship diagramming, hierarchy charts and data flow diagrams. None of the interviewees cited all of these tools as helpfu and several even pointed out that in some cases the use of a particular tool was not helpful for a particular project. However, each of these techniques in the right circumstances is held to be of value. The most frequently used technique recommended by the interviewees is use cases. The two interviewees who referenced prototyping individual projects referred to this technique as highly influential in creating successful projects. In both cases, the prototyping was used to document specification and particularly to demonstrate both user interface appearance and characteristics.

The mixed direction factors represent something of a miscellaneous group of variables that were frequently mentioned, but where each contains some ambiguity in application. These variables include maintainability, simplicity, production speed, cost, reusability/reuse, user satisfaction and scalability. These variables can act as measures of project success but can also be viewed as inputs to it. For example, a successful project may be one where the code is more maintainable, simpler, and is produced faster and at a lower cost. On the other hand, documentation techniques, project management approaches, and staffing activities that produce documentation that is more reusable, with tools that are cost effective and which generate user satisfaction will lead to better project outcomes. Each of these variables appears in the set of transcripts in multiple locations with varied usage.

Our grouping of these variables into categories tends to be influenced by consideration of variables in other MIS domains and traditional research that has proposed constructs influ encing project success. It is quite conceivable that practitioners would have other bases for categorization, perhaps by cost, perhaps by clusters of variables that are found together in practice, or perhaps by the degree to which they have had control over the variables within their particular organizational history. Some of the variables clearly reside at the periphery of the categories to which they have been assigned. Physical arrangement, for example, could be housed in ‘environment’ or ‘coordination’. Whichever category one would place it, however, would not detract from the contention that under some circumstances each can influence project outcomes. The purpose of categorization, therefore, is to extract themes rather than to create measurable and mutually exclusive constructs.

## DISCUSSION

The variables addressed above, although arranged in categories, should not be viewed as wholly separate and distinct but rather as somewhat interdependent with relationships observ able between categories. Fitzgerald (1998) presents a framework addressing similar issues. We find some contrast to that which he presented, but we also find that a number of our categories resemble the four categories proposed in Fitzgerald’s framework. We also find some differentiation of our findings from those presented in DeLone & McLean’s (1992; 2003) classic model of IS success. These three topics are addressed below.

## Relationships among variables and categories

Among the elemental categories that we have provisionally populated with specific variables, we can identify some beginnings of logical relationships (see Figure 3) and have represented these with bidirectional arrows. From the perspective of the project, the categories of environmental factors, organizational factors, staffing issues and coordination were viewed as forming an outer ring. In many cases the variables in these categories will be outside the direct and immediate influence of the project team participants. Although we have not indicated so explicitly with arrows in the diagram, we do not reject the possibility that some of these categories or the variables they contain may not influence one another.

Methods/process and staffing variables are likely to be outside the immediate and direct control of the project team, but perhaps to a lesser extent than the environmental and organizational variables. For example, the observed use of methods and processes by development team members in the actual circumstances of project work may have significant levels of choice by individual members – even though the parameters of which, if any tools are made available, and the degree to which such use is mandated may well relate to the overall company policies and practices. Similarly, staffing decisions may be made solely outside of the project team in terms of organizational hiring and training and assignment to projects and teams. Decisions regarding the use of contract workers, consultants and offshore outsourcing will rarely be part of the project team mission, although in some circumstances, teams or team members may influence these decisions. However, some staffing decisions. such as turnover, may be influenced by outside forces but also result from individual decisions and opportunities such as unexpected job offers or family crises. In spite of these differences, for the most part these factors will be largely outside the control of the project team. Finally, coordination appeared to refer to a subset of the interaction of these other four elements – how well the organization, environment, process/method and staffing categories of variables blended together. It would appear that coordination was influenced by these other ‘outer ring’ variables. We would suggest that these categories and the variables of which they are comprised are likely to have direct influences on the dependent variables, but that in many cases, differing values of these variables will have counterbalanced effects given different actions and reactions by the project team members. Thus, we place these variables in something of an outer ring relative to the remaining categories.

The remaining categories, OO and CASE tool use and specific modelling tools would seem generally in the hands of the project developers themselves based on our interviewees comments. At least we would see it this way among the array of CASE tools that are available at the time of the project. Truly it is not likely that individual teams or team members are going to buy expensive new CASE tools. But the quality, amount and consistency of use of these tools would seem something that the team should be able to determine. We would see that the overall approach to OO/CASE tools, the selection of specific modelling approaches, and the approach to documentation are highly interrelated. We would expect the sum of these variables to be influenced by the ‘outer ring’ variables; we would expect that for different teams working on different projects, the detailed quantity of impact on the variables comprising these categories would vary. By the same token, we would expect that these variables would act together in influencing project outcomes and that individual values of these variables may have additional influence on project success.

The final category, mixed direction factors, is included in the overall model for influences of dependent variables primarily as a reminder that these variables exist to the interviewees and may be causes, results, or measures related to development activities. Our ‘emergent’ mode is shown in Figure 3.

## Contrast with Fitzgerald’s (1998) framework

Based on a large-scale mail survey and on a more detailed field study involving eight companies, Fitzgerald (1998) presented a detailed, multidimensional framework for the IT development process. This framework consisted largely of the following high-level elements: the developer's environment, developer embodied factors, overt intellectual roles of the meth. odology, covert/political roles of the methodology, the developer/user influenced by the business context and information processing system.

The Fitzgerald framework and the emergent observations are similar in viewing both environmental and worker attributes as important in understanding the methodology’s role in IT development. Issues such as outsourcing and customization, working with legacy systems, and the size of the IT department and the projects appear in both. Similarly, the influence of developer skill, experience and expertise were also observed in both. We also see evidence of developers using parts of formal methodologies and adapting more general applications such as MS Word for supporting modified versions of the methods. Additionally, project management and facilitation of intercommunication among developers are noted by Fitzgerald within the ‘over/intellectual roles of methodology’ category.

A number of factors found by Fitzgerald (1998) did not show up in our data. Trust, for example, was not mentioned as an important issue in UML usage or various outcomes. Similarly, few covert/political roles were mentioned by any of the interviewees. There was some sense that the use of UML provided an audit trail in the technical sense of allowing developers to go back to see how decisions had been made and potentially to follow up on previously discarded work. As Fitzgerald (1998) emphasizes, developers often view a tradeoff between more rigor but lower productivity in the use of formal methodology; we saw tendencies towards this view among several interviewees and inferred that establishing dis: ciplined use of methodologies would cost political capital relative to business partners rathel than gain prestige or ‘raise the profile’ of the IT department. Most notably, we find the multiple and sometimes conflicting outcomes that we view as differing measures of success for different stakeholders. We also find what we call ‘mixed direction factors’ that in some circumstances can serve as outcome measures and in other circumstances as influences on the outcomes. We observed a significant emphasis on coordination and communication during the development process.

## Contrast with DeLone & McLean’s (1992; 2003) Model

DeLone and McLean’s Model (DMM) of Information Systems Success (DeLone & McLean, 2003) has been extensively used to guide empirical MIS research since its introduction in 1992 (DeLone & McLean, 1992) and serves as something of a benchmark for understanding the ‘success’ variable in MIS research. The model, as revised in 2003, treats success as a multidimensional construct with six elements. These elements are information quality, system quality, service quality, intention to use/actual use, user satisfaction and net benefits. Our findings contrast with this model in three major ways. First, the DMM is focused on a particular application or artefact to which quality assessments and other attributes can be assigned. This focus presents problems when considering individual applications as integrated parts of a larger portfolio that is dynamically being delivered through changeable infrastructure and organizational purpose. Second, the revised DMM simplifies issues of diverse stakeholder purposes when it adopts ‘net benefits’ as its ultimate construct. Our findings would suggest that at least four distinct success variables may move in different directions and present tradeoffs rendering their average or sum meaningless in some circumstances. In cases where both individual developers and the organization, for example, gain or lose, such a summation makes sense. However, how does one evaluate the net benefit where one new methodology results in individual developer gain but organizational loss in contrast to another that results in organizational gain and individual developer loss? Moreover, the quantification of that distribution may be less important than the specification relative to the status, power, and importance of the individuals or organizational components receiving benefit or net cost. Third, the idea of intention to use IT never arose in our investigation. DeLone & McLean (2003) discuss at some length the difficulties in evaluating different possible measures of use and conclude that the intention to use is a reasonable surrogate measure. Rather, our data would suggest that use, like success, is a broad umbrella under which a number of distinct and different constructs reside. Finally, the DMM is presented as a measure of success with the assumption that the introduction of IT will produce net benefits. We would argue that some projects fail and that excluding such from the inventory of MIS phenomena worthy of study would erode the credibility of accumulated findings within the field.

## CONCLUSION

This study has documented a number of observations about the role of UML, the OO approach to analysis and design, and CASE tool availability and use as part of a more comprehensive look at factors influencing development project outcomes.

## Implications for research

Observations in this study suggest that project success is only one of the four discernable outcomes of interest in the evaluation of development activities – application success, docu mentation success and project management success are also the key outcomes. The corre lation among these should be strong, but at times may involve tradeoffs among them. This study provides a basis for differentiating different types of success. Future research would do well to quantify the strength of relationships among these different dependent variables as well as catalogue the contingencies in which each merits predominance in the business environment.

This study identified a large number of variables that are of potential influence on develop ment project outcomes. On the one hand, such recognition helps to identify the range of issues that managers of development need to consider. On the other hand, not all variables will be of significance in all cases. A theory that is robust enough to be explanatory in the workplace needs to consider the full range of variables of potential influence to the outcomes of importance and also needs to be robust enough for application across workplace environments such that elements that are constant in a single location may define emphatically influential contingencies.

This study identified variables that are themselves complex. Most notably, the variables clustered in the ‘mixed direction factors’ category may be viewed in some ways as influencing while also being measures of outcomes at different levels. It is important for researchers to consider the nature of these variables and how they are viewed in context to assure clarity among influences, what is influenced and how both are measured.

It is also of note what we did not find in the interviews. Our reading of the literature on UML showed that a significant portion of research pertained to the intricacies of modelling complex relationships and exacting correct rules to apply to specific technical requirements. In no case did a study participant remark on the inability of UML as currently formulated to describe accurately the business logic required for formalizing existing requirements. The actual use of UML seemed guided more towards capturing a ‘big picture’ of the requirements with detailed logic to be worked out in the coding. This may or may not be an optimal use of UML, but it would appear consistent with actual use in the workplace.

## Implications for practice

In this study, a multitude of variables were identified as relevant to development outcomes. Although in general the use of UML and CASE tools was viewed as influencing project success and other outcomes, practitioners will do well to consider the array of potential influences on project success before committing to the use of these tools. The authors have extrapolated from the overall interviews and specific observed relationships to suggest a clustering of reasonably similar variables, although it is not clear that these variables would move together in direction from one project to another. The authors have suggested a model that would encompass ‘meta-relationships’ among categories of variables at an overview level. In particular, our data suggests strongly that project size should be accounted for in decisions regarding the use of UML simply because its employment in small projects is likely to result in ‘overkill’. We would also point out that in projects settings use of UML requires policies and practices that span organizational boundaries. It would be our hope that a model reflecting the full complexity of influences on the various types of success related to new software development might counteract premature commitment to subsets of variables which may exclude key elements in the practitioners’ particular circumstance.

Finally, we suggest that practitioners keep firmly in mind that policies designed to optimize success at one level may have unintended implications on other important success indicators. For example, investment in tools to optimize application success will only contribute to greater project success if their costs are outweighed by resulting benefits. However, if these costs are distributed across projects, they may result in both project and project management success over time.

## Future research

The ‘obvious’ extension of this research would be to test whether the eight proposed categories of variables contain elements that comprise genuine constructs that can be treated as entities in themselves for parsimoniously understanding, explaining and perhaps predicting the various types of success resulting from system development. We would further want to test the full array of posited relationships among the lowest level variables observed in this study. Such a testing could involve a more detailed investigation of particular cases and the isolation of many particular circumstances and actions investigating each across organizational settings. Alternatively, empirical study across a large number of organizations using techniques such as survey might constitute an effort to prioritize the relative frequency or potency of relationships with the aim of creating a differentiation between those that ‘must’ be managed more or less universally and those that need to be acknowledged periodically. Successful investigation of such based on consideration of the range of variables (rather than a priori selection) would add confidence that those left out of parsimonious models are less likely to have influence but can still be identified for consideration in special cases.

We would also see further investigation of the different types of success and under what circumstances they move together or in opposition as potentially valuable to research and practice. If precursors leading to positive movement across success elements can be identified, this would have great practical value, as would the observation of contingencies where particular circumstances or actions caused them to move apart or negatively in tandem.

As this study was carried out in one Western country, specific cultural factors such as individualism or power distance were not varied. Given the continued expansion of multicultural, multinational and global teams working on development projects, understanding the role of cultural factors in creating success outcomes would have important theoretical and practica consequences. Therefore, a cross-cultural study on the use of UML could enrich these findings.

## Study limitations

This study aimed at considering the interaction of what turned out to be many variables. Such a study tends to be ambitious, and as such, cannot help having some limitations. This study is based on 11 interviews, which yielded a large volume of transcript and many elemental relationships. Nevertheless, we cannot guarantee that the findings derived from other research sources and methods such as archives, experiments, or surveys would not yield different results nor that conducting additional interviews would not yield additional variables and relationships. The labelling and categorizing of variables requires extrapolation from the words themselves, the context, and also the tone of the interviewee. It is possible that some measure of inferred meaning from participation in the interview events influenced the ultimate labelling and categorization of underlying comments. Interviewers sought to clarify terms used within the interview setting – for example, interviewees discussing UML were often asked more specifically which models they referred to or those discussing success were often asked to clarify in what sense they were using the term. Nevertheless, some alternative meaning fo common terms may have escaped the observation of interviewers. Along these lines, Nandhakumar & Jones (1997) point to the additional dangers in engaged research stemming from distorted accounts of behaviour and deliberate misrepresentation. Although the researchers can never be certain that these behaviours did not occur, it was clear that interviews included a wide range of both critical and complementary commentary regarding organizational development policies and practices. However, the authors were careful not to predesignate labels or categories, which could have led to pre-established conclusions.

In the process of targeting questions to the development of new systems, where the use of UML is most logical, the interviewing process may have underemphasized the changing role of IT departments from builders of static applications to custodians of dynamic and emerging organizations (Truex et al., 1999). The use of UML in the context of supporting emergent organizations and blending abstractions from among systems created using diverse elements was not emphasized in this study.

## Contribution

In the end, the observation of such a large array of variables, the interaction among multiple levels of organization, project and individuals, and the multifaceted aspects of varied types of success indicators lead to the conclusion that UML is indeed a complex innovation that becomes embedded in complex organizational issues. Indeed, it can be argued that the environment is so complex that any particular approach, such as UML, must be absorbed in a large web of circumstances. This makes its evaluation in isolation difficult, perhaps impossible. We understand the utility of parsimonious explanations in their ease of explanation and application. However, we are not convinced that it is possible to converge on a subset of elements without a significant risk of leaving out the details that are genuinely critical in at least some instances. It is our hope that presenting the structured observations of our interviewees helps to portray some of this organizational complexity while shedding light on the role and potential role of UML. We see from our interviews that systems developers view UML as a potentially useful approach to system modelling. However, its actual use in an organization will vary with environment, organizational, personnel and other contingencies that we have organized into a set of eight categories as a first effort to provide organization to the vast array of variables without losing the complexity that comes from such an array of variables. Our findings suggest that we still have much to learn before claiming a complete understanding of the role of conceptual modelling approaches in the overall development effort.

UML as a standard, fully utilized approach to system documentation would require a serious and unified organizational effort from bottom to top, which might be difficult and costly to develop and sustain. Also, UML is a highly complex modelling approach that presents the danger of absorbing time and attention in its own right, thus distracting from the net benefits of the final system it is intended to support. Yet complex systems require complex methods and our data suggest that UML stands as a tool capable in the righ circumstances of taming some of that complexity. Following Parnas and Clements (1986), it is unlikely that UML would ever be followed absolutely in an organization. However, it is apparent that organizations may vary not only in the degree to which they comply with an ideal process but also in the rigor with which their ideal mirrors the full set of UMLrecommended models and practices. From a research perspective, this will create the confounding of two variables – the amount of UML aspired to and the rigor of compliance with the stated (or implicit) ideal. This, of course, presents measurement and inference difficulties that will challenge any assessment of the value of UML as applied in the field. Nevertheless, it is our hope that this study has provided some perspectives on the role of UML within the development process and as a precursor along with other factors in influencing various development outcomes.

Finally, from a methodological perspective, we believe that this study shows that the use o interviews across organizations can generate a rich data set. In the spirit of Grounded Theory, such a data set can lead to the reinterpretation of a study domain, which in turn may lead to a base for the development of more parsimonious causal theories within appropriately constructed contingencies.
