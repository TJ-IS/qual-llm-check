# Issues and Opinions

# IT Careers Camp: An Early Intervention Strategy to Increase IS Enrollments

Vivek Choudhury, Alexandre B. Lopes

Information Systems Department, College of Business, University of Cincinnati, Cincinnati, Ohio 45221 {vivek.choudhury@uc.edu, alex.lopes@uc.edu}

Doug Arthur

The INTERalliance of Greater Cincinnati, Atos Origin, Cincinnati, Ohio 45243, doug.arthur@atosorigin.com

his paper reports on a specific promotional initiative designed to spur enrollment in IT-related fields— Tan IT Careers Camp aimed at high school students. The camp was different from most prior computer camps in that it was not aimed at building skills such as programming or Web development. Rather, it was specifically designed to convince participants that (1) job prospects in the field are strong, and (2) IT/IS work is interesting and creative. To this end, the camp was designed in partnership with a number of corporations, and included as a central element a series of experiential opportunities for participants. Each day of the camp featured a visit to a corporation where the students took part in a hands-on activity that involved solving a business problem with IT. Qualitative and quantitative evaluations indicate that the camp was very successful in changing students’ perceptions about the nature of IT work and the IT job market. We believe the camp can be a useful tool to create a pipeline of well-informed students interested in IT careers. We present here details of the design and execution of the camp in the hope that others may wish to replicate our efforts.

Key words: summer camps; IS enrollments

## Introduction

Enrollments in information-technology (IT) related programs in the United States, including information systems (IS), have dropped significantly over the last few years (Becker et al. 2006, George et al. 2005). For instance, the Computing Research Association (CRA) reports that the number of bachelor’s degrees in computer science awarded by Ph.D. granting departments fell 43% from 2003 to 2004 to 2006 to 2007 (CRA 2008). While similar aggregate numbers for IS enrollments are not available, Panko (2008) writes that at the University of Hawaii, IS enrollments in 2005 were down over 50% compared to 2001. Discussions at department head meetings during the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS) the last two years suggest that similar losses have been experienced nationwide, including at the authors’ institution. Further, the phenomenon is not limited to the

United States (Panko 2008). There are some indications that enrollment has begun to stabilize (Looney and Akbulut 2007). For example, the CRA report (referenced above) notes that the number of new CS majors was flat in 2006 and grew slightly in 2007. Still, the number of new CS majors in Fall 2007 was half the number in Fall 2000.

Some have suggested that the declines in enrollment are a reasonable response to changes in the job environment, caused by such developments as the dot.com bust, the end of the demand generated by Y2K, and the growth of offshoring. Thus, Tennant (2008) argues that “   there’s not a shortage of IT professionals. There’s a shortage of qualified IT professionals.” Hayes (2008) observes that the current enrollment numbers simply represent a return to “pre-dot-com normal.” At the same time, however, Hayes acknowledges that “An IT worker pool at pre-dot-com levels won’t be enough.” In fact, the preponderance of evidence suggests that the drop in enrollment is not consistent with the current and expected demand for IT jobs. For instance, Panko (2008) notes that by 2007, the number of IT jobs was actually 6.9% higher than at the peak of the dot-com bubble. The Bureau of Labor indicates that of the five job categories expected to see the fastest growth between 2006 and 2016, two (including the number-one category) are in the field of IT.<sup>1</sup>

Thus, at least part of the downturn in enrollment seems inconsistent with market realities and can be attributed, we believe, to misinformation about two critical aspects of IT careers<sup>2</sup>:

1. Offshoring and Job Prospects: There is a strong perception among students (and equally importantly, their parents) that most IT jobs are being offshored, leaving fewer and lower-paid jobs in the United States, and creating a greater sense of uncertainty surrounding careers in IT (Becker et al. 2006, Gardiner et al. 2004, Granger et al. 2007, Looney and Akbulut 2007, Shao and David 2007). However, BLS statistics show that only 16,197 jobs in total (across all industries) were offshored in mass layoffs in 2004 (Panko 2008)— as Panko notes, “the number of offshored IT jobs is not reported but must have been much smaller” (p. 190). As discussed, the market for IT jobs seems to be strong even in the presence of offshoring.

2. The Nature of IT Work: Very few high school students understand the nature of IT work. Because most high school computing classes emphasize programming (Carter 2006, Lomerson and Pollacia 2006, Pollock et al. 2004), students cannot be faulted for assuming that IT work is primarily programming (Cale Jr. et al. 1991, Granger et al. 2007, Looney and Akbulut 2007). Therefore, many students, have an image of an IT professional as a nerd and IT work as boring and highly technical (Becker et al. 2006, Granger et al. 2007), involving long hours in front of a terminal, working in isolation. This is particularly troublesome for IS, which is probably the most distant from this image of IT work, considering the managerial career anchors for IS professionals (Crook 1997, Ginzberg and Baroudi 1992, Igbaria et al. 1991). In fact, many high school students (and, frequently, their teachers and counselors) are completely unaware of even the existence of IS programs in business schools. Students interested in technology therefore often choose computer science or information technology programs by default, even if their true interests would be a better match for IS.

IS departments have reacted in a variety of ways to the problem of low enrollments. These approaches can be broadly classified as either curricular or promotional.

1. Curricular: Some departments have changed their curricula, especially their introductory IS classes, hoping to attract new students or to turn IS into an attractive minor for business students in other disciplines such as accounting or operations (Becker et al. 2006, George et al. 2005, Granger et al. 2007, Looney and Akbulut 2007).

2. Promotional: Some have increased their promotional efforts to better explain the benefits of an IS major (Becker et al. 2006, Carter 2006, Granger et al. 2007). These efforts are typically targeted at undecided students in their freshmen or sophomore years in college.

These efforts clearly have merit—the problem of low enrollment has many dimensions and requires a multipronged attack. This paper reports on a different approach—an IT Careers Camp aimed at high school students—that we believe has high potential to influence attitudes towards IT careers and, ultimately, enrollments. Our decision to take this tack was driven by three primary considerations.

1. Early Intervention: The strategy of early intervention is based on the belief that it is easier to influence potential students and change their perceptions about IS before they make up their minds than it is to try to change minds once they have made an initial choice of majors (Cale Jr. et al. 1991, Carter 2006). Indeed, a survey at Brigham Young University revealed that 80% of the students in computer related majors in college had made the decision to study computer technology during middle school and high school (Helps et al. 2005).

2. Experiential Learning: Most promotional efforts at the college level, or even such initiatives as making presentations at high school computer clubs, are largely informational. We believe that truly changing perceptions about the nature of work in a profession (which, as we argue above, is one of our key challenges) is best accomplished by the kinds of immersive, hands-on experiences that were an integral part of the IT Careers Camp described in this paper.

3. Enduring, Viral Messaging: The IT Careers Camp has the potential to influence beliefs about IS not only for students who attend the camps, but also, through the principle of viral marketing, for their friends. An important design element of the camps was to include teachers and counselors who are often the primary source of career-related and college-related advice for high school students. Educating this audience, therefore, further helps to spread the message beyond just the students at the camps.

This paper reports on the design and execution of the IT Careers Camp. The camp was unique in its planning, funding, and programming, and appears to have been very effective in changing views about the profession. We hope that others may benefit from possibly undertaking similar initiatives.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows. We begin with a brief history of how the camps started, followed by a discussion of the specific activity plans of the camps and why we made some of the design choices. We present an evaluation of the camps, based on a survey administered to students on the first day of the camp and three weeks after the conclusion of the camp, as well as interviews with campers conducted by one of the authors. We conclude with a brief discussion of lessons learned.

## The IT Careers Camp

The origins of the summer camp can be traced back to informal discussions, starting in February 2005, between two of the authors of this paper and a director of IS in a Fortune 50 corporation. The goal was to take on the daunting challenge of formulating a strategy to address the problem of declining enrollments in IT-related fields.<sup>3</sup> Soon they invited two other senior IT executives to join the dialog. For the corporate members of the team, this is an effort to make a preemptive strike against a perceived impending shortage of IT talent. For the academic member of the team, of course, the motivation is increased enrollment. The ensuing dialog resulted in the formation of the INTERalliance, a nonprofit corporation, with a stated goal to identify, nurture, train, employ, and retain IT talent in the region. Initial discussions quickly led to the shared conclusion that they needed to intervene at the high school level, for the reasons outlined above. This resulted in the idea of the IT Careers Camp.

Summer computer camps are, of course, not new (e.g., Pollock et al. 2004, Purchase et al. 1997, Yehezkel and Haberman 2005).<sup>4</sup> Most computer camps are, however, skill oriented, focusing on such activities as programming or Web development. The IT Careers Camp we implemented, on the other hand, was driven by a belief that although these kinds of camps may reinforce the interest of students who already find this material interesting, they are unlikely to create interest among students who have traditionally believed IT to be “nerdy” or boring. In fact, they are likely to reinforce such stereotypes. Such camps are particularly unsuited to creating interest in IS because they focus purely on technology, rather than on business problem solving using technology, which is ostensibly the domain in which IS differentiates itself.

Thus, we set out to design a camp experience that was different, one that explicitly addressed the two major categories of misinformation noted earlier and that focused on the domain of IS, and not on skill building. The next step was, therefore, to determine the structure of the IT Careers Camp. What should the activities, the day-to-day schedule of the camp, be? We began by identifying some general design principles to guide the development of the camps’ detailed activity plans.

Design Principles. The overarching design principle we adopted was that the camp should not be perceived as overtly educational, but that there should be a pervasive element of “fun” (with the education happening as a natural consequence of the activities scheduled). This was important for many reasons: to distinguish us from prior computer camps, to attract the best students, and because one of our main goals was to change the perception of IT careers, as noted above.

On a more detailed level, we used four design criteria in planning the activities:

1. Reality-Based, Business-Academia Partnership: Given that our ultimate goal with these camps was to influence career (and consequently college major) choices, we felt it was very important that the activities during the camps be reality based and not perceived as hypothetical, academic exercises. A program created and administered solely by universities was likely to be seen as self-serving and strictly a recruiting effort, lacking credibility. Thus, a critical success factor, and perhaps the singular distinguishing feature of the IT Careers Camp, was that it was based on a close partnership between academia and business, and not organized and hosted completely by/within a university. For students to truly understand the nature of IS work, they have to see it in a real setting. Thus, the camp had to include site visits to local corporations. Similarly, a message of a positive job outlook is more credible when communicated directly by the businesses and executives that are the ultimate employers.

Seeking a partnership with the business community also served as a touchstone. As discussed earlier, some have argued that current IS enrollment levels are a reflection of the true demand for IT professionals. If this is true, IS departments need to accept this and adjust accordingly—businesses have no incentive to support efforts to increase enrollments. However, if low enrollments are based on misinformation, as we claim, the problem will eventually translate into a shortage of IT talent, and it is in the interest of the business community to support our efforts to promote IT careers.

To some extent, of course, we had a good start on this because the INTERalliance started out as a partnership between business and academia. However, we knew we had to seek a much broader base of support, involving many more businesses. Fortunately, we were very successful. In 2006, the first year of the IT Careers Camps, 6 large companies from a variety of industries—consumer products, food products, IT services, financial services, auto manufacturing, and maintenance services and supplies—financially supported the program. In addition, nonfinancial support was received from a medical device manufacturer (site-visit host), a software developer (donated software), and a publishing company (keynote speaker). For the 2007 IT Careers Camps program, 11 additional companies, again from a variety of industrial sectors—telecommunications, financial services (2), airline, cable, insurance, retail/grocery (3), software, and electrical supplies—joined the list of financial sponsors.

2. Fast-Paced and Intellectually Stimulating: It is well accepted that today’s youth are ardent multitaskers with limited attention spans (which parent has not seen their teenager instant messaging and listening to the iPod, all while claiming to do his/her homework?). Thus, it was important that the camps be fastpaced. We accomplished this largely by keeping each day’s schedule fully packed and by changing activities frequently. Variety, in this case, was thought to be critical. In addition, given our target audience of the best and brightest students (more on this later), the level of intellectual challenge needed to be high in order to sustain interest. In fact, when we asked students after the camp which of the activities was their favorite, a student pointed to one and noted simply, “We had to think.”

3. Competition: Nothing grabs the attention of a high school student more than a little friendly competition. Thus, each camp was divided into four teams of five students each. Each team was assigned to a corporate sponsor. Team members were given t-shirts with the sponsor’s name that they wore throughout the week. Wherever possible, we introduced an element of competition into the activities of the camp. For example, during the site visits companies were encouraged to design tasks that allowed teams to compete in some kind of problem-solving activity; a visit to a data center was followed by a “Jeopardy”- style activity, and there was a group service learning activity (described later) that was also competitive.

The teams received medals for winning individual competitions, and accumulated points based on their performance on the activities. Members of the overall victorious team received prizes at the end of the week. Based on informal feedback, there is no doubt that the competitive element was very popular and helped to keep the students engaged throughout the week. The assignment of corporate identities to teams and having the logos and colors on t-shirts also ensured visibility for the companies that were funding the camps.

4. Involve Multiple Stakeholders: High school students do not choose careers and college majors in isolation—two important influences are parents and high school counselors/teachers. Accordingly, we decided to include each of these stakeholders in the design of the camp. Each team of five students was assigned a high school teacher or counselor as an advisor. These advisors stayed with the students all week, traveled with them on the field trips, and worked with them in the execution of their service learning projects. For this, the teachers received a modest compensation of \$400/week. It was clear that the teachers and counselors also enjoyed the camps and learned about the true nature of IS/IT work and the number and variety of jobs available. Our hope is that these teachers/counselors will serve as our ambassadors in the high schools, bringing the message of the camps to students who were not able to attend. We will discuss later the parents’ participation in the camps.

## Camp Activities

We first offered the camps in the summer of 2006— two one-week camps, with 20 students who had just completed 10th grade in attendance each week. In 2007, we hosted four one-week camps, still with a goal of 20 students in each camp. The camps offered in 2006 were day camps, but in 2007 two of the weeks were residential, with the students staying in a dormitory at the host university. The feedback from the students has convinced us to change all future camps to be residential.

The camp started with a brief welcome, usually by the president of the university or the dean of the business school. This was followed by an introduction to the camp itself, conducted by the executive director of INTERalliance. The typical day (Monday–Thursday)

included two major activities: a site visit (typically to a corporation, but also to other interesting users of IT such as a university hospital that used robots for remote surgery), and time spent at the university, working on a service learning project.

Site Visits. Each corporate visit had the same general format. The students were received by a team from the host company, including at least one senior person from the IS department, frequently the CIO. The size of the corporate team varied from as few as 3 or 4 to as many as 20, depending partly on the activity they had planned (next). After an initial welcome, the students participated in an activity (or set of activities) designed by the host corporation in conjunction with INTERalliance. Each activity, by design, involved creating solutions for business problems by leveraging IT. For instance, at one retail corporation, the student teams competed on solving four different problems posed by the hosts:

Event 1: Developing Prototypes. Each team was asked to design a handheld device to be used by both customers and store associates. They were told that the tool needs to have a call button to be used by a customer to page for assistance while they are anywhere in a store. Two other buttons are also required: an “Enter” button, tied to a 10-key digital pad, and a “Feature” button utilized to toggle available functions on the device such as bar code scanning, voice, and keypad activation. The device will double as a scanner and an in-store wireless radio, and include an 8-line 32-character display. The students first discussed the device as a team. Then, each team member sketched out their own version. Eventually, the students combined their ideas and proposed a single device. Finally, the students observed a machine that is capable of generating 3D prototypes from a drawing. This is how the corporation, in fact, actually prototypes handheld devices today.

Event 2: Queue Improvements. Students observed a software product that displays simulated data from stores on the time customers spend in queues. The group had a discussion with the activity facilitator from the corporation, as well as the project manager, to understand the simulation software and data. Pictures of actual checkout lines and store front-ends were displayed on the walls of the conference room.

The project manager led the team through the sketching of a solution to reduce queue times. The teams’ solutions were scored based on the physical design, process, and creativity.

Event 3: Mobile Computing. Teams were challenged to propose a technology-based solution that would allow a store manager to generate/review/analyze 29 reports each day, as well as perform other office-bound computer-based functions, while still attending to the store floor. The corporation then demonstrated the mobile computing device they are deploying.

Event 4: Security Upgrade. Students acted as consultants hired to review a security challenge caused by the use of proximity swipes as door entry security systems. “Tailgating”—individuals gaining entry through a badge-protected doorway by walking close behind another individual—poses a significant security risk. Students were challenged to design a better solution using IT. The proposed solutions were evaluated on creativity and feasibility by security professionals at the corporation.

The appendix lists the corporations that were visited by students over the four weeks, as well as a brief description of the activities at each site (some of the site visits were repeated across different weeks whereas others were one-time events for students attending camp during a specific week).

The activities were typically followed by lunch, giving students a chance to talk to the corporate team members individually and hear about their jobs and careers. Typically, the senior executive from the host company also gave a brief talk during lunch, extolling the importance of IT and opportunities in the IT field broadly, and specifically within their companies. These senior executives, as well as other members of the corporate team, shared their business cards with the students and encouraged them to e-mail with any questions they may have about careers in the field.

Service Learning Activity. Student teams learned about the solution development life cycle—requirements gathering, designing, building, testing, and deploying—and put the first two phases into practice with a competition built on a service learning opportunity. In 2007, the “customer” of the student teams was Stepping Stones Center, a local charity that offers year-round programs for children and adults with disabilities, ranging from mental retardation and Down syndrome, to wheelchair-bound children with multiple sclerosis or muscular dystrophy. The campers visited the Stepping Stones Center day camp, observed the young people with disabilities, and interviewed the staff. They then returned to the university computer lab to design a “communication enhancer” that would help a person with a specific disability to communicate better. On the final day of each week, a panel of experts from Stepping Stones evaluated the students’ designs on 10 dimensions, including feasibility, affordability, ease of use, and creativity.

Parents’ Afternoon. The camp concluded on Friday afternoon with a session that included the parents of the campers. At this session, the students had lunch with another corporate representative while the parents were given a presentation on the week’s proceedings by the executive director of the INTERalliance. The presentation to the parents also discussed the motivation for the camp, chief among which was the need for additional IT talent, based on the strong job prospects in the field. The student teams presented their “communication enhancer” designs to the judges from Stepping Stones, with parents, faculty, and their competing student teams invited to observe. While the judges ruminated and scored the presentations, the high school faculty members who served as team advisors gave out special recognition awards. Finally, the winning team from the “communication enhancer” design competition was announced, and each student was presented with a DVD containing a video scrapbook of their weeklong adventure.

Overall, the activity plan for the camps attacked our goal of addressing the two categories of misinformation discussed above in multiple ways:

(1) Job Outlook: It was important that campers walk away with the knowledge that the future is bright for IT careers. This message was conveyed to the students in a variety of ways:

(a) Statistics and facts about job prospects in the field (such as the Bureau of Labor forecasts mentioned above) were discussed with the students beginning the first day of camp, in the welcome speech by the executive director of INTERalliance.

(b) The message was reinforced during the site visits, both by the host senior executives during their talks, as well as in informal lunchtime conversations.

(c) The message was reiterated to the parents on Friday afternoon.

(d) By exposing students to a wide variety of IT applications, we conveyed the idea that IT was so broadly applicable in today’s world that there was always going to be high demand for IT talent, and getting an IT degree would give them great flexibility in choosing any career they wished.

(e) Finally, we stressed to the participants that the corporations funding the camps would not be investing in this fashion unless they needed the talent.

(2) Nature of IT Work: We specifically wanted to change the image that IT professionals spend all their time programming, cooped up in cubicles in front of terminals. Rather, we wanted to convey the idea that IT work was fun, and involved people skills and teamwork to solve meaningful business problems. The skill set we wanted to emphasize was technologydriven problem solving. The main vehicle to do this was through the activities at the corporate site visits, which were all intentionally designed, to the extent possible, to emphasize the solution of business problems through the application of IT. In addition, during informal conversations with corporate team members, usually at lunchtime, campers learned about the variety of jobs these individuals were performing, and how few of them involved programming. The senior corporate executives, in their talks, spoke about the critical role IT played in their businesses, and how their jobs entailed as much business acumen as technological skills. Thus, at the same time that we exposed them to the wide range of domains in which IT is critical, we emphasized that the principal users of IT are still businesses, and that solving business problems with technology can be not only rewarding but also fun. One student, when asked about the main lesson he had learned during the camp, said simply that “it’s all about business.”

## Evaluation

Our goal with the camp was to influence perceptions of IT work and careers and, ultimately, to motivate students to choose IT as a career/major in college. We did both a quantitative and a qualitative assessment to see if we had been successful.

We administered a survey to students on the first morning, before they had done anything related to the activities of the camp or heard any presentations. These responses captured the beliefs and attitudes towards IT that students brought to the camp. Of the 76 students who attended the camps in 2007, all but one, who arrived late, completed this initial survey. The second round of data collection was done via an online survey (identical in content to the initial survey). We solicited each student individually via e-mail three weeks after they completed the camp. Two reminders were sent, as needed, approximately two weeks apart. Overall, we achieved an 82% response rate in the second round of data collection. Our goal, of course, was to assess changes in beliefs and attitudes towards IT, ostensibly as a consequence of attending the camp.

The survey instrument was based on a variety of published items dealing with perceptions about job prospects in IT (e.g., Berry et al. 2006, Gardiner et al. 2004), and the nature of IT work (e.g., Berry et al. 2006; McLean et al. 1993, 1991). We also asked participants about their inclinations to pursue a major/career in IT. We were interested in obtaining students’ views on as diverse a set of items as possible. Thus, we borrowed from multiple published instruments rather than limiting ourselves to a single set of scales.

In addition, one of the researchers traveled with the students during the site visits and asked questions related to the activities and theme of the camp. Each camper was interviewed twice, using a semistructured format with a small, predefined set of questions. The first interview took place in the first two days of the camp, asking students about their impressions of IT and IT professionals, and their reasons for attending the camp. The second interview took place during the last two days of the camp, covering such issues as the main takeaways from the camp, their favorite activities during the week, and the majors they intend to pursue in college. We also solicited open-ended feedback from the attendees— these are available at http://www.interalliance.org. These comments clearly reflect the overwhelmingly positive response to the camp.

## Results

“I am not sure what IT is    every day we learn something new    it is very diverse.”

This quote exemplifies the overall reaction of the students to the camp—they came in with a very narrow view of the world of IT and were amazed by the diversity of IT applications and uses to which they were exposed. More specifically, however, did the camp change perceptions in the two categories of misinformation we identified earlier—job prospects in IT, and the nature of IT work? Did it increase the likelihood of these students choosing an IT-related career/major? To assess this, we conducted paired ttests comparing the pre- and postcamp responses to our survey. The results are presented in Table 1 and discussed below in three sections, corresponding to the three major categories of items in the survey—job prospects, nature of IT work, and commitment to IT. The survey results are augmented with direct quotes from interviews with the students, shown in “italics and quotes.”

## Job Prospects.

“I thought IT was overflowing with personnel and I learned there is a strong need for people.”

As the results in Table 1 show, the camp was highly effective in changing beliefs about job prospects in IT. All the items except one show a significant change in the hoped-for direction. After the camp, participants were more likely to believe that IT professions entail higher pay (pre: 4.95, post: 5.87, t <sub>=</sub> 726, p < 0001) and offer more job security (pre: 4.89, post: 5.44, t <sub>=</sub> 395, p < 0001); that IT professionals enjoy great flexibility in their choice of jobs (pre: 5.34, post: 6.20, t <sub>=</sub> 679, p < 0001); and can apply their skills in a wide variety of domains (pre: 5.89, post: 6.44, t <sub>=</sub> 417, p < 0001). This allows for “great variety” in their jobs because IT is in the “background of many operations.” They also felt better-informed about the state of the IT job market (pre: 3.21, post: 5.40, t <sub>=</sub> 1081, p < 0001), and were more convinced both that overall there is high demand for IT professionals in America (pre: 5.56, post: 6.24, t  467, p < 0001) and that they personally would be able to easily obtain a job in the field (pre: 4.97, post: 5.34, t  230, p < 005).

The sole exception was the item related to the participants’ perceptions about offshoring (pre: 4.13, post: 3.70, t <sub>=</sub> 195, n.s.). Although the results were in the right direction, they were not significant and the standard deviations were very high in both rounds of data collection. One possible explanation may be that, before the camps, the level of awareness of this phenomenon varied greatly among the participants. Thus, as they got more information during the camp, some students’ anxiety may have actually risen, whereas others were reassured.

## Nature of IT Work.

“IT people are social, normal.”

“It’s all about business.”

Probably the most significant transformation caused by the camp was in the students’ perceptions of IT professionals. Early interviews revealed that many students began with a very stereotyped, unfavorable image of IT professionals—they work in a “back closet” and are “very pale, with glasses.” In fact, some students were reluctant to even admit to their friends that they were attending an IT camp: “I am not telling my friends that I am going to an IT camp. I like it to be my little secret.” Or “I told my cousin I was coming and he told me it was something for geeks.”

By the end of the camp, participants had a very different opinion,<sup>5</sup> saying that “IT professionals are not geeks; they are regular people,” “there are all kinds of people working in IT, no stereotype is valid,” and “I thought IT was something of a nerd thing, but my opinion has changed.” Or, as one student noted, “I thought the camp was for geeks and nerds but I am glad I was wrong.”

Their opinions about the type of work that IT professionals do also changed. ## Commitment to IT.

“I came here thinking about going into medicine, but now I want to go into IT.”

The statement above, of course, represents the ultimate example of success in terms of our goal of increasing enrollments in IT-related majors— “convert” students that were targeting different majors before the camps. However, the results in Table 1 reflect that, although the numbers are in the expected direction, the decision to pursue a career (pre: 3.65, post: 3.85, t <sub>=</sub> 122, n.s.) or a major (pre: 4.20, post: 4.36, t <sub>=</sub> 098, n.s.) in IT was, on average, not significantly influenced by the camps.

Although this may seem disappointing, in fact it is not surprising. Many students came in either with little idea of what they wanted to do or with strong notions of pursuing a career in a field other than IT. It is likely that three weeks (which is when we administered the second survey) is just not long enough for these critical decisions to be completely turned around—we would expect such results to take a little longer to manifest themselves. Indeed, there is now some anecdotal evidence to suggest this is happening. The students who attended the first set of these camps in 2006 are currently applying to college, and the IS department at the host university has received interest from at least three students who attended the camp, whereas two other students have indicated they will be pursing an IT major in college, albeit not necessarily at the host university. Camp participants did show a significant increase in the expectation that they would make a living as IT professionals (pre: 4.23, post: 4.56, t <sub>=</sub> 242, p < 005) and that their future work would require a high level of IT skills (pre: 4.79, post: 5.28, t  290, p < 001). What about students who were already interested in IT before the camp? The camp reinforced their decision and broadened their horizons in terms of possible careers. For one student who was struggling with the idea to pursue computer science because he thought it would mean being “in front of a computer the whole time,” the camp brought the realization that “there is more to IT than that.” For another student interested in an IT career, the camp opened her eyes to “more options such as the job of project manager.”

## Lessons Learned: Critical Success Factors

Our purpose in writing this paper is to provide a model that can be followed by others looking to implement similar initiatives. In this section, therefore, we will discuss what we consider to be the critical success factors for the implementation of a camp such as the one described here. These include some things we did well and some lessons learned on things we could have done differently.

Corporate Partnership. As noted above, we believe that a camp such as this one can only be implemented in partnership with the business community—the activity plan, the funding, and the credibility of the initiative all depend on such a collaborative effort. We are fortunate to be in a city with a large corporate community with which we could partner. What about schools located in small college towns? We believe it may still be possible for them to do something similar, in partnership with their principal corporate recruiters. These corporations can be invited to come to campus and lead the students through activities and talks similar to the ones described here. It would be a little more challenging and the nature of the activities would have to be adapted in most cases, but we believe that with some creative effort an interesting activity plan could be developed. The presence and support of the companies will add the needed credibility and financial support.

Creating the Right Activity Plans. A key factor in the success of the camps is the activity plan. In our view, the activities need to satisfy three criteria—they need to be thoughtful and challenging enough to hold the students’ interest without overwhelming them, they need to be related to the application of IT, and they should have a competitive element. Given the absolutely critical role of the activity plans, we would strongly recommend that the design team at the host university work in close cooperation with the corporate partners in designing the activities to ensure that they incorporate the three elements noted above.

Selecting the Right Audience. An important decision we had to make early was: Who should be invited to the camps? What is the right age to intervene and try to create interest in IT careers? We settled on students who had just finished 10th grade. During 10th grade, students begin an active exploration of what lies ahead for them in terms of college and career. We felt that younger students would lack the maturity needed to truly understand and appreciate the corporate visits and the experiential activities. The summer after the 11th grade would be too late because most students are already too far along in their decision process with respect to choice of major/college. We believe, in retrospect, that this was the right choice. Of course, a drawback of selecting

10th grade students is that one has to wait two years to assess the impact of the camps on enrollment.

We also intentionally decided not to advertise the camps broadly and let individual students sign up, but rather to work through the high schools. Thus, we picked a set of prominent high schools (initially, three public and three private, now a considerably larger set) in the region and approached the counselors/principals/teachers at the schools to help us identify the best and the brightest, ideally with potential interest in IT. Each school was given a quota, based on relative size. We wanted to target the “bestand-brightest” students because we felt that attracting them to the IT field would be of most interest to the members of the corporate community we were partnering with, not just in terms of writing a check, but rather in getting highly involved in program development and implementation.

Another reason to work through the schools is that, as some of the comments above indicate, at least initially, there is likely to be some resistance among the students. When 10th graders hear about a computer camp, there is a tendency to think it will be nerdy and boring and they will work on things like programming. Working through teachers and counselors affords the opportunity to deliver a more personal message (to the teachers/counselors and, through them, to the students) about the purpose and plan of the camps, and helps to overcome the initial reluctance.

One decision that we are going to revisit is letting the schools have the final say on the specific students to send to the camps. In a few cases, the schools picked students who had firmly decided on a different career and were mostly using the camp to add to their college resumes. These students are, of course, much harder to influence and not an efficient use of resources. We would prefer students who have expressed some interest in IT, or are undecided and have an open mind. For the next camps, therefore, we are developing an application form that includes questions about interest in IT and planned major in college. Interested students will be asked to complete this form, and we will select the specific attendees from nominees submitted by the schools.

Publicity. An important element of our strategy was to seek as much visibility as possible through coverage of the camps in the local media. Such publicity has two obvious benefits. First, the message reaches students and parents beyond those in the camps. Second, it gives our corporate sponsors publicity for their investments, and helps us gain visibility with potential new corporate sponsors.

The business section of the local daily newspaper and the main local business newspaper (a weekly) have both written multiple stories over the last three years on the growth of the program. A story editor at the local NPR affiliate contacted the executive director of INTERalliance in June 2007, requesting an interview after hearing about the camps from several participating high schools. The interview was aired in three segments in July 2007. Finally, the participating high schools and universities have featured stories about INTERalliance in their ongoing communications to their stakeholders.

Involve Multiple Stakeholders. As noted above, parents and high school teachers/counselors are clearly very important influences on students’ choices with respect to colleges and careers. Thus, it is important to include them as an integral part of the camp design—these stakeholders must also hear and believe the message of the camps. Teachers and counselors can also help in spreading the word to students who did not attend the camps. We have already discussed above the specifics of how we did this.

School Year Programming. Even though the camp was very successful and was perceived as exciting and enjoyable by the participants, it will not be effective as a one-shot, isolated effort. It is important to have ongoing activities throughout the school year that reinforce the message of the camp. To this end, we encouraged students who attended the IT Careers Camps to create “chapters” of INTERalliance at their high schools. A teacher/counselor from each school who served as team advisor during the camp typically takes on the duties of faculty coordinator for the chapter. Each INTERalliance chapter is then asked to plan and host at least one event each year, open to students from INTERalliance chapters all over the city.

Examples of recent INTERalliance chapter events include: a presentation by a guest from the IT department of a local children’s hospital on career opportunities in IT in medicine and health care; a “Wii

Olympics” and discussion about the use of IT and gaming in various careers; a “Groundhog Day Search Engine” competition, with a presentation by students on the various search engine technologies and advantages of each; and an “INTERalliance Student Film Festival” with shorts produced by high school students and judged by local faculty and industry celebrities in digital media.

Summer Internships. Corporate sponsors are encouraged to hire INTERalliance IT Careers Camp graduates for summer internships after 11th and 12th grade. This is a challenge, because most companies are not set up to hire high school interns—we are, however, working cooperatively with our corporate sponsors to develop templates that can be used broadly. We have had some success—examples of internships deployed so far include working in the network administration department of a company; working on the help desk in a company’s call center; doing e-commerce research for a company, exploring why the online shopping experience for one brand is more user friendly than a competitor’s brand; and helping with logistics, administration, and operation of the IT Careers Camps themselves.

## Conclusion

We set out to try to influence enrollments in IT related majors through a strategy of early intervention. The specific vehicle we chose was the IT Careers Camp. This paper described the design and implementation of the camps in some detail, including the activity plans, the alliances with industry partners and high schools, the critical success factors, lessons learned, and the outcomes. As the evaluations show, the camps were quite successful in changing perceptions about job prospects in IT and the nature of IT work. We believe these changed perceptions should ultimately drive enrollments. Of course, the evidence on the actual impact on enrollments is just beginning to come in as the first campers are starting to apply for college. However, as we reported above, there is some anecdotal evidence that it is having an impact.

Of course, for truly lasting effects, the camp cannot be a one-time effort. Instead, as a discipline, we need to consistently be in the business of informing and educating our potential students about our profession. This will not only help to soften the cycle of enrollment downturns and upswings that have characterized the field over the last 20 years; it will also result in a more informed incoming student population, who choose the major/career not just because of a perceived “hot” job market, but because the work itself has intrinsic appeal and is a good fit with their personalities/skills/career goals. If we limit our efforts solely to times of crisis, and only to publicizing the job prospects, as has often been the case in the past, our enrollment issues may temporarily turn around, but a new group of students will enroll in IS majors with little real knowledge about the profession, maybe attracted solely by the prospect of easily finding a job (George et al. 2005). IS faculty will revert to the task of weeding out students who do not fit (Cale Jr. et al. 1991), which might contribute to a future downturn.

Thus, we have made every effort to put in place an enduring coalition of business and academic partners (at both the high school and university levels) so that the IT Careers Camp is, in effect, institutionalized. To date, this seems to have been successful. At the time of writing, we are about to launch our third set of camps. We have expanded the number of universities serving as hosts, the number of corporations supporting the effort, and the number of participating high schools. In addition, as we discussed briefly above, we are putting in place year-round initiatives that will help to reinforce the message of the camps.

## Appendix. 2007 IT Careers Camps

Program Site Visits

University Data Center—Tour of the data center in the form of an information scavenger hunt, followed by a computer-based “Jeopardy” competition about data center terminology.

IT Services Provider—IT consulting problem-solving competition, including matching resumes to job descriptions against the clock, finding the flaws and bugs in a website, writing a “job aid” that accurately describes “how to,” and attempting to translate highly colloquial local English into global English.

Medical Device Manufacturer—Hands-on participation with surgical simulators and the DaVinci surgery robot, plus a tour of industrial design operations where surgical tools are designed and modeled using 3-D printers that produce wax models.

Maintenance Supplies/Services Provider—Operations problem solving using IT, including how to use automation to stuff the right quantity of aspirin packs in first-aid kits; how

IT helps to optimize the number of uniform sleeves and pant legs from a bolt of cloth while minimizing waste; and using IT to determine the best routes for drivers and the cheapest means of shipping.

Consumer Products Company—Using virtual reality technology to cost effectively measure consumer response to different packaging and on-shelf, in-store displays.

Financial Services Provider A—Using IT and applied technology to manage high-volume lockbox activities, sort and process incoming checks and payments, automate and optimize data entry, and create deposit information.

Airline—Using IT to calculate optimum flight routes, minimize fuel consumption, and manage operations at the flight center, plus an opportunity to try the flight simulators utilized to train pilots.

Insurance Company—Using IT to assess risk and determine applicable premiums, manage limited office space for a growing staff, and assemble highly complex policies using digitized content management.

Retail/Grocery Chain A—Designing a wireless in-store device, using IT to optimize customers’ checkout experience, harness mobile technology for management reporting, and improve store security.

Telecommunications Company—Disaster response and recovery, as students manage a hypothetical “city” after a tornado and create a strategy for restoring IT infrastructure to that city against the clock.

Financial Services Provider B—Students problem-solve a security breach scenario, discuss the challenges of going cashless, learn about issues with multiple IDs/passwords, and general on-boarding issues (the challenges of bringing new employees on board and how IT helps this process).

Retail/Grocery Chain B—A “reverse” scavenger hunt at a busy department store in which the students search for as many possible recommendations of where IT is NOT currently being used, but could be used to improve efficiency, cost-effectiveness, or the customer experience. Also, a visit to the credit/financial processing center to problem-solve issues related to customer credit ratings and credit card issuance using IT and technology.
