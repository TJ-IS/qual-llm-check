## Abstract

This study addresses three key issues related to media differences among computer-mediated groups meeting face-to-face, synchronously, and asynchronously: First, do groups using different media perceive characteristics of these media differently? Second, do media-related perceptions remain static or change as they are used over time? And finally, do media differences result in performance differences, i.e., do richer media result in better performance for equivocal tasks as predicted by bandwidth theories?

Some results of this study tend to support media-characteristics theories while others offer limited support for more evolutionary perspectives. For instance, initially, face-to-face groups found their medium to be warmer, have a better interface, and be more effective compared to their distributed counterparts. While many of these initial perceptions lingered over time, there was one notable exception. At the start of the study, face-to-face groups rated their medium as being more effective than synchronous groups; however, by the end of the study, no significant differences were apparent. Moreover, despite the persistently lower social presence of leaner media, distributed-synchronous groups performed better than their face-to-face counterparts. Finally, the two types of distributed groups—synchronous and asynchronous—did not differ significantly in their perceptions or performance. This study improves our understanding of distributed interaction while simultaneously highlighting the need to further investigate the relationships among tasks, technologies and teams over time.

Keywords: Communication media, computer-mediated communication, group performance, group perceptions, longitudinal research strategies

## Introduction

The increasing importance of group work and the emergence of a variety of group support systems, including relatively simple technologies such as e-mail as well as more sophisticated groupware applications, require modern organizations to evaluate which media will best support their teams. Researchers have long argued that media differ in their potential to convey both quantity and type of information in a given time frame (e.g., Short et al. 1976), i.e., they differ in their richness. Consequently, some media may be better suited to a certain type of task than others (Daft and Lengel 1986). A number of empirical studies (e.g., Kinney and Dennis 1994; Valacich et al. 1994) have examined media richness issues, although with somewhat mixed results.

Organizations are becoming increasingly distributed, often globally, as many seek to develop both new customer bases and new sources of labor. Distributed organizational teams need to be able to interact in a variety of temporal configurations. For instance, for some activities, they may need to exchange information in real time; for other activities delayed interaction may be acceptable (or even preferable). Interaction across widely disparate time zones presents groups with unique constraints and perhaps even unique opportunities. Supporting group work in this expanding, distributed environment requires exploring the capacities of new and old media. Research has largely focused on real-time interaction, both co-located and distributed, while asynchronous interaction and the media that support it have received considerably less attention. This study targets issues related to all three types of interaction: face-to-face, distributed-synchronous, and distributed-asynchronous.

Group work in organizations typically occurs over time and requires sustained interaction. Yet, with a few notable exceptions (e.g., Walther 1992), empirical studies have neglected issues related to time. Thus, while theorists have predicted that media differ in richness, empirical evidence about the persistence of these differences is lacking. In the context of the growing geographical dispersion of organizations and their work groups, this study examined: (1) whether users' perceptions about different media differ; (2) whether these perceptions remain static or evolve over time; and (3) whether media differences result in performance differences.

# Research and Theoretical Foundations

## Research Model

To examine the above issues, this study used an input-process-output model of group interaction (see Figure 1). The two input variables of interest were (a) media type, i.e., computer-supported communication in face-to-face, synchronous, and asynchronous settings, and (b) group history. Other input variables of interest, such as task characteristics, group size, and group composition, were controlled in this study. The research framework suggests that, as the input variables interact with each other, members of the group will develop perceptions about the medium with which they are working. These perceptions include evaluations of its social presence, its interface, and its effectiveness for the task at hand. (Details of these variables, how they were operationalized, and our expectations about them are provided in the following sections.)

The research model explicitly incorporates time into the group's interaction processes. Examining interactions longitudinally will reveal whether, with the passage of time, as members become more familiar with the medium and with each other, perceptions are likely to change. Thus, time is viewed as a critical boundary variable that impacts the group interaction process. The end result of the interaction process is the group's task outcome, i.e., a tangible deliverable that can be evaluated for its quality. Note, however, that this research model is limited to studying how media characteristics and time interact to affect outcomes. In the "real world," other factors, such as the medium's convenience, cost, or its availability, may also impact media use and, thus, outcomes.

Two theoretical perspectives are relevant to the above framework. The first of these perspectives focuses on characteristics of the media as primary determinants of outcomes. This approach, articulated by Daft and Lengel (1986), assumes that different media differ in their structural characteristics, such as social presence and richness. Thus, users' perceptions about these characteristics will also differ based on their choice of media. The second approach assumes that prop-

## A Media-Characteristics Perspective

Social presence is the extent to which one feels the presence of a person with whom one is interacting. Short et al. (1976) argued that some media convey greater social presence than others. For instance, face-to-face interaction is considered to be high in social presence, primarily because of the capacity of the medium to transmit proximal, facial, and other nonverbal cues relative to other media. In contrast, because some computerized media (such as e-mail) exhibit inherently lower bandwidth $^{2}$ than face-to-face interaction, they permit the transmission of fewer types of visual and non-verbal cues, thus restricting socioemotional communication (e.g., Rice and Love 1987). For instance, Sproull and

Kiesler (1986) found that computer mediated communication (CMC) was weak in conveying social context cues, increased self-absorption, reduced awareness of others, and impeded the conveying of “status” differences.

Moreover, the “store-and-forward” nature of computer-based communication facilitates and may even encourage non-synchronous interaction. Non-synchronous interaction results in messaging and feedback being dislocated in one of two ways: dislocated across space but synchronized in time, e.g., real-time computer conferencing; or dislocated across both space and time, e.g., e-mail. Greater delays between message and response (i.e., less immediacy of feedback) can contribute to the medium being perceived as having low social presence.

In addition to differences in social presence, Daft and associates (e.g., Daft and Lengel 1986; Trevino et al. 1990) argue that media vary in their capacity to convey information, i.e., the amount and types of information they can deliver within comparable time intervals. Media with relatively more capacity (i.e., more bandwidth) are considered rich media (e.g., face-to-face interaction), while those with less capacity are regarded as lean media (e.g., written letters). Rich media allow the simultaneous exchange of more types of information, allowing communicators to clarify and reduce message equivocality or ambiguity. For instance, the statement "he'll never make a mistake" may be interpreted as a favorable remark on the basis of the verbal content alone. Accompanied by a rolling of the eyes, however, the comment may convey a very different meaning. In such ambiguous situations, media that filter out one or more parallel or supplemental channels of communication may impede delivery of the intended meaning. Media that permit such augmentations essentially transmit a greater variety of language or cue sets; hence, they are considered richer relative to media that support fewer augmentations. For example, Kinney and Dennis (1994) found that dyads using different media rated face-to-face environments richer than synchronous CMC, which in turn was rated richer than asynchronous CMC.

Media richness theory further proposes that leaner media, given their limited cue-carrying capacity, will be less effective for groups performing ambiguous tasks (such as developing consensus or negotiating a contract) which require a variety of cues to be exchanged. In contrast, tasks requiring uncertainty reduction—such as notifying others of a scheduling change—may be better supported with leaner media. For instance, Valacich et al. (1994) found that face-to-face dyads reported greater media richness and performed better than CMC groups.

Despite some results supporting media characteristics-dependent theories, the overall empirical evidence has been mixed. For instance, in a longitudinal study, Dufner et al. (1994) found that groups with greater levels of computer support perceived the medium as being more personal, capable of increased feedback and richer. In contrast, a study by Chidambaram and Jones (1993) found that the addition of computer support decreased social presence for face-to-face groups but did not lower it for distributed groups (using audio conferencing). These results suggest that, in addition to the medium, the context in which it is used can influence perceptions and outcomes.

## A Time-Based Interaction Perspective

The media-characteristics perspective presumes that communication outcomes are determined by a medium's characteristics such as its bandwidth and immediacy of feedback. In other words, this view attributes media choice, use, and outcomes to the technical and static structures of the medium, i.e., a form of technological determinism. However, some researchers (e.g., Markus 1994; Walther 1992) contend that interactions with technology evolve over time and hence need to be examined in a broader and more dynamic context. For instance, Markus suggests that deterministic theories ignore a number of social forces, including users' intentions and critical mass, that affect outcomes. Similarly, Lea (1991) suggests that users can creatively employ computer-mediated communication over time to bring about varying degrees of social interaction, depending on their requirements.

Walther (1992) argues that while cue-carrying capacity may indeed differ among media, so called "lean" media such as CMC can still support the exchange of relational (i.e., social) information. His Social Information Processing (SIP) theory asserts that some amount of social information must be processed in order to form personal impressions and develop interpersonal relations. Walther concludes, however, that what is important for relational development is not the amount of information processed in a single exchange, but the accrual and processing of information over a series of exchanges. He demonstrated that relational development does occur via lean media: it just takes more time than with rich media. Thus, the rate of relational exchange may be slower in leaner media, but over time, relational exchange in lean media can be as effective as in rich media.

In fact, Walther (1996) maintains that, when manipulated intentionally, some structural characteristics in CMC (e.g., reduced cue capacity) may result in hyperpersonal communication. To illustrate, the reduced cue carrying capacity of CMC allows users to be very selective—perhaps even deceptive—in how they present themselves. Thus, SIP theory suggests that a lean medium can focus interaction on specific issues that users prefer to explore, effectively filtering out contextual factors that may distract those using richer media.

Using different means to study the dynamics of group communication, McGrath (1991) identifies three dimensions extant in any workgroup process: time, interaction, and performance (TIP). TIP theory contends that groups perform a number of functions simultaneously—production, well being, and member support—when working on a project. Because multiple functions are being attended to simultaneously, the TIP perspective concludes that interaction is critical for effective coordination of resources among the various activities and functions. The sequential nature of activities suggests coordination and action both occur at least partly as functions of time. In other words, neither task nor time can be examined independently of each other. Besides, neither of them can be considered without also considering their impact on interaction processes or task performance. TIP implies that the temporal characteristics of a medium, such as the amount of time delay for feedback, influence members' use of the medium and methods to support their interaction. These choices in turn affect performance. Thus, the TIP approach suggests that changes in patterns of choice and use are likely to occur as groups (1) experience shifts in their focus relative to the function currently consuming resources and (2) gain experience with a medium.

## Hypotheses

The above discussion highlights two key theoretical concepts:

\- Media differ in their characteristics: Media differences, theorists have argued, will affect perceptions, processes, and performance. In particular, the media-characteristics perspective argues that social presence, communication interface, and communication effectiveness will differ among users of different media.

\- Experience with a medium can change users' perceptions of it: The two time-based perspectives—TIP and SIP—discussed above offer a dynamic view of group work. They suggest that behaviors can change as a function of experience and recognize that time, interaction, and performance are inextricably intertwined with characteristics of the task, media, and social context.

Based on the two viewpoints articulated above, two sets of hypotheses are presented below for each of the three dependent variables related to perceptions: social presence, communication interface, and communication effectiveness. Both sets of hypotheses taken together suggest that group perceptions are expected to change over time. The first set of hypotheses, based on the media characteristics perspective, refer to expectations in the first session that perceptions will differ among groups using different media (H1, H3, and H5). In other words, initially, "richer" media will be perceived as being warmer, more effective, and having a better interface than leaner media. The second set of hypotheses, based on the time-based interaction perspective, refer to expectations in the last session that the initial differences in perceptions will not be significant (H2, H4, and H6). Thus, over time, the differences in perceptions will cease to exist. Finally, the section concludes with our hypotheses about group performance (H7).

## Social Presence

First Group Session: Media-characteristics theories suggest that rich media convey more social presence than lean media. They also suggest that a medium's richness depends on (1) its cue-carrying capacity, i.e., bandwidth, and (2) its potential for temporal dislocation, i.e., immediacy of feedback. It follows then that face-to-face meetings, given their greater bandwidth, are likely to have greater social presence than synchronous meetings. Moreover, due to its immediacy of feedback, synchronous interaction is expected to exhibit more social presence than asynchronous interaction.

Specifically we expect that,

In the first group session,

H1: Perceptions of social presence will differ among groups using different media.

H1a: Perceptions of social presence will be higher for face-to-face groups than synchronous groups.

H1b: Perceptions of social presence will be higher for synchronous groups than asynchronous groups.

Last Group Session: In line with the time-based interaction views, Carlson and Zmud (1994) suggest that as users gain experience with the medium, messages, and fellow participants, their perceptions will evolve. In particular, over time, users will likely experience an expansion in channel functionality. While characteristics of a medium (for instance, the amount of anonymity it permits or the speed with which it delivers a message) limit the extent to which users can adapt it, repeated use may provide opportunities to expand the functionality of a medium (Carlson and Zmud 1994). Typically, those in less flexible environments will try the hardest and experiment the most to expand their channel of communication. In other words, the rate of change in channel expansion—and the consequent increase in social presence—will be highest among the most constrained groups (i.e., asynchronous) and lowest among the least constrained groups (i.e., face-to-face) with the intermediate groups (i.e., synchronous) in between. Thus, while face-to-face groups, who presumably will experience the highest social presence to begin with, are likely to maintain their status quo, distributed groups, facing higher barriers, will strive harder to overcome them. Given enough time and ingenuity, distributed interaction may even rival face-to-face interaction in social presence. Thus we expect,

In the last group session,

H2: Perceptions of social presence will not differ among groups using different media.

H2a: Perceptions of social presence will not differ between face-to-face and synchronous groups.

H2b: Perceptions of social presence will not differ between synchronous and asynchronous groups.

## Communication Interface

First Group Session: Simplicity, accessibility, restrictiveness, and ease of interaction afforded by the medium characterize the communication interface (Chidambaram and Jones 1993). Richer media are generally characterized by intuitive interfaces: for instance, for most people, talking would be considered more natural and easier than typing. Additionally, media richness theory suggests that ambiguous value-laden activities are best performed with rich media (Daft and Lengel 1986). Thus, face-to-face groups, especially those working on complex, ambiguous tasks, are likely to rate their communication interface more favorably than distributed groups. Asynchronous groups, lacking immediate feedback, may find their medium more constraining than their synchronous counterparts. As a result, members of these groups are likely to appear less accessible to each other than synchronous or face-to-face members. Consequently, at least initially, we expect asynchronous groups to find their interface more restrictive than either face-to-face or synchronous groups.

## In the first group session,

H3: Perceptions of the communication interface will differ among groups using different media.

H3a: Perceptions of the communication interface will be more favorable for face-to-face groups than for synchronous groups.

H3b: Perceptions of the communication interface will be more favorable for synchronous groups than for asynchronous groups.

Last Group Session: Although the simplicity and ease of interaction afforded by face-to-face interaction may be superior to the more constrained distributed interaction, over time, as distributed groups become increasingly familiar with the medium, such distinctions are likely to attenuate (Carlson and Zmud 1994). Thus, what at first glance may have appeared as constraints, may, with increased experience, become less pronounced and ultimately may even be desired by users because they can now accomplish something they could not in earlier, more familiar and richer environments (Walther 1996). For instance, in distributed environments, responses need not be instant. This apparent constraint can, with practice, offer some advantages: the respondent's current train of thought need not be broken; the time taken to type a message can be used to think about responses in more depth (than in face-to-face settings); responses that need to be "delicately" phrased can be edited until the sender is satisfied; and responses can be prioritized in terms of different criteria. Empirical evidence in other contexts (e.g., Kantorowitz and Sudarsky 1989) also suggests that experienced users may eschew rich interfaces (preferred by novices) in favor of more "restricted" ones precisely because they can accomplish something they could not with the richer ones. In short, we believe experience will alter perceptions about a medium's interface.

## In the last group session,

H4: Perceptions of the communication interface will not differ among groups using different media.

H4a: Perceptions of the communication interface will not differ between face-to-face and synchronous groups.

H4b: Perceptions of the communication interface will not differ between synchronous and asynchronous groups.

## Communication Effectiveness

First Group Session: As discussed earlier, for complex and equivocal tasks (such as the one used in this study), media richness theory suggests that communication will be most effective with a rich medium. Because face-to-face environments are considered richer than distributed ones, they will be perceived as being more effective. Besides, early in the process, face-to-face groups may have an advantage in accomplishing tasks such as coordination and resource allocation. Further, temporal dislocation (i.e., amount of time separating messages) characterizes a medium's degree of interactivity and reciprocity (referred to in this study as "immediacy of feedback"). Increasing temporal dislocation reduces immediacy of feedback (Fisher and Ellis 1990) and may reduce perceptions of effectiveness.

## In the first group session,

H5: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will differ among groups using different media.

H5a: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will be higher for face-to-face groups than synchronous groups.

H5b: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will be higher for synchronous groups than asynchronous groups.

Last Group Session: As in the earlier instances, as time passes and distributed groups come to understand the structure of their environment, they may begin to develop ways to utilize the very leanness of the medium (Walther 1996). Some studies (e.g., Walther 1992) suggest that, over time, distributed groups will adjust and perhaps even thrive with their communication media. In so doing, they will find ways to employ the media to meet both task and relational needs (Walther and Tidwell 1995). Thus, we expect the most “disadvantaged” groups, i.e., the asynchronous ones, to report the greatest gain and the least disadvantaged, i.e., the face-to-face groups, to report the least gain (with the synchronous groups being in between). Over time, we believe that the perceptions of communication effectiveness will be similar across treatments.

## In the last group session,

H6: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will not differ among groups using different media.

H6a: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will not differ between face-to-face and synchronous groups.

H6b: Perceptions of communication effectiveness will not differ between synchronous and asynchronous groups.

## Group Performance

While the focus of group perceptions was on change over time, the focus of group performance was on the final quality of a group's finished document. (Thus, while perceptions of groups in the leaner conditions were expected to improve over time, such changes may not be reflected in cumulative measures of performance.) Characteristics-dependent theories claim that rich media are more conducive to equivocal tasks (Daft and Lengel 1986) because they enable the effective exchange of verbal and non-verbal signals, which assist in better interpretation of potentially ambiguous messages. Given that the task used in this study was a complex and ambiguous one, we expected face-to-face groups to outperform their distributed counterparts.

Performance, as measured in this study, was the result of the cumulative effort that groups expended over four sessions. Thus, the initial advantage that face-to-face groups were expected to have over their distributed counterparts (in resolving ambiguity) and the slow rate at which this advantage was likely to dissipate (i.e., over the four sessions) could result in better overall performance for face-to-face groups. Moreover, the reduced immediacy of feedback in asynchronous interaction will further hamper ambiguity resolution, particularly in the early stages, thereby impacting the final performance of groups in that environment. Thus we propose,

H7: Performance will differ among groups using different media.

H7a: Face-to-face groups will perform better than synchronous groups.

H7b: Synchronous groups will perform better than asynchronous groups.

## Research Design and Methods

The study employed a repeated-measures design, capturing data from each of four group meetings over a four-week period. A single factor, communication support, with three levels—face-to-face, distributed-synchronous, and distributed-asynchronous—was studied. All modes had the same group support system that included communication and task support. Following an hour-long training session, participants were randomly assigned to groups, and groups were randomly assigned to meeting environments. All groups met for a total of four hours, i.e., one hour during each of four once-a-week sessions. The choice of five weeks (one for training, four for sessions) was to a large extent determined by previous studies (e.g., Chidambaram 1996; Miranda 1990) and the need to sustain motivation and encourage participation among student subjects in the middle of a semester. Subject motivation was provided by offering a combination of course credit and monetary prizes.

## Subjects

The study used 33 groups of junior and senior management students; most groups (85%) had four members each with a total of 127 participants. The entire study, including training, comprised a total of 635 subject hours. Each of the three treatments had 11 groups comprised of randomly-assigned members who stayed in the same group through the duration of the study. Monetary prizes were awarded to the top six teams—two in each treatment—based on their output as evaluated by a panel of independent judges.

## Task

This study used a complex project development task that required groups to coordinate sub-tasks for the project, then generate, assess, select, and develop ideas, and finally integrate the ideas into a policy manual. According to McGrath (1991), such group projects are “complex sets of activities in the service of a goal or goals” (p. 151). The task was developed initially by the two authors, tested in two pilot studies and evaluated by human resources professionals. The external review process ensured that the task was realistic and complex and the pilot studies confirmed that it involved many facets of group work and went beyond decision making.

Campbell (1988) suggests that task complexity can arise from four sources: the presence of multiple paths to a desired end-state, the presence of multiple desired end-states, the presence of conflicting interdependence, and the presence of uncertainty. In all these dimensions, the task chosen for this study scored highly. For instance, there were different approaches to developing the policy manual, many conflicting objectives in developing it (e.g., ethical behavior versus the profit motive), and uncertainty in the environment (given the international aspect of the case). In particular, this task required groups to engage in a number of different activities including goal specification, coordination, planning, consensus development, and writing. These characteristics make the task employed in the current study different from earlier decision-specific tasks (e.g., Miranda 1990) and general discussions (e.g., Sproull and Kiesler 1986), yet representative of a variety of organizational projects.

All participants received background information pertaining to an international consulting organization of which they were to consider themselves members (see Appendices A and B for details of the task). Each group was to develop a policy manual, the purpose of which was to introduce newly hired managers to the organizational culture and to the standards of practice employed throughout the organization. Prior to the beginning of the study, the experimenters set up a blank document that was structured into six separate sections, one for each of the specific deliverables listed in the task.

The subjects were provided a set of guidelines regarding deliverables at the end of each session. They were required to organize themselves and generate a list of issues to be included in the document by the end of the first session, have a basic outline of the document by the end of the second session, begin fleshing it out during the third, and have it completed by the final session. While each session was focused on different aspects of the main task, the primary goal was to develop a policy manual. Thus, the four sessions had a common thread that linked them together. The final output, i.e., the policy manual, was the cumulative result of the work in all four sessions.

Subjects were informed that the final product would be evaluated against a specific set of criteria (see the evaluation form in Appendix C). These criteria along with hard copies of all instructions about logging in, the system, session protocols, and directions for communicating were made available to each participant during all sessions.

## Treatments

Face-to-Face: Members of face-to-face groups met at the same time in the Electronic Meeting Room of a large university. The four group members were arranged so that each was in full view of the others. Group members were instructed that they:

1. were to produce the document using the system

2. could make any comments using the application's annotation feature or communication section of the document (described below), and

3. could talk freely amongst themselves.

Distributed-Synchronous: Groups in the distributed-synchronous condition met at the same time but in four different rooms containing networked PCs, located throughout the university's College of Business Administration building. Groups began their session after all members were settled in their assigned offices. An experimenter ensured that there was no face-to-face interaction among members during meeting times. The distributed synchronous groups were required to communicate via the first two features of the system described above; they had no other communication channels available. Other than location and communication mode, procedures were identical for synchronous and face-to-face groups.

Distributed-Asynchronous: Asynchronous group members were not allowed to meet simultaneously; instead, they signed up in advance for different session times. Each team member signed on at the same time and day during each of the four weeks, but no member of the same team was permitted to sign on simultaneously. During a session, each team member logged onto the system, accessed their group's document, and worked on the project for an hour. All other procedures, i.e., using annotations and communicating electronically, were the same as described for the synchronous groups.

Groups in all three conditions (described above) developed their document using GroupWriter™, a co-authoring tool of a commonly available group support system. Two features of the group writing tool were used for communication. The first was an "annotation" feature that permitted users to embed comments in sections of the document without disrupting its flow. Thus, a comment related directly to a portion of the text was entered as an annotation at the relevant point. An annotation was visible as a number (representing the sequential number of the annotation) enclosed in double chevrons (e.g., $<<1>>$ ) at the point in the text where the author of the comment entered it. Participants were instructed that, when they saw such markings, they could "open" and read the entire annotation. The second method of communication was via a section of the document that was restricted to the posting of messages and was labeled the "Communication Section." General comments were to be placed in this section of the document.

Participants were also instructed to avoid discussing the exercise outside of the scheduled session times. The fact that subjects came from different classes decreased the likelihood of unmonitored external interaction. Session monitoring indicated no evidence of subjects bringing in any previously done work, such as computer disks or written notes. Additionally, exit interviews did not indicate that task-related interaction had occurred outside the sessions. Finally, an experimenter was present during all sessions for technical advice and to preserve procedural integrity (e.g., preventing dispersed participants from interacting face-to-face, etc.).

## Training

Participants were trained to log in, enter the document, and perform simple text editing functions including typing, cutting, pasting, copying, and deleting. They were also taught how to create and read annotations and to use the communication section for posting comments. A script was followed for all training sessions to ensure comparability across sessions; each session lasted one hour. A post hoc survey revealed that participants had not had any formal training in collaborative writing prior to this study.

## Measures of Perceptions

The instrument used in this study (see Appendix D) to measure perceptions of social presence, communication interface, and communication effectiveness is an adaptation of the scale used by Short et al. (1976). The adapted version of this scale has been validated in other studies (e.g., Chidambaram and Jones 1993). Social presence reflects the capacity of the medium to convey expressiveness and emotional content; items included participants' evaluations of the extent to which the medium allowed them to be personal, expressive and emotional. Reliability (Cronbach's alpha) of the seven-item, semantic differential scale (1 = low; 7 = high) measuring social presence in this study was 0.86. Communication interface measured perceptions about the medium's interface; it included items related to a medium's ease of use and simplicity. This construct was measured using a seven-point scale that consisted of four items; the scale had a reliability of 0.80. Communication effectiveness is an evaluation of a medium's performance attributes and included items related to its ability to permit quick, accurate, and meaningful exchanges. This construct was measured by an eight-item, seven-point scale with a reliability of 0.90.

## Performance Evaluation

A panel of 11 judges comprised of instructors of business or communication evaluated the final documents. Documents from each of the three treatments, coded to ensure anonymity of groups and treatments, were randomly assigned to judges. Due to the difficulty of judging 33 lengthy documents, each judge was assigned a subset (nine) of the documents to evaluate. Since not all documents were evaluated by all judges, inter-rater reliability was derived using Ebel's (1951) interclass reliability estimate for partial data sets. The reliability level of 0.61 was deemed to be within normally acceptable ranges for such data (Ebel 1951; Horst 1949). Also, to make the evaluations comparable over such a large number of lengthy documents, each document had at least three grades from as many judges. Thus, every judge evaluated nine documents, three from each treatment; consequently, three different judges evaluated each document.

The final score for each document was comprised of the mean of these three scores.

The judges were given an evaluation template to use (see Appendix C). Each section in a document was evaluated using a five-point scale, where 1 was the lowest and 5 was the highest, on four criteria: creativity, practicality, comprehensiveness, and positive tone. These criteria were developed based on the results of two pilot studies and discussions with practitioners. For instance, professionals in the human resources field indicated the importance of a positive tone in training manuals; based on their feedback, this criteria was included in the evaluation list. All four criteria were equally weighted and, as a validity check, judges also evaluated the document as a whole.

## Results

In general, for the hypotheses about the first session (H1, H3, and H5), we expected significant differences in perceptions across the three treatments. Conversely, for the hypotheses about the final session (H2, H4, and H6), we expected that the initial differences in perceptions would disappear, i.e., there would be no differences in the last session. Thus, while the hypotheses appear to be stated in the null form for the last session, in reality they represent the alternate hypotheses. Finally, we expected performance (H7) of all three groups to differ significantly. The hypotheses about the first session were tested using analysis of variance (ANOVA) along with Scheffe's post hoc contrast tests where warranted. The hypotheses about the final session were tested using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with first session scores as covariates; again, Scheffe's tests were used where warranted. Results of these analyses are presented below.

## Perceptions

Social Presence: Table 1 displays means (1 = low; 7 = high) and standard deviations for the first and last sessions along with the ANOVA and ANCOVA results. The primary hypothesis, H1, was supported (F = 66.11, p < .01, power = .97), i.e., initially, social presence differed by treatment. Results of post hoc tests show that during the first session, face-to-face groups reported greater levels of social presence than synchronous groups, supporting hypothesis H1a. However, there were no significant differences in social presence between synchronous and asynchronous groups, leading us to reject hypothesis H1b. Contrary to our expectations, hypothesis H2, which predicted no differences across groups in the last session, was not supported. The ANCOVA results confirmed that perceptions of social presence differed significantly among groups in the last session (F = 16.57, p < .01, power = .94). Follow-up analysis indicated that face-to-face groups continued to experience more social presence than their distributed counterparts (who did not differ significantly between themselves).

Communication Interface: Table 2 displays the means and statistical results for the first and last sessions. The primary hypothesis, H3, predicting differences across treatments was supported (F = 4.77, p < .01, power = .75). As expected, during the first session, face-to-face groups rated their communication interface significantly better than synchronous groups, supporting hypothesis H3a. However, because synchronous groups did not differ significantly from asynchronous groups during the first session, H3b was not supported. ANCOVA results for the last session (F = 2.39, p = .09, power = .54) support hypothesis H4 and suggest that groups did not differ significantly in their perceptions of communication effectiveness. However, given the relatively low power of the test, this result must be interpreted with caution. In other words, some of the initial differences may have lingered even in the last session, but we were unable to detect them.

Communication Effectiveness: Table 3 presents results about communication effectiveness during the first and last sessions. The primary hypothesis, H5, was supported (F = 10.79, p < .01, power = .98), indicating that there were significant differences among treatments in their initial evaluations of communication effectiveness. Consistent with our predictions, communication effectiveness was significantly greater for face-to-face groups than for synchronous groups during the first session, thus supporting hypothesis H5a. On the other hand, there were no significant differences between synchronous and asynchronous groups; thus, hypothesis H5b was not supported. During the last session, as predicted, there were no significant differences in perceptions of communication effectiveness between face-to-face and synchronous groups (supporting H6a) and between synchronous and asynchronous groups (supporting H6b). However, face-to-face groups continued to perceive their medium as being more effective than asynchronous groups even during the last session (explaining the overall significant effect, F = 3.36, p = .03, power = .70).

## Face-to-Face Versus Synchronous Groups

Perceptions: The results of our study indicate that a medium's ability to carry a variety of cues affects a group's ability to sense the presence of other members, i.e., high bandwidth facilitates high social presence. Thus, media that can carry a variety of cues, such as face-to-face interactions, will rate high in social presence, while media that can only carry a limited set of cues, such as computer-supported synchronous interactions, will rate low in social presence. Both of these results were seen in this study and are in line with bandwidth-dependent media theories. Moreover, these perceptions appear to be relatively invariant. Since repeated interactions with the medium or ongoing exchanges of information among interactants do not affect the cue-carrying capacity of a medium, perceptions of social presence did not change over time. As with other social opinions, we found initial impressions of social presence to be lasting.

In contrast to the above result, and in partial support of time-based theories, one perception—that of communication effectiveness—showed some signs of change over time. Perceptions of communication effectiveness were significantly better for face-to-face groups than synchronous ones during the first session; however, by the last session, these differences became non-significant. As Walther and Tidwell (1995) argue, computer-mediated communication can support a range of relational interactions resulting in a variety of perceptions, each of which can become more or less pronounced over time. Some aspects of communication, for instance attitudes toward the medium or the task, are more a function of the accumulation of information exchanges over time than of the amount exchanged at any one point in time. In other words, perceptions of a medium's effectiveness are likely to be determined by an ongoing series of information exchanges. Thus, as time passes and more information is accumulated, these perceptions may change, as was observed in this study.

Performance: While our results support some aspects of bandwidth-dependent theories, they also challenge other aspects, such as the notion that more bandwidth is better for equivocal tasks. Media richness theory suggests that richer media are better suited for tasks requiring equivocality resolution. However, the results of this study, in which groups working on an ambiguous task and using a leaner medium performed better than groups using a richer medium, question such an assumption. In other words, despite working on an equivocal task, groups in the richer face-to-face setting did not perform as well as their counterparts in the leaner synchronous setting. This result, combined with the consistently higher social presence of face-to-face groups, suggests that the increased bandwidth may have contributed to better perceptions (in line with bandwidth theories) but detracted from better performance, even with the equivocal task (in contrast to bandwidth theories).

One implication of these results is that, in some instances, even for equivocal tasks, less bandwidth may actually be more desirable. Even though the task used in this study was relatively complex and included value-laden issues, synchronous group members benefited from the reduced bandwidth of the leaner medium. Walther (1996) suggests that the ability to focus interaction on very specific issues in very particular ways—a feature of some lean media—enables hypercommunication. Hypercommunication enables users of lean media to focus precisely on the task (or message) and to be extremely selective about what information is presented and how it is presented. We found evidence of such communication among synchronous groups as the extract below exemplifies:

S3-120: "Good morning! This is Ruth. I deleted the announcements sections. I was experimenting the procedure [sic]. F2 to block the section and hit delete. How is everyone doing? Had a good weekend and two full days of school and work?"

S3-122: "Hey Ruth, This is Pam. My weekend was certainly eventful. Are you guys going to the big game on Saturday. I hope we don't get our ### kicked!!! Anyway I guess we should start actually writing and combining ideas. If any new ideas come to mind then just add them in. Next week maybe we should dedicate our time to editing, final revisions, and the conclusion. What does everybody think?"

In the above dialog, notice how Pam was able to reverse the task-to-relational sequence of comments used by Ruth that could have generated a non-task discussion. This sort of manipulation—referred to earlier as hypercommunication—may be difficult in face-to-face interaction where the need to be socially engaging, and consequently less task-focused, is greater. We also observed instances of such hypercommunication among other synchronous groups:

S3-254: "Hi, sorry to interrupt, especially when I do not have a joke to tell... we are deviating from our mission here today..." [followed by a list of task-related issues]

The “pointedly task focused” perspective of the synchronous groups is corroborated by other phenomena which were not observed in face-to-face groups. For instance, synchronous groups entered numerous annotations (three groups had 35, 26, and 18 annotations respectively in their documents), all of which were task oriented. One group actually crafted initial drafts of the policy document in dialog form. In contrast, the interactions of face-to-face groups were less task-focused, being frequently punctuated by tension release (joking, laughing, etc.), intermittent non-task discourse (where one person would talk and the rest of the group would stop typing and either listen or join in), and “switching” (from verbal to electronic chatter). While some of these activities are necessary for team building and increased satisfaction, they can and possibly did reduce focus from the task at hand (as seen in the performance results).

To summarize, high bandwidth, presumed to be important by media characteristics theories for resolving ambiguity, may actually detract from this task (while simultaneously fostering feelings of kinship and closeness). In other words, a lean medium—precisely because it is lean—may offer an advantage in resolving ambiguity that is not offered by a rich medium: hypercommunication, i.e., the ability to focus on a message (or task) in a very specific way that excludes other, possibly distracting, avenues (Walther 1996). Prolonged hypercommunication may enhance perceptions of the medium's effectiveness (given the heightened task-focused interaction) and ultimately even result in significant performance gains (even though it may never change the users' initial views about the medium's social presence).

## Synchronous versus Asynchronous Groups

Perceptions: We expected that temporal dislocation would cause perceptions to be poorer for asynchronous groups than for synchronous ones; however, the results did not support this contention. Delays in communication were inevitable as asynchronous participants came "online" one at a time. Thus, in the first session, only the last member of a team to participate had access to input from all other members. It was a week before the first participant had access to all that had gone on during the first session. Despite this delayed pattern of communication, perceptions of asynchronous groups were similar to that of their synchronous counterparts. Moreover, the lack of differences between the groups' perceptions persisted through the last session and is consistent with Gersick's (1989) observation that some perceptions can linger in states of relative equilibrium.

Qualitative analysis of transcripts and system logs revealed that groups in both distributed conditions (i.e., synchronous and asynchronous) had, despite structural differences, somewhat similar types of interactions (which may help explain the lack of differences in their perceptions). For instance, both groups used a medium that had the same “narrow” bandwidth that prevented them from exchanging verbal or visual cues and required them to communicate only in text form. Below are two examples of interactions within asynchronous groups (A1-250) and synchronous groups (S2-300):

A1-250: "We are running out of time? we need to combine ideas? it should look like a real document." [followed by an outline]

S2-300: "I agree with all of the above statements. But there are a lot more policies we should add." [followed by ideas for policies]

Both exchanges point to the explicitness in communication that reliance on text forced on the distributed conditions. Repeatedly, group members in both conditions articulated their views and ideas in written form—the only medium available at their disposal.

Another note of similarity between both distributed conditions was the phenomenon we term "feedback anxiety." In the absence of any visual or verbal cues about what others thought of their ideas or input, members of synchronous and asynchronous groups frequently solicited the opinions of their teammates. (Incidentally, the term "feedback" itself appeared numerous times in the transcripts of exchanges among the distributed groups in both conditions.) For example, the following exchanges are excerpted from a synchronous group member (S3-254) and an asynchronous group member (A3-300):

S3-254: "... a few serious things to tell and I need your opinions..."

A3-300: "Gee, some of the stuff I put down was pretty off the wall. PLEASE PLEASE do some editing"

Given the similarity of processes—reliance on explicitly written communication and the frequent solicitation of feedback—between the synchronous and asynchronous groups, the lack of differences in perceptions between the two groups is not surprising. What is surprising, however, is that the delayed feedback inherent in asynchronous interaction appeared to have played no discernable role in lowering the perceptions of the medium (relative to synchronous interaction) as predicted by media-characteristics theories. Hence, we see that bandwidth-based perspectives predict well in the richest context (such as in face-to-face interaction), but are somewhat less capable of predicting perceptions in leaner environments.

Performance: Group performance did not differ significantly between synchronous and asynchronous groups. In other words, the delayed feedback of asynchronous groups had little negative effect on their performance. As in the case of perceptions, qualitative analysis of transcripts revealed that synchronous and asynchronous groups exhibited some similarities in the way they accomplished the task. For instance, groups in both distributed conditions were very task-focused. Additionally, they both had to contend with essentially the same bandwidth. Hence, they had to rely on the written rather than the spoken word. Thus, all communication and content (of the documents) were recorded in a permanent and retrievable form. In a number of groups, in both synchronous and asynchronous environments, members frequently referred to and summarized earlier passages, sometimes presenting bullet-point lists of the discussion issues.

The permanent record of their discussions permitted members to periodically review stored information, summarize issues, and integrate ideas into later discussions. (Incidentally, such was not the case with face-to-face groups, where a lot of the integration was verbal, and thus ephemeral.) The archived records, which provided groups in both conditions similar communication mechanisms, may help explain the distributed groups' motivation (by being physically in front of them) and the means (by its content) for remaining pointedly task focused.

To better understand why delayed feedback had no impact on the outcomes of asynchronous groups, we examined the communication excerpts and session logs of these groups. Asynchronous groups appeared to have used one of two approaches to dealing with the inherent delays of their medium. In about half the groups, the first member to log in took the lead. Despite feelings of isolation, the "lead" member of such groups kicked off the process by generating ideas and creating a work context for the remaining members to follow. For instance, the first person to start off in an asynchronous group made the following remarks:

A1-154: "What's new! It's too bad that I'm all alone and all by myself in the matter of trying to do this document. ? I know I will do a fine job of doing this orientation packet." [followed by an outline of the document]

In the remaining groups, the process was more democratic, but rather tentative. For example, a member of one asynchronous group suggested the "need to combine and organize ideas." The next member to contribute agreed and suggested further that they needed to "come to agreement and produce an outline." The next member to work on the task wrote that "all of the ideas are good but we need to be putting them together."

This series of exchanges provides glimpses of what we found in some other asynchronous groups: a simultaneous urge to lead and follow. In other words, members felt the need to advise yet involve the others in the decision to move forward. Members of these groups appeared uncomfortable with the idea of proceeding—or at least proceeding too far—without the involvement of others (perhaps out of fear that they may have to backtrack or alter course if their ideas were not well received).

Regardless of which approach asynchronous groups used, they were able to effectively deal with the structural constraint of their medium: delayed feedback. In effect, both distributed conditions, one with immediate feedback and the other with delayed feedback, had more in common than media-characteristics theories suggest (so much so that groups in these conditions did not differ in either perceptions or performance).

## Conclusions

In this study, we found, contrary to bandwidth-dependent theories, that an equivocal task could be better performed with synchronous computer-mediated communication than with face-to-face computer-supported communication. While users may never consider such a lean medium as being "warm," they may, with repeated interaction, come to think of it as being as effective as its richer counterpart. Such a distributed medium—precisely because it permits less social interaction (which may sometimes be distracting)—allows users to focus on the task, thereby helping them reduce ambiguity more effectively than face-to-face interaction. While decreasing social interaction may not always be desirable, the appropriate mix of task versus social interaction is likely to depend on a number of factors including task type, organizational setting, and group characteristics. Additionally, we found that distributed groups, whether synchronous or asynchronous, did not differ in their perceptions or performance. Thus, the need for speedy feedback may be more a function of the task than a universal requirement for improving outcomes.

## Limitations

Of course, generalizing our results beyond this specific task with its corresponding time constraints must be done with caution. Although the task was a simulated case-study, it was complex, value-laden, and ambiguous (as confirmed by external reviewers). The reader may recall that the task involved developing a policy document in stages. While such an approach emphasizes different subtasks in each session, it does provide a common thread linking all sessions. The alternative of using different tasks in each of the sessions introduces a different set of artifacts. Also, the alternative of using the same task in each session may be appropriate in the context of recurring decisions (such as setting production quotas or making sales forecasts), but may not apply in the case of cumulative group outcomes (such as delivering a policy manual). Regardless of the approach taken, task type will affect outcomes.

Longitudinal studies such as this provide glimpses of behavior over time, but suffer from their inherent inability to continually monitor interactions (i.e., what happens outside the research framework). Although experimenter observations and exit interviews indicated there was little interaction among subjects outside the experimental sessions, some may have occurred. Also, the time constraints applied here, although stringent, may well be mirrored in real organizations where people are often committed to a number of different projects simultaneously and tight schedules force them to devote a slice of their time for each task.

Constructs are only as good as the methods and measures used to evaluate them. Although previously validated measures were used in this study, the instrument used to measure communication interface did not specifically include an item for temporal dispersion. However, it did include related items, such as the “constrainedness” and “restrictiveness” of media, that may subsume the issue of accessibility. Finally, we recognize that evolution of group perceptions may be a long-term process that may require months or even years to fully comprehend. Nevertheless, this study offers some insights to such changes over time.

## Future Research

This study raises three sets of questions that need further investigation. First, future research needs to identify other contextual variables—besides bandwidth and feedback—that mediate the media use-effects relationship. Second, the role of time should be examined in greater depth. For instance, will allowing additional time for asynchronous participants help offset their coordination difficulties (as Walther [1992] implies)? Should asynchronous participants be allowed to "log in" at any time (as is likely in organizational settings)? And finally, the dichotomy of perceptions—partly supportive of evolutionary theories and partly supportive of media characteristics theories—raises an important issue for future research: identification of the factors and circumstances that determine why some perceptions are static and others are dynamic. Thus, this study, while improving our understanding of distributed interaction, simultaneously highlights the need to further investigate the relationships among tasks, technologies, and teams over time.

## APPENDIX A

## Task Background: Global Associates, Inc. (GAI)

Global Associates, Inc. is a multinational consulting firm that specializes in helping large U.S. firms establish their first international operation. Their consultants are experts in a variety of multinational business areas such as marketing, finance, and international strategy. Global Associates, however, is unique in the area of international consulting because of their expertise in cultural awareness training. A large number of Global Associates' clients are from the U.S. In the course of operations, however, GAI has also established and maintained commercial and political connections in many Pacific Rim countries.

Consultants work in teams and all have considerable management education and experience. Teams are formed such that each member, in addition to their general background, also brings some specialized functional skills to the team. For example, if the team manager determines that a critical concern for the client will be the establishment of a marketing strategy appropriate for a specific market, then a consultant with the necessary marketing knowledge is assigned to that team. Similarly, if a situation exists requiring sophisticated financial expertise in a foreign country, the team manager will acquire a financial expert for the consulting team.

Cultural sensitivity: Mr. Robertson, the President and CEO, firmly believes that GAI's competitive advantage is a result of its practice of hiring only those consultants who demonstrate cultural sensitivity. All consultants are required to take a series of cultural awareness courses and follow up with periodic refreshers. Mr. Robertson feels that the failure of many U.S. companies in countries such as Hong Kong, Japan, and Taiwan is primarily due to their failure to understand the customs and cultural practices of the countries in which they operate. To provide the necessary cultural expertise for all of their clients, GAI trains its consultants in various international customs and cultures.

Integrity: One of the major problems that foreign investment consulting firms face is a negative public image. Several cases have been publicized recently suggesting that the entire industry engages in unethical business practices. These alleged practices range from fraud to the use of payoffs to governments in foreign countries to get a “foot in the door” for their client. In countries where it is traditionally “accepted” to do business in such a way, GAI seeks ethical solutions to the logistical and political problems which confront client firms. GAI has discovered that a better understanding of local cultural practices usually provides legal and ethical alternatives to improper practices.

Fairness: A central belief at Global Associates is that every customer has the right to be treated with respect and be given the service they want at a fair and reasonable price. GAI senior management will not compromise on this essential value. If a client does not feel they were treated fairly, Global Associates will do WHATEVER it takes to set things right with that client. Consequently, Global Associates enjoys an excellent reputation and is an industry leader in revenues.

The Problem: Because of the growing number of clients, Global Associates has stepped up hiring and training of consultants. More team managers must be hired to supervise the growing staff. Team managers must have excellent educational credentials and work references before they are considered for hire. In order to obtain managers with the necessary experience, GAI is forced to hire candidates from other firms whose objectives, standards, and overall corporate cultures are frequently very different from those at Global Associates.
