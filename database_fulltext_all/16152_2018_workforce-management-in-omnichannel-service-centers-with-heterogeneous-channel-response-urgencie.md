## a b s t r a c t

Workforce staffing and assignment decisions are of critical importance for meeting the challenge of minimizing operational costs while providing satisfactory customer service. These decisions are particularly challenging for omnichannel service centers, where customers can request services via different communication channels (e.g., phone, e-mail, live-chat, social media) that have different service quality and response requirements. We present a formulation of the omnichannel workforce management problem that accounts for variations in response urgencies of different channels as well as diminishing agent performances due to channel switching. We develop an algorithm that efficiently provides solutions for this problem and determines the number and channel allocation of service agents within the service center. Through numerical experiments, we study the performance of the algorithm among various service center configurations with equal cost characteristics. The results indicate that the proposed algorithm can identify service center structures that outperform many alternative structures, including those commonly-adopted in the real-world.

## 1. Introduction

Organizations all around the globe are increasingly relying on new digital communication mediums (e.g., social media, live-chat, e-mail) to connect with their customers. Widespread adoption of these technologies has lowered the communication barriers between customers and organizations, leading to a surge in the number of customer inquiries that organizations need to resolve every day [1]. Today's tech savvy customers do not only make phone calls, but also “live-chat”, “e-mail”, “tweet”, “text”, “Facebook post”, “use online forums”, etc. to request services and to convey their messages.

Unsurprisingly, many organizations are moving away from a traditional (i.e., phone based) call center model to omnichannel service centers. Omnichannel service centers provide greater customer loyalty and higher satisfaction by providing services through multiple digital mediums at customers' convenience [2]. Forrester Research [3] reports significant growth (15% - 20%) in the use of digital channels such as live-chat and online forums among service center customers between 2012 and 2014. According to Cisco [4], omnichannel service is one of the top three trends that emerged in customer relationship management. Newer service center platforms such as SAP's Hybris Service Engagement Center<sup>1</sup> allow firms to provide ticketing and support services through multiple channels, while enabling their agents to move across these channels seamlessly.

For all their potential benefits in improving customer relations, omnichannel service centers bring additional operational challenges for firms. Industry surveys indicate that one of the major challenges in omnichannel service center management is agent workforce management [5]. Allocating and assigning service agents across multiple channels with different skill requirements (e.g., talking over the phone, textbased messaging, social media monitoring) can be a complex task, especially when the service demands coming through different channels vary. Ideally, a firm could prefer to keep their best agents available on channels where they feel most comfortable. However, a high degree of specialization may lead to excessive hiring costs as well as inefficiency due to idle work times. The other end of this spectrum would be to allow service agents to dynamically move across all available channels. Undoubtedly, this approach also comes with downsides, including excessive training costs, diminishing agent performance due to cognitive limitations [6,7] and loss of quality due to lack of gaining specialized skill experience [8]. Workforce management is further complicated by the fact that it may be more preferable for the firm to delay certain service requests for the sake of others (e.g., delaying e-mail responses until the phone channel becomes idle).

Under this situation, identifying ideal service center structures that minimize overall operating costs while ensuring satisfactory customer service across multiple channels becomes an important problem for omnichannel service centers. Our study aims to address this problem.

In particular, we propose a novel method for solving the staff planning problem in an omnichannel service setting, in which different service channels may have different response urgencies. Staff planning is one of three main problems within the workforce management domain [9, 10]. The goal of this problem is to determine the long-term strategic staffing levels (hiring and training) for a planning period (e.g., year), while considering a cost-based objective function and performance-related constraints. Such staffing decisions are usually made several weeks and sometimes months ahead of time due to lead times associated with hiring and training employees [11]. From a managerial standpoint, our method addresses the following three questions regarding staff planning:

1) How many service agents should be employed within an omnichannel service center?

2) To which channel(s) should each service agent be assigned?

The first research question is concerned with determining the total number of agents needed in the service center for the entirety of the planning period whereas the second research question considers identifying the constant channel assignment of each agent. It is important to note that answering these questions requires considering the tradeoff between staffing costs (hiring and training) and penalty costs due to service delays. Our method incorporates this trade-off in the solution, as well as various other omnichannel considerations such as diminishing agent performances due to channel switching and variations in response urgencies of different channels.

There are three main motivating factors that distinguish our study from earlier work in the workforce management literature. First, we specify our problem within the context of omniservice service centers that have several unique routing and staffing considerations such as a hierarchical order among channels and diminishing agent performances. In particular, the first issue (response priority differences among channels) requires us to solve a routing problem at the realization of each time interval, in addition to the master staffing problem. To our knowledge, Wallace and Whitt [12] is the only study that considered solving routing and staffing problems concurrently, however their approach depends on a static priority-routing scheme in addition to having constraints on service agent skill sets. Our approach relaxes both of these assumptions. Second, we capture the trade-off between staffing costs and service quality by explicitly incorporating the quality related costs into the optimization model. A number of previous studies have used external simulation models to estimate service levels, which are used as optimization constraints at each iteration of the solution process. However, this approach is less satisfactory for instances where it may be impractical, or even impossible, to simulate the system. In addition, we believe that representing service quality (i.e., service shortage) as a penalty cost is a natural way of quantifying the outcomes by firm managers and decision makers. Third, our approach aims to simultaneously fulfill two distinct but interconnected goals: determining staff levels and determining staff flexibility. To our knowledge, there is little research that attempted to satisfy these two goals collectively in the omnichannel service center setting. Further, a majority of previous studies in the literature have focused on only one of these issues, while keeping other variables and outcomes constant. Yet, from a management (practical) standpoint, there is no inherent reason to pre-determine or set any values when the single overall goal of the firm is to minimize total operating and service delay costs, as shown in Aksin et al. [13]. We believe that the comprehensiveness of our approach makes it highly practical and applicable to real-world omnichannel service centers.

We evaluate the performance of our approach using numerical experiments that mimic possible different scenarios in an omnichannel service center. To assess the performance of the proposed solution, we develop a discrete-event simulation program and compute the waiting times and overall service levels under a variety of system parameters.

The results indicate that the proposed method can identify service center structures that outperform many competitive structures, including several commonly adopted in the real-world.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the related literature. In Section 3, we present the model formulations and describe our solution algorithm. In Section 4, we run numerical experiments to evaluate the performance of the algorithm. Finally, in Section 5, we conclude the paper with a discussion of the findings, limitations, and potential future work.

## 2. Related literature

There exists a large body of literature that addresses workforce prob lems in service centers. Our study draws from two main research streams within this context. The first stream focuses on determining flexibility levels for employees that can be cross-utilized in different skills. It has been widely known that cross-utilization of employees among departments with varying service demand is a well-recognized policy for coping with service shortages [14,15]. Jordan and Graves [16] was one of the earlier works that showed that certain flexibility configurations can achieve almost all the benefits of full server flexibility. They introduced the concept of chaining (in the context of manufacturing), which is a special structure that connects, directly or indirectly, all demand and all server nodes in the system via flexibility links. Brusco and Johns [17] and Hopp et al. [18] extended the idea of chaining to cross-training of employees in service operations. On the other hand, Gurumurthi and Benjaafar [19] showed that chained configurations may not always be the best solution, especially when asymmetric systems are considered. Under this consideration, Iravani et al. [20,21] developed performance indices to compare and rank pre-defined service flexibility structures. These indices characterize the ability of cross-training structures (such as cross-trained call center employees) to respond effectively to variability in demand. More recently, Aksin et al. [13] compared different flexibility structures including twoskill chain, adjacent-level [22], nested, and overflow from a profit maximization perspective. They particularly focused on the value of flexibility under each structure after optimizing the server capacity levels (as opposed to considering fixed capacity levels). Besides the evaluation of pre-defined cross-training structures, there is also research on identification of good cross-training structures. Notable work in this area was conducted by Chou et al. [23], who adopted the concept of graph expansion to analyze worst-case performances in general demand settings and suggested heuristics based on the results for designing flexible process structures. Another contribution was provided by Andradόttir et al. [24], who used the notion of bottlenecks in heterogeneous systems to identify certain characteristics of desirable flexibility structures. Also relevant to this stream is the work of Bassamboo et al. [22], who inves tigated optimal flexibility configurations within newsvendor network models.

A majority of these existing studies consider staffing levels to be constant for flexibility analysis. On the other hand, there exists a separate research stream in the literature that particularly focuses on determining staffing levels. For example, Cezik and L'Ecuyer [25] proposed an algorithm to minimize the staffing costs of a multiskill service center subject to service-level requirements. A particular strength of their work is differentiating server pools in terms of skill preferences. However, their approach suffered from long computation times due to the need of simulating the system at each iteration of the algorithm, and this issue was explicitly addressed by Pot et al. [26] via a heuristic algorithm that avoided simulations. While these two studies primarily investigated optimal staffing allocation that is constant over a longer term period, a different body of work focused on staffing decisions over shorter term schedules. For example, Harrison and Zeevi [27] developed a novel method for sizing server pools that are only fixed within shorter planning periods, such as multiple shifts within 1 day. Similarly, Atlason et al. [28] suggested a method to find approximately optimal

Of particular interest to our study is the work by Wallace and Whitt [12], which proposed an algorithm for collectively solving both routing and staffing problems in call centers with skill-based routing. Their approach allowed for arbitrary skill assignments with subject to the constraint that each service agent has precisely have two skills.

## 3. Model formulation and solution approach

## 3.1. Definition and notation

Omnichannel centers receive service requests through multiple communication channels. Assume that there are N different channels being operated in a service center. Channels have different response urgencies, meaning that the cost of delaying service requests vary across different channels. The service center employs a total of C agents, with each agent operating on at least one channel and each channel being served by at least one agent. We refer to the channels that a service agent can process as the channel set of this agent. An assignment structure can, then, be represented as a matrix, $A = [ a _ { i j } ]$ of size $C \times N ,$ with $a _ { i j } = 1$ if channel j is included in the channel set of agent i and $a _ { i j } = 0$ else. Each row in this matrix would indicate a channel assignment decision for a particular agent. We assume that each agent has the same fixed hiring cost (i.e., salary) denoted by $c ^ { h }$ and also imposes training costs on the firm based on the agent's channel assignment. We allow training costs to vary from one channel to another and denote the training costs of operating on channel $j \mathsf { a s } c _ { j } ^ { r } .$ Furthermore, we characterize each communication channel by the arrival rates and service times of its customers during a period of time. Mean arrival rates and service times can be measured within periodic time intervals and we assume that there are T equal time intervals. Intervals are defined as time periods over which the arrival and service rates can be considered constant – e.g., 10, 15, or 30 min periods [29]. Let $p _ { j t }$ represent the mean utilization rate for channel j at time period t. $p _ { j t }$ can be computed as $\begin{array} { r } { p _ { j t } { = } \lambda _ { j t } / \mu _ { j } , } \end{array}$ , where $\lambda _ { j t }$ is the mean arrival rate at time period t and $\mu _ { j }$ is the mean service rate of channel j. Le $\mathbf { \partial } _ { [ } \mathbf { x } _ { j } = ( p _ { j 1 } , . . . , p _ { j T } )$ represent the vec tor of mean utilization rates of j during T. Within this framework, the problem is to determine the total number of agents C and the agentchannel assignment matrix A, while minimizing the overall operating costs. The objective of the model is to minimize the total operating cost of the service center, which consists of three individual cost items: 1) agent hiring costs, 2) agent training costs, and 3) penalty costs associated with unserved demand. The constraints in Eqs. (3) and (4) ensure, respectively, that each channel is served by at least one agent and each agent operates on at least one channel.

It should be noted that the unserved demand d (A) depends not only on A but also on the decision of which agents to route (i.e., assign) the demand to, given A. In other terms, finding the $d . The objective function of this problem in Eq. (5) is to minimize the total penalty costs based on unserved demand. Note that solely minimizing the total unserved demand may not always be the optimal solution for this problem, since different channels have different penalty costs associated with them. The constraint in Eq. (6) ensures that total agent demand for a given channel cannot exceed the utilization request and the constraint in Eq. (7) confirms that the total utilization of a given agent during a given time period cannot be greater than productivity of this agent. We define productivity decrease coefficient q to be a monotonically increasing function based on the total number of channels that the agent can serve. The productivity of an agent is maximum – i.e., 1, when he/she is specialized on a single channel. The constraint in Eq. (8) makes sure that the amount of routed demand cannot exceed the service capacity of agents. Finally, the constraint in Eq. (9) ensures that if an agent is not assigned to a channel, then the routed demand from this agent to the channel has to be zero – i.e., agents can only process demands from the channels that they were assigned to.

## 3.4. Solution algorithm

As described earlier, the main problem involves three interdependent cost components that not only need to be optimized simultaneously, but also require implementing a routing model at each iteration of the search process. We offer an evolutionary metaheuristic method based on genetic algorithms (GAs) for solving this problem. GAs are commonly used to search for solutions to complex optimization problems such as ours that cannot be solved via exact integer linear programming procedures [30,31]. Our method integrates linear programming with a novel GA implementation, in which each possible solution in the search space corresponds to an agent-channel assignment matrix, $A = [ a _ { i j } ]$ . A fixed number, M, of possible solutions constitute a generation, G. At each iteration of the algorithm, the members of a particular generation are evaluated based on the objective function – i.e., the value of the total operating costs of the service center, given A. Operating costs consist of three main components: 1) agent hiring costs, 2) agent training costs, and 3) penalty cost associated with unserved demand. The first two components can be directly computed, given A and a set of cost parameters, whereas the last component requires solving the routing model in Section 3.3. At the start of the algorithm, we initialize G with an arbitrarily large C (while satisfying budget requirements) and we successively reduce the size of C at each iteration by trimming agents, who were not assigned to any jobs. In this respect, we relax the constraint in Eq. (4) of the main problem during the solution generation process (i.e., creating new generations), until the solutions are evaluated. We create new generations by keeping the top n solutions from the previous generation and performing cross-overs between the solutions in G until M− n additional solutions are generated. Solutions to cross-over are selected using a roulette wheel procedure and single point cross-over is performed on these solutions with the swapping point being a row vector in A. Before finalizing new generations, we use genetic algorithm mutations to satisfy the other two constraints in the main model, Eqs. (2) and (3).

The algorithm starts the search with an arbitrarily large staffing allocation and proceeds by shrinking the staffing size in the service center, while considering the trade-off between staffing costs and penalty costs due to service delays. ## 4. Numerical experiments

We conduct a set of numerical experiments to evaluate the performance of the proposed method. The evaluation is based on the comparison of queue waiting times among different service center structures, which can differ in terms of the number of agents employed and the channel assignment of each agent. To obtain the performance scores, we develop a discrete-event simulation program of a service center and estimate the average waiting times under a variety of different configurations. Specifically, our experiments simulate an omnichannel service center in four different scenario settings. These scenarios cover a range of different channel response priority configurations as well as varying agent task switching effectiveness. Next, we present the parameters and conditions used throughout all of the scenarios and also discuss the differences in various scenario settings.

The service center has six unique channels and observes time-varying service demands for each channel. It is common in the call center literature to model call arrivals and service times using Poisson and exponential processes, respectively [32]. Mean arrival rates and service times can be measured within periodic time intervals and we assume that there are sixty 10-min intervals during a work day. Accordingly, we generate the utilization parameter $p _ { j t }$ based on random variables that follow Poisson and exponential distributions. Fig. 1 displays channel utilization requirements used in the simulations.

In terms of staffing costs, we consider the annual salary of a service center employee to be \$35,170 based on industry averages provided by the United States Department of Labor.<sup>2</sup> It is important to reiterate that our goal is to help service centers with their long term employee hiring and training decisions $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ staff planning problem) that are usually made several weeks and sometimes months ahead of time due to lead times associated with hiring and training agents [11]. In this respect, we do not consider short term shift scheduling decisions (that can vary day-to-day) to influence the initial hiring and training costs. We also assume that agents are trained on a one-time basis (per year) and training costs vary from one service channel to another. Based on industry reports for companies with fewer than 500 employees [33], we consider the average training cost to be \$1900 and we let individual channel training costs vary between \$1200 and \$3000 per channel across different channels, while keeping the \$1900 average constant. In terms of budget, we assume that the service center has a maximum annual budget (U) of \$2000,000 for its staffing expenses. Maximum budget indicates the upper limit that is allocated by the firm and the realized costs (i.e., costs based on the solution) may be lower than the maximum budget after considering the actual arrival rates and the trade-off between staffing and unserved demand costs. Agents, who work in multiple channels suffer a 10% performance penalty (per channel) due to cognitive limitations and switching delays. Furthermore, service requests have different response priorities (based on the channels that they are arriving from) and this information is represented using c<sup>p</sup>, i.e., the per unit penalty cost of unserved request for channel j. We let the penalty costs vary between \$15 - \$30 per unserved request across different channels based on the range of penalty rates used in Robbins and Harrison [29].<sup>3</sup> Our baseline scenario considers this setup of a service center with varying channel response priorities. To test the robustness of the method and enhance its external validity, we also run three additional scenarios – two of which with different channel priority configurations and the last one with no performance penalties due to task switching. The fully specialized and full trained structures are of particular interest, as they have been adopted in almost 50% of real-world omnichannel service centers collectively [3]. We also conduct a brute-force search to identify the best performing solution in 10,000 randomly generated structures with equal (or approximately equal) total staffing costs. Upon convergence, our algorithm generated a service center structure solution with 41 service agents. Annual total agent salaries were \$1,441,970 and training costs were \$141,200. ## 4.2. Performance comparison vs. complete chain structures

In the second experiment, our goal is to compare the performance of our solution with a particular type of cross-training structure known as complete chain. A chain is a well-known flexibility structure that connects, directly or indirectly, a group of channels and service agents in the system via cross-training (i.e., assignment) links. In terms of graph theory, a chain is a connected graph [16]. A complete chain is a chain with two specific properties: 1) all nodes (channels and agents) in the system are interconnected (i.e., contained in a single chain), 2) the entire graph can be traversed by starting at any node, traversing the entire graph and returning to the starting node without traversing any arc more than once [34,35].

It has been argued that a complete chain structure can perform almost as good as a fully connected system, in which all customer types/ channels can be served by any agent [34]. It is evident that chain type structures would be more robust to unexpected changes and stochastic fluctuations in the demand because of their ability to shift resources between any given nodes in the system. However, our main aim is to see if setting up a chain structure would be unnecessarily costly and redundant, when information about demand patterns and costs are already available.

To test this proposition, we run two numerical experiments with two different complete chain classes and compare our algorithm solution with the results. The first class of complete chain is called the minimal complete chain. Jordan et al. [35] define a minimal complete chain to be a chain with the minimal number of cross-training links needed to be a complete chain. Specifically, they conceptualize workers (i.e., agents) to belong to pools/groups according to the primary task (i.e., channel) that they are assigned to and assume that only a single cross-training link from one pool to another one would be enough to link all of the workers within these two pools (see Fig. 1 left pane in Jordan et al. [35] for an illustration). In other terms, a minimal complete chain is an extension of a fully specialized structure with one and only one agent from each channel being assigned to another channel. A second class of complete chain that we analyze in this study is called the 2- skill complete chain, as defined in Hopp et al. [18]. In this class, each agent from each channel is cross-trained for another channel (hence, 2-skill) and the chain is complete – i.e., satisfies the two properties discussed earlier. Clearly, the difference between the minimal complete chain and the 2-skill complete chain is that in the former one, only one agent in each channel is cross-trained whereas in the latter one, all the agents in each channel are cross-trained.

It is important to note that while these two classes define the overall structure of a system, they do not specify the individual links/assignments in order to establish the chains. As a result, there are many different channel–agent formations that can be considered under the same chain class. For example, Fig. 3 illustrates four different 2-skill complete chain structures for the same center with six channels and six agents. Jordan et al. [35] argue that the performance of a complete chain is insensitive to the specific links that the chain was established. Another goal of ours is to see if this observation indeed holds in our context.

To test these assumptions, we randomly generate 500 service structures for each of the complete chain classes (minimal complete chain and 2-skill complete chain) for an omnichannel service center with 6 unique channels. We ensure that the total staffing (hiring and training) costs of these structures are approximately<sup>8</sup> identical to the costs in our algorithm solution. Second, we find that there exist significant performance fluctuations across different complete chain structures and even between the structures within the same class. For example, for the 2-skill complete chain class, the worst structure is 4 times slower than the best structure in terms of system wide average queue waiting time. Similarly, there is more than oneminute difference between average queue waiting times of the best and worst structures under the minimal complete chain class. This is an interesting finding that contradicts the previous observations in the literature regarding the performance of chains being insensitive to how they are formed [35]. Finally, we observe that within any complete chain structure, there can be significant variation between individual channel performances, which are largely affected by demand rates. In other terms, the notion that chain structures enable full sharing of service resources do not hold when it comes to high demand service environments such as omnichannel service centers where it may be difficult to shift server capacity effectively. Furthermore, without additional priority based routing policies, chain structures will not be able to differentiate among service request priorities as well as tailored solutions such as the one generated by our proposed algorithm.

## 5. Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the problem of identifying ideal workforce structures for omnichannel service centers, in which service agents can be trained to operate on multiple channels that can have varying response urgencies. This is a special case of a class of problems known in the literature as flexibility design problems, which are particularly difficult to solve due to their computational complexity and multi-level decision requirements [36]. Our analyses aimed to answer two important and interlinked questions within this context: 1) how many agents should be employed within an omnichannel service center? 2) to which channels should each service agent be assigned? To our knowledge, there have been few attempts in the literature to collectively answer these questions in the omnichannel service setting. To address this research gap, we developed a cost minimization model and proposed a metaheuristic optimization algorithm that takes into account the trade-off between staffing costs within the service center and the penalty costs due to service delays. A particular benefit of the model is that it enables managers and decision makers to quantify service quality using penalty costs of unserved demand, which can be of different importance across the channels. We believe that such a representation is a natural way of thinking about service shortages in general customer service settings. The algorithm we used to solve this model is based on the genetic algorithm metaheuristic with unique features such as idle agent trimming and mutations to satisfy budget and structural constraints. The algorithm initializes a solution generation with an arbitrarily large staffing allocation and proceeds by shrinking the staffing size, while considering the penalty costs due to service delays of requests with varying response urgencies.

Using numerical experiments, we have shown that the proposed model and the algorithm are useful in identifying tailored service structures that can perform better than many different configurations with equal cost characteristics. In particular, we have found the algorithm solution to significantly outperform fully specialized and fully trained service structures, which have been adopted in almost 50% of real-world omnichannel service centers [3]. The results have also shown that even complete chain structures, which have been found to perform almost as good as a fully connected systems in certain settings may be inferior to the algorithm solution, especially when variations in channel response priorities are considered.

Despite providing valuable insights, our results must be interpreted within the boundaries of the study. For example, we focused on the workforce management issue as a long-term strategic staffing problem, in which staffing decisions are made ahead of time and staffing levels are kept constant throughout the entirety of the operational period (e.g., one year).
