# A longitudinal study of leader influence in sustaining an online community

Joyce Yi-Hui Lee<sup>a</sup>, Chin-Sheng Yang<sup>b</sup>, Carol Hsu<sup>c,⁎</sup>, Jhong-Heng Wang<sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Department of Marketing and Distribution Management, National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology, Taiwan

<sup>b</sup> Department of Information Management, Yuan-Ze University, Taoyuan, Taiwan

<sup>c</sup> Department of Management Science and Engineering, Tongji University, Shanghai, China

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Online community Online leadership Networked influence Social network analysis Content analysis

## A B S T R A C T

Previous research suggests that online leaders play an important role in sustaining community activities. Although the research has contributed to our understanding of how leaders develop efective ways to operate a community, most only provide a snapshot view of online leadership, thus paying little attention to changes in leaders in a communal context. In this study, we adopt the theory of networked influence to investigate the dynamics of online leadership using a longitudinal analysis. Data were collected from an online community in operation for 10 years. By conducting social network analysis using qualitative methods, we identified several types of emerging and coexisting online leadership, i.e., responsive expert leader, multiboard connectors, and social bond leader. The community’s sustainability did not rely on the same leaders throughout its temporal development but rather a “relay event” involving passing on the baton among diferent leaders with their specific leadership styles, which had a significant positive impact on the sustainability of user participation.

## 1. Introduction

Social media has become an important online social venue where people can connect and communicate. In particular, online communities help people to find others with common interests, passions, questions, or problems and they subsequently share information and knowledge and give mutual support [1]. By connecting with known or unknown others in these communities, one’s knowledge can be publicly visible through a variety of online messages posted, read, and shared [2,3]. Because this kind of social media can provide significant value for users, it can also be of great benefit to society, and an important source of social capital across multiple dimensions (see [4], for a review). However, researchers have discovered that participants are not necessarily as active online as once believed [5–7]. Studies have found that many online communities are initially active but subsequently fade away, thus turning into “cyber ghost towns” [8], p. 722. Over 70% of online communities including easyJet and the BBC fail to embrace their users [9,10] and are then unable to serve as conduits for online com munication or knowledge sharing [2,11,12].

The role of online leaders in the sustainability of online communities has attracted much attention in both research and managerial practice [13,7]. Previous research suggests that leaders play an important role in sustaining an online community, evidenced by the increasing growth of research on identifying the roles of leaders in an online community [14,15], the interactions between leaders and other members [15–18], and the driving forces from leaders that influence others’ participation and retentions [14,19,20]. However, most previous research has only provided a snapshot, and little attention has been paid to investigating the change or coexistence of online leaders. Literature takes a relatively static view that may limit understanding of the dynamic nature of an online community [21-24]. To bridge the research gap, this study aims to investigate the changes in online leadership in a longitudinal analysis and these leaders’ influences in helping to sustain a community. This study focuses on two research questions: How do diferent online leaders emerge in the development of a community over time? and How do online leaders influence community members’ participation over a prolonged time frame?

Given the nature of an online communal context, this study adopts the theory of “networked influence” [25,26] as a guideline to investigate network patterns where one influences the behavior of others in an online network environment [27]. We used the theory to investigate an online automobile community centered on the car model N1 (a pseudonym) that emerged from an online open discussion forum URcar (also a pseudonym). The N1 automobile community has attracted a large number of automobile amateurs and experts, who repeatedly and continuously post their opinions and user experience. It began in February 2007 with a discussion board entitled “N1 owners, please sign in here” (abbreviated to Sign-in). It was the longest carrelated discussion on this forum lasting for approximately 10 years. While the Sign-in discussion was constantly expanding, we found three other discussion boards under the same car model (abbreviated as “Carlife,” “Life-together,” and “DIY”) created by N1 members and relevant to the Sign-in discussions. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the participants’ interaction, we took the view that it is essential to consider all four N1-related discussion boards as units of a single community. We collected all online posts from the four discussion boards, which included 1077 participants and a total of 15,959 messages. Using this substantial dataset, we discovered the dynamic changes of online leadership throughout the community’s formation, development, and then sustainment over 10 years.

We conducted social network analysis with qualitative content analysis. Adopting this joint method gave us a clearer understanding of online leadership in relation to its network influences. We investigated the community’s development over time and identified the specific leadership roles influential in sustaining the online community. The research results reveal interesting findings. In particular, we identified three types of emerging leadership: “responsive expert leaders,” “multi board connectors,” and “social bond leaders,” with each having a dis tinct influence on their communities. The responsive expert leaders provide direct knowledge in a Q&A format, the multi-board connectors enhanced interactions and discussion by directing participants to find the relevant knowledge across four different boards, and the social bond leaders strengthened the participants’ relationships. We also found that the community did not rely on the same particular leaders to sustain participants’ communication. Rather, passing on the baton like in a “relay event” between diferent leaders had an influential efect on the prolongation of online communication. Moreover, this relay event led to the community transitioning from being information-sharing oriented to relationship oriented. While previous research has indicated that a successful online community should be provided with informa tion-sharing and social support [1,11,28], the findings of this study extend this understanding by uncovering the diferent orientation needed in diferent stages of community development, which led to the participants’ networked communication moving forward. These re search findings contribute to the field of online community sustain: ability by investigating the dynamic changes of online leadership. Un derstanding the phenomenon of online leadership not only helps in comprehending how a community sustains itself but also provides in sights regarding changing online leadership and its influence in the online networked environment. Regarding the practical implications of the findings, the emergence of diferent online leadership found in ou study signals that the participants need diferent functions during the development of an online community so that it is enriched and sus: tained. Managers and marketers can learn from our study to become more sensitive to the nature of interactions and therefore can learn to manage online communication activities effectively. This study also makes a methodological contribution by demonstrating the use of qualitative content analysis and social network analysis for an in-depth understanding of online behavior.

In the following sections, the literature review discusses online leadership and community sustainability as well as the theory of networked influence. Thereafter, the research methodology is explained, including data collection and analysis. Next, the research findings and discussion are presented. The final section concludes the paper with theoretical and practical implications as well as future research directions being proposed.

## 2. Online leadership and community sustainability

The popularity of social media has led to exponential growth in the volume and significance of online communities. However, research to date showed that managing online communities has met with dificulties in user participation (e.g., [8]), membership retention (e.g. [29]), and hence community sustainability (e.g. [30],). In an open communal environment, participants can join and leave freely without obligation [22], with accounts usually managed and run by the members [67] without commonly accepted organizers [31]. Community activities can become hit-or-miss afairs [32] and consequentially weaken members’ willingness to contribute valuable information ([2,19,30];). As most participants probably have not met in person, it is dificult to develop strong individual trust [33] and attachment to the community [34]. Despite the increasing popularity of online communities, relatively few have been successful in enhancing user participation and interactivity ([2,8,7];).

Previous research suggests that online leaders play an important role in sustaining an online community. In this field, one research stream concerns the emerging roles of online leaders at an individual level and another focuses on the interpersonal processes within a community. The former looks closely at leaders’ individual behaviors regarding the degree of their participation and involvement. Under this lens, researchers have pointed out that online leaders participate actively in communication activities and thus have higher visibility within the community [35–38]. Their active participation and involvement in discussions identify them as leaders adding value to the community [37–40] and playing more critical roles than other parti cipants [41]. Studies have also highlighted how leaders are frequent contributors who actively difuse information and knowledge [42,43]. They can be “super-posters” who post a large bulk of messages and can dominate collective opinions in particular discussion groups [35].

Nevertheless, Wasko and Faraj [31] noticed that online leaders are not necessarily “chatty.” They can be silent leaders [44] who encourage others to express their opinions and facilitate information exchange rather than posting a substantial number of messages. Researchers [6,45,46] have also addressed the issue of “lurkers,” those whose presence within a community is invisible but who can still difuse online content to a wide audience, thus leading to knowledge transfer. Lurkers are a key part of an online community despite their silence. Clearly, diferent forms of online leadership can be found in online communities, and thus, the level of participation defining an online leader remains an open question.

In addition to the above literature concerning online leadership at the individual level, another important research stream has addressed the interpersonal processes concerning how online leaders influence others’ participation. From this perspective, online leaders are those capable of inducing a large number of members to participate [47.29]. thereby enhancing the exchange of information and knowledgeable conversations [2]. They can spark dialogues, prompt feedback, and shape how participants discuss a subject [14]. They also encourage others to “talk” [44] and engage in conversations [15]. In addition to the leader’s capability for enhancing information and knowledge exchange, research shows that a good leader in a community provides social and emotional support to the participants, thus demonstrating how the community can be valuable to those who use it [12,28,48]. Huy and Shipilov’s investigation [49] found that a leader developing emotional capital (i.e., feelings of authenticity, pride, attachment, and fun) results in a successful community in work settings. Through the devel oping of interpersonal bonds, participants’ attachment to the community can be enhanced [50]. Indeed, high responsiveness and interactivity among participants are beneficial for community members [30,42] and thus online communities can continue to flourish under such conditions [30]. With the emphasis on user participation and community sustainability, an online leader is an emerging role and, in this study, defined as a participant recognized by others as being influential in determining what activity the community does and how [14.15,51].

## 3. Networked influence theory

Within the online network environment, a consensus is increasingly gathering in support of the view that the network structure can help to identify frontline leaders as well as leadership in online communal settings. The networked influence theory [25], developed from the concept of “networked individualism” [26], provides a mechanism for studying interpersonal behavior in an online setting. It relies on the notion that actions and interactions of individuals lie within a net worked environment, not a context of isolation [26,27]. This is particularly important for this study because we are investigating the influence of online leaders in a communal setting, identifying the leaders and their characteristics, and how leaders influence others.

Moreover, the networked influence theory connected with social network literature ofers a structural perspective of how one individual’s behavior can identify them as a leader, how a leader can influence others in communication networks, and how some leaders bring about a network structural change [14,15,21,24]. From this perspective, studies of online behavior show that leaders are often positioned centrally in their surrounding networks, meaning they are highly connected with many members of the community or discussion entities. The communication network positions, in terms of network centrality (i.e., the core of the network), afect the likelihood of someone becoming identified as a leader [21,52–54]. Freeman [55] delineated the relevant network centrality concepts: degree centrality and betweenness centrality. Degree centrality is an indicator that counts for the number of an individual participant’s direct connections to other members, thus indicating the influence of a certain individual in a network. Betweenness centrality refers to quantifying the number of times an individual participant acts as a bridge along the shortest path between two participants. Overall, network centrality indicates how well positioned an individual participant is to be connected with others (high degree centrality) and to be a gatekeeper or broker in a network (high be tweenness centrality). People who are centrally located in a network, constantly serve to increase their status, amplify their reputation, and contribute to their ability to influence others [56]. Consequently, they are seen as being more influential and contributing more to a commu nity than those located in the peripheral network positions [57,37,54].

Beyond network positions, researchers [14,15,38] have found that leadership is associated with linguistic use in communication. Hufaker [14] investigated leadership behaviors in online communities using a linguistic analysis approach, which revealed that online leaders influ ence others by employing afective, assertive, and linguistic diversity in their online messages. Johnson et al. [15] argued that participants who posted a large number of positive, concise messages with simple language familiar to others are likely to be perceived as leaders, whereas Zhu et al. [38] found that diferent leaderships are associated with diferent language use in their online communication.

The literature in this field has provided valuable insights into online leaders mainly from the perspective of identifying them, uncovering what they do and say to influence the community, and whether or how they change their behavior over time (see Table 1 for a summary). However, most studies give only limited attention to the dynamic changes of online leaders that may occur within a community over time. Fleming and Waguespack [24] and O’Mahony and Ferraro [57] have pointed out the need to study the behavior change of online leaders; however, their studies take as their focus the same leaders and lack a comprehensive investigation into changing leaders. Thus, the extant research provides limited explanations regarding the changing role of online leaders in relation to the growth, decline, or sustainment of an online community over time. Researchers have provided evidence that network patterns in the communal context rarely remain static but rather are constantly changing [23]. That is, the network structure is usually fluid [22] and conversations among participants are a sequence of events, not once-in-a-lifetime ones [30]. We contend that given the community context constantly changes, online leadership will not necessarily be held by the same person(s). Diferent leaders could emerge in the development of an online community over time, and subsequently, these diferent people could influence the network in idiosyncratic ways. If we only consider the existence of online leaders as a static process without thinking the roles change, this would limit our understanding of online communities in a complex environment. To address this limitation, a longitudinal study was conducted to gain better comprehension regarding the dynamic development of online leaders and their influences over time.

## 4. Research methods

To address the research objectives, we conducted social network analysis applied with qualitative content analysis to investigate a popular online automobile community on a longitudinal study. In this section, the methods of data collection and data analysis are described. For confidentiality reasons, some information has been changed and names presented as pseudonyms, but these changes do not afect the results.

The online automobile community for this study centered on the model N1 (a pseudonym) that emerged from an online open discussion forum URcar (also a pseudonym). The N1 automobile community began in February 2007 with a discussion subject entitled “N1 owners, please sign in here” (abbreviated to Sign-in). It was the longest car-related discussion on this forum, lasting for approximately 10 years. While the Sign-in discussion was constantly expanding, we found that three other discussion subjects under the same car model (abbreviated as “Car-life,” “Life-together,” and “DIY”) were created by N1 members and were also relevant to the discussions of the Sign-in. Initially, it looked as if the N1 online discussion was featuring in four separate discussion boards, but when we observed the content of the discussions, it emerged that they were all related: we found that one single user’s name appeared on more than two different boards and the content of boards was closely related. To understand this community more comprehensively, we collected all online posts from the four discussion boards, which included 1077 participants and a total of 15,959 messages (data retrieved in June 2016). Using this substantial dataset, we discovered the dynamic changes of online leaders throughout the community’s formation, development, and sustainment over 10 years.

To analyze the content of the communications within the developing stages of this community, textual data were collected from online posts. The basic information from a post included the username, the poster’s picture (some were left blank and some were replaced with other pictures), posting date and time, an ordinal number of the post given by the site in relation to the discussion board, and the post’s main text. In addition, to make the data collection and analysis more eficient, a web crawler was developed to collect, periodically, the posts in HTML format from the selected boards. Given the dataset’s significant volume and rich content, this case of the online community has provided a good oppor tunity to study its dynamic development over time. With a substantial amount of textual data collected, we understood that text-mining processing techniques could be useful for data analysis. This particular method was adopted to support reaching a general understanding of the participants’ conversations. However, owing to its limitation for analyzing the newly developed and changing web languages [62], we needed to rely on a qualitative analysis approach as the main method to dig further for profound meaning in the participants’ conversations.

Guided by networked influence theory, social network analysis and qualitative content analysis were applied. Social networks were investigated in the community with an emphasis on the post-and-reply approach. This included people who posted messages, who replied to one another, whose messages were cited, and who asked or answered questions directly one to another. In the social graphs presented in this report, the nodes represent individuals who posted messages, while the lines illustrate the links between individuals in relation to questions asked and answered. In addition, the size of the nodes reflects the number of an individual’s posts and the thickness of the lines pertains to the level of the individual links (number of interactions). In so doing, the network analysis software UCINET, which has been widely used for prior studies [27,31,56], was adopted to assist in organizing and managing the dataset. Borgatti et al. [63] suggested that the efectiveness of network analysis can benefit from using this software. In particular, the measurement technique centrality provided by this software, including degree centrality and betweenness centrality [55], was particularly adopted for identifying online leaders in the community. Degree centrality $C _ { D } ( u _ { i } )$ of a user $u _ { i }$ is calculated as follows: $\begin{array} { r } { C _ { D } ( u _ { i } ) = \sum _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ( u _ { i } , u _ { j } ) } \end{array}$ , where n is the number of users in the network and $( u _ { i } , u _ { j } ) \dot { = } 1$ if a link exists between u and u ; otherwise $( u _ { i } , u _ { j } ) = 0 . \mathrm { A }$ user with high degree centrality has direct connections to many other users in the network, thus indicating the influence of a certain individual in a network. Betweenness centrality $C _ { B } ( u _ { i } )$ of a user $u _ { i }$ is calculated as follows: $\begin{array} { r } { C _ { B } ( u _ { i } ) = \sum _ { s < t } \frac { N _ { s t } ( u _ { i } ) } { N _ { s t } } } \end{array}$ , where $N _ { s t }$ is the number of all shortest paths between $u _ { s }$ and $u _ { t } \left( s \vec { \neq } t \right)$ and $N _ { s t } ( u _ { i } )$ is the number of shortest paths between $u _ { s }$ and $u _ { t }$ that also pass through $u _ { i } .$ A user with high betweenness centrality has considerable influence with a network by virtue of the individual’s control over information passing between other members. Following the measurement, online leaders in the community can be identified based on their network positions.

## 5. Analysis and findings

Over a period of 10 years, participants came and left the online community. Some participants continuously posted messages, others participated intermittently, and some stopped posting altogether. The information in Fig. 1, demonstrates the rise and fall of all participants as well as the number of posts over the period. When considering the overall number of participants and posts (Fig. 1) and their interactions (Appendix A), the 10-year development of the online community can be divided into three stages: developing, active, and sustained.

The period from 2007 to 2011 is the developing stage, as there were on average only 40 messages posted by 19 members each month. The participants’ networks were not yet interactive, although increasing numbers of participants were joining the discussions. The active stage was from 2012 to 2014, with significantly more participants posting messages (on average 432 messages by 70 participants per month). The boundary of this community was also enlarged, mainly due to the in itiation of Car-life, Life-together, and DIY. Following the active stage, the posted messages between 2015 and 2016 began to decline (an average of 152 messages and 47 participants per month). Nevertheless, while the overall discussion and participants’ interactions were mundane, DIY still performed actively and sustained participants’ communication in the community.

By conducting social network analysis, we identified diferent online leaders who emerged in diferent development stages of this community. By investigating the participants’ posted content, we found that these leaders influenced this community in distinct ways that sustained the participants’ communication and interaction. In the following section, relevant findings are discussed pertaining to each of the three stages.

## 5.1. The developing stage: a responsive expert leader and Q&A conversations

The Sign-in discussion board was initiated in 2007 and soon attracted dozens of N1 car owners who posted their cars’ background information. At this stage, while the participants posted messages, they did not reply to or receive replies from others. In other words, conversations among these initial users were not interactive. Moreover, a person called Sport, who initiated this discussion board, became invisible and did not post any messages apart from the one sent to start the N1 discussion. The N1 discussion became self-organizing without the board creator’s involvement.

We noticed that after a time some people started to raise questions about their cars’ maintenance issues and received useful feedback from others. The atmosphere of a knowledge-sharing community began to emerge. That is, some posted messages and others replied, allowing information and knowledge about the N1 to be exchanged among the posters. The messages posted by Airtiger and Pink show the nature of the knowledge sharing in the communal environment.

[Airtiger wrote on Sign-in, 13.02.2007, #15] My N1 was made in 1999. I like it very much as it is very quiet when driving which makes my family and myself very comfortable … Although I like its quietness, it sometimes makes me annoyed to hear noises clearly even though they are low volume $\cdots$ Recently, I have had the problem of a noise coming from the dashboard area, but I have no idea what causes the noise …

[Pink replied to Airtiger on Sign-in, 14.02.2007, #16] I have never had such problem, but I suppose that the noise was caused by two possible reasons: a tire or the transmission shaft. [Detailed information was given] … Regarding the first, it can be sorted out easily, but if it is because of the second situation, it could be a serious problem. It would be best for you to find a garage to check your car thoroughly.

[Airtiger replied to Pink on Sign-in, 14.02.2007, #17] Thanks for your reply. I am sure that the noise is from my dashboard, not somewhere else. But you are right, I definitely need to find a garage to carefully check my car.

Although two-way conversations emerged, these intercommunications took the form of simple questions and answers (Q&As). The Q&Alike interactions did not extend into long threads, typically ending within three posts. The car maintenance issues were discussed intermittently. We came to realize that a person named Hsaio was appearing to play a key role in stimulating the discussions. He frequently posted messages and seemed to play a central role in the networked communication. As an example, Fig. 2 shows the communication network for 2011, the year with the most people involved in this stage. The social network analysis indicates that Hsiao played a significant role in the number of posts, with higher centrality than the others. An analysis of Hsiao’s posts reveals that he gave detailed answers to others who drew on his professional knowledge. In the example below, he gave a detailed explanation to Cheese about how a generator can be fixed. The original message was approximately 600 words (our edits here were meant to clarify the message’s purpose).

[Hsiao replied to Cheese on Sign-in, 04.11.2008, #333] [Cheese’s post was cited] If it is because the electronic generator has broken, it would not be warned by just a flicker light at the dashboard.

With his leadership, information and knowledge exchanges gradually increased year on year. Hsiao’s central role in the communication network served as a generous knowledge provider in the Q&A conversations.

## 5.2. The active stage: multi-board connectors and cross-board communication

Participants' interactions became more active from 2012 to 2014 averaging more than 1000 messages posted by more than 200 people, and 100 or more newcomers joined in these discussions. Moreover, we found that as the number of participants and posts were increasing, the community began to expand. We observed that the three discussion boards (i.e., Car-life, Life-together, and DIY) created by the original Sign-in members allowed participants more communication space to cover a variety of topics related to the N1 discussion. We identified a small group of “multi-board connectors” wandering among the discussion boards, who formed a leadership network that resulted in participants’ cross-board communication. Consequentially, the four discussion boards formed the N1 community as a whole, rather than independent discussion entities.

## 5.3. The sustained stage: Social bond leaders strengthened participants relationships

Temporary changes within this community become clear when we consider the long-term postings data for the four focal boards. From 2015 onwards, we noticed a decline in the overall discussion and the DIY board’s performance was significantly diferent. That is, while the overall discussion was declining on Sign-in, Car-life, and Life-together, the number of posts, participants, and newcomers on DIY continued to increase. The result derived from the text mining approach indicated that “engine,” oil consumption,” and “genuine accessories” were the most discussed topics in the developing stage and “car parts,” “maintenance plant,” and “modification” in the active stage. The car maintenance and repair discussion issues continued in the sustained stage. Interestingly, more noncar-related discussion (such as greetings, tea, cofee, food, and travel information) and emoji symbols appeared in the participants’ conversations. We consider that the community in this stage was gradually changing from information-sharing oriented to social-relationship oriented.

Content analysis of the online posts revealed that the board creator, Mr. Right, had a particular form of leadership behavior that involved an idiosyncratic communication style, which strengthened the participants’ relationships. This led to the N1 discussion remaining interactive in DIY.

Mr. Right’s influencing power was noticeable in the content of his posts. When we compared his posts on DIY and Sign-in, we identified his idiosyncratic communication style. For his DIY posts, Mr. Right used many emoticons, diferent colors, and special characters (e.g., Zhuyin, a phonetic symbol in Mandarin), but this communication style was not evident in his contributions to Sign-in. The idiosyncratic communication behavior consequently influenced the atmosphere of this discussion board, making it more free, casual, and fun while continuing to discuss serious car problems. As the example below shows, Hsiao was also starting to use emoticons frequently in his posts on DIY.

## 6. Discussion

This study has led to the identification of diferent types of online leaders who were more influential than other members and who sig nificantly contributed to sustaining the online community (see Table 3). We found that the online community’s sustainability was made possible by diferent leadership bringing diferent forms of influence across the diferent development stages. First, there was the responsive expert leader Hsiao, whose generous response to questions led to the emergence of strong support. He behaved as a knowledge provider and this gradually led to the formation of an online community. Second, the multi-board connectors (e.g., Su, Cheese, and Tom) acted as informa tion navigators, which led to cross-board interactions, thereby showing how four discussion boards were merged into a robust community. Finally, a social bond leader, Mr. Right, acted as a cheerleader. With an idiosyncratic communication style, he changed the communication atmosphere, strengthened the participants’ relationships, and thus sustained the community. The community was maintained by passing on the baton of the involved leaderships, rather than counting on one specific leader or the same leaders throughout its development. This relay event behavior led to the community to moving from information sharing oriented toward being relationship oriented, with had an in fluential efect on the community’s prolongation.

The phenomenon of online leadership shifting implies that diferent functions were needed at diferent stage so that the community could be sustained. In our case, at the early stage, the community participants may have joined the community with the aim to seek information or knowledge to maintain their cars. When the community was expanding and information was accumulating, it might become dificult to locate relevant information. Thus, there were people who adopted a novel leadership role of generously guiding others toward information that they sought. Finally, while knowledge exchange remained important, the strengthening of relationships among the community members can be seen as being critical to sustaining the community’s interactions.

We believe that our findings are significant and contribute to the research field of online leaderships in several respects. First, the research outcomes contribute to the literature on the dynamics of online community development, in particular, with regard to the changing role of leadership over time. This study involved conducting a long itudinal analysis and thus provides a greater understanding of the changes over time of an online community and how one such community develops, becomes robust, and how it can be sustained. Given an insuficient understanding of network-level interaction dynamics, previous studies in this field have called for further research [19,21–23,30]. The findings of this current longitudinal study significantly increase our understanding of the dynamic nature of an on line community. While previous research has provided valuable insights into the influential factors sustaining an online community [14,15,36,64], much of it has been based as a snapshot view, devoting limited attention to the dynamic changes that occur in such leadership. Our study’s findings provide evidence that leadership in network pat terns in the communal context rarely remain static, but rather are constantly changing.

Second, we have identified three types of online leaders whose influence on online communication ensuring that participants’ interactions will continue. From the theoretical perspective of online leadership roles, researchers have shown an interest in identifying diferent typologies of online leaderships. Some have studied active participants, such as “super-posters” and “message contributors” [35,42,43], while others have focused on inactive roles, e.g., “silent leader” and “lurkers” [31,44,45]. In response to previous studies on leadership roles, this study has determined that the levels of online leaders’ participation and presence can be critical for a community. Moreover, our research has found that the “relay efect” between online leaders in diferent stages significantly impacts the development of an online community. Although three types of online leaders were identified, moderator leader roles with formal authority were not found. In short, the reason why some people in a networked environment matter more than others is not just about the number of participants or levels of participation, for the manner in which they influence the community is relevant.

Third, we found that the multi-board connectors at the active stage served the capacity of facilitating cross-board communication and thereby expanding the boundaries of the community. Previous research has indicated that participants who integrate and bind diferent networks are associated with leadership roles [60]. This role requires extra information or resources exceeding the boundary of an organizational setting and contributing to leader efectiveness [58]. Fleming and Waguespack’s [24] investigation into an open innovation community identified that boundary spanners communicate with peers in other organizations, earn the respect of their colleagues, and thus advance into management and technical leadership positions. Although the leadership role as a boundary spanner is not new to this research, our study adds to the literature by addressing a new rule for expanding the boundary: the leadership roles integrate an individual network entity into a robust community by enhancing participants’ cross-board interactions. Clearly, if one considers the development of network influences as a stand-alone entity, this would provide a limited understanding of online communities in the complex environment [29,45,65]. In response to previous research, this study contributes toward filling this research gap.

Through this study, we also make a methodological contribution by demonstrating the use of qualitative content analysis and social network analysis for an in-depth understanding of online behavior. Hufaker [14] and Johnson et al. [15] have applied social network analysis with a linguistic approach in studying online leadership that contributes relevant knowledge regarding the emergence of online leaders and how they are recognized by others. In addition to previous research focused on the visibility of an online leader, our research involved undertaking an in-depth investigation to garner further understanding of online leadership behaviors and their influences on a community.

## 7. Conclusions

With a longitudinal perspective, we conducted social network analysis with qualitative content analysis for this study. Adopting this joint method gave us a clearer understanding regarding online leadership in relation to its networked influences in sustaining an online community. We investigated the development over time of the online community and identified specific roles of leadership influential in sustaining it. The research results reveal three types of emerging leadership: responsive expert leaders, multi-board connectors, and social bond leaders. The relay type of activity between them had an influential efect on the prolongation of the online communication. This led the community from being information-sharing oriented toward relationship oriented. These research findings contribute to the field of online community sustainability by identifying the dynamic changes of online leadership.

While the increasing growth of social media has transformed how organizations structure their business and specifically the methods they use to communicate with their consumers, managers and marketers have faced considerable challenges in embracing the development of social commerce [13,49]. In this study, with its focus on investigating customer-hosted online community, managers and marketers can learn from emerging online leaders who not only provide informative knowledge, for they also can create a fun atmosphere. The identified leaders cooperated with existing active participants in the community and took care of newcomers. Moreover, while managers and marketers have paid much attention to providing information or knowledge re garding the products or services they represent, they have overlooked the importance of understanding how participants in online communities interact socially, which is believed to be of paramount importance if they are to engage efectively with members. Moreover, when an online community lasts for a long period of time, the participants will come and go, and their interests when participating will probably change. Thus, diferent types of leader need to exist to care for diferent participants and interests at diferent development stages.

This study focused on a customer-hosted online community based on an online discussion forum. The findings provide useful insights into understanding the nature of communal contexts. However, one limitation is that the results cannot be generalized to all online communities, because the data were sourced from one particular online community. Previous studies [11,66] have shown that business communities created by organizations or their marketers may operate diferently from those created by general users or customers. The community contexts in various online platforms (such as Facebook and Twitter) would not be the same. Consequently, further research is needed to make comparisons between the marketer- and customer-generated communities to clarify the nature of this diference, with variant forms of online community for the purposes of social commerce being considered. Developing a complementary understanding based on various forms of online community would provide a clearer understanding of how organizations can engage customers’ online communication profitably.

Moreover, given our substantial dataset, this study’s data analysi benefited from text mining techniques we developed. However, this approach has limitations when it comes to analyzing newly developed web slang and abbreviations. Considering that this research was aimed at gaining an in-depth understanding regarding online communication, a traditional qualitative research approach was deemed appropriate for this study. For future studies, it would be worthwhile to develop a text mining processing mechanism to improve the efectiveness of online content analysis [66].

In sum, discovering the phenomenon of online leadership not only helps in providing understanding how a community can be managed efectively and hence, sustain. For, it also delivers insights regarding the changing online leaders and their influences in the online networked environment. In practical terms, managers and marketers in commercia fields through considering our findings can acquire a better understanding regarding the online communal context and thus, manage online communication activities more efectively. Participants’ common interests may change. That is to say, the original reason why they wanted to participate in a specific online community could be diferent to why they are continuing to do so. In the case of customer-hosted online community, it has demonstrated that a shift in leadership based on different functions can result in the sustainability of an online community.
