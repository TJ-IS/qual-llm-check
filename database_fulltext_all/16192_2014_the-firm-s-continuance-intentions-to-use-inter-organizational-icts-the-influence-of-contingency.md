# The firm’s continuance intentions to use inter-organizational ICTs: The influence of contingency factors and perceptions

## Abstract

We study the influence of contingency factors on the firm’s intentions to continue using an inter-organizational ICT. This influence is mediated by perceptions and satisfaction with the ICT. The contingency factors analyzed are the firm’s environment and ICT compatibility with the organizational culture. Perceptions are the usefulness, ease of use and security of the ICT. Findings demonstrate that contingency factors directly explain the firm’s perceptions and, indirectly, satisfaction and continuance intentions. This study contributes by adapting the predominant behavioral models in the technology arena to the analysis of continuance and explaining why firms perceive inter-organizational ICTs as useful and secure.

Keywords: Continuance intentions, Inter-organizational ICTs, Contingency factors, Perceptions, Environment, Compatibility.

## 1. Introduction

The increasing competitiveness that has emerged in organizational environments in recent years has entailed important business changes that are necessary for survival. One of these changes is the need to introduce Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs). These are defined as technologies like hardware, software, networks and media that facilitate the collecting, storage, processing, transmission and presentation of information (voice, data, text, images) and also enable communication between human beings, organizations and applications [167]. In the business context, inter-organizational ICTs are especially important. These ICTs allow the firm to electronically integrate other organizations into the implementation of its activities and processes. They establish linkages between trading partners and send business information beyond the firm’s borders instantly. Taking into account the opportunities and benefits related to the implementation of ICTs, further studies about the experiences of user firms are needed to understand them better.

Most research on technology in the organizational context is focused on the reflective cognitive processing inherent to the prior adoption and initial use of ICTs and is based on behavioral models [67]. It attempts to determine the motivational factors of adoption and employs frameworks such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), and the Innovation Diffusion Theory (IDT), [3, 35, 128]. It considers potential user perceptions related to the intrinsic attributes of the technology (usefulness, ease of use, etc.) and proposes that adoption is the consequence of a rational and deliberate decision derived from an analysis of the desirable expected outcomes. The boom and initial success of these models led to the proliferation of numerous studies which consider the same perceptions and relationships, regardless of the moment and the technology tested.

It must be taken into account that behavioral models emerged during the 80s and 90s, when ICTs began to be applied in many contexts. Understanding the adoption decision of potential users meant breaking a fundamental barrier to achieving wider dissemination. These ICTs were evolving and users started to use them regularly, leading to continued use which is also called continuance [12]. Continuance is closely related to the prior adoption of ICTs so the literature usually elaborates both concepts within similar frameworks and extrapolates the results produced for one to the other. Indeed, an overall review of the literature may give the impression that the two concepts are perfect substitutes. Consequently, most studies about continuance intentions are mainly focused on user perceptions and ignore the relevance of other factors that may have an influence after experiencing the characteristics of the ICT. The differences between adoption and continuance demonstrate the importance of adapting behavioral models and identifying the specific drivers of the firm’s intentions to continue using an ICT.

The motivation driving our research is to bridge the gap in behavioral models between adoption and continuance, paying attention to what potentially determines a firm’s intentions to continue using an inter-organizational ICT. It is undeniable that the firm’s perceptions about the intrinsic attributes of the ICT are primary predictors of user behavior and intentions [146]. Nevertheless, these perceptions may not fully capture user continuance behavior, especially in the organizational context, where not only ICT issues but also internal and interorganizational drivers, as contingency factors, come into play.

## 2. Aim and contributions

The aim of our research is to study the influence of contingency factors on the firm’s continuance intentions to use an inter-organizational ICT, proposing that this influence is mediated by the perceptions and satisfaction of the firm following its employment. We build a model that analyzes the effects of (1) contingency factors on perceptions about the ICT (2) perceptions on satisfaction with the ICT and (3) satisfaction on the ICT continuance intentions (Figure 1). The contingency factors we study are ICT compatibility with the organizational culture [154] and the inter-organizational environment with which the firm interacts [68]. Perceptions come from behavioral models, are derived from users’ evaluations after the ICT implementation and refer to usefulness, ease of use and security [35, 57, 100]. Inter-organizational ICTs are appropriate for all types of businesses [14], as long as they can be properly integrated into all processes. However, this integration can be difficult for small firms with a low turnover and limited technological resources to carry out, so we check the stability of the proposed relationships across size, financial performance and the ICT level. Likewise, we will determine whether there are any differences in the characteristics of user firms and whether the results obtained can be generalized.

The study is focused on electronic invoicing (e-invoicing), an inter-organizational ICT that uses computer hardware, software and the Internet to gather all the information on a commercial transaction into one electronic file and then transmit it electronically [50]. The seller sends the file to the purchaser to certify the sale or supply of services. E-invoicing is fundamental for commercial trade because it manages the whole cycle of orders electronically, linking the logistic and commercial aspects with the administrative and financial ones and, consequently, with the payment systems. To ensure the authenticity and integrity of data, the European Commission, in the VAT Directive [43, 44], requires the inclusion of an advanced digital signature in the document. This ICT allows firms to substitute some internal manual processes, accelerate invoicing, improve the relationships with the firm’s trading partners and eliminate non-value added activities [11, 132]. Nevertheless, there are also some barriers to the diffusion of this ICT, including legal uncertainty, lack of trust and of standardization [107], with the result that the number of user firms is still significantly lower than initially expected.

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to empirically analyze the use of e-invoicing, the previous literature on this ICT being largely conceptual and descriptive [48, 59]. This study allows non-user firms interested in e-invoicing to know real experiences with the ICT, to check if user firms are satisfied and, in short, to realize if its adoption and continuance is really worthwhile. User firms of e-invoicing are a more credible source of information than biased sources such as service providers. In this way, non-user firms will obtain further evidence and will be more motivated to adopt the ICT. Our results will also support the development of public authority campaigns to promote the use of e-invoicing. Finally, sellers and/or providers of einvoicing systems will determine where to focus their marketing efforts when they design and update specific software to achieve customer satisfaction and improve user retention.

Although the relationship between contingency factors and the firm’s perceptions about an ICT is fundamental to ensure successful continuance in the organizational context, it has hardly been tested [61, 140]. Our study will contribute to research on ICT by going beyond mere adoption, opening the black box of perceptions and shedding light on why firms are satisfied with an ICT and intend to continue using it. It will demonstrate the importance of adapting predominant behavioral models in the technology adoption arena to study ICT continuance and of delineating two antecedents of perceptions: compatibility and environment.

Our model and findings can be applied to other inter-organizational ICTs, such as e-SCM, extranets, B2B e-commerce and e-payment systems. These ICTs allow the development of electronic markets or customer-supplier relations as well as networks that establish alliances to promote collaboration between firms. These relationships and collaborations require the user firm to perceive the usefulness, ease of use and security derived from the ICT application. We also propose that, due to the nature of these ICTs, their continuance and benefits are only realized if they are compatible with the organizational culture and if the environment with which the firm interacts positively influences its perceptions and intentions.

## 3. Theoretical background

## 3.1. The firm’s continuance intentions and satisfaction

Continuance is defined as the persistent use of an ICT beyond its first use [12], that is, the continuous employment of a technology on a regular basis [104]. It is not entirely an alien concept in ICT research since it has been called implementation, incorporation, routinization and infusion [33, 104]. Even when an ICT is satisfactorily adopted, users later re-evaluate their initial decision and may decline the ICT [13, 61]. So, continuance is a concept temporally and conceptually different to adoption and is considered the cornerstone for improving the performance of ICTs [38, 41].

Research has adopted various measures of continuance, highlighting self-reported use and intentions to continue using [115]. Despite its apparent interest, self-reported use has serious limitations, including its subjective nature, the difficulty of measuring it and its significant differences with actual use [106, 113]. These limitations have meant that most studies have considered it a poor surrogate for ICT continuance, preferring intentions to continue using as the best proxy of user behavior [29, 74, 114]. Intentions to continue using refer to expected future consumption or usage of an ICT and are closely related with actual use, which is demonstrated by the high correlation between the two concepts [137, 159]. Intentions to continue using are a consistent and reliable measure and have a strong relationship with other concepts such as perceptions [114]. According to Agarwal and Prasad [1], for ICTs, the analysis of intentions is more important than the analysis of usage because the latter refers to the past while the former refers to the future and has longer-term implications. Moreover, when users gain experience with the ICT, their familiarity is higher and their uncertainty is lower, so intentions become more comprehensive, stable and nonprovisional. Therefore, the study of continuance intentions is important to predict users future performance and to know if the ICT will be viable. Nevertheless, intentions are sometimes overestimated and may not respond to the user's free will. In these cases, continuance intentions should be complimented with measures related to the ICT success, as user satisfaction, and the relationship between them should be checked [115].

Expectation Confirmation Theory (ECT) holds that satisfaction with the use of a product or service is a primary motivation for individuals to continue their behavior [110]. Following a period of initial consumption, satisfaction comes from the comparison of the individual’s original expectations with his/her perceptions about performance. If expectations are confirmed, the individual will be satisfied and show greater intention to continue consuming that product [110]. The importance of satisfaction lies in the fact that it is based on a real judgment derived from experience and involves a greater degree of stability than assessments based on attitudes [12].

In the ICT arena, it can be affirmed that user satisfaction with prior use is an important antecedent of their continued use of the ICT [74]. Satisfaction comes from the confirmation of initial expectations about the benefits of using the ICT, and is essential to build and retain users. It is an experience-specific affect and a surrogate of the success of the ICT [12, 169]. At the individual level, the literature has confirmed the relationship between user satisfaction and continuance [28, 60]; however, research at the organizational level is not conclusive and requires further analysis, as Petter et al. [115] stated.

In the case of e-invoicing, firms that decide to adopt the ICT expect several benefits that have been explained in the campaigns conducted by different public and private organisms. These expectations should be supported by the user firms’ experiences with e-invoicing. Satisfaction with e-invoicing reflects that the ICT has met the firm’s needs and requirements [166], and is considered a major influence to explain continuance intentions [13, 29, 120]. We consider that, if user firms are satisfied with e-invoicing, they will have greater intentions of continuing to use it in the future:

H1. Firm satisfaction with the use of e-invoicing has a positive effect on intentions to continue using it

## 3.2. Perceptions about ICT: Perceived usefulness, ease of use and security

Perceptions linked to the intrinsic attributes of an ICT have been widely considered in research into its adoption. Among behavioral theories, the TAM proposed by Davis [35] and Davis et al. [36] stands out because of its robustness and high explanatory power [80]. TAM is developed from the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) and was initially proposed to analyze the first adoption of ICTs, focusing on two key constructs: Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) [35, 36].

PU is the degree to which users of an ICT consider that its use will improve their job performance or reduce their workload [35], providing greater organizational advantages than either the status quo or the ICT precursor [168]. PU refers to the perceived benefits of an ICT and is similar to the “relative advantage” proposed by Rogers [128]. It is closely related to outcome expectations and can be an important predictor of user satisfaction [36, 110, 151].

Research based on DeLone and McLean’s model of information system success [37] includes PU as a determinant of user satisfaction [135], while Bhattacherjee [12, 13] considers that the user who judges the ICT to be useful will be more satisfied with it and use it more frequently [12, 13, 54]. Devaraj et al. [38] demonstrate that PU is a significant antecedent of consumer satisfaction in the e-commerce arena, and Liao et al. [91] find that PU is positively correlated with satisfaction at three levels of technological experience. According to the ECT, users’ perceived usefulness has a positive effect on their satisfaction with an ICT by working as a baseline for reference against confirmation expectations [102, 151]. Therefore, usefulness seems to be a primary determinant of user satisfaction, indirectly explaining the intentions to continue using the ICT [8, 13, 97].

The benefits of e-invoicing determine the usefulness perceived by the firm, in comparison to its initial expectations about the ICT. If the ICT is considered to be useful for carrying out the firm’s activities, this perception will positively influence user satisfaction. We hypothesize the following relationship:

## H2. Perceived usefulness of e-invoicing has a positive effect on user firm satisfaction

PEOU consists of the idea that using a specific ICT will not require additional effort [35, 127, 134]. This factor has an inverse relation with the concept of “complexity” proposed by Rogers [128], who defined this as the degree to which an innovation is perceived to be difficult to understand, learn or operate. A complex ICT requires greater operational efforts and technological abilities for its implementation [33, 147]; this may influence other perceptions during the use of the ICT. The relationship between PEOU and PU varies according to the user and the ICT analyzed. Several studies confirm its significance [136, 138] and consider that ease of use is a main requirement for users to appreciate the usefulness of the ICT. Other research rejects the relationship [2, 158], arguing that the two perceptions are independent, and a third group of research does not even consider it [98].

This controversy about the relationship between PEOU and PU requires an examination of whether a direct effect exists between the two factors in the case of e-invoicing. We consider that the fact that e-invoicing involves formats and activities known to the user removes certain barriers to its diffusion. This may increase the ease of use perceived during use of e-invoicing and would increase the usefulness perceived by firms. We propose the following relationship:

## H3. Perceived ease of use of e-invoicing has a positive effect on perceived usefulness

In recent years, some TAM research has introduced new variables in order to better explain the effect of usefulness and ease of use on technological behavior [111, 136]. These additional variables have increased the explanatory power of the TAM and adapted it to the characteristics of new ICTs, following Davis [35] and Davis et al. [36] in their pioneering studies.

One variable included in research into ICT development for business is perceived security [85, 116]. Security refers to the perception of the reliability of online payment methods, data transmission and storage mechanisms [82, 124]. Several technological advances have increased the security of web-based transactions, particularly in certificates, einvoicing and digital signatures [170]. However, firms are still concerned about sending sensitive information and conducting their relationships with suppliers and customers via the Internet, so security is one of the most important barriers to the development of some ICTs [79, 83]. If the perceived confidentiality and integrity of the communication carried out via the ICT are not ensured, users are unlikely to employ it until solutions are implemented [79]. Therefore, perceived security has a significant effect on user satisfaction and is an important contributor to intentions to continue using the ICT [22, 70, 170].

E-invoicing must satisfy strict security requirements in order to become part of a firm’s financial practices [73]. Some of these security requirements are related to their relationships with other agents (authentication and non-recognition of transactions) while others are derived from the firm’s technological culture (security policy, electronic storage of data, etc.). All these security characteristics influence how users behave with respect to e-invoicing. If users perceive that this ICT is secure, they will feel more relaxed and, therefore, experience greater satisfaction. We analyze the effect of security on the firm’s satisfaction with einvoicing, following other authors who have tested this relationship for ICTs such as Intranet, e-commerce and e-government [64, 90].

## H4. Perceived security of e-invoicing has a positive effect on user firm satisfaction

## 3.3. Contingency factors: environment and compatibility with the organizational culture

Contingency Theory considers that an organization’s structure and characteristics (e.g. culture, environment, technology, size or activities must fit its context if it wants to survive or be effective [42]. Although one of the major criticisms of this theory is that the concepts are not clearly defined, one of the three key dimensions generally considered is the environment. The firm’s environment consists of a network of firms that are directly or indirectly connected, establishing relationships that provide information and condition its activities and decisions. The firm must adapt itself to the characteristics of the environment in which it performs and has to compete because, the higher the coherence between them, the greater the organizational profits [154, 162]. Likewise, if a firm operates in an environment favorable to the use of the ICT, it will be more highly motivated to undertake innovative behaviors and continue using a new ICT. As a result of this continuance, the firm may improve the efficiency of its transactions and its relationships with the environment through informationsharing and communication. On the contrary, if there is no significant environmental influence, a firm will have little incentive to use ICTs [31]. It must be highlighted that the influence of the environment on a firm’s intentions and decisions may depend on what stage of the diffusion process the ICT is at [146].

The influence of the environment is especially relevant for inter-organizational ICTs which require collaborative structures and electronic partnerships for their continuance [56, 77, 168]. These ICTs involve network effects since the benefits derived from their employment grow as the number of user firms increases in the environment. Classic network effects, analyzed in individuals, assume that the environment is impersonal so they do not distinguish between the members of the network [76, 118]. Moreover, they are usually focused on the prior adoption of the ICT. In the organizational context, we consider that all members are not equally valuable to the network during ICT continuance so they influence with different intensity. Likewise, the firm obtains higher utility from interacting with the organizations in its local network, i.e. environment, due to the strong business linkages existing among them [15, 144]. We propose that the agents of the firm’s environment influence employment of and perceptions about the ICTs that support their relationships.

On the basis of Porter’s force competitive model [119], we study the effect of the environment considering four groups of agents: competitors, public administrations, customers and suppliers [18, 123, 153]. These agents can help the focal firm to improve its links with the ICT and generate positive effects derived from its joint use [173]. The study of the environment through the influence of these agents has been undertaken for other technologies in the organizational context [see, for example, 57, 84, 123]. Nevertheless, research usually applies a partial approach, focusing on the influence of the agents separately or analyzing the importance of only one or two of them. For this reason, there is limited empirical evidence regarding the overall effect of the environment and the way in which each agent contributes to it.

Competitors influence firms’ decisions about ICTs because firms that contend fiercely with their rivals employ new ICTs in order to reduce business-process costs and/or increase their efficiency, strengthening their performance and ensuring their survival [65, 109, 121]. Therefore, when an ICT is more widely diffused among competitors, firms in that industry are more likely to adopt and continue using it [152]. Public administrations can convince firms to adhere to institutional ICT expectations and policies, adopting and using a certain ICT [7, 77]. They may foment the training and maintaining of an adequate ICT workforce and give different types of aid to firms interested in implementing an ICT [139]. Finally, we consider the influence of trading partners, i.e. suppliers and customers. They exert a greater influence when there is a high degree of vertical dependence and there are inter-locking relationships among them [52]. Thus, when a firm’s dominant suppliers or customers use an ICT, the firm may follow them and adopt the ICT to show both its fitness to be a business partner and its capacity to employ the ICT in the relationship between them [141, 168].

In spite of the lack of research into e-invoicing, the influence exerted by the environment seems to determine the firm’s perception of security. If the firm’s trading partners do not consider e-invoicing to be secure, they will not be willing to exchange their confidential information, so the firm will not perceive security and will not use the ICT. On the contrary, if the environment in which the firm operates believes that the ICT is secure, it will disseminate this feature and try to convince the firm to use it in its commercial relationships. Finally, we should highlight the numerous policies of public administrations that try to demonstrate the security of e-invoicing. The EU, in its Invoicing Directive [43], established a number of security requirements connected with the firm’s practices during the use of e-invoicing. This Directive may modify user firms’ perception of e-invoicing security [73]. We formulate the following hypothesis:

## H5. The firm’s environment has a positive effect on the perceived security of e-invoicing

With respect to e-invoicing and perceived usefulness, the firm’s relationship with its environment informs it of the benefits of the ICT. This information is especially important if there is a high level of collaboration and commitment within the environment [95]. Following Zhu et al. [174], the effect of the local environmental network influences the firm’s expected and perceived benefits from the ICT [see also 93], so it will also influence perceived usefulness [94, 96]. Moreover, if environmental agents are pushing a firm to continue using einvoicing, that use will improve the relationships between them and generate positive network effects [118]. Consequently, the firm will perceive a greater usefulness of the ICT. We must also consider the communication campaigns carried out by different public administrations which have sought to improve the image of the ICT and to promote recognition of its benefits. Therefore, we propose that the influence exercised by the firm’s local environment will increase perceived usefulness [9, 103] and hypothesize the following relationship:

## H6. The firm’s environment has a positive effect on the perceived usefulness of e-invoicing

Some authors, such as Tosi and Slocum [154], concluded that the Contingency Theory fails because it does not take into consideration the role of other variables such as culture. In the organizational context, culture is defined as a complex entity of values, beliefs, behavior models, practices and symbols [62]. Applied to the ICT arena, the firm’s knowledge and expertise facilitate the acquisition of competences accumulated over time [129]. These competences make up the technological culture [130], provide information about the characteristics and importance of new technologies, and facilitate their adoption and continuance [78]. In this way, some authors try to establish what cultural factors are important in using an ICT, defining cultural archetypes that give competing views about organizational issues and determine the suitability of an ICT. Differences in these cultural factors between firms explain some of the contradictory consequences of an ICT [89].

Compatibility is the degree to which an innovation is considered consistent with existing organizational goals, values, systems and experience [140]. To be compatible with the organizational culture, an innovation must fit in with the firm’s behavioral patterns and its past and present experiences. In the ICT arena, Fuller et al. [51] claim that compatibility refers to the appropriateness of a technology for the task for which it is contemplated and that it is closely connected to firms’ experience and behavior patterns [112]. This factor shows the link between organizational culture and the continuance of an ICT so that, for a successful technology assimilation, either the ICT must fit the organizational culture or the culture must be shaped to satisfy the behavioral requirements of the ICT [20]. In recent years, numerous studies about ICTs have demonstrated that compatibility is an antecedent of other user perceptions [79, 145]. The relationship between compatibility with organizational culture and these perceptions can improve user satisfaction and lead to ICT continuance [145].

We are going to study the compatibility of e-invoicing with the organizational culture from several perspectives, including beliefs, values, and preferred work practices (e.g. existing technology infrastructure) [57, 164]. As we have already explained, the main reasons for using e-invoicing are increased efficiency and competitiveness, as well as reduced operation costs. Thus, the application of e-invoicing may be especially important for firms whose organizational culture focuses on values related to productivity [32] and on preferred work practices that try to achieve success in predetermined economic goals. These firms seek to apply ICTs that aid their management, promote their ability to maximize profits and encourage interaction with their environment, as is the case with e-invoicing. We consider that the compatibility of e-invoicing with the organizational culture modifies perceptions and encourages the implementation of the ICT.

Although some studies have tested the influence of compatibility and perceived security on the use of an ICT separately [88, 133], the relationship between them has rarely been tested. A high level of compatibility of the ICT with the organizational culture leads the firm to acquire a greater understanding of it and decreases the risk associated with its employment [71], allowing the firm to properly value the characteristics of the ICT and to forget its concerns about security [87]. Kim et al. [79] consider that the firm’s usual transaction procedures (similar to preferred work practices) influence the perceived security of an ICT. For e-invoicing, if the user firm experiences more compatibility, it will identify security mechanisms inherent to the ICT, and perceived security will improve. Therefore, we formulate the following relationship:

H7. Compatibility of e-invoicing with the organizational culture has a positive effect on perceived security

The effect of compatibility on usefulness has been analyzed in several studies focused on inter-organizational ICTs such as EDI [5, 69], e-logistic systems [156] and ERP [145]. The greater the compatibility of the ICT with the firm’s existing culture and systems, the greater the chance of the firm valuing it positively [19], and the greater the perceived usefulness [145, 156]. The compatibility of e-invoicing with the organizational culture means that the user firm will appreciate greater usefulness, since it will be able to recognize the benefits of employing this ICT. For e-invoicing, compatibility has more influence on usefulness when the firm’s main objective is to maximize profits, i.e. an organizational culture called productivity [32], since the use of e-invoicing allows it to obtain better economic results. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H8. Compatibility of e-invoicing with organizational culture has a positive effect on perceived usefulness

## 4. Methodology

We analyze the case of Spain because this country has an adoption rate of e-invoicing similar to the European average and because its public administration considers the diffusion of this ICT to be one of its main technological objectives. We focus on only one country to avoid the inclusion of distorting effects in the study. Firstly, we consider that discrepancies in national regulations on e-invoicing may generate differences in the use of this ICT between firms of several nationalities. Secondly, we bear in mind that previous research has demonstrated that cultural features may modify the effect exerted by user firms’ perceptions of an ICT [34, 143]. Thirdly, we consider that the pluralism of e-invoicing solutions in Europe may also prevent the comparison of firms from different countries. Analyzing only one country allows us to delimit the type of software used by the studied firms, ensuring that all of them apply a similar e-invoicing solution. We require all the firms to use either the free standardized software developed by the Spanish public administration, called Facturae, or any other software developed ad hoc for the firm, based on and compatible with Facturae. We do not analyze firms that use EDI since, although this ICT allows firms to exchange invoices, it is too different from other e-invoicing solutions.

Like TAM research at business level [4, 61, 172] and some academic surveys of firms [e.g. 46, 58, 86], this study applies the key informant method. We focus on the perceptions and opinions of managers responsible for the decision to adopt ICT who were requested to answer on behalf of the firm. To ensure respondents’ representativeness, clear and concise statements describing the purpose of the study were provided at the beginning of the questionnaire [21, 109, 125]. According to Lu and Yeh [99], this method achieves a more objective and accurate picture of business reality since the manager’s evaluations of the ICT make it possible to understand the firm’s global decisions and explain why an ICT is adopted and later used.

The key informant method takes a different approach to the employee-based focus for analyzing ICT acceptance by firms, which has been applied to address the use of e-mail, EDI and ERP [4]. We consider that the study of employees may not be adequate because their

#

perceptions depend on their specific conditions and objectives (to keep their jobs, the possibility of promotion or achieving greater prestige), which are very different to those of the firm (to maximize profits or long-term market value). At the same time, the fact that employees can be obliged to use a certain ICT may mean that their perceptions are unreliable predictors of the firm’s intention to continue using it and, consequently, may be rejected. With the key informant method, we eliminate the effect generated by the individual circumstances of each employee and the deviations derived from the coercive character of the employee's use, allowing us to study the manager’s perceptions which determine the firm’s global decisions.

To guarantee the representativeness of the sample, a Computer Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI) survey was carried out, with random quota sampling according to sector and size criteria. A total of 1,443 telephone calls were made and 1,193 valid surveys were obtained. Our sample is representative of the distribution of Spanish firms with respect to the cited criteria. Sample error is 2.76% (95.5% confidence level). The questionnaire had a first common part for all firms and a second part which included different questions depending on whether the firm was an adopter or non-adopter of e-invoicing. From the surveys received, we selected the 102 firms that had sent and created e-invoices by themselves, employed software compatible with Facturae, and included an advance digital signature. After the refining process, a total of 100 valid cases were analyzed, giving a response rate of 7%. This response rate of firms that use e-invoicing is similar to the adoption rate in Spain at the time of study (6.1%, according to Telefonica, S.A.), so that we can confirm that the size of the sample is adequate and that the firms analyzed represent the population tested. Since the research of Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer [39], there has been an emphasis on the need to adequately determine the formative or reflective character of the constructs, due to the serious consequences that an incorrect specification would entail [66, 101]. Formative indicators are those which cause a construct to be created (or formed), while reflective indicators are consequences of the construct. Jarvis et al. [66] provide a set of practical guidelines to distinguish between the two types. Following these guidelines, we consider that the environment constitutes a formative construct since indicators, i.e. groups of agents, are causing the effect of the environment rather than being

#

caused by it. Each indicator conceptually captures a specific dimension of the construct’s domain so, taken together, they can be considered adequate for empirical prediction [130]. Thus, dropping a formative indicator may omit a unique part of this construct and change the meaning of the concept. It is important to highlight that, in formative constructs, including measures that refer to the same dimension should be avoided since they would be highly correlated, not improve the explanatory capacity of the construct and generate multicollinearity problems [17, 40]. For these reasons, we have considered only one indicator for each environmental agent. Previous research has already considered formative factors to measure environmental aspects such as supply market dynamism, environmental pressure and environmental controls [31, 157].

Initially, we calculated the mean of the satisfaction factor (5.8) with the aim of checking whether user firms were satisfied with the results obtained by e-invoicing. The mean of this factor is higher than the average of the scale, i.e. 4, so we can state that, in general terms, the ICT has fulfilled the firms’ expectations. Thus, we eliminate the possibility that the firms intentions were only due to an external coactive effect, i.e. “I do not want to use it but I have to do so”, since, in that case, firms would not manifest their satisfaction with the ICT. The next step was to test the influence and relationships between factors included in the model to explain satisfaction and continuance intentions. To do this, we apply the Partial Least Squares (PLS) technique to measure the structural model.

PLS methodology is a components-based structural equations modeling technique similar to regression and simultaneously models the structural and measurement paths. It does not assume equal weights for all indicators of a scale and allows each indicator to vary in how much it contributes to the composite score of the latent variable. Thus, indicators with weaker relationships to related indicators and the latent construct are given lower weightings. In this regard, PLS is preferable to techniques such as regression which assumes error-free measurement [23]. In recent years, the PLS procedure has been gaining importance and use among ICT researchers [92, 131, 160]. According to Chin et al. [26], this is because, firstly, PLS has a great ability to model latent constructs under conditions of non-normality and, secondly, it is appropriate in the case of small sample sizes due to the partial nature of the estimation procedure in which only a portion of the model is involved at any one time [24].

## 6. Theoretical discussion and contributions to the literature

Although TAM has been applied to explain ICT adoption in different domains for several decades, there is still a gap related to the analysis of continuance in the organizational context. The present research tries to fill this gap by adapting a behavioral model, integrating contingency factors to explain the firm’s continuance intentions to use an inter-organizational ICT. Likewise, it provides a joint vision of continuance that, as far as we know, has not been previously considered. Findings demonstrate that the environment and the compatibility of the ICT with the organizational culture directly determine the firm’s perceptions and, indirectly, its satisfaction with the ICT and its continuance intentions. It is important to highlight that we have checked that findings are stable for firms of different size, financial performance and ICT level. Like Teo and Ranganathan [148], we conclude that, once firms have acquired experience with the inter-organizational ICT, there are no significant differences between them.

The present research demonstrates that the environment and compatibility with organizational culture are necessary but not sufficient conditions for the use of an interorganizational ICT. On the one hand, it is true that contingency factors are necessary to influence the user firm’s behavior because, if its environment does not encourage the employment of the ICT or if this ICT is not compatible with its organizational culture, the firm will not perceive usefulness and security and, therefore, its satisfaction will be lower. On the other hand, contingency factors are not sufficient conditions per se to improve the firm’s intentions since they must be accompanied by favorable perceptions of the ICT to modify satisfaction. Therefore, this study contributes to the literature on the ICT field, identifying the role of contingency factors as antecedents of perceptions during continuance and demonstrating the importance of perceptions as key mediator variables that lead to continuance intentions.

In spite of the significant importance of environment, its inclusion in behavioral models has been scarce because they usually omit influences related to the firm’s local network and the implementation of business partners (e.g. see e-mail in 75; v-voice in 27). The consideration of the environment is especially relevant for inter-organizational ICTs since they require the participation of at least two parties. If a firm decides to use an inter-organizational ICT, this decision influences firms in the environment that do not yet use the ICT and also benefits others that already employ it, because the latter can then operate with one more firm. Thus, as more firms of the local network apply the ICT and make greater efforts to encourage its diffusion, users will be under increased pressure and have better perceptions about the ICT. We propose that continuance in the organizational context is an inherently social process, influenced by business-related networks (see Gibbs, 55, and Suarez, 144, who study social networks in the individual acceptance of ICT). Therefore, future research on ICT continuance should consider the influence of the firm’s environment since, although its behavior depends on its own evaluations about the intrinsic attributes of the ICT, these evaluations are conditioned by the local network in which it operates. It is interesting to highlight that the effect of the environment cannot be applied to systems in which the market associated with the user is not relevant for continuance. For example, the choice of using a system internally developed in a factory for controlling specific production machinery will be affected by the specific production machinery but the environment will probably not play a decisive role in the diffusion of the system.

The formative measure applied to test the influence of the environment on e-invoicing continuance allows us to discuss the overall effect of this factor but also the specific relevance of each agent that integrates it. This measurement approach differs from that applied by other organizational studies that are focused on reflective factors and analyze one or two agents, which are noteworthy for a specific ICT [77, 152, 168]. It must be borne in mind that the nature of the formative construct can change from model to model depending on what this construct is predicting [63, 165]. To avoid interpretational confusion, the comparison of our findings with previous research on ICT should be undertaken with special care, so we focus our discussion only on the most relevant agent within the environment. For e-invoicing, competitors have the greatest influence on user the firm’s perceptions. If the firm belongs to a more competitive environment, it will feel a greater need to use e-invoicing so as not to lose competitive efficiency. These findings are similar to those obtained by To and Ngai [152] and Waarts et al. [161] for organizational early adoption of e-commerce and ERP, respectively. They conclude that competitive pressure influences firm behavior more than relationships with suppliers and customers. Nevertheless, these results differ from the conclusions drawn by Teo et al. [149], who determine that supplier and customer pressures are the most important factors in financial EDI adoption. Similarities among all these inter-organizational ICTs lead us to suppose that these discrepancies may come from the stage of the diffusion process the ICT was at [146]. E-invoicing, e-commerce and ERP were in the early stages when they were studied while EDI had been operating for several decades when it was studied, so it was at a later stage. During the early stages, the firm may receive a great influence from its competitors since the use of the ICT is considered as an opportunity to achieve competitive advantages (pioneer advantages). Nevertheless, during a later stage, the ICT is not new, pioneer advantages are disappeared and many business partners are already users. These partners may influence the firm to establish beneficial relationships through the ICT. We can not make definitive conclusions about the effect of the stages in the ICT diffusion process so we propose that further studies should be carried out.

If the ICT is compatible with the organizational culture, the user firm will be more able to evaluate its characteristics and will have better perceptions derived from its employment. Following the classification of Cooper [32] for cultural archetypes, we consider that organizational values and work practices oriented towards improving productivity promote the use of inter-organizational ICTs and, in particular, of e-invoicing because these ICTs aim to improve business results, reduce costs and maximize the benefits derived from the firm’s relationships. This study obtains similar results for e-invoicing to those observed in small and medium-sized firms for ICTs such as EDI and ERP [69, 145, 161] which increase their efficiency in the execution of business activities as long as the ICT correctly fits with their values and practices. For the same level of ICT investment, strong compatibility with the organizational culture may lead the firm to better appreciate the security of an ICT and to perceive superior effectiveness (usefulness). Therefore, the development of an interorganizational ICT based on compatibility will improve perceptions and, indirectly, the firm’s satisfaction and intention to continue using the ICT.

During the continuance stage of an inter-organizational ICT, the firm’s perceptions have to overcome its initial expectations in order to achieve satisfaction after use. Firstly, the ICT must be perceived as secure since firms use it to exchange commercial information with other firms. It must guarantee the original content and ensure that is transmitted correctly. Secondly, the ICT must be perceived as useful since this perception is the most important driver of satisfaction. These results are consistent with other investigations into technology acceptance by individuals [53]. Likewise, in the continuance stage, when users have gained knowledge and experience with the ICT, perceived usefulness exerts a greater influence on the intentions to continue using the ICT and becomes the key determinant of acceptance. Perceived ease of use has no direct linkage with usage and operates indirectly through usefulness [36, 171]. As Benbasat and Barki [10] state, “perceived usefulness is a key, if not the key, influential belief influencing use”, so future research on the business environment should analyze this perception to explain the user firm’s continuance behavior.

Finally, our research corroborates that as a firm’s satisfaction with an interorganizational ICT increases, so does its intention to continue using the ICT. Satisfaction is derived from knowledge acquired during the use of the ICT. Consequently, its predictive power regarding the user’s intentions and behavior is higher than that achieved by other factors such as attitude. According to Bhattacherjee [13], users’ attitude is formed potentially via second-hand information from other users, popular media or other sources, sources that may be biased. For future research, we propose to replace attitude with satisfaction to study the continuance of inter-organizational ICTs because the latter better explains the future behavior of user firms. Moreover, satisfaction can reflect ICT performance when the user firm’s behavior does not only depend on its own will. In cases in which network effects or external coactive forces exist, the firm can not just stop using the ICT once it starts, so satisfaction may reflect some aspects which are not contained in continuance intentions.

## 7. Business implications, limitations and future lines of research

Inter-organizational ICTs can evolve from being office tools to become organizational resources for business strategy. Nevertheless, it is fundamental to understand the drivers that

#

promote continuance to design correct marketing and management strategies. Our study has several implications for firms, managers, providers of ICTs and public administrations that can improve the continuance of inter-organizational ICTs, in general, and e-invoicing, in particular:

\- Providers of inter-organizational ICTs should bear in mind the importance of perceived usefulness and security for promoting the user’s continuance intentions. Regarding perceived security, inter-organizational ICTs should be accompanied by a security policy. This policy guarantees data protection and the correct transmission of the information, clarifying security issues that are important for the agents with whom the firm interacts and presupposes a trust framework between firms. A security policy allows the firm to gain confidence in the different applications of the new ICT, thus improving its level of satisfaction. In order to promote perceived usefulness, the advantages of the ICT should be adequately communicated by providers. In this way, firms will know what this ICT really is and consider it an opportunity instead of a threat. It should be taken into account that ease of use accelerates the learning process and means that the benefits obtained will be more highly valued. Providers should design systems that are intuitive and effortless, and that make it easy to acquire the necessary knowledge. Moreover, establishing standards based on existing frequently-used business practices, instead of developing proprietary solutions, and coordinating the system applied may also improve perceived usefulness [126, 163]. These standards will facilitate implementation, encourage satisfaction and increase continuance intentions.

\- The firm perceives and uses an inter-organizational ICT in accordance with the environment in which it carries out its activity. Whether or not the firm’s environment applies an ICT can become a fundamental requirement for trading with suppliers, offering customers an efficient service, competing effectively and, consequently, keeping the firm in the market. Local network effects inherent to inter-organizational ICTs enhance the value and benefits obtained by user firms, so these firms will encourage their suppliers and customers to use the ICT. Thus, user firms will act as the agents of change within each sector, building support, overcoming resistance and creating a breeding ground for the development of the ICT. Providers interested in achieving a greater diffusion of an inter-organizational ICT should launch it in an area where there is a large base of established users and then develop the market by gradually including more application areas. Another way to increase ICT diffusion would be to obtain a critical mass of users in the market through incentives and, thus, attract the remainder to also adopt the system [45].

#

Regarding e-invoicing, the influence of the environment on adoption and continuance has been demonstrated in sectors where a leader firm has influenced its partners. This would be the case of Carrefour Spain. This firm phoned all its suppliers and asked them to adopt einvoicing. In seven months, it increased the use of this ICT from 25 to 1,622 users (93% of the total). Moreover, Carrefour complemented this action with the design of a website that seeks to facilitate operability of the ICT.

\- Public administrations play a key role in improving the firm’s perceptions of an inter organizational ICT and continuance intentions of using it. They must standardize the regulation and characteristics of the ICT, guarantee aspects relative to its security and present the advantages of the ICT over the alternatives. In this way, user firms will improve their perceptions of the ICT and non users will be more willing to adopt it. Moreover, in their attempt to increase the chance of success, public administrations should aid firms with innovative structures and cultures which are already aware of and using the ICT. They could offer grants and guidance to user firms, give them priority in public contracts and, in short, support them to encourage the use of this ICT.

\- The compatibility of an inter-organizational ICT with organizational culture means that the firm can appreciate advantages that arise as a result of its employment and that it will be more satisfied. Different organizational cultures require different type of information [155], process that information differently [150] and, thus, evaluate the application of each ICT differently. Organizational culture has been acquired over the years and conditions technological knowledge and future business development. Management support is fundamental in obtaining this culture and knowledge because it is the manager who must decide to invest resources in training to facilitate ICT diffusion. This training will help to improve perceptions about an inter-organizational ICT from the beginning of its implementation, which, in turn, will increase the overall satisfaction of the firm, its performance and effectiveness. Moreover, providers of inter-organizational ICTs should attempt to enhance the perception of the ICT as usable and adaptable to the peculiarities of different organizations and sectors.

As with any investigation, our study suffers from certain limitations which need to be mentioned and resolved in the future. Firstly, it should be emphasized that we have omitted various aspects related to the dynamic process of innovation diffusion which may influence the results. Secondly, we should mention that the use of cross-sectional data means that our analysis lacks a temporal dimension, so we cannot observe the evolution of firms’ perceptions relating to the technology acceptance. Therefore, in future research, we want to test the model presented here over a continuous period, thereby determining the variation in the importance of the perceptions in successive years.

In summary, this research has attempted to adapt behavioral models to study the continuance of inter-organizational ICTs, focusing on e-invoicing. It analyzes the influence of contingency factors on the firm’s continuance intentions, considering the firm’s perceptions and satisfaction with the ICT as mediators of this influence. Findings demonstrate that, during the continuance stage, contingency factors such as the environment and the compatibility of the ICT with the organizational culture precede and explain the firm’s perceptions of usefulness and security. Moreover, these perceptions determine satisfaction and continuance intentions. We conclude that, in order to study and achieve the continuance of interorganizational ICTs, behavioral models should contemplate not only the firm’s perceptions but also contingency factors.
