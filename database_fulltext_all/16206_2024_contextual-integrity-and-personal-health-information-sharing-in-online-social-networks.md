# Contextual Integrity and Personal Health Information Sharing in Online Social Networks

Han Li,<sup>1</sup> Xin (Robert) Luo,<sup>2</sup> Rathindra Sarathy,<sup>3</sup> Heng Xu<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of New Mexico, USA, hanli@unm.edu <sup>2</sup>University of New Mexico, USA, xinluo@unm.edu <sup>3</sup>Oklahoma State University, USA, rathin.sarathy@okstate.edu <sup>4</sup>University of Florida, USA, heng.xu@ufl.edu

## Abstract

Many online social network (OSN) users exchange health-related information on OSNs to manage their health, obtain support, and offer assistance to others facing similar medical challenges. Nonetheless, OSN users are concerned about the privacy of their health information. Extant studies on online information privacy have largely resorted to a calculus perspective, focusing on the costs and benefits of information exchange with commercial vendors. Such a perspective, however, fails to capture the nuanced flows of personal health information (PHI) on OSNs, which involves more than two parties. To address this gap and further advance the research on information privacy, this paper embraces the theory of contextual integrity to explore the distinctive contextual idiosyncrasies of PHI flow on OSNs. In particular, we examine how context-specific values and norms affect OSN users’ personal privacy norms and their PHI disclosure intentions. Our theory-driven empirical results suggest that contextual values and normative expectations related to technology control, legislation, and peer users collectively influence OSN users’ personal privacy norms. In addition, perceived personal value and perceived altruistic value serve as motivators to promote PHI disclosure intentions, working in tandem with personal privacy norms. The key findings of the study also have important implications for policymakers and OSN providers in the context of OSN-mediated healthcare through (1) advancing our understanding of the complex dynamics of PHI flow on OSNs and (2) designing effective information privacy measures (i.e., policies, technical features, and programs) to uphold contextual integrity and nurture the development of patient-centered healthcare paradigms.

Keywords: Online Social Networks, Health Information Privacy, Contextual Integrity, Contextual Informational Norms and Values, Personal Privacy Norms

## 1 Introduction

Presently, the vast majority of US internet users engage with at least one online social networking site (OSN) (Statista Research Department, 2021), with many of them seeking and sharing various healthrelated information, such as symptoms, diagnoses, feelings about their health conditions, medications, and reviews of medical treatments, doctors, and hospitals (Fox, 2011; Kordzadeh & Warren, 2017). According to the 2019 Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS), approximately 17% of US adults shared health information on OSNs in the past 12 months (Chou et al. 2021). Such information sharing not only allows patients to better understand their health and manage their health conditions but also to receive emotional support from peers and friends. Health information transmitted through OSNs can also help clinicians and researchers gain insights into medications and diseases, discover new knowledge, and improve healthcare delivery by facilitating communication with patients or caregivers. Notably, the vast majority of social media users believe that their personal health information (PHI) should be utilized to assist others facing similar medical conditions (Grajales et al. 2014).

Notwithstanding the numerous benefits associated with sharing health information on OSNs, such a practice could expose users to various perils, including the potential for unintentional data breaches, unauthorized disclosures, and the misuse of their PHI by various entities, including identity thieves, employers, and insurance companies, among others (Li 2013). Consequently, patients and their caregivers are increasingly concerned about the privacy of their personal information, which represents a significant barrier preventing OSN users from disclosing their PHI. For instance, a survey of PatientsLikeMe members revealed that they are concerned about insurance companies using their personal health records to deny healthcare benefits or employers using them to make recruiting decisions (Grajales et al. 2014). Similarly, another survey of general social media users suggests that the majority of social media users with medical conditions are willing to share their health data only if their privacy is protected (Conn 2014).

At present, there is a dearth of privacy research that explores the effect of the complex contextual factors shaping the intricate flows of PHI on OSNs. The disclosure of PHI on OSNs poses an elevated risk to individual privacy due to the inherently sensitive nature of health information and the multitude of information recipients, such as peer users, medical researchers, and insurance companies, on open-ended social media platforms. To counteract infringements on individual privacy engendered by disruptive technologies and practices, the Privacy Bill of Rights (2012) explicitly calls for “respect for context” in which consumers exercise their discretion in providing personal data. It is important to note, however, that the exploration of these contextual factors is devoid of sufficient theory-driven contextualization (Xu and Zhang 2022) in the extant information-privacy literature. To address this shortcoming, we draw on the theory of contextual integrity (CI) (Nissenbaum 2009) to identify specific contextual factors that influence individuals’ personal privacy norms about sharing PHI and the disclosure of PHI on OSNs. By doing so, we seek to provide a comprehensive and nuanced understanding of how context-specific values and norms impact individuals’ personal privacy norms and their decision-making processes vis-à-vis the disclosure of PHI on OSNs.

While the conventional privacy paradigm is based on control over the flow of personal information (Bélanger and James 2020), CI theory offers a new perspective that defines information privacy as the appropriate flow of personal information, which is also called contextual integrity (Nissenbaum 2009). Violation of contextual integrity is also labeled as a violation of information privacy. Contextual integrity is achieved when individuals deem the flow of personal information as appropriate within a specific context involving specific contextual parameters, such as actors (i.e., information subject, sender, receiver), the types of information, and the entrenched informational norms, among others. Notably, sharing information is not equivalent to relinquishing one’s information privacy if the flow is deemed appropriate by an individual, such as sharing one’s PHI with a doctor or others who suffer from a similar health issue. This notion of information privacy framed as contextual integrity emphasizes the importance of context and has a normative root from the judgment concerning the appropriateness of a personal information flow. Therefore, an individual’s judgment about the appropriateness of a personal information flow in a specific context is a personal normative belief regarding the appropriateness of a contextual information flow and is labeled as “personal privacy norms” in this paper. Personal norms refer to “an individual’s conviction that acting in a certain way is right or wrong” (Bamberg et al. 2007, p.191).

Benthall et al. (2017) highlight that CI theory is “a philosophical theory” and call for a more actionable version that provides “a theoretical account of how social spheres [or contexts] relate to sociotechnical situations” (p. 56). One such sociotechnical situation that warrants investigation is the disclosure of PHI on OSNs. However, there is currently a scarcity of empirical studies that identify and examine the effect of contextual informational norms and values in this context. To address this important gap, our study aims to (1) map abstract CI theory to a concrete sociotechnical privacy situation and (2) identify and validate the specific contextual informational norms and values. To advance this line of research, we propose three key research questions that drive this study: (1) What contextual informational norms and values influence OSN users’ personal privacy norms in sharing their PHI? (2) How do these contextual norms and values influence OSN users’ PHI disclosure intentions? (3) How do the motivators of PHI disclosure intentions differ from the purpose of disclosure, i.e., inrole behavior such as seeking help from others versus the altruistic behavior of helping others?

To answer the above research questions, we developed a comprehensive research model based on CI theory to identify contextual informational norms and values influencing personal privacy norms and PHI disclosure on OSNs. The research model was empirically validated using an online survey of 513 OSN users. Our theory-driven empirical results suggest that contextual values and normative expectations related to technology control, legislation, and peer users collectively influence OSN users’ personal privacy norms. In addition, perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value motivate PHI disclosure intentions, along with personal privacy norms.

Importantly, our study offers significant contributions to the literature on information privacy. We undertake an initial effort to utilize CI theory to investigate the complicated flow of PHI on (1) OSNs characterized by sensitive information and nondyadic information flow to peers and other stakeholders and (2) OSNs that cut across the contexts of healthcare and social networks. In essence, our study paves a new epistemological avenue for future research to develop a contextual understanding of information privacy through the use of contextual informational norms and values. This is also one of the first studies to explore the lens of contextual integrity to explain privacy behaviors, validating the distinct yet related roles of personal privacy norms and contextual values in driving individuals’ privacy behaviors. Moreover, our study has practical implications for policymakers and OSN providers seeking to design effective and tailored information privacy measures (i.e., policies, technical features, and programs) that align with users’ values and support their personal privacy norms. Ultimately, our study advances the understanding of information privacy in complex sociotechnical environments and offers actionable insights for stakeholders to safeguard sensitive information while promoting effective information sharing.

## 2 Literature Review and Theoretical Foundation

## 2.1 Prior Research on Personal Information Sharing on OSNs

To advance the research on information privacy in the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, it is crucial to differentiate the sharing of personal information from that of nonpersonal information. The former is a type of privacy behavior, while the latter is not since the essence of information privacy pertains to the flow of personal or called private information instead of nonpersonal information. Following the definition by the European Union Directive, personal information is related to an identifiable person who can be identified directly or indirectly “by factors specific to his physical, physiological, mental, economic, cultural or social identity” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.4). As OSN users care about their online identities established over time through the curation of their profiles and other posts (Wu, 2019)

and because PHI disclosed on OSNs could be linked to their online identity, we argue that PHI disclosure on OSNs is one type of privacy behavior, even if PHI disclosed online cannot be easily linked to an individual’s real offline identity.

Given our focus on the issues of information privacy arising from PHI disclosure, we delve into a review of extant studies of personal information disclosure on OSNs in this subsection. The literature summary in Appendix A reveals that the disclosure of personal information on OSNs has been investigated through a plethora of theoretical lenses, including privacy calculus theory (Krasnova et al., 2010; Lin & Armstrong, 2019), communication privacy management theory (Lin & Armstrong, 2019), and identity theory (Wu, 2019), among others. However, most of these studies have primarily focused on antecedents such as personal benefits, privacy risk/concern, trust, and general control, while neglecting the crucial role of contextual factors in shaping individuals’ personal information disclosure behavior on OSNs. As such, this represents a significant gap that limits our understanding of the privacy paradox phenomenon and hinders the practical relevance of research findings in addressing the privacy challenges arising from disruptive technologies and practices, such as the disclosure of PHI on OSNs, which spans the domains of healthcare and social networks.

Additionally, the extant IS privacy literature, including the studies examining personal information disclosure on OSNs in Appendix A, has mainly centered on the effect of personal interests through privacy calculus (Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Choi et al. 2018; Dinev and Hart 2006; Kordzadeh and Warren 2017; Teubner and Flath 2019; Xu et al. 2009), with scant attention paid to examining the effect of altruistic value. Indeed, many individuals disclose PHI on OSNs not only out of self-interest but also to benefit others through improved public health management, for example. With the rise of OSNs, it is critical to transcend the traditional, egoistic individual interests of privacy calculus and investigate how altruistic value, together with other contextual factors, influence individual privacy judgment and PHI disclosure on OSNs. This approach will enable a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the underlying factors that drive individuals’ decisions to disclose PHI. Therefore, we embrace CI theory to address these research gaps by incorporating the effect of contextual informational norms and values that include both personal and altruistic value, reflecting the specific context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, such as helping others better manage their personal health. Section 2.3 provides a more detailed explanation of CI theory, including its paradigmatic shift in reframing the concept of information privacy as contextual integrity as well as its core constructs, relationships, and tenets.

## 2.2 Prior Research on Health Information Sharing on OSNs

Based on our best knowledge and the findings presented in Appendix A, there exists no theory-based research on PHI disclosure on OSNs. Subsequently, we examine studies explaining the disclosure of general health information that is not personal on OSNs in this subsection. The existing studies have often adopted the theory of planned behavior, the uses and gratifications theory, and/or the social cognitive theory to understand the effects of sharing attitude, behavioral control, self-efficacy, subjective norms, and various gratifiers such as entertainment and status-seeking (see details in Appendix A). The gratifiers center around the personal benefits to be gained from sharing health information while ignoring the effect of altruistic value. Additionally, as expected, these studies do not consider the effects of privacy-related factors due to their context of sharing nonpersonal health information. Interestingly, the research models in these studies often incorporate subjective norms to understand the effects of normative expectations from important others, such as family and friends, about whether to share health information over WeChat (Hong et al. 2021; Wu and Kuang 2021). Along a similar vein, we note the importance of examining the expectations of other OSN members. Therefore, to fit into the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, we utilize community norms to capture the normative expectation from most OSN users about the appropriateness of sharing PHI on OSNs and investigate its role in shaping individual OSN users’ privacy judgments and their subsequent privacy behaviors.

## 2.3 Information Privacy and the Theory of Contextual Integrity

In prior research, privacy has predominantly been coined as a form of control (Nissenbaum 2009). The control perspective represents the prevailing stance in IS privacy literature (Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Bélanger and James 2020; Cheng et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2011a), where information privacy is defined as “the claim … to determine for themselves when, how, and to what extent information about them is communicated to others” (Westin 1967, Page 7). Although the control perspective captures an essential aspect of information privacy, it disregards the collection of values associated with privacy (Liu et al. 2023), since there exists no absolute privacy. The right to control one’s personal information may conflict with general, as well as context-specific, moral values, such as the right of human society to learn about certain diseases.

In light of this, Nissenbaum (2009) proposes the theory of contextual integrity (CI), in which contextual integrity serves as a benchmark for information privacy. Table 1 summarizes the key terms of CI theory. Contextual integrity is defined with respect to both contextual informational norms and values. First, it is “defined in terms of [contextual] informational norms: It is preserved when informational norms are respected and violated when informational norms are breached” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.140). CI theory additionally augments the prior definition of CI by incorporating the role of values, which may supersede the role of contextual information norms. CI is preserved when the violation of contextual information norms can be generally justified “insofar as they support the attainment of general as well as context-based values, ends, and purposes” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.181). “Novel flows of information might trump entrenched flows when these novel flows are more effective in promoting general and context-relative values, ends, and purposes” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.232).

Based on the discussion above, CI theory goes beyond the traditional control perspective of privacy since control no longer defines information privacy. Instead, control is recognized as one among many possible transmission principles of contextual informational norms in CI theory (Nissenbaum, 2009). Contextual informational norms are much richer and include transmission principles and other contextual parameters, such as the information type, sender, recipient, and subject. Additionally, the availability of control is neither a universal transmission principle governing information flows nor a sufficient determinant of whether contextual integrity is upheld or violated. This is exemplified in the context of OSNs, where the disruptive nature of social media platforms has diminished OSN users ability to control the access and use of their personal information disclosed on OSNs. Yet, this loss of control may not necessarily be perceived as a privacy violation by OSN users if the platform’s benefits, such as relationship building, are deemed more valuable.

CI theory has been applied to address privacy challenges arising from disruptive technologies to law and public policy (Nissenbaum 2009) and to identify entrenched contextual informational norms (Bloom and Emery 2022; Martin and Nissenbaum 2016). Due to the consideration of rich contextual parameters, CI theory also provides a formal structure for identifying technical configurations that support people’s expected privacy norms.

This has enabled the design of actionable technical measures to meet people’s privacy expectations across various application areas such as artificial intelligence (AI) and human-computer interaction (HCI) (Benthall et al. 2017; Criado and Such 2015). These studies have extended CI theory—an abstract philosophical framework—to be more actionable to enforce contextual integrity. Despite the extension, these studies tend to have a narrow technical angle while overlooking the broader implications of values and purposes as well as the diverse social roles of actors within a context, such as the roles of powerful third-party users on OSNs (Benthall et al. 2017).

To properly employ CI theory in empirical research, it is important to highlight two core tenets of the theory. One is that the contexts of information flow are “structured social settings characterized by canonical activities, roles, relationships, power structures, norms (or rules), and internal values (goals, ends, purposes).” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.132). Some common examples of contexts are education, healthcare, the workplace, and friendship, among others. Certain contexts are not mutually exclusive and may coexist for a given information flow. For example, PHI sharing on OSNs spans the contexts of healthcare and social networks. The norms and values of both contexts could influence individuals’ decisions to share PHI on OSNs.

For PHI sharing on OSNs, the context of the information flow could be characterized as personal health information (information types) shared with peers (intended information recipients) on OSNs. As information privacy has enormous variability at the individual level (Nissenbaum 2009), it is of paramount importance to examine the contextual factors that influence individuals’ personal privacy norms. Therefore, we position information senders as the central actors in order to understand their personal privacy norms. We are interested in how personal privacy norms are influenced by contextual informational norms, values, and personal interests. The subjects of PHI sharing are implicitly presumed to be patients in this study. Besides peers, information made public on OSNs may also be used by other interested stakeholders, such as medical researchers, commercial advertisers, and insurance companies. Although these third-party users may not be the directly intended information recipients, they may harm subjects or amplify “undesirable inequalities in status, power, and wealth.” (Nissenbaum 2004, p.155). Therefore, in line with the suggestion by Nissenbaum (2004), we stress the existence of power users (e.g., commercial advertisers and insurance companies) who could glean undue benefits from PHI shared on OSNs and highlight the need for government regulations to control the flow of PHI. For the purposes of PHI disclosure, we are interested in both in-role disclosures to seek support from others and altruistic disclosure to help others. Besides information types, actors, and purposes, we also underline the salient role of technological platforms in defining the context of PHI flow on OSNs due to the quickly evolving technologies for information sharing and protection.

The second core tenet is that contextual information norms and values are two fundamental building blocks of CI theory that jointly shape an individual’s personal privacy norms about the appropriateness of a contextual information flow. Contextual informational norms may be explicitly codified as governmental regulations or laws, such as HIPAA in the healthcare context (Nissenbaum 2019). They may also be implicit, reflecting entrenched expectations of most users about an information-sharing practice. A practice that violates contextual informational norms will raise a red flag signaling a potential privacy violation (Nissenbaum 2009). For example, many OSN users deem it appropriate to share their PHI with others with similar health issues. However, OSN providers sharing such data with commercial companies for targeted advertisement would raise a red flag for privacy violation and reduce personal privacy norms because the practice disrupts the contextual informational norms regarding intended information recipients. Commercial companies are the intended audience in the context of commerce but not healthcare.

Besides contextual informational norms, values are important touchstones influencing people’s personal privacy norms since “values are crucial, defining features of contexts” (Nissenbaum 2009, p.134). Information practices could not be meaningfully evaluated without referring to contextual values. The legitimacy of a given information flow reflects both general (e.g., public health and national security) and context-specific values (e.g., the altruistic value of helping peer OSN users in the context of PHI disclosure).

## 3 Research Model

Based on the core tenets of CI theory illustrated in Figure 1, we submit that the contextual informational norms and values of information flows are important contextual factors influencing personal privacy norms. They could take different forms in different information privacy contexts. In the following subsections, we further introduce a comprehensive research model (Figure 2) to elaborate on these driving forces and their effects on PHI disclosure intentions on OSNs.

From Figure 2, personal privacy norms serve as the central tipping point at which contextual norms and perceived altruistic value influence privacy decisions (Figure 2). Besides personal privacy norms, PHI disclosure intentions are directly enhanced by perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value. The model disentangles the two types of PHI disclosure and differentiates their nomological networks such that perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value exert distinct influences on the two types of PHI disclosure intentions.

## 3.1 Personal Health Information Disclosure Intentions

Prior IS studies have explored different privacy decisions and behaviors in healthcare settings, such as the adoption of electronic health records (Angst and Agarwal 2009) and stand-alone personal health record systems (Li et al. 2014), as well as the intention to disclose PHI (Anderson and Agarwal 2011) and share genomic data (Thiebes et al. 2017). Anderson and Agarwal (2011) found that the purpose of intended use specified by requesting stakeholders, such as hospitals and pharmaceutical companies, moderates the effect of privacy concern on PHI disclosure intentions, such that the negative effects of privacy concern are attenuated when information is requested to facilitate patient care. In a Delphi study by Thiebes et al. (2017), altruistic reasons (e.g., general research and medical research) and personal benefits were identified as the top two motivators for the disclosure of genomic data.

In light of the findings in these prior studies and recognizing the dearth of understanding of purposes of information flows from the perspective of disclosing stakeholders (i.e., information senders), we further advance the literature by examining two types of health information disclosure intentions: disclosure to seek help from others and disclosure to help others. The former is more egoistic, while the latter represents altruistic behavior that individual OSN users intend to perform to benefit others rather than themselves. To seek help from other OSN users, people may choose to disclose a variety of health information, such as lifestyle, health conditions, and medical information, among others. Likewise, OSN users can help others understand their health issues by providing pertinent health resources, knowledge, and recommendations. The differentiation between these two types of disclosure intentions based on the purposes of information senders (i.e., seeking help vs. helping others) can help us better understand the relatedness and differences in their nomological networks. We propose that personal privacy norms elevate both types of disclosure, while perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value are unique motivators for the disclosure intention to seek help and the disclosure intention to help others, respectively. In this study, personal benefits in the form of building one’s own social network, receiving emotional support, and better managing personal health are largely egoistic in nature. Thus, they are not considered direct motivators for the altruistic PHI disclosure intention to help others in this study.

## 3.2 Personal Privacy Norms and PHI Disclosure Intentions

Information flows can take different forms that vary with contextual factors. Disclosing one’s PHI on OSNs is one form of information flow tied to a specific context. In line with the emphasis of CI theory on the need to clarify the contextual parameters of privacy norms, we define personal privacy norms from the perspective of the information sender as the perceived appropriateness of sharing one’s PHI on OSNs in this study. Similar to personal norms in other research contexts such as corporate crimes (Paternoster and Simpson 1996) and tax compliance (Wenzel 2004b), personal privacy norms establishes normative clout for regulating an individual’s decisions and behaviors from the appropriateness-based definition. Those who deem the sharing of PHI on OSNs to be appropriate would be more likely to disclose PHI to seek help from others or to help others suffering from similar diseases. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H1: Personal privacy norms have a positive impact on the PHI disclosure intention to seek help.

H2: Personal privacy norms have a positive impact on the PHI disclosure intention to help others.

## 3.3 Perceived Personal Benefit and PHI Disclosure Intentions

People join OSNs for a variety of personal benefits, such as fostering social relationships and exchanging information (Chen et al. 2014; Choi and Bazarova 2015). OSNs also provide enormous potential for people to glean health-related benefits, such as emotional support and a better understanding and management of one’s health conditions (Laranjo et al. 2015; Yan and Yan 2014). For example, the use of OSNs can help older adults improve their emotional well-being by making them feel more connected and less lonely (Yu et al. 2016). In recent years, people with chronic illnesses have increasingly turned to OSNs such as Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok to share their stories and connect with others experiencing similar symptoms (Martínez 2022). By interacting with similar others, individuals can find a sense of belonging and better manage their chronic conditions and symptoms. OSNs have also been suggested as an effective platform for alleviating stress and loneliness (Nabi et al. 2013). Building on this stream of literature, our study focuses on three potential personal benefits that individuals can derive from sharing PHI on OSNs: relationship-building (i.e., connecting with others experiencing similar health issues), emotional support (i.e., improving emotions such as reducing anxiety and loneliness), and health management (i.e., better management of personal health by, for example, receiving useful advice and making informed health decisions). By exploring these benefits, we can better understand why individuals may choose to share their PHI on OSNs from an egoistic angle.

Disclosing personal information and receiving personal benefits reflect a common understanding of the rights and responsibilities of OSN users. OSN users are obligated to disclose their personal information in order to receive personal benefits. Those who perceive a higher potential for personal benefit related to an action have been found to be more motivated to voluntarily share their personal information on OSNs (Xia et al. 2012). In the privacy literature, personal benefit has been included as a key pillar explaining information disclosure in several prevailing theories such as privacy calculus (Dinev and Hart 2006) and social contract theory (Li et al. 2014). Along the same vein, perceived personal benefit is expected to increase the individual incentive to disclose PHI to seek help from others. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H3: Perceived personal benefit has a positive impact on the PHI disclosure intention to seek help.

## 3.4 Perceived Altruistic Value and PHI Disclosure Intentions

In the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, information flow influences not only those who disclose personal information but also the ends and purposes of other individuals with similar health concerns as well as healthcare organizations and researchers working on disease treatment and prevention. In the healthcare context, contextual values such as preventing health threats to the public may legitimize an information flow even if it violates the confidentiality typically expected for patient health information (Nissenbaum, 2009). Indeed, many individuals are willing to disclose their personal health information to altruistically benefit others (Conn 2014; Thiebes et al. 2017). The effect of altruistic motivation may be particularly salient in the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs since the sharing of health information has the potential to benefit others suffering from similar medical conditions. Perceived altruistic value is defined here as the perception that others, such as patients, medical researchers, and health organizations, will accrue healthcare benefits. The majority of social media users with health issues are willing to share their PHI to help others (Alexander 2014). Thus, in this study, we identify perceived altruistic value as a contextual value that factors into one’s privacy decisions on OSNs. Those who believe that sharing their PHI would lead to health benefits for others are expected to be more likely to engage in PHI disclosure. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H4: Perceived altruistic value has a positive impact on the PHI disclosure intention to help others.

## 3.5 Perceived Personal Benefit and Personal Privacy Norms

CI theory holds that the interests of affected parties and values are the basis for judging the appropriateness of information flows (Nissenbaum 2015; Nissenbaum 2019). The interest assessment could be performed by individual parties influenced by the information flow and involves a calculative evaluation of the personal costs and benefits that may arise from the information flow. Individuals often expect sufficient personal gain to justify the privacy risks from the disclosure of their personal information (Culnan & Bies, 2003). Higher perceptions of receiving personal benefits would be expected to elevate personal normative beliefs about the appropriateness of an information flow. The positive effect of perceived personal benefit on personal norms has received some support in the literature on proenvironmental behaviors where personal interests represent desirable outcomes that help offset perceived costs and activate personal norms for supporting proenvironmental behaviors (Kim and Seock 2019). In this study, we focused on individual users who disclose their

PHI on OSNs. The positive effect of perceived personal benefit on personal norms is particularly prominent in our research context for two reasons. One is that information flows on OSNs are governed by the reciprocity principle, which states that people feel obligated to contribute so that others can benefit and return the favor, eventually resulting in their own benefit (Molm 1991). Another reason is that the development and growth of OSNs hinge upon the information contribution of individual users. Disclosing personal information and receiving personal benefits constitute a common understanding of the rights and responsibilities of OSN users. With such a common understanding, high perceptions of personal benefit are more likely to activate OSN users’ obligation to disclose their personal information. Consequently, we posit that the prospect of receiving appealing personal benefits will help individuals legitimize the flow of PHI on OSNs and nurture favorable personal privacy norms for supporting such information flows. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5: Perceived personal benefit has a positive impact on personal privacy norms.

## 3.6 Perceived Altruistic Value and Personal Privacy Norms

CI theory asserts that values legitimize privacy norms regarding the appropriateness of an information flow (Benthall et al. 2017). The influence of values on privacy norms has been echoed in studies applying the norm activation model (Schwartz 1977) where perceived altruistic value is confirmed as a key factor activating personal norms for supporting recycling (Park and Ha 2014) or filing environmental complaints to protect the environment (Zhang et al. 2017). In the context of OSNs, the personal interests of different parties using OSNs may not align well. For example, medical researchers are interested in collecting PHI with minimum constraints to promote the health of the general public. This is likely to conflict with the privacy interests of OSN users with medical conditions, as most of them are willing to disclose their PHI only when their privacy is protected (Conn 2014). Perceived altruistic value can help resolve conflicting privacy interests among different parties and inform the appropriateness of information flow at the community level. Perceived altruistic value, as a community value characterizing PHI flow on OSNs, could make individuals think beyond their personal interests to consider the benefits to other healthcare-related stakeholders, i.e. other patients, medical researchers, and health organizations. We propose that perceived altruistic value in promoting others’ healthcare benefits will increase individuals personal privacy norms regarding PHI disclosure on OSNs. Thus, we hypothesize:

H6: Perceived altruistic value has a positive impact on personal privacy norms.

## 3.7 Contextual Information Norms and Personal Privacy Norms

According to CI theory, contextual informational norms can influence people’s personal privacy norms not only through external pressure to conform to what constitutes “appropriate” practices or expectations in certain situations but also through personal self-regulation by making people believe they are obligated to perform certain practices. Indeed, informational norms circumscribe the nature of information within a given context in terms of whether it “is allowable, expected, or even demanded to be revealed.” (Nissenbaum 2004, p.138). For example, under the influence of contextual informational norms, a patient may believe it is appropriate or even feel obligated to share PHI with their physician in healthcare contexts, but it would be completely improper to do so with their employers.

Transmission principles are a key subpart or dimension of informational norms (Nissehbaum, 2009). They are constraints governing information flows and can take many possible forms such as control over information, restricted information access, and reciprocity, among others. The specific forms of transmission principles vary across the contexts of information flow depending on other contextual parameters such as actors and the type of information (Nissehbaum, 2009). In the OSN context, we identify control as one of the transmission principles constraining the flow of PHI due to the sensitive nature of PHI and the multitude of information recipients. Control of the flow of PHI may be a consequence of technology, industry self-regulation, and government regulation (Culnan and Bies 2003; Xu et al. 2008a; Xu et al. 2012). With industry self-regulation, private sectors (e.g., organizations and third-party policing groups and associations like TRUSTe), rather than the government, develop privacy rules and enforcement procedures (Swire 1997; Xu et al. 2012). Since industry self-regulation is mostly implemented through privacy policies of organizations (e.g., OSN providers like Facebook and Twitter), its efficacy inevitably depends on the goodwill of organizations for not engaging in opportunistic behaviors (Li et al. 2011). Industry self-regulation offers only rudimentary protection against data breaches caused by friends, service providers, third-party applications, and other firms. For example, the confidential information of an OSN user could be duplicated and posted by friends on a public page accessible to unknown people. Personal confidential information could also be used by OSN service providers to launch targeted advertisements. Moreover, PHI disclosed with the intent to help other patients may be used by employers to make hiring decisions or used by insurance companies to deny claims. All of these highlight the importance of enforcing control over data breaches through technology assurance and government regulations. Therefore, in this study, we examine the effect of technology control and legislative control on personal privacy norms.

In the extant privacy literature, controls are predominantly examined as general privacy controls (see examples in the literature review of privacy research in the context of OSNs in Appendix A). They make no distinction between specific types of controls with regard to privacy assurance agents (e.g., technology and government regulation). Technology assurance and government regulations are treated as antecedents for explaining the overall perceived privacy control and privacy concern (Xu et al. 2008a; Xu et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2012). In this study, we explicitly integrate privacy assurance agents (i.e., technology and government regulations) into the privacy control construct to gain a more fine-grained understanding of the nature of different types of privacy controls. To the best of our knowledge, our study is also the first that investigates the effect of technology control, and legislative control on people’s normative judgment regarding the appropriateness of a contextual information flow.

Technologies can drastically alter information flows and have been touted as both a cause and a solution to the problem of information privacy (Nissenbaum 2009). Research suggests that technology control is a key factor weighed in one’s privacy decisions in the context of location-based services (Xu et al. 2008b) and social commerce (Wang et al. 2022). In this study, we operationalize technology control in a way similar to the structural assurance construct in the trust literature (McKnight et al. 2002) to capture the extent of structures like guarantees and procedures that technologies offer to make OSNs a robust environment for safeguarding the flow of PHI. In particular, technology control refers to one’s belief in the efficacy of technology assurance for protecting PHI against privacy violations from internal (e.g., unauthorized access by peer OSN users and/or employees of OSN providers) and external (e.g., hackers performing phishing attacks) sources. Privacy assurance technologies are hardware, software, or communication technologies that help individuals better constrain the flow of their PHI and safeguard their information privacy, either directly (e.g., by information disclosers who change the privacy settings of their OSN accounts) and/or indirectly (e.g., by built-in access control and antiphishing techniques that are not managed by OSN users).

Conversely, defective technology infrastructure, such as the poor security of the database for storing personal information, may destroy contextual integrity, reducing individuals’ confidence in OSN technologies (Jiang 2022). Considering the sensitive nature of PHI and the rapid evolution of OSN technologies for collecting and disseminating personal information, technology control is expected to play a salient role for users of OSNs seeking to evaluate the privacy context before disclosing their PHI. Technology control enabled by privacy assurance technologies can serve as a signal to OSN users about the extent of privacy protection available on an OSN platform (Wang et al. 2022). High technology control may increase OSN users’ confidence in the technical soundness of OSN platforms for creating a reliable and secure environment supporting the flow of PHI and help persuade OSN users that their information privacy will be protected. Prior privacy research suggests that people care about the protection of their information privacy in online environments (Li et al. 2011). Many online consumers are willing to consider disclosing personal information if there is sufficient implementation of privacy protection measures (Ackerman et al. 1999). Therefore, we posit that technology control as structural assurance helps safeguard the flow of PHI and engender more favorable normative beliefs about the appropriateness of disclosing PHI on OSNs. We hypothesize:

## H7: Technology control has a positive impact on personal privacy norms.

Due to the high confidentiality of personal health information, the US Department of Health and Human Services introduced the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) to protect the flow of medical records. HIPAA, however, is a federal law that governs the health information practices of healthcare organizations and their employees, but it does not apply to commercial companies. PHI disclosure on OSNs occurs at the intersection of commercial and healthcare contexts, giving rise to potential conflicts between individual users and powerful profit-seeking commercial users such as pharmaceutical firms. These power users can directly harvest user profile information and perform sophisticated data mining and data aggregation to draw new inferences about individuals beyond the original information disclosure intent of OSN users.

The wide influence of power users can be exemplified by Facebook privacy scandals. In 2019, Facebook allowed advertisers to collect the names and emails of users who joined “closed” Facebook groups on health issues such as gene mutations afflicting women. Recently, Facebook was sued for secretly collecting the private health information of users who visited the websites of more than 664 hospital systems or medical provider websites (Payerchin 2022). The social media giant then used the health data and other user information to group Facebook users into separate medical categories for targeted advertising (Versel 2016). Indeed, many Facebook users have been perplexed by Facebook advertisements tailored to their health statuses, such as pregnancy and childbirth. The unbalanced power of stakeholders in a privacy context warrants the need for legislative control to govern the flow of personal information (Nissenbaum 2009).

With legislative control, government legislators have developed privacy regulations that explicitly codify penalties in order to deter potential offenders from committing privacy violations (Xu et al. 2012). For example, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) of the European Union imposes a maximum fine of €20 million or 4% of annual global turnover for companies violating GDPR. Government regulations are effective in promoting individuals’ belief in the extent of protection of their private information (Xu et al. 2012) and increasing personal information disclosure for social commerce (Wang et al. 2022). In this study, legislative control refers to one’s belief in the efficacy of government regulations and their enforcement for protecting PHI from privacy violations. In light of the sensitive nature of PHI and the threats from power users in the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, legislative control has paramount importance for OSN users in that it performs proxy control or indirectly safeguards their information privacy. In fact, government regulations were found to be the most powerful privacy assurance influencing OSN users’ privacy decisions to share personal information for social commerce (Wang et al. 2022). Strong legislative control that holds potential offenders accountable for privacy infringements can help increase OSN users’ confidence in the safety of sharing their PHI on OSNs. Therefore, we propose that legislative control is a key transmission principle for OSN users to delegate their control over the flow of PHI. Users who perceive high legislative control will tend to believe that the flow of PHI is protected by laws and form more favorable personal privacy norms about disclosing their PHI on OSNs. Thus, we hypothesize:

## H8: Legislative control has a positive impact on personal privacy norms.

Although information disclosure on OSNs is voluntary, individual privacy beliefs are inevitably influenced by normative expectations from peers. Thus, we identify community norms as contextual informational norms that influence the flow of PHI on OSNs. As social norms, community norms, can be internalized and shape individual beliefs (Kelman 1958; Wenzel 2004a). For example, in the context of IS security policy compliance, community norms regarding non-workrelated internet usage at the workplace shared by most of the people in an organization is an important information source for individual employees to develop their own personal norms (Li et al. 2010). In this study, community norms are defined as the normative expectation of most users on OSNs about the appropriateness of sharing PHI on OSNs. The information flow on OSNs is multilateral and governed by the principle of reciprocity, which asserts that people participating in the exchange are obligated to contribute in order for others to benefit and return the favor (Molm 1991). Information sharing would cease if the expected positive reciprocal actions did not occur. In line with the reciprocity principle and prior research on personal norms (Kelman 1958; Li et al. 2010; Wenzel 2004a), OSN members are expected to align their privacy perceptions with the overall expectation from peer community members. If most peer members consider PHI disclosure on OSNs to be appropriate, OSN users would tend to deem it as permissible and feel obligated to participate. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H9: Community norms have a positive impact on personal privacy norms.

## 4 Research Methodology

## 4.1 Covariates

Besides latent constructs in the research model, we control for five individual differences, including age, gender, health knowledge self-efficacy, poor health condition, and health information privacy concern. Li et al. (2014) found that age influences privacy behaviors. Gender may also influence PHI disclosure since most family healthcare decisions are made by women (Lincoff 2014). According to Ray et al. (2014), high knowledge self-efficacy is associated with a strong willingness to share knowledge in online communities. Health knowledge self-efficacy is expected to play a similar role in promoting PHI disclosure intentions on OSNs. Besides health knowledge self-efficacy, personal health conditions may also influence PHI disclosure intentions. Those in poor health may be more likely than healthy individuals to disclose PHI on OSNs to seek help or help others with similar conditions.

In addition, privacy concerns regarding PHI may also reduce PHI disclosure on OSNs. Following Malhotra et al (2004), we define health information privacy concern as one’s general tendency to worry about personal health information privacy on OSNs. Despite being specific to the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs, health information privacy concern largely reflects one’s privacy personality, as it does not capture key defining characteristics of the contextual information flows highlighted by CI theory, such as information recipients, nor does it capture contextual values and purposes. A considerable body of privacy research has pointed out the existence of the so-called privacy paradox, which occurs when an individual’s stated level of information privacy concern deviates from or even contradicts the person’s privacy behaviors (Li et al. 2017; Pavlou 2011; Smith et al. 2011b). For example, a significant proportion of individuals with a high privacy concern do not read privacy policies. This aligns with the assertion by Laufer and Wolfe (1977, p. 22) that “individuals’ concepts of privacy are tied to concrete situations in everyday life.” This is further supported by a recent study comparing the effect of privacy concern with that of contextual factors (Martin and Nissenbaum 2016). The study found that people’s judgment of privacy violations is highly dependent on contextual factors, such as the information recipient and purpose of the information flow, while the effect of privacy concern is unstable.

## 4.2 Variable Measurement

Most of the constructs were measured using established scales with slight rewording to fit our research context (see Tables B1 and B2 in Appendix B). Perceived personal benefit was operationalized as a second-order construct, consisting of three underlying dimensions identified from the review of prior literature (Laranjo et al. 2015; Yan and Yan 2014), including personal benefits for health management, emotional support, and relationship building. Following Confetelli and Bassellier (2009) and MacKenzie et al. (2011), we treated perceived personal benefit as a formative construct since the three underlying personal benefit dimensions are not interchangeable and may not covary. For example, for an OSN user who shares PHI, better health management may not coexist with improved emotional well-being such as feeling less lonely. All other constructs of this study were gauged using reflective first-order measures on seven-point scales.

## 4.3 Study Design and Procedure

Users of OSNs represent the target population of this study. The research model was examined using an online survey. To check for language ambiguity and undertake an initial reliability analysis of the survey instruments, we first administered the survey using a pilot sample of 46 students from a major northern US university. Subsequently, the final survey was disseminated to research panelists from Qualtrics, who were recruited and contacted by Qualtrics and remained anonymous to the researchers throughout the data collection process. The first page of the online survey notified subjects about the research purpose, potential risks/benefits, and the voluntary nature of their involvement. The subjects were then prompted to specify the online social network sites (OSNs) they use, such as Facebook, Twitter, PatientsLikeMe, and others. Those who chose “I do not use personal online social networking websites” were excluded from the study. As a result, only OSN users were allowed to proceed to answer the rest of the survey questions. In the end, a total of 513 usable responses were collected from the Qualtrics research panel and used to validate our research model.

The demographic characteristics of these 513 survey respondents are summarized in Table 2. Most of the subjects are female. The age shows a wide distribution with an average of 46, suggesting the use of OSNs across diverse age groups. We further analyzed the extent of poor health conditions. 51.28% of respondents reported experiencing major pain or discomfort for an extended period of time with moderate to high frequency, while 52.24% of subjects suffered from moderate to very severe chronic conditions.

## 5 Data Analysis

We analyzed the measurement quality and tested the research hypotheses using SmartPLS 3.0 (Ringle et al. 2015), which is a variance-based structural equation modeling (SEM) technique. In comparison with covariance-based SEM techniques such as AMOS, SmartPLS has an unmatched advantage for analyzing complex research models with both reflective and formative constructs, which is particularly suitable for our research model. In addition, SmartPLS does not depend on the assumption of normal distribution and interval scales, which makes it work well for testing binary covariates such as age and gender.

## 5.1 Measurement Model

We first evaluated the measurement quality of the formative measure—perceived personal benefit— before confirming the validity and reliability of reflective scales. We also checked the extent of common method variance (CMV) to ensure that CMV did not bias our data analysis results by examining the correlation matrix in Table C1 in Appendix C and the VIF values of the structural model and applying marker-variable technique from Lindell and Whitney (2001). The detailed results and discussion are reported in Appendix C.

## 5.2 Structural Model and Hypothesis Testing

We evaluated the overall fit of the estimated structural model using standardized root-mean residual (SRMR), the unweighted least squares discrepancy (d\_ULS), and the geodesic discrepancy (d\_G) (Henseler et al. 2014). As shown in Table 3, SRMR is below the suggested threshold of 0.08 and the two discrepancy values are below the 99% quantile of the 5000-sample bootstrap distribution. All these suggest a satisfactory overall fit of our path model with the data.

The research hypotheses were tested based on the signs of path coefficients and their significance levels computed using 5000-sample bootstrapping. Figure 3 and Table 4 summarize the results of the hypothesis testing. Standardized path coefficients and their significance levels are displayed along each path in Figure 3. The research model was able to explain 66.8%, 63.2%, and 74.3% of the variance in the PHI disclosure intention to seek help, the PHI disclosure intention to help others, and personal privacy norms, respectively. Therefore, our model is strong in its explanation of personal privacy norms and PHI disclosure intentions.

The test results for all nine hypotheses are statistically significant (p-value < 0.050) but only eight of the path coefficients have signs in the correct direction. Therefore, all hypotheses except for H8 (Legislative control → Personal privacy norms) are supported. Legislative control unexpectedly had a significant negative effect on personal privacy norms, which will be discussed in the following section. Overall, the research model is well supported, confirming the effect of contextual norms and values on personal privacy norms and individual PHI disclosure intentions.

Among the five covariates for explaining PHI disclosure intentions, older Facebook users tend to have lower intentions to disclose their PHI. Men were less willing to share their PHI on OSNs to receive help from others than women. Health knowledge selfefficacy increased individuals’ intentions to disclose their PHI to help others. Poor health condition significantly increased both types of PHI disclosure intentions. High health information privacy concern only marginally reduced the PHI disclosure intention to seek help but had no effect the PHI disclosure intention to help others. These empirical findings are consistent with the extant privacy literature regarding the weak or even contradictory role of privacy concern in explaining privacy behaviors (Hui et al., 2007; Jaspers & Pearson, 2022; Li et al., 2017; Li et al., 2011; Pavlou, 2011; Van Slyke et al., 2006). Therefore, when individuals make privacy decisions, the effect of their stated level of privacy concern may be mitigated or even superseded in the presence of other contextual factors, such as the contextual norms and values found in this study, monetary incentives (Hui et al., 2007), and trust in companies providing IoT services (Jaspers & Pearson, 2022). Our study provides another perspective for understanding the so-called privacy paradox using CI theory.

## 6 Discussion

## 6.1 Summary of Key Findings

In this study, we advance the information privacy literature by utilizing a contextual perspective to understand the development of health information privacy judgments and subsequent PHI disclosure on OSNs. Drawing on CI theory, we propose that personal privacy norms are governed by context-specific perceptions of personal benefit, altruistic value, and norms. Our empirical findings confirm the essential roles of perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value in molding OSN users’ personal privacy norms. Notably, OSN users take not only their personal benefits into account in their privacy decisions, as found by prior research (Choi et al. 2018), but they also consider the altruistic value for other individual OSN users and related healthcare stakeholders. To better reflect the research context, we further decomposed perceived personal benefit into three subdimensions, including health management, emotional support, and relationship building. The improvement in personal health management and receiving emotional support are two significant personal benefits that increase personal privacy norms.

Besides context-specific personal benefits and altruistic value, we identified three contextual norms (i.e., technology control, legislative control, and community norms) and empirically proved their influence on OSN users’ personal privacy norms.

Technology control emerges as the most prominent control that enhances personal privacy norms. OSN users are also influenced by community norms when assessing the appropriateness of sharing PHI. Interestingly, we found that legislative control significantly reduced personal privacy norms although the bivariate correlation between the two was positive (i.e., 0.57 in Table C1). Since excessive multicollinearity may be a cause for this unexpected changing sign of the relationship (i.e., the positive bivariate correlation became a negative regression coefficient), we examined VIF for all predictors explaining personal privacy norms. Their VIF values ranged between 1.5 and 3.2, which is far below the threshold of 10 (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001) and suggests a lack of multicollinearity.

Another plausible explanation is the existence of net or cross-over suppression (Cohen and Cohen 1975). Cross-over suppression refers to a situation in which two predictors and a criterion variable have positive bivariate correlations among them, but the inclusion of these variables in a regression equation elevates the regression coefficient of the stronger predictor (i.e., the predictor that has a higher correlation with the criterion variable) and changes the sign of the weaker predictor (Cohen and Cohen 1975). The weaker predictor acts as a suppressor that elevates the effect of the other predictor. To verify the existence of the cross-over suppression effect or the role of legislative control as a suppressor, we first checked the bivariate correlations among technological control, legislative control, and personal privacy norms (see Table C1). All three variables are positively correlated. The correlation between technology control and personal privacy norms (i.e., 0.78) is stronger than that between legislative control and personal norms (i.e., 0.57), suggesting that legislative control is the weaker predictor. We then tested a reduced research model by removing legislative control and compared the results with those in Figure 3. We note a clear increase in the path coefficient of technology control from 0.40 in the reduced model to 0.48 in our original research model. The preceding observations clearly support the role of legislative control as a suppressor.

Following the suggestion by MacKinnon et al. (2000), we further performed the Sobel test to check the statistical significance of the suppression effect. The Sobel test result supported the existence of a significant cross-over suppression effect with a p-value of less than 0.01. Therefore, legislative control appears to be a significant suppressor that amplifies the effect of technology control by suppressing its unwanted variance (Cohen and Cohen 1983). The effects of legislative control and technological control may intermingle, potentially in complex ways. This would be an interesting phenomenon to explore and deserves further investigation in future studies.

This study further extends CI theory to explain two types of privacy behaviors, i.e. the PHI disclosure intention to seek help from others and the PHI disclosure intention to help others. Our results suggest that perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value not only indirectly influence PHI disclosure intentions through personal privacy norms but also serve as direct motivators for PHI disclosure intentions. In addition, our findings indicate that the motivators for PHI disclosure intentions differ with the purpose of the disclosure. Perceived personal benefit significantly increased the PHI disclosure intention to seek help for oneself, whereas perceived altruistic value promoted the disclosure intention to help others. The direct positive influence of perceived personal benefit on self-interest-driven privacy behaviors echoes the extant studies based on privacy calculus theory (Bélanger and Crossler 2011; Choi et al. 2018; Dinev and Hart 2006; Kordzadeh and Warren 2017; Teubner and Flath 2019; Xu et al. 2009).

Our results also augment the privacy calculus perspective by identifying the indirect effect of perceived personal benefit on self-interest-driven privacy behaviors through the adjustment of personal privacy norms. We further confirmed the importance of this indirect path using the bootstrap method proposed by Shrout and Bolger (2002). The ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect was computed for 5,000 bootstrap samples. The mean and median were 31% and 30%, respectively. The 95% bootstrap percentile interval ranged from 0.20 to 0.44. About 30% of the effect of perceived personal benefit on the PHI disclosure intention to seek help for oneself was mediated, supporting the salient role of personal privacy norms.

## 6.2 Research Contributions and Implications

This study has several important contributions and implications for research on information privacy. First, to the best of our knowledge, our study is the first empirical study in IS privacy research that systematically investigates and robustly validates the core constructs and relationships of CI theory. Our study contributes to the IS privacy literature by identifying and empirically verifying (1) the novel role of personal privacy norms as the benchmark for gauging information privacy as the perceived appropriateness of a contextual information flow and (2) contextual informational norms and values as the fundamental forces that jointly shape an individual’s personal privacy norms. Additionally, our study is the first to show that personal privacy norms act as the central tipping point at which contextual informational norms and perceived altruistic value influence privacy decisions/behaviors.

Our empirical findings suggest that CI theory is a scientifically valuable lens for examining personal privacy norms (1) in contexts marked by sensitive information and nondyadic information flow to peers and other stakeholders and (2) in contexts that cut across healthcare and social networks. CI theory helps us not only better capture the distinctive idiosyncrasies (e.g., information type, actors, and purpose) that define the context of PHI disclosure via OSNs but also to identify relevant contextual informational norms and values. In particular, our study makes an important contribution to the privacy research on PHI sharing on OSNs by validating perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value as the specific contextual values and confirming community norms, technology control, and legislative control as the specific contextual informational norms that influence personal privacy norms. Therefore, we conclude that CI theory has the potential to advance our understanding of the formation of personal privacy norms in a complicated information disclosure context. Our study lays a foundation for future studies to embrace CI theory to further investigate the intricate dynamics of information flow and personal privacy norms.

Second, there currently exists a notable dearth of research that empirically investigates how various context-relative factors shape one’s personal privacy norms. Our study seeks to address this gap in the literature by offering a novel and in-depth understanding of the effect of these contextual factors on personal privacy norms. This, in turn, can contribute to the development of more effective privacy policies and practices that are better aligned with individuals’ needs and expectations in different contexts. For example, the effect of technology control has been found to be the strongest contextual norm for promoting OSN users personal privacy norms, while legislative control may act as a negative suppressor that elevates the positive effect of technology control. This observation underscores the importance of jointly examining the combined effect of these two types of controls in future studies. Ignoring legislative control may yield misleading findings suggesting a weak influence of technology control.

Third, this study further extends CI theory to explain privacy behaviors by incorporating personal privacy norms as the enabler of information disclosure with contextual values as the motivators. This endeavor positions our research as one of the first efforts to employ the lens of contextual integrity to elucidate privacy behaviors and verify the distinct yet related roles of personal privacy norms and contextual values in driving people’s privacy decisions. Our work opens a new avenue for future IS research to explain IS privacy behaviors.

Fourth, our results support the distinct motivating roles of perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value in individual privacy decisions. Their roles vary with the purposes of PHI disclosure intentions. So far, the purpose of information disclosure as a contextual parameter and the effect of perceived altruistic value have received sparse research attention in the IS literature. Consequently, it is imperative for future studies to recognize the different purposes of information disclosure and to disentangle different types of motivations when examining privacy decisions. The effect of perceived altruistic value may be particularly salient for information disclosure among peers and in situations where communal benefits and/or social welfare may be gained.

In light of the above discussion, we conclude that CI theory offers a much-needed novel perspective for understanding the intricate dynamics of privacy judgments and decisions. It also resonates with the recent research call for context-specific approaches to understanding IS research phenomena (Hong et al. 2014; Karjalainen et al. 2019).

## 6.3 Practical Implications

This study provides several important implications for policymakers and OSN providers to effectively design information privacy measures (i.e., policies, technical features, and programs) to achieve contextual integrity and support the flow of PHI. First, the tenets of CI theory could be used as decision aids guiding the design of information privacy measures for promoting the appropriate flow of personal information on OSNs. Policymakers and OSN providers need to start with characteristics defining the context of information flow such as the type of information, actors, and purpose of the disclosure. The significant role of technology control verified in this study suggests that the technology platform supporting the information flow is another key defining characteristic of the privacy context. Besides these characteristics, policymakers and OSN providers should integrate contextual norms and values into the design of information privacy measures. This step would address the recent call for organizations to incorporate contextual informational norms into their privacy policies to avoid privacy disasters (Culnan 2019) and would bridge the gap between decision aids, helping firms design better information privacy measures (Dhillona et al. 2018). The next step would be to investigate how personal privacy norms and privacy behaviors vary across research contexts in order to expand the understanding of how CI theory applies to information disclosure on IT platforms. Such an understanding would provide further evidence-based guidelines for practice.

Second, we note that both technology control and legislative control are important for promoting the appropriate flow of PHI on OSNs. Technology control is the prevailing contextual informational norm shaping personal privacy norms. OSN providers should give high priority to the design and deployment of technical measures to safeguard the confidentiality of PHI or de-identify PHI through user intervention or by default without user actions. Moreover, advanced technologies deployed on OSNs have greatly expanded the power of businesses including OSN providers to perform the collection, aggregation, analysis, and sharing of PHI. Given the existence of power users and the nonapplicability of HIPAA to these power users of OSNs, it is critical for governments to create acts and laws to formally regulate the information practices of these power users.

Third, our findings suggest that community norms, despite their weak effect, are contextual informational norms that OSN providers could leverage to develop contextual integrity. Indeed, sharing PHI on OSNs is largely intended to get help from or support peers. Currently, OSNs act as an important health knowledge repository to empower patients and support the promising patient-centered healthcare paradigm. OSN providers could play a key role in cultivating community norms that favor PHI sharing through enhancing peer engagement and the awareness of peer sharing. For example, OSN providers could build separate subscription channels based on users’ health interests, push health-related posts through these channels, and encourage or remind users to rate posts and/or provide response comments to help others in similar situations.

Lastly, we note that perceived personal benefit and perceived altruistic value are imperative for nurturing favorable personal privacy norms and motivating OSN users to disclose PHI in the healthcare context. OSN providers could explicitly work on improving health management and emotional support for individual OSN users through peer engagement and the development of a trustworthy health knowledgesharing community, as discussed above. OSN providers could also work on increasing the perceived altruistic value of helping others. For example, OSN providers could serve as a bridge to actively connect medical researchers with OSN users or rely on trustworthy facilitators to monitor and aggregate PHI to produce central awareness and alert users to potential health-related issues.

## 6.4 Limitations and Future Research Opportunities

This study has some potential limitations. First, although Facebook users are a good representation of OSN users, future studies should validate our research model using subjects from health-specific OSNs such as PatientsLikeMe. The forms of contextual informational norms and values and their effects could differ among participants of health-specific OSNs, necessitating a more targeted analysis. Second, while our study examined three types of contextual informational norms (technology control, legislative control, and community norms), it would be interesting for future studies to explore other potential contextual informational norms, such as the justice perceptions of OSN users regarding the potential secondary use of PHI. Third, the altruistic motivation of helping others may be purely altruistic without expecting any reward in return or it may be mixed with expectations for egoistic benefits such as the feeling of happiness from helping others (Andreoni 1989). In this study, we did not differentiate pure altruism from impure altruism in order to focus on the effect of the perceived altruistic value of sharing one’s PHI with others such as other OSN users suffering from similar health issues and medical researchers, among others. Future studies could further investigate the effect of egoistic interests that may accompany altruistic motivations to help others in the context of PHI disclosure on OSNs. Lastly, this study adopts a survey-based approach to validate the research model, which has inherent limitations in establishing causality. We encourage future research to further verify our research model by using alternative methodological techniques, such as experimental designs that manipulate the three types of contextual informational norms.

## 7 Conclusion

As one of the key goals of healthcare quality (Institute of Medicine 2001), patient-centered care is transforming current healthcare systems. OSNs have become integrated components of patient-centered care systems that engage and empower patients through healthrelated knowledge and social support. There is a compelling need to build a repository of health knowledge based on the PHI of OSN users to support patient-centered care. However, such a repository may introduce potential privacy concerns that could disrupt contextual integrity and reduce OSN users’ willingness to disclose their PHI. To address this dilemma, this study employs the theory of contextual integrity to uncover the defining contextual idiosyncrasies of PHI disclosure on OSNs and the related contextual informational norms and values necessary for cultivating contextual integrity in terms of the appropriate flow of PHI. The empirical findings of this study enhance our understanding of the normative expectation of PHI sharing among peer users and controls through technology and government regulations. This study also distinguishes between privacy-related in-role behavior (seeking help from others) and altruistic behaviors (helping others) and sheds new, important light on the role of altruistic value in promoting the PHI disclosure intention to help others.

## Appendix C: Measurement Model Statistics

Following the suggestions by MacKenzie et al. (2005) and Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer (2001), we assessed the measurement quality of a formative construct based on the statistical significance of path weights and the variance inflation factor (VIF). Among the three dimensions constituting perceived personal benefit, health management and emotional support are significant with p-values of less than 0.001. Although relationship building is not a significant dimension, it was retained in the following data analysis since a formative construct may contain nonsignificant indicators to maintain its content validity (Mathieson et al. 2001). The VIF values are between 3.22 and 3.92, which are well below the cutoff of 10 suggested for excessive multicollinearity (Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer 2001, p. 272). Therefore, the issue of multicollinearity is not a serious concern for this study. Overall, the measurement of perceived personal benefit has good quality.

We then examined the reliability and validity of all reflective instruments. From Table C1, all of them have satisfactory reliability since their composite reliability and average variance extracted are above the suggested cutoff values of 0.7, and 0.5, respectively (Bagozzi and Yi 1988). Regarding convergent validity, we examined factor loadings and their statistical significance and found that all measurement items loaded significantly on their respective latent constructs and had loadings above the recommended minimum threshold of 0.6 (Bagozzi and Yi 1988; Gefen and Straub 2005). We then tested the discriminant validity by checking cross-loadings (Table C2a), the Fornell-Larcker criterion, and heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) values (Table C1). Discriminant validity can be established if (1) each item in a latent construct has cross-loadings smaller than its loading on that construct and the differences between cross-loadings and the loading are at least 0.1 (Gefen and Straub 2005), (2) the square root of a construct’s AVE exceeds the correlations of that construct with other constructs (Fornell and Larcker 1981), and (3) HTMT values are below 0.9 (Henseler et al. 2015). Among the three criteria, the HTMT approach has the highest sensitivity in detecting the lack of discriminant validity if it is an issue with the measurement model and thus serves as a reliable and conservative benchmark (Henseler et al. 2015). We first checked cross-loadings in Table C2a to identify specific items for removal to meet the suggestion by Gefen and Straub (2005). In Table C2a, only the first item in legislative control construct (i.e., LCL1) failed the cross-loading test as its highest cross-loading was only 0.03 smaller than the corresponding loading. Therefore, we removed LCL1 from the following data analysis and provided the updated loadings and cross-loadings in Table C2b. In Tables C1-C2b, all three criteria for discriminant validity are met. Therefore, our measurement model has satisfactory quality in both reliability and validity.

We found that all VIF values of the structure model were below 3.3, which is the suggested threshold for indicating “free of common method bias.” (Kock, 2015, p. 7). Lastly, we adopted the marker-variable technique from Lindell and Whitney (2001) and identified the second smallest positive correlation among all the indicators as a conservative estimate of the influence of CMV (or rm). It was found to be 0.002, or the correlation between RBD3 and HPC3 in this study. After partialing out rm from the correlations in Table C1, the significance levels of all correlations stay the same, suggesting that it is unlikely that CMV biased our findings.</td><td></td></tr></table>
