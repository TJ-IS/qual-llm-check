RESEARCH ARTICLE

# Toward a better understanding of behavioral intention and system usage constructs

Jiming Wu and Hongwei Du

Department of Management, College of Business and Economics, California State University, East Bay, U.S.A.

## Abstract

To understand user behavior, researchers have examined intention to use, and system usage through some common conceptualizations such as actual usage, reported usage, and assessed usage. Although this entire body of research has produced important findings, it has yet to appreciably advance our theoretical understanding of behavioral intention (BI) and usage constructs. To fill this gap, this paper critically examines and compares these core variables as well as their relationships with key technology acceptance determinants. We find that (1) BI has a much higher correlation with the determinants than does usage, and thus more variance in BI than in usage can be explained; (2) BI is not a good surrogate for usage; (3) among the three usage constructs, assessed usage is the most and actual usage is the least highly correlated with BI; and (4) researchers should examine both actual usage and assessed usage in their every single study to bring to light the true relationships between system usage and its antecedents. This study thus helps IS scholars expand their baseline knowledge of these core variables, interpret the important messages conveyed by the extant literature, and conduct more fruitful and illuminating future research on user behavior. ## Introduction

User behavior has long been a central and fundamental research topic in the information systems (IS) field. To explain user behavior, IS researchers have examined system usage and come to recognize it as the primary indicator of technology acceptance (e.g., Davis, 1989; Adams et al, 1992; Karahanna et al, 2006). In a related vein, many scholars have grounded their studies on the theory of reasoned action, which asserts that the most important predictor of a behavior is an individual’s intention of performing the behavior (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Thus, these scholars investigate user behavior by focusing on both usage and behavioral intention (BI) (e.g., Taylor & Todd, 1995; Szajna, 1996; Venkatesh et al, 2002). Consequently, in the behavioral literature, system usage and BI have been the two most essential and commonly used dependent variables.

Despite its centrality and long history in IS research, system usage has so far received little theoretical scrutiny (Chin & Marcolin, 2001; DeLone & McLean, 2003). The consequence of this dearth of theoretical assessment is the lack of consensus on how to conceptualize system usage and thus the diversity of measuring it (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). For example, system usage has been measured as actual usage (e.g., system logs of email messages sent) (Szajna, 1996), reported usage (e.g., users’ estimates of number of hours) (Adams et al, 1992), and assessed usage (e.g., frequency and amount of time on a six-point ordinal scale) (Davis et al, 1989). This indicates that, indeed, different conceptualizations and operationalizations of system usage do exist; a usage construct in one study may greatly differ from that in another one (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007; Venkatesh et al, 2008).

Most studies focusing on system usage have explicitly examined its antecedents. They report the relationships to be significantly positive (Davis, 1989), weakly positive (Szajna, 1996), weakly negative (Bajaj & Nidumolu, 1998), and significantly negative (McCloskey, 2003). These mixed results indicate a clear need for closer examination of different usage constructs and their relationships with prominent antecedents. Given that the utmost objective of user behavior research is to explain system usage, it is necessary and desirable to investigate further these relationships. An omission in this regard will lead to a superficial understanding of system usage and to critical flaws in future research endeavors.

The objective of this paper is to address four main research questions: (1) How is system usage distinct from BI in terms of their relationships with prominent antecedents? (2) Is BI a good surrogate for usage? (3) Whether different usage constructs diverge from each other, and if so, how do they differ? (4) Theoretically, which usage construct(s) should be the target of future research? By exploring these questions, this study helps IS researchers expand their baseline knowledge of BI and usage, better conceptualize and operationalize the variables, and more effectively design and conduct future research on user behavior.

Recently, a noticeable phenomenon in behavioral research has been the intensive focus on BI rather than system usage (Straub & Burton-Jones, 2007). For instance, in a meta-analysis of research on system-use behavior, Lee et al (2003) find that among 99 individual studies, only 15 of them investigate usage and all of the remaining concentrate entirely on BI. Similarly, Wu & Lederer (2009) observe that only 21 studies out of 71 scrutinize usage. This interesting phenomenon indicates that most IS researchers prefer to use BI rather than usage as the ultimate dependent variable in their theoretical framework. It also indicates that they consider BI as a good surrogate for usage and hold that it is rational and logical to study user behavior without assessing system usage. In other words, they embrace, explicitly or implicitly, the notion that if BI is predicted, then usage is guaranteed, no matter what.

It is of interest to note that limitations of usage construct may also contribute to the phenomenon. As pointed out earlier, there is a dearth of research in the IS literature that richly theorizes about system usage, provides a theoretical basis for the idea of construct validity, and discusses how theory informs a reliable approach to measuring it (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006).

Besides theoretical limitations, there are limitations pertaining to the clarity and purpose of the measurement of usage (Venkatesh et al, 2008). As highlighted by Jasperson et al (2005), much prior research has either treated usage as a black box, or used a ‘lean’ measure of usage without providing any theoretical justification for choosing it. Other limitations are related to the difficulty in measuring usage. As observed by Lee et al (2003), it is never an easy task to use system logs to longitudinally measure actual usage under diverse restrictions such as security policies and privacy rights. Additionally, it is also difficult to incorporate system features into the measurement of system usage, or to measure usage in multiple dimensions according to the study context (Jasperson et al, 2005). To sum up, all of these limitations may encourage IS scholars not to use system usage but to include BI in their research models.

However, for several reasons, it may not be fully justifiable to study user behavior without assessing system usage. First, it has not been based on a solid and academically rigorous foundation. Specifically, there is little empirical evidence or even theoretical grounding that in the IS field, researchers can study BI instead of usage and that intention tells the whole story of user behavior. Second, nearly all previous studies have measured BI by using the same method – a seven-point Likert scale with one-point representing strongly disagree and seven-point indicating strongly agree (Straub & Burton-Jones, 2007). This method differs from those measuring usage constructs, as described earlier. Such a difference indicates that intention and usage constructs are distinct in nature in terms of how they are conceptualized and operationalized, and the user-behavior information captured by usage constructs is unique and may not be replaced or removed. Third, BI is formed prior to system usage, and the gap in time can be large (Bagozzi, 2007). Not focusing on usage but only on intention results in overlooking this crucial time gap, where the BI formed earlier may change (Sheeran & Orbell, 1998).

In summary, although usage differs from BI, previous research has paid little attention to how they diverge from each other, and instead it tends to treat them as being interchangeable. This can be evidenced by unjustified, arbitrary selection of ultimate dependent variable in previous studies. That is, most past studies choose an ultimate dependent variable for its appearance in prior empirical research rather than for theoretical reasons (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). More importantly, previous studies have placed even less emphasis on an in-depth, comparative analysis of these key dependent variables. This lack of emphasis can result in a stereotypical understanding of these core behavioral constructs, in overlooking their divergence, and in losing opportunities to identify critical implications for future research.

To fill this gap and address the four research questions, the current study (1) summarizes the correlations between system usage, BI, perceived usefulness (PU), and perceived ease of use (PEOU), (2) analyzes and compares the correlations between PU/PEOU and usage with those between PU/PEOU and BI, (3) synthesizes the correlations for different usage constructs, and (4) analyzes and compares the correlations between different usage constructs and their prominent antecedents. PU and PEOU are also investigated because (1) they are the two most prominent antecedents of system-use behavior in the literature, and (2) analyzing their effects on BI and usage provides an efficient means to address the four research questions. By doing so, the current study not only addresses the four main research questions but also helps to ascertain whether PU and PEOU differentially influence BI and usage. This particular issue is of profound theoretical and practical importance, but so far it has not been directly and systematically investigated.

This study employs a meta-analysis approach to tackle the research topic. A meta-analysis is very useful here because the empirical studies included in the analysis offer a variety of results of interest, which are unlikely to be obtained from a single study. Next, we briefly review previous studies on this topic, especially those in psychology, that challenge the link between BI and actual behavior. Then, we present the research approach and describe the results of data analysis. Finally, we discuss implications, contributions, and limitations of this research.

## Intention and behavior

## The intention of user behavior

BI is defined as ‘a person’s subjective probability that he will perform some behavior’ (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975, p. 288). A number of social psychology models explicitly hypothesize BI to be an immediate determinant of behavior, including the theories of reasoned action (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975), planned behavior (Ajzen, 1985), protection motivation (Rogers, 1975), and interpersonal behavior (Triandis, 1977). In IS research, BI has been modeled not only as a predictor of system usage but also as a consequence of PU and PEOU (Davis et al, 1989). It should be noted that all these theories and models endorse the principle that behavior, not its intention, should be the ultimate dependent variable.

Past IS research suggests that the strength of intentions is usually different for novice vs experienced users. More specifically, intentions measured for novice users tend to have a weaker association with usage behavior than intentions measured for experienced users (Szajna, 1996). This is in line with Ajzen’s (1987) claim that the intention–behavior linkage will become stronger when the users obtain more experience. Following the recommendations of Ajzen & Fishbein (1980), IS researchers generally measure BI using three items on a seven-point Likert scale (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Wixom & Todd, 2005). One example item is, ‘I intend to use the system in the future’.

## The user behavior: system usage

As a central construct in behavioral research, system usage is defined as ‘a user’s employment of a system to perform a task’ (Burton-Jones & Gallivan, 2007, p. 659). In addition to its key role in technology acceptance models (Davis et al, 1989; Szajna, 1996), system usage is also a vital variable in IS success models and has been identified as the most important surrogate measure for IS success (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Sabherwal et al, 2006; Petter et al, 2008). The importance of system usage is further highlighted by its serving as a fundamental variable in a contextualized framework of individual performance (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). However, with the exception of early decision-making research (Barkin & Dickson, 1977), very few studies have provided a sound theoretical basis for system usage or its conceptualization and operationalization (Jasperson et al, 2005; Venkatesh et al, 2008).

Due to these theoretical limitations, IS researchers have yet to forge a consensus on how to measure system usage. Our comprehensive review of the literature suggests that system usage has been measured by both objective and subjective methods, and all of these measures can be categorized into three types: actual usage, reported usage, and assessed usage. The objective method involves one type of measure – actual usage. Most commonly, IS researchers use system logs to collect longitudinal data for actual usage. For example, Lu & Gustafson (1994) set the target system to record how many times the subjects use the system during a 5-month period. Szajna (1996) gathered data from computer logs of email messages sent over a 15-week semester. Similar actual usage measures include number of user activities performed on a system (Devaraj et al, 2008), amount of time logged into a system (Venkatesh et al, 2002), and number of communications initiated through a system (Klein, 2007).

In contrast, the other two measures are both subjective. Reported usage is commonly measured by asking research participants to report on their duration and frequency of using a target system. In general, duration is determined by number of hours used per day or week (e.g., Martins & Kellermanns, 2004; Zhang et al, 2006), while frequency is indicated by number of times of using the system daily or weekly (e.g., Keil et al, 1995; Yi et al, 2005). Occasionally, reported usage is measured by asking respondents the number of tasks performed by using the target information system. For example, Adams et al (1992) asked their survey subjects to report the number of email messages they send and receive on a typical day. Taylor & Todd (1995) required the student research participants to reveal the number of different assignments and projects completed by using a computing resource center.

Assessed usage gauges the intensity and extent of using an information system on an ordinal scale. Davis (1989) and Igbaria & Parasuraman (1989) are among the first researchers to introduce this type of measure into the study of system-use behavior. Their measure primarily focuses on the amount of time and frequency of using an information system. Specifically, users were asked to indicate the amount of time spent on a target system per day on a six-point ordinal scale ranging from (1) ‘almost never’ to (6) ‘more than 3 hours per day’, and to report their frequency of usage on another six-point ordinal scale ranging from (1) ‘less than once a month’ to (6) ‘several times a day’. Apparently, the application of an ordinal scale constitutes the key difference between assessed usage and the aforementioned two measures for actual usage and reported usage. In subsequent work, researchers have extended ordinal scale measure to investigate other dimensions of system usage. For instance, using a five-point ordinal scale ranging from (1) ‘not at all’ to (5) ‘a great extent’, Igbaria (1993) assessed the extent of using different software packages and the extent of using a target system for different business tasks. Other scholars have enriched the ordinal scale measure by evaluating the scope of using different system functions (e.g., Karahanna et al, 2006) or applications (e.g., Thompson et al, 1994).

Based on the discussions above, at a conceptual level, actual usage may be defined as the system-recorded amount of time or number of times that an individual uses the system; reported usage can be seen as the user-reported amount of time or number of times of using a system; assessed usage refers to the ordinal-scalemeasured intensity and extent of using a system. Past research suggests that results derived from the objective measure (i.e., actual usage) greatly differ from those based on the subjective measures (Lee et al, 2003; Karahanna et al, 2006; Straub & Burton-Jones, 2007). One possible explanation is that when reporting the amount of time or frequency of using a system, light users tend to overestimate their usage, while heavy users have a tendency to underestimate it (Collopy, 1996). A similar explanation is that subjects generally have difficulty in recalling their past usage and thus are highly error-prone in selfreporting (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). This explanation is consistent with the view advanced by Wagner & Gooding (1987) that users are normally poor estimators of aspects of their own behavior. Consequently, researchers conclude that subjective measures of system usage are likely to be biased and thus may not be an appropriate surrogate for actual usage (Szajna, 1996; Lee et al, 2003). Researchers have also concluded that subjective usage and objective usage are independent and should be modeled as two distinct constructs (Straub et al, 1995; Barnett et al, 2006). In line with this conclusion, the current study treats the three different measures of usage as three separate variables. As such, this research can more deeply and thoroughly investigate different usage constructs as well as their relationships with the prominent antecedents – PU, PEOU, and BI.

## The intention–behavior relationship in IS

While most IS studies report a positive intention– behavior relationship. a wide variance in the correlation is present across studies, ranging from as low as 0.12 (Horton et al, 2001) to as high as 0.76 (Sanchez-Franco & Roldan, 2005). Such a wide variance indicates that the results are not consistent, and the significance of the relationship is not guaranteed. This can be evidenced by the observation that intention plays an insignificant role in predicting usage (Horton et al, 2001) or it can only explain as low as approximately 4% of the variance in usage (Devaraj et al, 2008). The inconsistent findings in the literature suggest that the intention–behavior relationship is not stable and remains open to questions.

Extant IS research also suggests that intention– behavior relationship might be affected by whether system usage is mandatory or voluntary (Devaraj & Kohli, 2003). Specifically, when individuals must use an information system, their usage may only mean conformance to a superior’s requirements or compliance with organizational policies. Therefore, the importance of their intentions as antecedents to the performance of the behavior is likely to be minimized (Brown et al, 2002). In other words, individuals might not intend to use the system, but they use it anyway, because they are required to do so. This is in line with the observation that when using a system is mandatory, there is often little variance in usage, and thus it is difficult to predict the behavior empirically (Hartwick & Barki, 1994).

## The intention–behavior relationship in psychology

The intention–behavior relationship has also been the research subject of psychologists for a much longer time. While some of them think the link is strong and consistent, others suggest the effect of intention on future behavior is modest and the reliability of the effect is debatable (Sheeran, 2002). Here, we review some thoughts and findings of those others.

Warshaw & Davis (1985) argue that many human behaviors are unreasoned, that is, they are determined not by conscious intentions but by non-cognitive habits or mindless scripts. They further argue that actual behaviors can also be influenced by a variety of factors over and above the present intention, such as anticipated changes in intention, ability limitations, and possible environmental facilitators/constraints. In particular, events causing a change in intention may occur between the measurement of intention and the observation of behavior, and thus reduce the predictive accuracy of the measured intention. Similarly, environmental and/or personal impediments may prevent the behavior from being enacted. In line with the arguments, Warshaw & Davis (1985) suggest that present intention may not be the direct and effective determinant of future behavior.

Focusing on the variability in the intention–behavior relationship, Rhodes et al (2003) also suggest that intentions are not always tightly linked to what people really do. This is because intentions are often under cognitive control while actual behaviors are usually performed impulsively, even unconsciously. For this reason, Rhodes et al contend that researchers need to do more work on evaluating the accuracy of the data regarding intentions. Specifically, they should follow up to see if individuals surveyed actually perform the behavior of interest over the specified time periods.

The time gap between measurement of intention and performance of behavior also draws other researchers’ attention. In a study investigating the issues related to intention–behavior consistency, Pieters & Verplanken (1995) point out that when people change their mind during the time gap, the original intention will also no longer correspond with the actual behavior. That is, intention–behavior consistency will be very low and the behavior is unlikely to occur. Similarly, Sheeran & Orbell (1998) find that the intention–behavior relationship tends to diminish when the time gap between the two exceeds a few months.

As noted earlier, behaviors are often measured in a format that differs substantially from a seven-point Likert scale used to assess BI. Previous research suggests that such a difference may affect the intention–behavior relationship. For example, focusing on repeated behaviors, Courneya (1994) finds that employing distinct measures for BI and behavior can attenuate the correlation. This indicates that the intention–behavior relationship may vary across different measures of behavior (Borg & Gall, 1983). Relating this to system-use behavior, it suggests that the intention–usage relationship may vary across different usage measures. Nevertheless, IS researchers so far have given little attention to the potential variation of the intention–behavior relationship caused by the different methods of measuring system usage.

Synthesizing the above thoughts and findings, some issues regarding the intention–behavior relationship emerge. First, it appears that the type of behavior (e.g., reasoned or unreasoned) influences the degree of intention–behavior consistency. That is, if using an information system (e.g., surfing on the Internet) is unreasoned but determined by a non-cognitive habit, then the intention–behavior consistency will be very low and the behavior may not be predicted by the intention. Second, the time gap between forming intention and performing behavior may jeopardize the relationship. Third, environmental and/or personal factors not considered at the time of reporting intention may prevent a person from executing the behavior. Fourth, the method of measuring behavior may affect the magnitude of the intention–behavior relationship. That is, when system usage and BI are measured in substantially different ways, their correlation will be much lower than when they are measured in a similar way. This may well be that when common or similar methods are employed to measure two variables, measurement errors in the two scores covary, inducing an inflation of their observed correlation (Sharma et al, 2009). This phenomenon is known as common method variance (CMV) and is detailed in the ‘Discussion’ section. Fifth, usage is operationally and conceptually different from BI and has been examined separately from BI in many prior studies, such as Davis (1989), Adams et al (1992), Igbaria et al (1997), and Karahanna et al (2006). In short, our review indicates that the intention–behavior relationship may not be stable and unitary, but dynamic and multiple. Therefore, more extensive validation is needed to fully explore the link. As a step toward this direction, we undertake the following meta-analysis.

## The meta-analysis approach

Meta-analysis refers to a set of statistical techniques that combine results from many individual studies addressing the same research question (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990). Compared with narrative review – the traditional technique for drawing general conclusions from similar studies – meta-analysis is more objective, systematic, rigorous, and reliable (Glass, 1976; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). For this reason, the use of meta-analysis has been increasing in the last two decades (Field, 2001). Recently, meta-analysis has also been employed to resolve some issues in the IS field (e.g., Lee et al, 2003; King & He, 2006; Sabherwal et al, 2006; Sharma et al, 2009).

The current study utilizes the meta-analysis approach for several reasons. First, it is an established method for integrating findings of past independent studies examining the same research question (Sharma & Yetton, 2003). Second, it enables us to use appropriate statistical procedures to conduct important quantitative analyses (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990). Third, it allows previous non-significant or even contradictory results to contribute to a pooled conclusion (Sabherwal et al, 2006). Fourth, it is also a useful method to highlight gaps in our knowledge and to suggest further studies on the patterns found in the analysis (Lee et al, 2003). Fifth, it enables us to investigate the four different dependent variables simultaneously and thus helps us to address the key research questions of this paper. Finally, it provides us with the opportunity to gain useful insights into the relationships among PU, PEOU, BI, and system usage constructs; such insights are unlikely to be obtained from a single study.

## Sample

Because journals are more likely to publish studies with significant, hypothesis-supporting results (Rosenthal, 1979; Sharma & Yetton, 2003), it is vital to include studies from non-journal sources. Thus, books, dissertations, and conference proceedings are also the potential sources of studies to be included in this meta-analysis. To find studies, we searched bibliographic databases and electronic bibliographies in these sources. Examples of the bibliographic databases are Business Source Premier, ScienceDirect, ABI/INFORM, ProQuest Dissertation and Thesis, WorldCat Dissertation and Thesis, and those for proceedings of various conferences such as the International Conference on Information Systems and the

Americas Conference on Information Systems. We manually searched some journals whose back issues were unavailable in bibliographic databases. For the electronic searches, we used such key words as adoption, acceptance, technology acceptance model (TAM), use, usage, TAM, behavioral intention, perceived usefulness, and perceived ease of use. To locate additional studies, we also scanned bibliographies of the papers identified, and checked their potential for inclusion. This comprehensive search strategy enables us to reduce the source bias, maximize the number of studies, and thus increase the quality of this meta-analysis (Ma & Liu, 2004).

Studies were chosen if they satisfied the following criteria: (1) they operationalized PU/PEOU and BI/usage; (2) they reported sample size; and (3) they reported at least one type of correlations between PU/PEOU and BI/usage, or they reported other statistics that could be converted to correlations (see Wu & Lederer, 2009). Typical examples of such statistics are shared variance and covariance. The former can be transformed to correlation by calculating its square root, while the latter can be converted to correlation by applying the formula, $r = ( \mathrm { c o v } ( x , y ) / \sigma _ { x } \sigma _ { y } )$ , where r is the correlation, $\mathsf { c o v } ( x , \gamma )$ is the covariance, and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation.

To ensure the uniqueness of each study chosen for this research, we carefully compared its description and statistical data with those of others. In this way, this research is in keeping with an important assumption that each study included in a meta-analysis should be independent of others (Ma & Liu, 2004). Specifically, if two or more studies reported in different articles, proceedings, and dissertations were based on the same data set, they were treated as one study, and only one was chosen. On the other hand, when a study presented multiple data sets collected from the same sample, the data sets were combined by simple averaging. According to Heneman (1986), this is in line with the assumption. On the contrary, when a study presented multiple data sets collected from different samples, each data set was treated as an independent study. Again, such treatment is appropriate and does not violate the assumption (Hunter et al, 1982). Finally, when a study presented two or more data sets based on the same sample but for different dependent variables (i.e., BI and different usage constructs), the data sets were treated as separate studies for the purpose of this research.

Appendixes A, B, and C show the identified BI and usage studies and the data collected from them. The final sample of this meta-analysis includes 169 journal articles, 28 conference proceedings, and 21 dissertations. All these articles, proceedings, and dissertations contributed 195 studies investigating BI, 103 studies examining system usage, and 38 studies focusing on both. Of the 103 usage studies, 9 used system logs to track actual usage, 23 asked subjects to self-report usage, and 71 employed ordinal scales to measure usage.

## Data analysis

We employ the following procedures to analyze data and report findings. First, we aggregately analyze the correlations among PU, PEOU, BI and usage combined as a whole (i.e., the three usage measures are combined into a single variable). Second, we compare the correlations of interest and examine the explained variances in BI and usage. We then concentrate our attention on three different usage constructs and synthesize their correlations with prominent antecedents. Finally, we analyze and compare these correlations as well as the variances explained in these usage constructs.

Analyzing the correlations for BI and usage Because different individual studies are usually conducted with different sample sizes, the best estimate of average r-value is not the simple mean across studies but a weighted average in which each correlation is weighted by the sample size in that study (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990). Therefore, the best estimate of the population correlation is given by the formula $\bar { r } ~ = ( \sum [ N _ { i } r _ { i } ] / \sum [ N _ { i } ] )$ , where r- is the weighted average correlation, $r _ { i }$ is the correlation in study $i , \ N _ { i }$ is the sample size in study $i ,$ and thus $N _ { i } r _ { i }$ stands for weighted correlation for study i.

## Validity of meta-analysis

Previous research suggests that the validity of a metaanalysis depends on several factors, including the thoroughness of the literature search, the selection of studies for inclusion, the use of appropriate data analysis methods, and the sizes of the meta-sample and the total sample (Cook & Campbell, 1979; Hwang, 1996; Sharma & Yetton, 2003). While meta-sample size refers to the number of individual studies included in a meta-analysis (i.e., Number of observations in the tables of this study), total sample size refers to the total number of subjects who have participated in one of these studies. Among these factors, meta-sample size may be an issue of this study, because it is as low as 5 (for reported usage, see Table 3). However, the total sample size of these five studies is large, with a value of 1775. Researchers suggest that total sample size may be more important because it is the key to the accuracy of the estimate of mean correlation (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990; Hwang, 1996). Therefore, given the large total sample sizes (which can be calculated by using the data in Table 3), the statistical conclusion drawn in this study is likely to be valid and reliable.

## Discussion

This research is motivated by the need for a deeper understanding of the distinct dependent variables: BI and different usage constructs. The main objective of this study is to accurately grasp how these variables diverge from each other and thus to answer the four main research questions. Using the data collected from the literature, we employ aggregate, mean-difference, and variance analyses to investigate the relationships between these dependent variables and their prominent antecedents – PU and PEOU. The results enable us to arrive at the following answers to the research questions.

## Distinctions between BI and usage

The aggregate analysis and t-test results in Tables 1 and 2 show that PU and PEOU are much more strongly correlated with BI than with usage, suggesting that PU and PEOU have a much stronger effect on BI than on usage. This suggestion is further supported by the finding that PU and PEOU together explain 33% of the variance in BI and only 14% in usage.

We also find that for BI studies, none (0%) of the 189 observations (see Table 1) report insignificant PU–BI correlation, and only 13 (7%) of the 175 observations report insignificant PEOU–BI correlation; however, for usage studies, 12 (12%) of the 102 observations report insignificant PU–Usage correlation, and 19 (20%) of the 95 observations report insignificant PEOU–Usage correlation. These numbers also lend support to the distinction between BI and usage. That is, BI has a much higher correlation with both PU and PEOU than does usage, and thus more variance in BI than in usage can be explained by PU and PEOU.

## Is BI a good surrogate for usage?

An interesting phenomenon is that most studies employ BI rather than usage as the only ultimate dependent variable. One of the main causes of the phenomenon is that most IS researchers hold the notion that BI serves as a good surrogate for usage and tells the whole story of user behavior. This phenomenon is confirmed by a roughly 1:2 ratio of usage studies to BI studies included in this meta-analysis.

The results of this study not only suggest that PU and PEOU are more highly correlated with BI than with usage, but also indicate that the chance of yielding insignificant results for usage studies nearly triples that for BI studies (12% vs 0% and 20% vs 7%). These findings strongly suggest that when BI is used as the ultimate dependent variable, significant, hypothesis-supporting results are very likely to be obtained; however, when usage is employed, significance may not be observed for the predicted paths. In other words, system usage does not necessarily occur with BI, and even if a research model can predict BI, it may still not be able to predict system usage. This implication is of great importance to IS researchers. It suggests that BI may not serve as a good surrogate for usage, and it may not be appropriate and justifiable to study user behavior without assessing system usage. It further suggests that to conduct beneficial and informed behavioral research, scholars should focus their main attention on usage. They should follow-up to see if individuals surveyed actually perform the behavior of interest. From a perspective of model building and testing, they should incorporate usage into their theoretical framework and directly investigate whether usage can be predicted. Meanwhile, editors, reviewers, and readers need to be highly circumspect of behavioral studies not investigating usage but only individuals’ intentions.

## Divergences among different usage constructs

This study finds that among the three usage constructs, assessed usage is the most and actual usage is the least highly correlated with BI. This divergence is in line with the view advanced by Borg & Gall (1983): intention– behavior relationship may vary across different measures of behavior. Our aggregate analysis shows that the correlations between BI and each of the three usage constructs – actual usage, reported usage, and assessed usage – are 0.35, 0.41, and 0.49, respectively. These numbers clearly demonstrate the difference among the three usage constructs. A t-test further reveals the significance of the difference between the first and third r-values (Table 5), and thus it provides additional evidence for this divergence.

Similar to the above divergence, among the three usage constructs, assessed usage is the most and actual usage is the least highly correlated with PU and PEOU. This finding is mainly supported by the results of variance analysis. Specifically, we find that PU and PEOU together explain 17% of the variance in assessed usage, 9% in reported usage, and 7% in actual usage. It is also partially supported by the t-tests and aggregate analysis, which establish that the correlations between assessed usage and PU/PEOU are higher than the corresponding correlations for actual usage and reported usage. This finding thus suggests that not only the results derived from objective and subjective measures of usage may diverge greatly from each other, but also the results based on different subjective measures.

## Which usage construct(s) should be the target?

This research finds that the effects of PU, PEOU, and BI on usage depend upon which usage construct is being studied. The results in Figures 3–5 show an interesting pattern; that is, when usage construct shifts from actual usage to reported usage and then to assessed usage, the variance explained in it becomes larger and larger. This pattern suggests that statistically significant findings are most likely to be obtained when assessed usage is being studied, and they are least likely to be observed when actual usage is being examined. This indicates that to obtain hypothesis-supporting results, researchers need to use assessed usage rather than actual usage or reported usage as the ultimate dependent variable.

We contend that such a pattern may be due to the different methods of measuring system usage. As discussed in the ‘Intention and behavior’ section, actual usage is evaluated by using system log records, reported usage is measured via user estimates, and assessed usage is measured through an ordinal scale. Of these three different methods, the third one is closest to a sevenpoint Likert scale used to measure the three antecedents – PU, PEOU, and BI. Past research suggests that the higher the correspondence of the scales, the stronger the correlation of the variables (Borg & Gall, 1983; Courneya, 1994). Thus, it is both logical and reasonable to find that assessed usage is more highly correlated with the antecedents than are the other two usage constructs. Similarly, the reason that most correlations for reported usage are higher than those for actual usage may well be that research participants tend to over- or underestimate their past usage to fit in with their reported intention to use or their reported perceptions of usefulness and ease of use (Collopy, 1996; Devaraj & Kohli, 2003).

It is important to note that scale correspondence raises the issue of CMV. CMV refers to the variance that is attributable to the measurement method rather than to the constructs that the measures represent (Zhang & Mao, 2008). Because the method measuring assessed usage is similar to those measuring PU, PEOU, and BI, the risk of common method variance is very high. Likewise, the high correlations between PU/PEOU and BI may also be attributed to common method variance (Straub & Burton-Jones, 2007; Sharma et al, 2009). While these scholars suggest that CMV can be a serious problem to behavioral studies in IS field, others conclude that the bias caused by CMV is not substantial and does not pose a major validity threat to the published findings (Malhotra et al, 2006). These mixed conclusions indicate the necessity of further studies of CMV within the context of user behavior. An ideal way of doing such future research would be to investigate both actual usage and assessed usage in a single study and compare the results. To our surprise, very few studies have so far investigated system usage in this way (Szajna, 1996).

The above discussion brings us right to the answer to the fourth main research question: Theoretically, which of these usage constructs should be the target of future research? The answer is that future research should target both actual usage and assessed usage at the same time. Future studies on actual usage are urgently needed because they are very scarce in the literature (only nine so far). Moreover, investigating actual usage motivates researchers to gather data from a system log that is independent of the scales measuring other variables. Therefore, researchers may minimize the potential detrimental effect of CMV. On the other hand, by studying assessed usage, they are likely to find higher correlations and thus increase their chance of obtaining significant results. Besides these benefits, studying the two usage constructs simultaneously also enables researchers to compare, validate, and combine the results, and thus bring to light the true relationships between system usage and its antecedents.

## Implications for researchers

This study not only yields preliminary answers to the main research questions but also has important implications for researchers. The current study finds that the correlations between PU/PEOU/BI and usage vary across different usage measures. This finding suggests that to better understand system-use behavior, researchers need to enhance their conceptualization and measure of system usage. Therefore, it may be beneficial for future research to use a richer measure, which allows scholars to measure assessed usage in multiple dimensions based on the study context. For example, Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) suggest that assessed usage may be measured in two additional dimensions, employment of the system (cognitive absorption) and use of the system in the task (deep structure usage). Developing such richer measures requires researchers to use a staged approach to reconceptualize system usage. Burton-Jones & Straub (2006) offer evidence that by using a richer measure, researchers can obtain more persuasive and meaningful results in a study of system usage.

## Implications for practitioners

The findings have important implications for practitioners as well. Focusing on the measures of business value of IT, Chidambaram et al (2005) have identified 31 widely used metrics in IS practice, and two of them are related to system usage. In particular, the two metrics are mainly used to track actual usage: CPU hours utilized and hours logged per employee. The findings of the current research suggest that such metrics of system usage may provide incomplete and/or inaccurate information about how system-use behavior is influenced by user perceptions of usefulness and ease-of-use of the system. We therefore suggest that practitioners choose system usage metrics that also incorporate measures evaluating assessed usage such as the extent of using the system for different tasks.

## Main findings

BI and usage variables have long been regarded as central to behavioral research, and we offer a careful exploration on how they diverge from each other, a rational guideline on which usage construct to choose, and a scholarly investigation into whether it is justifiable to study user behavior without assessing usage but with BI as the only ultimate dependent variable. The analyses and tests that we have conducted here take a systematic approach to addressing and resolving these issues, and thus provide a crucial step forward in understanding these core variables, their divergences, and their relationships with prominent antecedents.

System usage should not be substituted by BI but should be included as a required ultimate dependent variable. We find that the variance explained in usage is much less than that explained in BI, suggesting that a research model predicting BI may not predict system usage. Given IS researchers’ primary objective of predicting usage and identifying its determinants (Straub & Burton-Jones, 2007), it is ironic and perhaps unsettling that most extant studies have focused on BI but not on system usage. In an attempt to reverse this phenomenon, the current study highlights the need and urgency for researchers to turn their major attention to system usage.

We provide new insights into the three conceptualizations of system usage and to justify which of them should be the target of future research. Understanding the conceptualizations of system usage is an important step in the direction of better combining theories with empirical studies (Burton-Jones & Straub, 2006). We treat the three conceptualizations of usage not as different measures of a single construct, but rather as three different usage constructs. By doing so, we theoretically differentiate these conceptualizations and empirically compare their results. More importantly, the insights garnered through this research help us to decide on which usage constructs to focus in the future, and thus may reorient IS scholars toward more fruitful and illuminating studies of user behavior.

This study finds that researchers can get higher r-values from their studies by improving the conceptualization and measurement of variables. The current study finds that higher r-values are more likely to be obtained when similar measurement methods are used for system usage and its antecedents. This finding indicates that scale correspondence may be critical to the results of a study. According to Courneya (1994), this may well be that the variation obtained in a dependent variable will be congruent with the variation obtained in its independent variables when scale correspondence is achieved. The finding thus adds substantially to the pool of constructive insights that are unlikely to be visible from an individual survey study.

## Limitations

The findings and contributions of this study should be interpreted in light of a particular limitation. In the current study, the results on actual usage in Figure 3 are based on meta-samples of 8/9 individual studies, and the results on reported usage in Figure 4 are based on metasamples of 5/22/23 individual studies (see Table 3). As noted earlier, these numbers are relatively small compared with the meta-sample sizes for assessed usage in Figure 5 (63/71) and BI in Figure 2 (175/189, see Table 1). Although the small meta-sample sizes are comparable to those of other meta-analysis studies (Sharma & Yetton,

2003; Ma & Liu, 2004), they may moderate the generalizability of some of our findings.

## Failsafe N and sensitivity analyses

For any relationship of interest, failsafe N refers to the number of additional studies (with non-significant results) needed to render the results for that relationship non-significant at a predefined level $( P { \leqslant } 0 . 0 5$ in this study) (Williams & Livingstone 1994). To obtain failsafe N, we apply the formula $N = k ( \bar { r _ { k } } / \bar { r _ { c } } - 1 )$ , where k is the number of studies included in a meta-analysis, $\bar { r _ { k } }$ is the mean of the correlations, and $\bar { r _ { c } }$ is the predefined value and is determined by the formula $t = \bar { r _ { c } } / \sqrt { ( 1 - \bar { r _ { c } } ^ { 2 } ) / ( n - 2 ) }$ , where $t = 1 . 9 6$ when P is set at less than or equal to 0.05 and n is the average sample size (Hunter & Schmidt, 1990). By employing the minimum average sample size (199, see Table 1), $\bar { r _ { c } }$ of this study can be estimated at 0.138. The failsafe Ns range from 89 to 564, with an average of 277. These large failsafe Ns provide confidence in the robustness of this metaanalysis with respect to the possible exclusion of studies with non-significant results.

Sensitivity analysis allows researchers to investigate the robustness of a meta-analysis by looking more narrowly at a drawn subset of the included studies. To examine the sensitivity of the correlations analyzed in this metaanalysis, a subset of studies has been selected based on the fact that they use a nonstudent sample. It is widely believed that student sample studies may lack generalizability and nonstudent sample is perhaps a better surrogate for general populations. Table D1 shows the results of the sensitivity analysis by comparing the correlations obtained from the nonstudent sample studies (i.e., the subset) with those obtained from all the studies included in this meta-analysis (i.e., the complete data set). The results suggest that exclusion of student sample studies affects the correlations only marginally and there is no statistically significant difference between the subset and the complete data set. The sensitivity analysis thus provides additional evidence for the robustness of this meta-analysis.
