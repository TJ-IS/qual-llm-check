# Price Mechanism for Knowledge Transfer: An Integrative Theory

MING-HUI HUANG, ERIC T.G. WANG, AND ABRAHAM SEIDMANN

ABSTRACT: Knowledge transferred in the open market via a price mechanism enjoys the benefits of avoiding internal competition, learning from external competitors, and accumulating diversified knowledge. In the market, users can access a repository of knowledge for a single price (repository pricing) or knowledge items in the repository can be sold individually (knowledge pricing). However, users have been found to prefer repository pricing but not the knowledge in the repository. This irrationality can cause market failure because users derive a suboptimal level of utility from the knowledge repository, and vendors have contradictory pricing and knowledge strategies. We empirically examine a joint explanation from two competing theoretical perspectives that accounts for this inconsistency nicely: The mental accounting perspective endorses repository pricing because it entices users with the benefits of the whole repository, whereas the transaction decoupling perspective finds expression in individually priced knowledge because it prevents the discrete benefit of knowledge from becoming obscure. By integrating the two theoretical perspectives and considering price, knowledge, and user characteristics simultaneously, the results offer important implications for the market transfer of knowledge. Repository pricing attracts users and is essential to initiate the transfer process, whereas knowledge pricing generates knowledge preference and is thus an effective approach for learning.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: knowledge markets, knowledge pricing, knowledge transfer, mental accounting, multinomial logit model, ordered logit model, transaction decoupling.

CAN A PRICE MECHANISM FACILITATE KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER in an economic market? If so, are knowledge users always rational in their price and knowledge preferences? This paper provides answers to these two key questions. We take an economic approach to examine two competing theoretical perspectives in explaining users’ irrational price and knowledge preferences. Knowledge users’ preferences for commercial repositories and the reasons for their inconsistent preferences bring new insights for the market transfer of knowledge.

The management of knowledge transfer has focused traditionally on collaborative organizational contexts because of the contextual dependence of knowledge, such as multiple departmental units’ collaboration for software process improvement [71], inhouse knowledge sourcing [28], virtual teams [30], virtual organizations [52], networks of practice [83], or information and communication technology (ICT)–enabled virtual collaboration [69]. Collaboration provides the context for knowledge transfer, yet the knowledge gained by collaboration can be used for competition. For example, Menon and Pfeffer [59] find that users prefer outsider knowledge because such knowledge avoids the status costs of validating a direct internal competitor. A market mechanism can be a viable approach for knowledge transfer within [15] and across [31] organizations to overcome this competitive collaboration dilemma. In addition to the advantage of avoiding internal competition, a market mechanism represents an opportunity to learn from external competitors [60] and to accumulate more abundant and diversified knowledge because vendors and users share at most weak-tie relationships [26, 46].

We investigate the viability of a market mechanism for knowledge transfer that has been important for information markets. Although distinctions have conventionally been drawn among data, information, and knowledge, and different information systems (IS) have been developed to fit these disparate categories, such distinctions may, in fact, be too rigid and limit the transfer of knowledge (e.g., [14, 81]) or an unambiguous distinction is not available [27]. For example, Desouza [14] argues that the distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge is tricky. Tacit knowledge resides in a person’s mind; hence all knowledge contained in databases is explicit in nature. However, not all knowledge in databases is truly explicit; for example, suggestions on problem-solving methodologies or comments on personal experiences of software design. Gray and Durcikova [27], therefore, make no distinction between information and knowledge repositories in investigating technical support knowledge repositories.

In the knowledge market, knowledge is managed as an economic good that is captured in knowledge repositories to be transferred between users and vendors [30]. Examples of knowledge goods are documents, best practices, experiences, case studies, research reports, working papers, slide shows, blueprints, scientific journal articles, reusable artifacts, and downloadable software [2, 15, 23, 28, 49, 56]. The knowledge market reflects a repository-based approach to knowledge management that emphasizes the codification and central storage of knowledge to facilitate the reuse of it through access to the codified expertise [15, 23, 39]. For example, KShop, the central knowledge portal of Infosys, provides a single entry point to the company’s codified knowledge [23]. The technical support knowledge repositories discussed by Gray and Durcikova [27] store codified customer support knowledge, which serves as a common resource for analysts to solve customers’ product- or service-related problems. Knowledge repositories can take the form of intranets, corporate Web sites, information portals, databases, software packages, or expert systems [2, 27, 47, 55]. Several public knowledge markets, such as Knexa.com, Uclue.com, and bananask.com are in operation today [16].

Adjusting the prices users pay for knowledge is an incentive to mobilize the transfer of knowledge [10, 14, 31]. Bock et al. [10] identify economic rewards as important extrinsic motivators for knowledge sharing. Every organization they interviewed had implemented monetary incentives, points toward promotion, or both. Many firms use proxies, such as knowledge purchase points and coupons, to represent real monetary value for knowledge [15]. Real-world examples include Siemens’s ShareNet project, which uses explicit rewards; Samsung Life Insurance’s knowledge mileage program, which uses redemption points [10]; and Fujitsu’s knowledge management market system for engineers, which uses internal transfer prices [16] to motivate knowledge transfer. In addition to these corporate knowledge repositories, examples of popular knowledge repositories for individual users include the New York Times’s TimesSelect, which offers an annual subscription, a monthly subscription, or single-article purchase from its archive; the Wall Street Journal Online, which offers standardized, unlimited annual access and personalized tracking for different categories of content up to a certain number of items; and Harvard Business Review, which offers annual basic and premium subscriptions with the latter, including access to its archive. A volume discount is offered when more articles or cases are purchased.

However, previous research suggests that users’ price preferences for knowledge repositories may not be rational. Users have been found to overwhelmingly prefer repository pricing (pricing knowledge at the repository level, where users pay one price to access the entire repository) to knowledge pricing (pricing knowledge at the individual knowledge level, where users select and pay for units of knowledge) (e.g., [20, 21, 42, 43]). However, repository pricing is not cheaper for users who do not use the repository sufficiently [37, 42], and this preference may not translate into knowledge preferences [20] (i.e., the knowledge retrieved may not be perceived as useful and satisfactory, and hence users may have a lower intention to recommend and repurchase knowledge from the repository). For example, monetary rewards have been found to motivate participation in knowledge transfer, but not the quality of the knowl edge [23] or a favorable attitude toward sharing [10]. Garud and Kumaraswamy [23] report Infosys’s unsuccessful use of a knowledge currency unit (KCU) to promote its knowledge portal, KShop. Within a year, over 130,000 KCU’s were generated, but the explosive growth in the number of knowledge assets only overloaded the KShop with lower-quality knowledge that resulted in higher search costs for users. The empirical results of Bock et al. refute the hypothesis that “the greater the anticipated extrinsic rewards are, the more favorable the attitude toward knowledge sharing will be” [10, p. 92]. Instead, a significant result in the opposite direction is obtained.

How can we explain this deviation in price and knowledge preferences? The mental accounting and transaction decoupling perspectives, both built upon the sunk-cost effect, offer competing explanations. The two perspectives focus on reasons why users may not be rationally motivated to choose and use knowledge. The sunk-cost effect is “a greater tendency to continue an endeavor once an investment in money, time, or effort has been made” [4, p. 124]. This is displaying irrational attention to nonrecoverable past costs while making current decisions [73]. The mental accounting perspective [79] suggests a preference for repository pricing over knowledge pricing because it focuses users’ attention on the potential benefit of the whole knowledge repository. Alternatively, the transaction decoupling perspective [74] suggests a preference for individually priced knowledge over repository-priced knowledge because it brings users’ attention to the immediate benefit of individual units of knowledge. By empirically testing the two effects, we offer a satisfactory explanation for users price and knowledge preferences and predict pricing models that address incentives for the transfer of knowledge.

## Theoretical Foundations

BELOW, WE DELINEATE THE OPERATION OF THE TWO competing perspectives in moderating price and knowledge preferences. The two mechanisms agree in that price and knowledge preferences are influenced by sunk costs, but differ in whether attention to sunk costs leads to increased or decreased preferences. Mental accounting predicts a preference for repository pricing but not knowledge priced in this manner, whereas transaction decoupling predicts a preference for knowledge that is priced individually but not the pricing option. The following discussion shows that this disconnection between price and knowledge preferences can be rigorously explained by the two mechanisms.

## Mental Accounting for Price Preferences

The mental accounting mechanism [35, 79, 80]<sup>1</sup> increases the preference for repository pricing. There are two decision stages involved in mental accounting—opening an account and balancing the account. When users face the choice of paying for the entire repository or paying for individual knowledge items, they assess the anticipated benefits of the two options, then make their choices based on the total expected benefits in order to achieve a desired return on their expenditures [35]. The benefit of the entire repository is attractive because of the perception of greater value, compared to individually priced knowledge. Consumer “bias for the whole” has been observed such that the whole is preferred to equivalent parts [61].

Reward pricing decreases the preference for repository pricing because it imposes additional cognitive burdens on users for tracking the account balance. After opening a mental account, users’ mind-sets shift from looking at the benefits to balancing the account. The account is initially negative, reflecting the price paid for accessing the entire repository, but it moves toward the positive as the benefits of knowledge are realized [79, 80]. To get the reward, users need to track their mental accounts closely. This introduces the costs of monitoring to users that vendors bear in repository pricing [76]. Hence, repository pricing with reward (e.g., typically a two-part tariff or a fixed-up-to option) presents users with a trade-off between costs of using the repository and the savings obtained [62].

The accountability of the mental accounting perspective for repository pricing is subject to user heterogeneity. Several studies have shown that user characteristics are likely to affect price preferences, although they provide insufficient indication of the direction of impact. For example, gender differences have been demonstrated in price sensitivity [54] and the willingness to take risk [84], and income may influence the choice of price nonlinearly because of more economic resources for consumption [62, 66] but higher stakes in wealth risks [22]. Another factor to consider is that the level and field of expertise are the basis on which expectations concerning the value of knowledge are formed [47].

The mental accounting mechanism decreases the preference for knowledge that is priced at the repository level. When users pay for the entire repository, they may wish to continue retrieving knowledge from it until the knowledge is exhausted, whereby loss through sunk costs would be minimized [63]. However, repository pricing, despite its attractiveness, presents several difficulties for users in balancing the mental account. First, although a repository typically offers access to enormous amounts of knowledge, users are unlikely to exhaust the entire repository to balance the mental account. This is the argument made in Geng et al. [24] that the correct unit of analysis for information-good bundles should be the total price, not the average price, because the average of large numbers of random variables is vague and unrealistic. Second, when choosing repository pricing, users are likely to overestimate their demand for knowledge [42], which is bound to incur sunk costs. For example, flat rates are believed to encourage waste and lead to economically inefficient subsidies of heavy users [43]. Third, the retrieved knowledge may not always be valuable. For the unlimited usage benefit of repository pricing, users may do a simple key word search and download a whole bunch of knowledge items, then screen to see which ones are useful. Therefore, the overall precision rate for the downloaded knowledge to meet demands would be lower.<sup>2</sup> Fourth, one way of keeping the mental account balanced is to limit usage. This is especially true for repository pricing with rewards. Mental budgeting is found to result in underconsumption because consumers are conservative in setting their budgets to avoid sunk costs [36]. It has been shown that in facing “fixed-up-to” nonlinear pricing, mobile phone service subscribers trade off between overage and underage, and may knowingly or unknowingly go beyond the flat-rate range, thus paying the overlimit charge [53]. In this pricing option, consumers often limit their usage to less than their desired usage rates because of their unwillingness to pay more than a certain amount for the information service [20].

## Transaction Decoupling for Knowledge Preferences

The transaction decoupling perspective<sup>3</sup> [25, 74], on the other hand, provides an explanation for the irrationality of “preferring repository pricing but not the knowledge in the repository.” Repository pricing, although enticing users with the benefits of the entire repository, leads to a disassociation or “decoupling” of a cost from its corresponding benefit, because users find it more difficult or are unwilling to identify the costs and benefits of individual units of knowledge. Users, therefore, are likely to ignore the sunk cost and use only part of the repository.

When knowledge is priced individually, the cost of any single unit is coupled with its benefit; this keeps the price paid for the unit fresh in users’ memories, so the users are more likely to use and appreciate the knowledge. Specifically, several factors contribute to this recoupling. First, the notion of “recovery through use” of sunk costs [63] suggests that when paying for individual items of knowledge, users are more careful with their searches and more selective with their actual knowledge retrievals in order to minimize sunk costs. Hence, every paid item is more likely to be useful and relevant, which leads to higher knowledge preference.<sup>4</sup> Hardy et al. [33] observe that many users in the academic and publishing communities favor per unit pricing because it allows easy monitoring of the actual use of materials. From the vendor’s perspective, usage-based pricing also allows vendors to more effectively implement quality differentiation to better meet user demands [43]. Second, the psychology of “underestimation bias” of usage for choosing knowledge pricing [42] is likely to operate to increase knowledge preference. Those users who initially underestimate their demand and choose knowledge pricing are more likely to realize later that there are many useful items to download. They will appreciate the usefulness of the knowledge to a greater extent for the fresh benefits associated with those items. Third, the short time lag between paying and consuming strengthens the cost–benefit association that guarantees the benefit will be fully realized. Knowledge pricing does not require users to allocate funds in advance for the amount of knowledge they will consume, and thus it avoids underconsumption induced by mental budgeting.

The knowledge characteristics of large repositories and complementary content facilitate the transaction decoupling effect that leads to knowledge preferences. A sizable repository facilitates the coupling effect because it increases the chance of the knowledge in the repository meeting users’ needs; thus, once retrieved, the unit of knowledge will result in a more favorable preference. For example, Bakos and Brynjolfsson [7] demonstrate that simply bundling similar information goods together has a higher chance of capturing consumers’ valuations of the goods than selling the information separately.

Complementary content increases the utility of consuming any additional unit of knowledge in the repository [7], which enhances the benefit of the knowledge to users and increases the preference for the knowledge. Estelami [19] argues that complementary products present buyers with a preengineered combination designed to operate as a system. This increases the need for consumers to use all of the products. Harlam et al. [34] find that consumers’ intentions to purchase were higher for bundles composed of complements than for bundles of similar or unrelated products. In a study of the software industry, Lee [45] shows that bundling a supplemental good with a base good generates welfare enhancements.

In addition to price and knowledge characteristics, user characteristics introduce other variances to the use of knowledge in a repository. For example, in the context of knowledge sharing, it has been shown that there are individual user differences in the decision to use knowledge management systems [78]. User characteristics serve as control variables that remove the additional source of variance in knowledge preference models because the information retrieval skills of users and their knowledge levels influence their evaluations of the knowledge.<sup>5</sup> Specifically, it has been found that those who consider themselves more knowledgeable and have a longer tenure in the field participate more actively in knowledge transfer [83], and prior work experience in IS has been demonstrated to explain substantial variation in the types of mechanisms used for effective knowledge transfer [71].

## The Conceptual Framework

THE ANALYSIS OF KNOWLEDGE PRICING PRESENTED here adopts the knowledge market perspective. A knowledge market is a collection of users and vendors who interact to determine the price of knowledge. Knowledge is transferred as an economic good in the market in the form of a commercial repository. Users retrieve explicit knowledge and integrate it with their tacit knowledge to aid decision making. Vendors capture tacit knowledge, convert it to explicit knowledge, and make it available on the market.

We hypothesize that users first decide on their price preferences, which are functions of price characteristics and are subject to the influence of user characteristics. In this price choice stage, price characteristics obscure the issue of sunk costs in different manners and to different degrees. Then, users respond to price incentives for knowledge market participation (differently, in keeping with their individual characteristics).

After paying for and using the knowledge, users then decide on knowledge preferences, which are functions of price and knowledge characteristics. In the knowledge usage stage, price characteristics frame knowledge preferences for the prepaid sunk costs. Knowledge characteristics concerning whether a repository is sizable and whether the knowledge is complementary exaggerate the impact of the sunk costs. User characteristics are controlled to avoid double counting their impact on the two stages and to single out the impact of price and knowledge characteristics on users preferred pricing and evaluations of knowledge.

Price and knowledge preferences are moderated by mechanisms of mental accounting and transaction decoupling. The mental accounting effect increases price preferences but decreases knowledge preferences, whereas the transaction decoupling effect increases knowledge preferences but decreases price preferences. The following price and knowledge preference hypotheses sum up this joint prediction:

Hypothesis 1 (the price preference hypothesis): The mental accounting perspective will be more accountable for the preference for repository pricing; however, a volume reward decreases this price preference. This accountability is subject to user heterogeneity.

Hypothesis 2 (the knowledge preference hypothesis): The transaction decoupling perspective will be more accountable for the preference for individually priced knowledge; this is especially so for a repository that is large and contains complementary knowledge.

The two sunk-cost effects are unobservable, but their effects on price and knowledge preferences are observable. This conceptual framework is shown in Figure 1. The components of, and the theoretical support for, the framework are described below.

## The Knowledge Market

A knowledge market is an environment in which users and vendors trade their knowledge via price mechanisms [15, 16, 31, 47]. The market can be a Web site or portal where knowledge is traded (e.g., [23]).

The users and vendors, also known as knowledge workers, interact in this environment to determine the price of knowledge [37, 47, 85]. The users are individuals and organizations that pay to retrieve knowledge from vendors. They require knowledge that has been decontextualized, which can then be recontextualized for their unique settings [51].

The vendors make knowledge available for public access and can also create original knowledge content (being vertically integrated with producers). For example, research articles capture the tacit knowledge of those who produce knowledge, such as scientists, and make it available for users in commercial knowledge repositories such as journals and databases.

The economic good for exchange in the market is the explicit knowledge that can be transferred effectively through media such as databases [14, 30]. We do not make a distinction between warehouses and repositories of data, or between information and knowledge, but concern ourselves with their tangible and codified representations. A knowledge repository stores representations of knowledge as information in databases. These representations include online newspapers, magazines, journals, and searchable databases, as well as services offering education, consulting, expertise, and ideas. The Privacy Knowledge Base, for example, is an online database that provides in-depth coverage of privacy practices and data protection [70], and the ProQuest information service is a compilation of numerous databases that offer business and economic data, information, and knowledge.

Commercial repositories available in the market transfer knowledge beyond the context in which it is created. These repositories are becoming essential sources of knowledge. The market facilitates the movement of knowledge from its point of generation or codification to the point of use [31], where it contributes to disclosure, dissemination, transmission, and communication of knowledge [47, 69]. In the market, knowledge is transferred from vendors to users through market transactions.

Knowledge markets can exist within or across organizations, depending on whether users and vendors belong to the same organization [47]. Our research concerns knowledge transfer in the external market, with prices serving as the transfer mechanism. Knowledge markets enhance the transfer of knowledge by expanding users’ reach to more extended, although perhaps weaker, connections that expose users to more new ideas [2].

## Price Characteristics

Prices influence users’ willingness to access repositories and vendors’ willingness to provide them. The difficulties of motivating employees to use a knowledge repository have been well documented (e.g., [10, 27, 47]). Many firms use proxies to represent real monetary value for knowledge, including knowledge purchase points, coupons, or points toward promotion. For example, one financial organization provides its trading analyst free lunch coupons for signing up during the first week of opening the knowledge market [15].

Knowledge can be priced at the repository level, where users pay to access the entire repository (repository pricing), or at the individual knowledge level, where users select and pay for units of knowledge (knowledge pricing). The two pricing approaches allow knowledge to be exchanged as individual entities or as a collection of interrelated knowledge bundles [15]. For example, a customer of IBM’s zSeries software can opt to pay a flat fee for unlimited usage, or pay for actual software usage as tracked by a reporting tool [76].

In repository pricing, users make an advance payment to access the entire content of a knowledge repository, pay according to the length of the time they are connected to the repository, or purchase a package service of knowledge assembled in a repository using CD-ROMs [8, 11, 32, 33, 37]. Price is not related to the amount of knowledge searched for or retrieved. For example, the Wall Street Journal Online offers unrestricted access for a fixed annual fee. RealOne SuperPass, by Real Networks, offers digital contents as a single online service [24]. Common variations in repository pricing are to offer volume rewards to motivate purchases, such as a two-part tariff with one-time subscription charges, in addition to variable usage charges [53, 76, 85], and forward buying at a reduced price [32]. For example, a “fixed-up-to” pricing charges a fixed access fee up to a certain usage level and shifts to usage-based prices beyond that level [53]; the Online Computer Library Center (OCLC) provides information services and industry research reports at both fixed-fee and usage-based prices [76]; and academic articles charge institutional subscribers on an annual basis and individual users on a per article basis.

Alternatively, in knowledge pricing, users pay a fixed fee for each unit of knowledge (e.g., a record or a successful search) viewed or downloaded [32, 33, 37, 85]. The unit of knowledge is a discrete, logical unit that provides the required knowledge, which can be a page, a report, or a project (see, e.g., [37]). For example, corporate software is priced based on the total processing speed of the servers on which the software is licensed to run, and Apple’s iTunes music service is priced on a per song basis [76]. Similarly, knowledge pricing can be offered with volume rewards such that the more knowledge used, the lower the fee per unit of knowledge at the margin [11]. Reward pricing encourages consumption by reducing user cost, and has been extensively used in marketing (e.g., [19, 74, 75, 86]) to set prices based on actual usage that promises users a reduced price on additional items of knowledge used.

## Knowledge Characteristics

In addition to prices, user preferences depend on knowledge characteristics such as repository size and variety of content. Size refers to the number of units of knowledge in a repository. Thousands of knowledge items are offered in successful and thriving knowledge markets. Large repositories may entail higher search costs for users in locating units of knowledge [8, 23] and create a poverty of attention [15], but such repositories may fulfill user needs more effectively and provide extra utility [3, 7]. For example, when third-party add-in products are bundled with base software, such as operating systems, for those consumers who want add-ins, they value the bundle higher; however, for those who do not want add-ins, the sum value of the base and add-in software is less than if they are sold separately [18].

Knowledge content in a repository can be similar and unrelated [82, p. 249] so that the utility of consuming additional units of content from a repository decreases [7]. It is the norm rather than the exception that users’ average value for consuming a stream of information goods, Web sites, weather forecasts, music, and news declines with the number consumed [24]. Complementary content, on the other hand, is interrelated and interdependent so that the repository provides a greater total value than the sum of the values of the individual units [6]. Complementary content increases the utility derived from the repository [7]. Hence, user valuations of complementary knowledge are likely to decrease more slowly with the number of items consumed. It has been shown that bundling is optimal if consumers’ values do not decrease too quickly [24].

## Price and Knowledge Preferences

We use price preferences to gauge the knowledge repository’s ability to attract users. Users’ preferred price characteristics capture their perceptions of the value in the repository, which determines whether they will purchase. Knowledge preferences provide real evidence that knowledge retrieved is meeting users’ demands. We focus on four aspects of knowledge preferences. Usefulness and user satisfaction, the two dependent variables that have been widely used in IS and marketing, have been used to gauge the performance of knowledge transfer [77], knowledge management [65], information searches [58], electronic commerce [17], and price bundling [38]. Will ingness to recommend and intent to repurchase are used to gauge users’ behavioral commitment to transfer knowledge via the market mechanism. Behavioral intention has been used to gauge the performance of knowledge sharing [10].

## Methodology

## Design

stage experimental design was adopted. In the first price choice stage, users were asked to choose one of four price options to use the knowledge repository. Users’ price choices expressed their price preferences. Similar methods of offering a menu of competing price plans for choice have been used to induce users to reveal their demands [53]. In the second knowledge usage stage, users were randomly assigned to one of four versions of the knowledge repository to browse and download the knowledge. Their preferences for the knowledge in the repository were then assessed. The value of knowledge, as experience goods, can only be appreciated after download [15]. This two-stage design reflects the reality of the knowledge market, in which price serves as an incentive mechanism for user participation, and the utility realized depends on what has been offered.

Our design resulted in a 4 (price characteristics: repository versus knowledge pricing, both with a volume reward variation) by 4 (knowledge characteristics: complementary versus substitutive content, both varying in repository size) full factorial between-subjects design that produced 16 different combinations. User characteristics were measured rather than manipulated. Figure 2 shows the number of users in each combination. The unequal number of users in each treatment suggested the degree of popularity of the price options.

## Price and Knowledge Manipulations

Price characteristics were manipulated by offering users a menu of four price options to choose from. The four prices were calculated to be of approximately equivalent value and adjusted to reflect an acceptable price range for users (based on a pilot study showing that, on average, a user downloaded 15 items for writing up the project and was willing to pay \$9.00 for using the knowledge repository) in order to hold the direct effect of price constant.<sup>6</sup> Based on the average download of 15 items, the total cost of each option was \$9.00. Volume rewards can have numerous variations. The two volume options are designed to accommodate the nature of the pricing options and encourage usage. The price options were as follows:

1. Repository pricing: Pay \$9.00 for unlimited downloads.

2. Repository pricing with volume reward: Pay \$5.00 for the first five downloads, and \$0.40 per download for any additional download. This is a variation of the “fixed-up-to” pricing [53] that allows unlimited download pricing to accommodate volume reward.

3. Knowledge pricing: Pay \$0.60 per download.

4. Knowledge pricing with volume reward: Pay \$0.90 per download with a download-two-get-one-free volume reward to encourage downloading more different items of knowledge, rather than downloading more units of the same item.

Knowledge characteristics, both at the repository and knowledge levels, were constituted through four combinations, as shown in Table 1. Repository size varied with the amount of knowledge included with (1) a small repository combining the knowledge of two repositories, and (2) a large repository combining three repositories. Because all of the included repositories were real-world sizable repositories, we expect the gap to be sufficient. We manipulated knowledge content by offering knowledge of different content domains with (3) complementary knowledge comprising general and specific knowledge, and (4) knowledge of substitutes comprising only general knowledge.

The study proceeded as follows:

• Page 1 (the instruction): The welcome page of the study showed the following instruction to users: “Assume that you are requested to work on a course project. Please prepare your project by visiting the knowledge repository. The grading of the project will largely depend on the quantity and quality of knowledge retrieved from the repository, which in turn is critical for whether you will pass this course.” This instruction motivated users to engage in retrieving external knowledge rather than consult their internal tacit knowledge. According to Gray and Durcikova [27], the full benefits of knowledge reuse would not be realized if users were reluctant to draw on knowledge repositories for existing solutions but spent extra time on re-creating their solutions. The grade impact was to make the knowledge users’ choice have a real impact on their knowledge usage.

• Page 2 (the price menu): Users saw the price menu and read the instruction: “Please choose one of the four prices to use the knowledge repository.” Their price choice was recorded as the index of price preference.

• Page 3 (the knowledge repository): Users entered one of the four versions of the repository at random, began to retrieve knowledge from the repository, and exited when they had downloaded sufficient knowledge items for writing up the project.

After retrieving the knowledge, users were redirected to the questionnaire page, and their demographic characteristics and knowledge preferences were then assessed. The entire experiment was constructed as Web pages and placed on the Internet to simulate the knowledge retrieval situation when using the Web as a technology platform.

## Measurement

## User Characteristics

We measure four aspects of user characteristics to consider the influence of user heterogeneity in price and knowledge preferences: gender and income as proxies for user willingness to pay for knowledge, and education and IS major as proxies for users’ ability to realize the benefit of the purchased knowledge. All four proxies were self-rated. The users’ education in years and income in dollars were determined. Two dummy variables were created: if a user majored in IS or was male, he or she was given a score of 1, and 0 otherwise.

Of our total 377 users, 124 (32.9 percent) were female and 253 (67.1 percent) male. Their income levels were evenly distributed, with the number of users in approximately ascending 20 percentiles being 78 (20.7 percent), 80 (21.2 percent), 74 (19.6 percent), 76 (19.6 percent), and 69 (18.9 percent).

Users’ years of education ranged from 14 to 22, with junior and senior undergraduate students (38.3 percent) and graduate students (44.8 percent) as two majority groups. A majority (73.5 percent) of the users majored in IS, with the remaining (26.5 percent) users having majored in various business fields.

## Knowledge Preferences

Knowledge preferences were assessed in terms of knowledge usefulness, user satisfaction, willingness to recommend to others, and intention to repurchase. Respondents were asked to give their assessment of the relevant performance metrics on a Likerttype scale in which 1 was “strongly disagree” and 7 was “strongly agree.”

Knowledge usefulness and user satisfaction, the two dependent variables that have been widely used, were used to gauge users’ psychological commitment to the knowledge repository. Davis [13] advocates a technology acceptance model in which perceived usefulness is a fundamental determinant of user acceptance of information technology. The perceived usefulness scale he develops has been replicated in various settings, including electronic mail, voice mail, word processing, spreadsheets, graphics [1], and, recently, computer-mediated knowledge transfer [77]. We modified the wording of this scale to fit the context of this study. The coefficient alpha reliability of the six-item scale was satisfactory at 0.958. We measured user satisfaction using Oliver’s [67] 12-item Likert-type scale. The coefficient alpha reliability of this 12-item scale was 0.500; analysis showed that deleting four unreliable items with negative or negligible item-total correlations would improve this value to 0.863. A factor analysis with the remaining eight items indicated one factor with an eigenvalue greater than 1 (4.13), explaining 52 percent of the variance. Items loaded from 0.791 to 0.579. This one-factor solution is consistent with other validation reports of this scale (e.g., [50, 68]).

Willingness to recommend measures the probability that users will recommend the repository to other users. This measure is especially important for knowledge transfer because online user recommendation is important for building trust for transactions [5]. Intention to repurchase measures the probability that users will purchase and use the repository again. Intention is a person’s expectation of future behavior toward the object. Intention to repurchase is important because learning requires repeated use of knowledge. The decision to repurchase may depend on the consumption experience [74]. The two behaviorally oriented variables require a lesser degree of inference, and single-item measures should be appropriate [44]. Similar single-item scales have been used elsewhere (e.g., [40]). To further ascertain the validity of the four sets of measures for gauging knowledge preference, we carried out an exploratory factor analysis using all of the purified knowledge preference indicators to see how many factors emerge. The results showed that two factors, corresponding to knowledge usefulness and user satisfaction, had eigenvalues greater than 1 (9.094 and 1.470) that accounted for 57 percent and 9 percent of the variance, respectively. The two behaviorally oriented indicators, willingness to recommend to others and intention to repurchase, loaded on the satisfaction factor, suggesting a close association between them. Given the strong theoretical basis of the four sets of measures, we then performed a confirmatory factor analysis specifying a three-factor solution (knowledge usefulness, user satisfaction, and behavioral intention) to verify the exploratory results.<sup>7</sup> The t-values for all loadings were significant $( p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . The low modification indices of the two behaviorally oriented indicators on the usefulness and satisfaction factors (all less than 0.50, far below the threshold value of 3.84 for cross-loading) indicated that it was more appropriate to treat them as a separate aspect of knowledge preference.

## The Analysis Model

WE USE THREE CLASSES OF ANALYSIS MODELS to estimate price and knowledge preferences according to differences in characteristics of users, price, and knowledge.

For price preference, we employ a multinomial choice model to estimate probabilities for price choices. This model is chosen because the four price choices are categorical and unordered in nature [56].

For knowledge preference, two classes of analyses are used. We perform the ordered logit model (OLM) to estimate users’ willingness to recommend the knowledge repository and their intention to repurchase from the knowledge repository. This choice is based on the consideration that the two behaviorally oriented knowledge preferences are ordered ordinal variables measured with single-item scales. With OLM, it is assumed that there is a natural ranking in the seven possible values of the scale. We then apply ordinary least square (OLS) methods to the two multi-item preference metrics—knowledge usefulness and user satisfaction. By adding or averaging the scale items, multi-item scales transform the ordinal nature of the measures into continuous ones that can be estimated efficiently and consistently with the widely used OLS methods [64, p. 243].

In the next section, we illustrate the use of the multinomial model in price choice and the ordered probability model in knowledge preference, but omit the OLS model because of its popularity and acceptability.

## The Multinomial Price Choice Model

The probability that a user will choose a particular price option is assumed to be a function of a user’s demographic characteristics, reflecting the person’s risk tolerance and expected utility from the repository. We then use the multinomial probability choice model to estimate the price choice probabilities. The model considers the price choices as discrete and unordered, based on a random utility model [56]. Users can choose only one of the mutually exclusive J + 1 price options; hence, $\Sigma { P _ { i j } } = 1$ . The multinomial choice model allows simultaneous estimation of the differential effects of explanatory variables on users’ discrete price choices by providing J (i.e., three in this study) sets of parameters. The $\beta$ vector can be obtained with maximum likelihood estimates.

$$
P _ {i j} = \frac {\exp \left(\beta_ {j} x _ {i}\right)}{1 + \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \exp \left(\beta_ {j} x _ {i}\right)}, \quad j = 1, 2,..., J, i = , 1, 2,..., n,\tag{1}
$$

where $P _ { _ { i j } }$ is the probability of ${ \bf { \dot { \rho } } } _ { j , \pm }$ given user $i ,$ with characteristics $x _ { i } ; \beta _ { j }$ is the vector of the effect of user characteristics on the price choices; and $x _ { i }$ is the user characteristics of gender, income, education, and major.

## The Ordered Probability Preference Model

We employ the ordered logit analysis of McKelvey and Zavoina [57] as implemented in LIMDEP [29] to estimate users’ willingness to recommend and their intention to repurchase the knowledge, as functions of price, knowledge, and user characteristics. OLM is derived from a standardized logistic probability distribution. The knowledge preferences, $Y _ { i } ,$ take on seven discrete values, ordered from 1 (the lowest) to 7 (the highest). Following Maddala [48, p. 47], we define the OLM with m $( m = 7 )$ discrete values and with a set of constants $\mu _ { _ i }$ such that $\mu _ { { } _ { - 1 } } = { } - \infty , \mu _ { { } _ { m - 1 } } = { } + \infty$ , and $\mu _ { _ 0 } < \mu _ { _ 1 } < . . . < \mu _ { _ { m - 1 } } .$

Let the underlying response model be described as

$$
Y _ {i} ^ {*} = \beta^ {\prime} x _ {i} + u _ {i} (i = 1, 2,..., n),\tag{2}
$$

where $Y _ { _ i } ^ { \ast }$ is the underlying response variable, x is a set of explanatory variables, $\beta ^ { \prime }$ is a set of the estimated parameters that tells us how a one-unit increase in the explanatory variables increases the log-odds of being higher than category i, and u is the error term. $Y _ { i } ^ { * }$ is unobserved, but we know which of the m discrete values it takes. It takes the $j \mathrm { t h }$ value if

$$
\mu_ {j - 1} <   Y _ {i} ^ {*} \leq \mu_ {j} (j = 0, 1,..., m - 1).\tag{3}
$$

There are three groups of explanatory variables: (1) price characteristics of repository, repository/reward, knowledge, and knowledge/reward charges; (2) knowledge characteristics of repository size and knowledge content; and (3) user characteristics of gender, education, and major. The OLM employs maximum likelihood methods to estimate values of β and thresholds $\mu _ { 0 } , \mu _ { 1 } , . . . , \mu _ { 5 } .$ For identification, $\mu _ { 0 }$ is set to zero. We use identical sets of the explanatory variables in the four knowledge preference equations.

## Discussion

EXAMINATION OF THE TWO COMPETING PERSPECTIVES offers explanations for the roles price, knowledge, and user characteristics play in the formulation of price and knowledge preferences in an economic market. As predicted, for price preference, repository pricing outperforms knowledge pricing, whereas for knowledge preference, individually priced knowledge outperforms repository-priced knowledge. Specifically, we find that males and middle-income users prefer repository pricing, whereas educated users prefer individually priced knowledge. The results lend support to the joint explanation that the mental accounting mechanism operates to entice users with the high benefit of knowledge priced at the repository level with the belief to balance the mental account, whereas the transaction decoupling mechanism operates to keep the value of the individually priced knowledge fresh, so that it encourages users to realize the benefit of the paid units of knowledge.

Users consider the price characteristics of the knowledge repository in making choice decisions under the impact of the two sunk-cost effects. Price, as an incentive for knowledge transfer, thus must deal with the two aspects of irrational decision making—mental accounting and transaction decoupling—to achieve the dual purpose of attracting and satisfying users with varying levels of risk tolerance and knowledge capability. Knowledge pricing may start the transfer with an insufficient user base, whereas repository pricing may conclude the transfer with less satisfactory knowledge preferences. The design of pricing strategies for maximizing user benefits and vendor profits, therefore, will need to take the two irrational mechanisms into consideration. Using economic modeling, Sundararajan [76] established that, in the presence of nonzero transaction costs, offering fixed-fee pricing in addition to a nonlinear usage-based pricing scheme improves vendors’ profits. Studies that address mixed pricing that offers the attraction of the whole repository and the benefit of individual units of knowledge can provide a balanced view of knowledge transfer.

## Knowledge Characteristics

Users experience knowledge characteristics for determining their knowledge preferences. We consider two characteristics in this study—repository size and knowledge content. Repository size is not retrieval size. Because a repository typically contains nearly unlimited units of knowledge, at what retrieval level do users’ mental accounts balance? Users can manage to use up all units of prepaid goods, such as season tickets, to balance their mental accounts, but they retrieve a sufficient amount of knowledge, rather than exhaust the entire repository, in order to complete tasks. Consequently, the concept of mental balance will need to accommodate this characteristic. A sufficient retrieval set, rather than the entire repository, serves as the realizable benefit to gauge whether a mental account is balanced.

The impact of knowledge content on price and knowledge preferences can be examined in terms of search costs. In contrast to past sunk costs, search costs are incremental costs in knowledge retrieval, which is rational from an economic perspective. Search costs may exaggerate the effects of sunk costs. Nonnegligible search costs will reduce the attraction of repository pricing because users are discouraged by the high likelihood of turning price into sunk cost. Similarly, search costs will further reduce the attraction of knowledge pricing because no purchase will occur if users cannot find the units of knowledge to buy. For example, Bashyam and Karmarkar [8] compare the search costs incurred by users of a package information service (distributing information using CD-ROMs) and an online service (delivering information using an online hosted database). The package service offers lower search costs for users, given the longer access times required for an online service. They show that high-volume users choose the lower search cost option, whereas low-volume users choose the higher search cost option. Rational search costs, therefore, may interact with irrational sunk costs, and both aspects of costs can be considered in future studies when developing a sound theoretical accounting for knowledge transfer.

## User Characteristics

When inspecting the estimated coefficients of user characteristics in the four knowledge preference equations, we see that respondents measuring high in education find the knowledge more useful $( \beta = 0 . 0 8 7 , t = 2 . 7 3 7 , p = 0 . 0 0 7 )$ , and they are more satisfied with it $( \beta = 0 . 0 5 1 , t = 1 . 9 5 5 , p = 0 . 0 5 1 )$ , more willing to recommend it $( \beta =$ 0.152, $t = 3 . 3 9 6 , p = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , and more likely to repurchase it $( \beta = 0 . 1 4 0 , t = 3 . 1 4 7 , p =$ 0.002). Together with the results from the price preference model, our findings lead us to consider two characteristics of potential users of knowledge repositories—the willingness to use the knowledge repository and the ability to use the knowledge. In terms of sunk cost, the theoretical underpinning of mental accounting and transaction decoupling, the two characteristics fall roughly into the two dimensions of perceived risk—uncertainty and payoff (e.g., [9, 12]). Risk-tolerant users are more willing to bear the uncertainty of incurring sunk cost to choose repository pricing, and knowledgecapable users are able to make better use of individual knowledge items and realize a higher level of payoff.

The two characteristics suggest four user segments. First, risk-tolerant and knowledge-capable users choose repository pricing and realize as much knowledge benefit as possible to balance the mental account. We do not observe this segment in our study, suggesting that it is difficult to balance the mental account for a knowledge repository. Second, risk-tolerant and less knowledge-capable users choose repository pricing but forgo many of the retrievals; they are the irrational users who prefer repository pricing but not the knowledge in the repository. Third, risk-intolerant and knowledge-capable users choose knowledge pricing and realize the benefit of each unit of knowledge; thus they evaluate the knowledge highly. Finally, risk-intolerant and less knowledgecapable users are not interested in the repository at all, and a market mechanism for knowledge transfer may not be suitable for this segment.

Our results indicate that users respond to price characteristics differently, so the accountability of the two theoretical perspectives for knowledge transfer will need to consider user heterogeneity in risk tolerance and knowledge capability. On an exploratory basis, we linked several demographic variables (e.g., gender, income, education, and major) to user reactions to sunk costs. Because a market transfer of knowledge is interested in understanding users’ differential patterns of reaction to price incentives, given the results of this study, appropriate price incentives can be identified to encourage knowledge transfer that suits users’ heterogeneous needs.

## Limitations

There are several limitations of the study. We used real-world repositories and recruited mainly senior undergraduate students and graduate students to ensure a certain level of familiarity with the knowledge repository and the ability to use it. This approach enhances the external validity of the study, but given that all users are student researchers, the results from this study may not be applicable to all organizations, especially business organizations. This is especially so when the deployment of a corporate knowledge repository can be very expensive, perhaps costing thousands of dollars, and the price options structure can be very different from the commercial knowledge repositories for individual users investigated in this study. To address this concern, a follow-up study allowing two-way transfer (i.e., knowledge contribution and usage) is under way that investigates the business use of knowledge repositories using realworld knowledge workers. The success of knowledge repositories involves people contributing knowledge to repositories and people seeking knowledge from repositories for reuse [39]. We expect the ongoing study to corroborate the results of the current study; together they can provide a balance account for knowledge transfer.

Motivating users to use a knowledge repository for better solutions remains a challenge for knowledge transfer. We investigated the price mechanism in this study, which is critical for cross-organizational knowledge transfer, but may not always be beneficial when used for transfer within an organization. Some researchers advocate the establishment of an internal knowledge market to reward employees (e.g., [15]), but others disagree on the grounds that a price mechanism may reduce employees’ “joy of sharing” (e.g., [10, 23]). We propose that a price mechanism is used to motivate the participation and use of a knowledge repository, but that while the choice of price has grade impact, it has no monetary impact on users. Although there is a limitation because these choices may not fully reflect their price preferences, our design may capture, to a certain degree, the impact of the social-psychological mechanism on knowledge transfer. The complementarity or substitution effects of price and other nonmonetary mechanisms for knowledge transfer, such as social-psychological (anticipated reciprocal relationships and sense of self-worth) and sociological (fairness, innovativeness, and affiliation) rewards that have been suggested in the literature [10], can be investigated to provide an integrated view of the market transfer of knowledge.

## Conclusion

IT IS FREQUENTLY OBSERVED THAT USERS ARE IRRATIONAL in their price and knowledge preferences, but little is known about the mechanism behind this irrationality. We develop a theoretical account for this irrationality by integrating two seemingly incompatible theoretical perspectives. Repository pricing is confirmed to be attractive, especially among those who are willing to bear the sunk-cost risk to try the repository; this is essential to initiate the transfer process. Nevertheless, this pricing strategy is bound to lead to lower knowledge preferences if users lack the ability to use the knowledge or suffer other constraints limiting knowledge use. Alternatively, knowledge pricing is unattractive, yet those who are more knowledgeable prefer it, and it is thus an effective approach for learning. Those who have the ability to use the knowledge prefer to pay for what they use rather than for what they expect.

To the best of our knowledge, our study is one of the first to examine a market mechanism for knowledge transfer from the perspective of users, and empirically investigate knowledge transfer that comprehensively considers the effects of user, price, and knowledge characteristics on the choice and usage of online knowledge repositories. The feasibility of transferring knowledge via market mechanisms such as auctions in an organizational setting has been demonstrated [6, 72]. Pioneering studies have relied predominantly upon economics modeling (e.g., [6, 15, 32, 47]). We provide empirical evidence that the open market mechanisms that have been important for traditional databases are also viable in the context of intelligent knowledge repositories. Thus, these mechanisms can serve as channels for learning across organizations. Future studies can continue this endeavor by developing and testing various market mechanisms for knowledge transfer to meet the divergent knowledge needs and characteristics of knowledge workers.
