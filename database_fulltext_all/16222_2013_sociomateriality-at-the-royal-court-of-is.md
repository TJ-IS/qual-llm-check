Karlheinz Kautz <sup>a,</sup>⁎, Tina Blegind Jensen b

## a b s t r a c t

The notion of ‘sociomateriality’ has recently gained in popularity among information systems (IS) scholars in their search for providing new ways of investigating and theorizing about IS in organizations and society at large. While some scholars put forward arguments and research accounts that lead to new insights concerning the concept, others expose a cursory treatment and partial appreciation of the idea. In addition, sociomateriality as a new worldview has been criticized for introducing yet more academic jargon monoxide. Although existing research and debates show some potential for progressing the theorizing of ‘man–machine’ recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations, we point to the necessity of a deeper exploration of the term. Inspired by the Alternative Genres Track at the Furopean Conference on Information Systems 2012 the purpose here is to take a fresh look, to evoke new insights and to gain deeper understanding of the notion of sociomateriality and its use in the IS discipline. We invite the reader to attend a prolonged monologue – characterized by honesty, frank observations and wit – at the royal court of IS. The monologue is delivered by the court jester and directed to the two sovereigns who, based on their conceptual contributions, have played a decisive role in the spreading of the idea of sociomateriality in IS. The intention is to contribute with a stimulating debate about sociomateriality that builds on open-minded questions in the pursuit of key re<sup>fl</sup>ections to inform our research discipline. Throughout the monologue, the jester uncovers some contradictions and limitations in and between the sovereigns' writings and points to some possible resolutions.

## 1. Setting the Stage

The notion of sociomateriality has recently gained popularity among information system (IS) scholars in their search for providing new ways of investigating and theorizing about IS in organizations and society at large. Sociomateriality highly challenges and expands the prevailing modus operandi of IS scholarship and practice by inviting IS scholars to question and rethink “the supposed ontological separation among the social and the technological,” as expressed by Cecez-Kecmanovic, Galliers, Henfridsson, Newell, and Vidgen (2010) in their call for a special issue on the topic in Management Information Systems Quarterly.

To date, a number of in<sup>fl</sup>uential conceptual contributions on sociomateriality have been published (see e.g., Leonardi & Barley, 2008, 2010; Leonardi, 2010, 2011; Orlikowski, 2006, 2007, 2009; Orlikowski & Scott. 2008: Scott & Orlikowski. 2009). These writings have inspired scholars in the IS community to offer a sociomaterial perspective on various IS-related topics such as enterprise system implementation (Wagner, Newell, & Piccoli, 2010), mobile IT usage (Leclerq, Carugati, Giangreco, Da Cunha, & Jensen, 2009), work collaboration in Second Life (Orlikowski, 2009), digital innovation (Svahn, Henfridsson, & Yoo, 2009), computer simulation technology for automotive design (Leonardi, 2011), the impacts of social media (Scott & Orlikowski, 2009), software usability (Riemer & Vehring, 2010), digital entrepreneurship (Davidson & Vaast, 2010), plagiarism detection systems (Introna & Hayes, 2011), global software development (Johri, 2011), sensor-driven information systems in petroleum production (Østerlie et al. 2012) and information systems development as sociomaterial practice (Doolin & McLeod. 2012).

Recent use of sociomateriality goes as far as proposing it as a basis for a de<sup>fi</sup>nite identity in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld (Hassan & Hovorka. 2011). as well as for theorizing new ways of understanding information within the IS discipline (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2011). In addition, a number of activities have been initiated that search for advancing the sociomateriality research agenda in the IS discipline. For example, call for papers for special issues in highly pro<sup>fi</sup>led journals such as MIS Quarterly 2010 (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 2010) or conference calls invite panelists to discuss the methodological challenges of sociomaterial theorizing (e.g., the International Conference on Information Systems in 2012).

The use of sociomateriality introduces a new vocabulary with concepts such as ‘sociomaterial assemblage,’ ‘constitutive entanglement,’ ‘performativity,’ ‘relationality,’ as well as ‘imbrication.’ While some scholars argue that these concepts lead to potential contributions for progressing the theorizing of ‘man–machine’ recon<sup>fi</sup>gurations (e.g., Suchman, 2007), other scholars argue that sociomaterial analyses expose a cursory treatment and only partial appreciation of the idea. Furthermore, sociomateriality as a new concept has been criticized for introducing yet more academic jargon monoxide (Sutton, 2010): “…it [sociomateriality] appears to be on an important subject, but I hope the authors can find a simple word or two to explain what they mean by it to normal human beings.”

A discussion of sociomateriality and how it can inform IS research becomes essential in a <sup>fi</sup>eld where scholars struggle to reconcile the human/social and the technological dimensions of IS, and to investigate them in an inclusive and coherent way. Inspired by this debate, it is timely to gain deeper understanding, take a fresh look, and to evoke new insights (Avital & Mathiassen, 2011) of the notion of sociomateriality in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld.

## 2. The jester and the sovereigns at the royal court of IS

Towards this aim, we invite the reader to attend a prolonged monologue which builds on honesty, frank observations and wit about sociomateriality in the IS research <sup>fi</sup>eld. The monologue takes place at the royal court of IS and is delivered by the court jester and directed to the two sovereigns who, based on their conceptual contributions, have played a decisive role in the spreading of the idea of sociomateriality in IS:

“The jester is symbolic of common sense and of honesty […] the court jester is a character used for insight and advice on the part of the monarch, taking advantage of his license to mock and speak freely to dispense frank observations and highlight the folly of his monarch. This presents a clashing irony as a “greater” man could dispense the same advice and <sup>fi</sup>nd himself being detained in the dungeons or even executed. Only as the lowliest member of the court can the jester be the monarch's most useful adviser” (Wikipedia).

The jester takes both the role of the sovereigns, by presenting their positions based on their writings, and that of a ‘devil's advocate’ by exposing these positions through provocative questions. The jester approaches the sovereigns, both the queen and the king, who have different positions on the issue in question.

In the jester's monologue we use wit as in ‘geist’ and ‘esprit’ and of course not as in ‘joke’ to make our argument. Our intention is not to use the metaphors of queen, king, jester and royal court to create associations of well-esteemed colleagues as medieval royalty detached from reality. We do not use irony to dodge serious argumentation and replace it with ridicule nor do we want to portray fellow scholars in an unfavorable, silly light. Rather we use the monologue as an alternative framing of a conceptual study and a reframing of a literature review based on selected writings of two highly respected academics to create attention for a discourse which we feel is necessary and which will advance our <sup>fi</sup>eld.

On this backdrop, the writings by Orlikowski (2007, 2009), Orlikowski and Scott (2008) and Scott and Orlikowski (2009) represent the queen's view on sociomateriality and are addressed by the jester in the <sup>fi</sup>rst part of the monologue. The king's view is based on writings by Leonardi and Barley (2008) and Leonardi (2010, 2011) and addressed in the second part of the jester's monologue. While the king aims mainly at making a contribution to a better understanding of the ‘materiality’ of IS, his work has frequently been cited in the context of the sociomateriality discussion, and in his own writings he links to the idea of sociomateriality as a contributor to the thinking about organizing as a sociomaterial process (Leonardi, 2011).

Based on current observations and a general curiosity of how the concept of sociomateriality is treated in the IS research <sup>fi</sup>eld, the overall questions that the jester puts forward are: What is sociomateriality and how consistent are its advocates' understandings of the concept? How can the different views on sociomateriality inform IS research? How can sociomaterial issues of IS be studied in practice? Through the monologue, the court jester seeks to obtain clari<sup>fi</sup>cations from the sovereigns regarding the new insights provided by sociomateriality in IS research. In doing so, the jester uncovers some contradictions and limitations in and between the sovereigns' writings and points to some possible resolutions.

Engaging in a discussion, instigated by the jester's monologue, will help initiate a refreshing debate where open-minded questions about sociomateriality can be asked. The aim of this writing thus is not to come up with <sup>fi</sup>nal answers to the questions but rather to serve as a starting point for a conceptually challenging, practically relevant and inspiring debate, contributing to and stimulating advancement of knowledge. Dealing with such a challenge comes at a price: this treatise is not an introduction to sociomateriality, but presupposes some familiarity with and knowledge of the concept and the most prominent literature on the topic to appreciate the issues raised in the monologue.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, the jester's monologue is presented in the outlined form. Section three contains a summary and discussion of the main issues in which the jester's re<sup>fl</sup>ections are presented in the form of appraisal and critical acclaim of the queen's and the king's writings as well as the inconsistencies and shortcomings in and between the different points of view. Based on the insights from the monologue and as a starting point for further dialogue, the paper concludes with the jester's proposal for possible directions for IS research concerning how to approach the problems at the heart of the IS discipline, which the queen frames as (how to take seriously) ‘the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice’ (Orlikowski, 2007) and which the king sees as (how to handle) ‘the entwining of the material and the social’ (Leonardi & Barley, 2008).

## 3. The jester's monologue

## 3.1. Part 1: The Jester Addresses the Queen

Dear Queen, you say that:

“The challenge for organizational scholars is to <sup>fi</sup>gure out how to take seriously the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice. A number of particularly interesting ideas for doing so have been emerging in sociology, and science and technology studies over the past decade: for example, actor–networks (Callon, 1986; Latour 1992; Latour, 2005),<sup>1</sup> sociotechnical ensemble (Bijker 1995), mangle of practice (Pickering 1995), object-centered sociality (Knorr Cetina 1997), relational materiality (Law, 2004) and material sociology (Beunza et al. 2006). These concepts challenge and transcend conventional distinctions between the social and the material” (Orlikowski, 2007, p. 1437).

And based on this tradition you want:

“…to propose a shift in our conventional framing […] seeing organizational practices as ‘sociomaterial’ — to borrow a term given recent currency by scholars such as Mol (2002) and Suchman (2007) […] what Suchman (2007) calls ‘a creative sociomaterial assemblage’” (Orlikowski, 2007, p. 1438–1440).

Building on the notions of ‘sociomateriality’ and ‘sociomaterial assemblages' you argue that we shouldn't revert to:

“…a limiting dualism that treats them [the social and material] as separate (even if interacting) phenomena […] A sociomaterial approach […] advances the view that there is an inherent inseparability between the technical and the social” (Orlikowski, 2007, p. 1437; Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 434).

In this way you put forward that the term ‘sociomateriality’ can overcome the dualism, but my question is: Why do you consider this dualism as limiting and what do you in fact mean by sociomateriality?

You use the following four de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of what sociomateriality stands for:

“the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice” (Orlikowski, 2007, p. 1437).“the constitutive entanglement of the social and the material in everyday organizational life” (Orlikowski, 2007, p. 1438).“the constitutive intertwining and reciprocal interde<sup>fi</sup>nition of human and material agency” (Orlikowski, 2009, p. 13).“…the way in which phenomena are “reciprocally and emergently intertwined”” (Scott & Orlikowski, 2009, p. 4).

To illustrate this, you provide examples of Google-based searches and the use of BlackBerrys:

“These temporally emergent results are not dependent on either materiality or sociality, nor on some interaction between them (to the extent that these are seen as distinct domains). Rather, the performance and results of a Google-based search are sociomaterial […] we see that the communication changes enacted at Plymouth emerge from the performativity of the BlackBerrys as engaged in members' everyday practices […] The performativity of the BlackBerrys is sociomaterial” (Orlikowski, 2007, pp. 1440–1444).

So, in your examples do you actually take the ‘constitutive’, ‘recursive’ and ‘emergent intertwining’ of the social and material seriously or do you in fact separate the humans (and their engagements) and the artifacts (their performativity)? Doesn't the notion of ‘enactment’ entail some sort of interaction between the two, thus hinting at dualism?

If we here pursue the notion of ‘performativity,’ which according to you is a central idea entailed in sociomateriality, then you say that:

“While related to the notion of performance, performativity is not synonymous with it. Where “performance” refers to the doing of some activity (as when a physician “performs” a medical examination, or a musician “performs” in front of an audience), performativity refers to enactment […] the notion of performativity draws attention to how relations and boundaries between humans and technologies are not pre-given or <sup>fi</sup>xed, but enacted in practice” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, pp. 460–462).

You say that:

“…people and things only exist in relation to each other, and as Slife (2005, p. 159) puts it: “They start out and forever remain in relationship”. In other words, entities (whether humans or technologies) have no inherent properties, but acquire form, attributes, and capabilities through their interpenetration. This is a relational ontology that presumes the social and the material are inherently inseparable” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, pp. 455–456).

So you argue for a relational ontology that presumes the inseparability of the social and the material. But if they are in relation, as you argue, don't they then have to be separated to be recognized as part of a relation? Of course, you might say that:

“The portmanteau “sociomaterial” (no hyphen) attempts to signal this ontological fusion. Any distinction of humans and technologies is analytical only, and done with the recognition that these entities necessarily entail each other in practice” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 456).

How come you juxtapose ontology with an analytical distinction and not with epistemology? Isn't the analytical distinction part of a different ontology? Can you get away with talking about a relational ontology while at the same time argue that the distinction between the social and material can be done for analytical purposes? I wonder what the king will say about this but let us leave this discussion for later.

If we take your point of departure seriously, then how do you see that the notion of sociomateriality can inform IS research? You say, citing Wikipedia, that:

“Technology is a broad concept that deals with a species' usage and knowledge of tools and crafts […] “technology” can refer to material objects of use to humanity, such as machines, hardware or utensils, but can also encompass broader themes, including systems, methods of organization, and techniques” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 437).

## And you continue:

“While we learn much by considering technology as a speci<sup>fi</sup>c organizational event or process, such a view also obscures ways of seeing how all organizational practices and relations always entail some sort of technological (or material) mediation” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 454).

Based on this view of technology, is technology then the same as material? And to take the argument further, what then is information technology, is it a material object? And what exactly is material? In the pursuit of de<sup>fi</sup>ning materiality, you require that we:

“…replac[e] the idea of materiality as ‘pre-formed substances’ with that of ‘performed relations’ […] a considerable amount of materiality is entailed in every aspect of organizing, from the visible forms – such as bodies, clothes, rooms, desks, chairs, tables, buildings, vehicles, phones, computers, books, documents, pens and utensils – to the less visible <sup>fl</sup>ows – such as data and voice networks, water and sewage infrastructures, electricity and air systems” (Orlikowski, 2007, pp. 1436–1438).

So are you then saying that data are material and not a pre-formed substance, but a performed relationship? I wonder how to take this. You say:

“…that additional and alternative ways of examining these entailments are required […] a grounding in relationality, performativity, and sociomaterial assemblages (rather than either discrete entities or mutually dependent ensembles) may afford some empirical and conceptual innovations that will increase our understandings of the practices of contemporary organizational life” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 467).

So by grounding our investigation of the social and material in ‘relationality’, ‘performativity’ and ‘sociomaterial assemblages’ we can be innovative in the way we study these relationships. I wonder what else you say about how we should study sociomaterial assemblages in practice.

You argue that the challenge:

“…is to <sup>fi</sup>nd ways of establishing a corpus of data under <sup>fi</sup>eldwork conditions that are distributed, constantly recon<sup>fi</sup>guring, fragmented into enclaves, or restricted by partial access (Law & Urry, 2004). Possibly promising approaches for addressing these include work on narratives (Czarniawska, 1998; Pentland & Feldman, 2007)” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 464).

I wonder whether the narrative approach is an acceptable way to present theory and our empirical data. Are narratives really an alternative to existing research approaches? And how do we include the ‘voice’ of the material in a narrative? Do we need other techniques to capture this such as visual media?

In one of your studies, you:

“… conducted an exploratory netnography […] focusing on the content and structure of the website” (Scott & Orlikowski, 2009, p. 9).

But you do not explain in detail what this research approach entails and how it was carried out in practice. Another important (and related) challenge that you bring forward is that of language. You argue that:

“Part of the problem, as we have noted, is linguistic. Suchman (2007, p. 263) suggests that “our language for talking about […] persons or artefacts presupposes a <sup>fi</sup>eld of discrete, self-standing entities […] Part of the dif<sup>fi</sup>culty in discussing the new perspective is that our language makes it dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to express indissolubility. We are used to dividing, separating, and distinguishing. Thus, even terms such as “mutual constitution,” “entanglement,” “assemblage,” and “relationality” allude to separateness, even as they try to move beyond it” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, pp. 463–368).

If language is limited, do we then need to use a whole new vocabulary? And if language is the problem, why don't you provide a new precise and understandable language?

But, you write:

“A Web search conducted with the Google search engine is sociomaterial “all the way down,” entailing computer code written and updated by software engineers, executing on computers (con<sup>fi</sup>gured with particular hardware and software elements which were designed and built by computer engineers and production workers), whose operation depends on the millions of people who use computers to create and update Web pages every day, and the millions of people around the world who enter particular search criteria into their Web browsers running on still other computers designed and built by yet other people, and so on” (Orlikowski & Scott, 2008, p. 465).

Why are your examples so general and hardly provide narratives in which the key concepts are explicitly recognizable? Maybe they are so general because there is no clear language and most important of all, because there is no clear ontology.

Enough!

## 3.2. Part 2: The Jester Addresses the King

Dear King, I'm not even sure that you talk about sociomateriality, although you keep on referring to the sociomaterial tradition in your writings:

“…as scholars begin to push us to think about organizing as a sociomaterial process, the imbrication metaphor helps us to explain how the social and the material become interwoven in the <sup>fi</sup>rst place and continue interlocking in ways that produce the infrastructures that people use to get their work done” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 165).

You say that:

“Although we may make the ontological claim that routines and technologies are indistinguishable phenomena because they are both constituted by human and material agencies, we must be mindful that the ways in which those agencies are weaved together produce empirically distinct <sup>fi</sup>gurations.

Latour (2005, p. 53) de<sup>fi</sup>nes <sup>fi</sup>guration as the process by which agencies take on observable properties” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 151).

So contrary to the queen, who claims that we should build on a relational ontology that presumes that the social and the material are inherently inseparable, you talk about agencies that are woven together. Interesting! You distinguish between ‘human agency’ and ‘material agency’ and emphasize that:

“Human agency is typically de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the ability to form and realize one's goals […] [and the] human agency perspective suggests that people's work is not determined by the technologies they employ” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 147).“Material agency is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the capacity for nonhuman entities to act on their own, apart from human intervention. As nonhuman entities, technologies exercise agency through their “performativity” (Barad, 2003; Pickering 1995); in other words, through the things they do that users cannot completely or directly control” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 148).

## And you argue that:

“People who have goals and the capacity to achieve them (human agency) confront a technology that does speci<sup>fi</sup>c things that are not completely in their control (material agency). In the enactment of their goals, then, people must contend with the material agency of the technology […] Taylor and colleagues (2001, p. 71) provide a useful language for reconciling the notions of human and material agency […] They argue that although human and material agencies both in<sup>fl</sup>uence people's actions, their in<sup>fl</sup>uence is disproportionate because human agency always has a “head status,” while material agency has a “complement status”” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 148–150).

In this sense, in agreement with Taylor and colleagues, you do not privilege humans over non-humans, but human agency over material agency. Isn't this the easy way out?

## You argue that:

“By treating the relationship between human and material agencies in this way, Taylor et al. are able to successfully incorporate into the human agency approach the recognition that technologies have a material agency that transcends changes in context while still giving primacy to the people who design and use them. The ability to do this rests on the use of a metaphor of imbrication” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 150).

## And you refer to Sassen (2006, p. 345) who suggests

“… the term imbrication to capture the simultaneous interdependence and speci<sup>fi</sup>city of each the digital and the nondigital. They work on each other but they do not produce hybridicity. Each maintains its distinct irreducible character […] the imbrication metaphor asserts a slightly different relationship: people have agency and technologies have agency, but ultimately, people decide how they will respond to a technology. As Cooren (2004, p. 377) suggests, “To say that nonhumans do things does not mean that human contributions are passed over … Humans can appropriate what nonhumans do” […] the metaphor of imbrication [is] a way of recognizing that human and material agencies are distinct phenomena but that they are fundamentally interdependent” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 151–152).

These assumptions are very different from those of the queen. Interesting! Does this mean that you also have another take on how sociomateriality (or your thoughts on human and material agencies) can inform IS research?

When talking about materiality of information technology, you contend that this topic:

“…remains grossly under-theorized […] individuals in a wide variety of occupations and organizations routinely interact not only with people but with information technologies. The latter indisputably have material properties” (Leonardi & Barley, 2008, p. 161, 171).

## You go further by arguing that:

“…a technological artifact is a “bundle of material and symbol properties packaged in some socially recognizable form, e.g., hardware, software.” Because most information technologies are software rather than solid physical objects, it may seem odd to say that information technologies have “material properties.” But, when material properties are construed as features that provide opportunities for, or constraints on, action, the metaphor seems warranted” (Leonardi & Barley, 2008, p. 162).

Well, you talk about material properties, but what is materiality? And when you say that people interact with information technology, do you then disagree with the queen on the notion of separateness? You explain what a technical artifact is, but why do you reduce your deliberations to information technology and software, rather than extending them to information systems?

You say:

“Although it has no physical properties, software clearly does not exist in the conceptual domain because it provides hard constraints and affordances in much the same way as physical artifacts do. Indeed, many researchers suggest that software, intangible though it may be, can be described in terms of its materiality (Hutchby, 2001; Jackson, 1996; Leonardi, 2007; Orlikowski, 2007; Suchman, 2000; Volkoff, et al., 2007)” (Leonardi, 2010).<sup>2</sup>

You guide us further by providing:

“… three de<sup>fi</sup>nitions of materiality … materiality as (1) matter; (2) practical instantiation; and, (3) signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. I argue that if materiality is de<sup>fi</sup>ned simply as matter, that digital artifacts cannot be said to have materiality. However, when materiality is understood to represent the practical instantiation and the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of an artifact, digital artifacts can clearly be seen to have materiality” (Leonardi, 2010).

In other words, you use:

“…these two alternative de<sup>fi</sup>nitions as lenses through which to understand the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of “digital materiality” in the organizing process” (Leonardi, 2010).

Does this mean that ‘to materialize’ is ‘to make it happen?’ If this is so, what then characterizes digital materiality, or for that matter, materiality?

You say that:

“…the adjective “material” seems to refer to some property of the technology (in these cases, software) that provides users with the capability to perform some action. Calling these properties with the adjective “material” seems a ploy to remind the reader that the software-in-use does things that cannot be reduced to human intention or action … calling something material emphasizes its performativity — the notion that it provides people with capabilities that they can use to accomplish their goals (Pickering, 2001) […] there is something important about software or other intangible artifacts that distinguish them in key ways from patterns of interaction, talk or other social practices, and that the word “material” points to that distinction” (Leonardi, 2010).

## You further explain:

“… perhaps what matters most about an artifact is not what it's made out of, but what it allows people to do […] No matter whether those artifacts are physical or digital, their ‘materiality’ is determined, to a substantial degree, by when, how and why they are used. These de<sup>fi</sup>nitions imply that materiality is not a property of artifacts but a product of the relationships between artifacts and the people who produce and consume them” (Leonardi, 2010).

So, based on your de<sup>fi</sup>nition of materiality, you de<sup>fi</sup>ne performativity and relationality slightly different than does the queen. I wonder what this entails; in particular, I'm curious to know whether your ideas differ from those of the queen on how we should then go about studying human and material agencies in practice?

You say that we should investigate the:

“… interweaving of human and material agencies as a process of imbrication. To imbricate means to arrange distinct elements in overlapping patterns so that they function interdependently. […] The verb imbricate is derived from names of roof tiles used in ancient Roman and Greek architecture. The tegula and imbrex were interlocking tiles used to waterproof a roof” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 150).

## And you put forward that:

“Taylor and his colleagues (2007, p. 399) suggest that this integrated structure is an organizational structure: “…we consider [imbrication] to be the way that interagency relationships are interweaved to form…infrastructure”” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 151).

You challenge the queen's concept of inseparability by introducing the term imbrication which recognizes separability of distinct elements. So, since imbrication is really different from sociomateriality, is there also a difference between ‘interweaving’ and ‘interlocking’ — and what the queen calls ‘entanglement’?

You then relate imbrication with human and material agencies by arguing that:

“Human and material agencies, though both capabilities for action, differ phenomenologically with respect to intention. Thus, like the tegula and the imbrex, they have distinct contours, yet they form an integrated structure through their imbrication” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 151).

## You talk about affordance as a way to:

“…explain why human and material agencies become imbricated: Technologies have material properties, but those material properties afford different possibilities for action based on the contexts in which they are used” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 153).

## You <sup>fi</sup>nish with the argument that:

“As the imbrication framework suggests, structuring involves simultaneous and interactive changes between the features and routines of the technology. By mapping these changes over time, we may be able to gain new insights into the dynamics of socio-technical change and its role in the constitution of organizations” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 165).

So what you suggest is that we should try to map out these processes of imbrication with a particular focus on affordance, but you do not relate to the performativity of human and material agencies here. How do you then see the relationship between performativity and affordance? And I wonder if the concepts of imbrication and sociomateriality are compatible after all, or if you just go piecemeal with the concept of sociomateriality to study socio-technical change?

Well, that is enough!

## 4. Aftermath: the jester's re<sup>fl</sup>ections

The monologue has come to an end. Silence falls over the court. The jester is puzzled, and thoughtfully he re<sup>fl</sup>ects upon the queen's and the king's roles at the court and what has been said:

The queen and the king are newcomers at the court of IS. It seems as if they both are forgetting decades of system sciences which have prevailed at the court of IS (van Gigch & le Moigne, 1989). The queen's works are, however, valuable as they have renewed and redirected the interest of IS scholars to tackle the problem at the heart of the IS discipline, which she frames as ‘the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice’ (Orlikowski, 2007) and which the king conceptualizes as ‘the entwining of the material and the social’ (Leonardi & Barley, 2008). The queen provides a new vocabulary with concepts such as ‘constitutive entanglement,’ ‘relationality,’ ‘performativity,’ and ‘sociomaterial assemblages.’ These concepts form part of a language which the queen considers problematic, but somehow she contributes to this rather than helping in getting things clearer — an issue worth coming back to later in these re<sup>fl</sup>ections. The king supplements with the metaphor of ‘imbrication’ and the concept of ‘digital materiality.’ His language seems somewhat clearer, but the differences between the queen's and the king's conceptualizations go further. Through the monologue two different views on sociomateriality within the queen's and the king's writings are identi<sup>fi</sup>ed: that of ‘constitutive entanglement,’ which subscribes to a relational ontology and the inseparability of the social and the material, or as the queen puts it, the inseparability between humans and technology; and that of ‘imbrication,’ to which the king subscribes, which is a representational ontology and which accepts the separability of the social and the material — these are the king's own words.

These differences may pragmatically be explained by the different empirical settings in which the queen and the king conduct their investigations. While the king writes in the context of IT support in the car manufacturing industry, where ‘things’ are built out of separate parts, the queen writes in a context of information technologies in situated use, e.g. Google searches, where ‘things’ are more easily appreciated as wholes. This means that the queen's and the king's writings are in contradiction to each other and thus the question arises whether sociomateriality can be a concept within two different ontologies; one of inseparability and another of separability. If ‘constitutive entanglement’ presumes inseparability and ‘imbrication’ implies separability, the confusion about what is ‘sociomaterial imbrication’ – the inseparability of separability? – as introduced by Introna and Hayes (2011), becomes even greater.

But let us get back to the queen and the king! Beyond the contradictions between the two, there are also other concerns that might originate from their handling and understanding of the literature they quote and rely on.

The king states that nonhuman entities exercise agency through their performativity, “…through the things they do that users cannot completely or directly control” (Leonardi, 2011, p. 148) and he seeks support for this position from Barad (2003). Barad (2003), however, makes another argument, namely that in her understanding of performativity the distinction between humans and nonhumans, between subjects and objects and per se identi<sup>fi</sup>able entities, does not exist. In this context the king also seems to see agency – he distinguishes between human and material agencies – as an attribute of an entity, while in Barad's (2003) words “agency is not an attribute, but the ongoing reconfigurings of the world” (p. 818); it is a process! Furthermore, the king's understanding of material as a property of technology or as a product of the relationship between artifacts and people – the consistency of these two positions is in itself debatable – is not consistent with Barad's (2003) understanding of matter as a substance in its becoming, where matter refers to the materiality of phenomena as the primary ontological units not to a <sup>fi</sup>xed property of abstract independently existing objects

The jester's re<sup>fl</sup>ections will come back to Barad's (2003) work, but <sup>fi</sup>rst a <sup>fi</sup>nal concern about the king's views. The king argues that materiality is determined by when, how and why artifacts are used. Beyond the above discussed issue of materiality as a property, it could be said that this argument has been covered by the affordance concept already in its original connotation by Gibson (1977, 1979); this leaves the question open as to how sharp and consistent the concepts of materiality, performativity and affordance are actually de<sup>fi</sup>ned by the king.

But what does all this mean for the jester's guiding questions? There are contradictions and inconsistencies between the queen's and the king's understandings of sociomateriality. The king does not really subscribe to the concept of sociomateriality; in fact, he hardly uses the term nor does he explain what he thinks it is. Thus, he studies – as other scholars do – sociomaterial issues of IS in practice in the form of classic case study research inspired by and with a few borrowings from sociomaterial writings. In contrast, the queen explicitly de<sup>fi</sup>nes and uses the concept of sociomateriality. She argues for inseparability, but talks about entanglement, relations, entities and assemblages, all of which, in particular the latter presuppose the existence of separate things. However, if the queen considers inseparability as an ontological consequence, then it is contradictory when she puts forward that something is made up as an assemblage of something else. The question arises of what is actually her ontology and what is ontological in her stance: the entities being assembled or the inseparable entity? Moreover, this is clearly not in line with Barad's (2003) position, as explained earlier, whom the queen too uses to support her standing towards sociomateriality.

In the context of inseparability, the queen seems to mix two different aspects of sociomateriality as well, namely that the social always entails the material and that materiality is performed. In this regard her argument about sociomateriality remains unclear; is it a matter of inseparability or performativity and how do these go together? Interestingly, the queen does not make use of Barad's (2003) work here, who offers a clari<sup>fi</sup>cation based on her concept of phenomenon as the primary ontological unit. In Barad's terms, phenomena are ontologically primitive relations, i.e., relations without pre-existing relata. The relata exist only within phenomena; “…phenomena are the ontological inseparability of agentially intra-acting “components”” (Barad, 2003, p. 815, quotation marks in the original). The notion of intra-action constitutes a reworking of the traditional notion of causality. Intra-actions within a phenomenon enact agential separability, a local condition for exteriority within a phenomenon, and they enact agential cuts which effect and allow for local separation within a phenomenon. Agential cuts, however, are not about separation or separating constitutive elements by simply tearing them apart; they are rather about emphasizing and magnifying details through a lens and exploring and analyzing what is rendered visible in an intra-action through a magnifying glass. Hence, within inseparable phenomena agential separation is possible. In Barad's (2003) relational ontology, which she calls an agential realist ontology, performativity then is understood as the iterative intra-activity within a phenomenon.

On <sup>fi</sup>rst sight, Barad's (2003) work, which seems to underlie much of the queen's as well as the king's work – although she herself never uses the concept of sociomateriality – might qualify as jargon monoxide, an accusation Sutton (2010) directed towards the queen's work. This accusation might not be quite fair given that it is an undertaking of academic work to offer a speci<sup>fi</sup>c language to discuss phenomena and practices, and that the issue under investigation is a complex one which is not easy to come to terms with by the use of mundane language. On second sight, however, it becomes more obvious that this vocabulary, if consistently explained provides a solid ground for tackling a complex problem within IS research and practice. It allows us to identify and to better understand IS-related phenomena by investigating them in their inseparability as well as in their local separability, intra-action and agency through agential cuts, both in the context of utilization and the development of IS and IT. Finally, it solves another issue within the queen's work.

At times there is a lack of a clear and concise language in the queen's writings which might be the reason why her presented examples are very general and why it is so dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to identify in them what sociomateriality means as well as what consequences it might have for the use and design of IS and IT. If the new vocabulary, which is provided through the queen's sociomaterial approach, is hardly used and does not lead to a clearer analysis of any IS-related issues, is it then really necessary and useful? The queen bemoans a challenge in language, but why does she not de<sup>fi</sup>ne and provide the discipline with a more precise language when talking about the ontology of inseparability? Rather, at times she seems to obscure the issue with a number of terms such as entanglement and assemblage, which all seem to mean the same or something similar. Thus, unfortunately, beyond directing the IS community to an important and complex theme in IS research, an achievement she should be applauded for, she gives little help as to how sociomaterial issues of IS can be studied in practice to provide new insights. But then she and the king both point to Barad – another merit of their writings – who offers an ontology of inseparability and a language, which might present a possible way to investigate the sociomateriality of IS-related issues.

Recognizing these contributions and appreciating the found way out of the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed dilemma and challenge, the jester's thoughts wander off to the distance. A relational holistic ontology, which acknowledges relations and thus transcends Cartesian dualism and representationalism, while recognizing that “components” in phenomena can be identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as local parts of a whole, a holon, might provide a solution to the problem of ‘the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice’ and ‘the entwining of the material and the social.’ Also in practice it will make a difference whether an ontology of inseparability with local agential separability or whether an ontology of separability prevails.

But a last doubt remains. Some might argue that such approaches already exist at the court of IS and might put forward that approaches such as systems thinking (Checkland, 1981; Checkland & Scholes, 1990) and the concept of sociotechnical systems (Mumford, 1987) provide a solution towards ‘the recursive intertwining of humans and technology in practice’ and ‘the entwining of the material and the social.' In this context, also actor-network theory (see e.g., Callon, 1986: Latour. 1991, 2004. 2005: Law 1988) has been used in the IS <sup>fi</sup>eld for some time now and has been proposed as a basis for sociomaterial analysis (Law, 2004). Another answer might be present in the writings by organizational and management researchers of the software tool and material approach (Budde & Züllighoven, 1992; Ehn 1988), which is based on Heidegger's phenomenological analysis of tools and materials (1927, 2006).

Yet, others might argue that all these approaches individually have shortcomings. None is as clear about a relational ontology and an ontology of inseparability, none is as clear about the phenomenon, practices rather than an object, thing or entity as a primary ontological unit of analysis as it is suggested in the notion of sociomateriality as discussed and re<sup>fl</sup>ected upon here. So no, sociomateriality is a new approach and not academic jargon monoxide after all, or is it?
