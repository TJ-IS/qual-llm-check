# CROWDFUNDING SUCCESS EFFECTS ON FINANCING OUTCOMES FOR STARTUPS: A SIGNALING THEORY PERSPECTIVE<sup>1</sup>

Sunghan Ryu USC-SJTU Institute of Cultural and Creative Industry, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, Shanghai, CHINA {shryu@sjtu.edu.cn}

Keongtae Kim CUHK Business School, Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, NT, HONG KONG {keongkim@cuhk.edu.hk}

Jungpil Hahn School of Computing, National University of Singapore, SINGAPORE {jungpil@nus.edu.sg}

This study adopts a signaling theory perspective to examine whether and how crowdfunding (relative to angel financing) influences subsequent venture capital (VC) investments in startups. We used a bivariate probit model with propensity score matching to address the potential endogeneity of the initial funding choice. Subsequently, we found that crowdfunded startups have a lower chance of receiving VC funding than angel-financed startups and that the effect is more negative for startups located outside of startup cluster cities. We show that corporate VCs, unlike independent VCs comprising the majority of VCs, favor crowdfunded startups. Our study contributes to the literature on crowdfunding, startup finance, and the transformative effects of IT-enabled platforms. This study further discusses the practical implications of crowdfunding in startup finance ecosystems.

Keywords: Crowdfunding, angel, startup, venture capital, corporate venture capital, signaling theory

## Introduction

Information technology (IT) disrupts industries and marketplaces (Burtch et al., 2018; Dedrick & Kraemer, 2005; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Zervas et al., 2017). Recently, the information systems (IS) literature has focused on how ITenabled platforms are transforming business practices and organizational management in various industries, including accommodations (Chang & Sokol, 2020; Zervas et al., 2017) and transportation (Burtch et al., 2018). Novel IT-enabled platforms and services have begun to resolve market inefficiencies for various resources, including data, computing power, expertise, and knowledge (Greenstein & Zhu, 2018; Guerin et al., 2019; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Mindel et al., 2018). These platforms have transformed many industries by expanding the reach of networks, lowering transaction costs, and democratizing access to resources. The industry-level transformation driven by IT-enabled platforms has also made incumbent firms respond to disruptions (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Yoo et al., 2010). For example, firms have had to reconfigure their organizational structures and processes in response to disruptive transformation (Chan et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2021).

Crowdfunding, a novel form of IT-enabled fundraising, has changed the landscape of the startup financing industry for early-stage startups and impacted the organizational strategies and business practices of existing investors in the industry (Geva et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2018; Kim & Hann, 2019; Ryu, 2019). Traditionally, early-stage startups with high growth potential have been financed by a de facto source, angels, or groups of professional investors (Ibrahim, 2008). Angels invest in promising startups, give them valuable advice, and provide startups with access to their networks to help grow their businesses. However, angels have traditionally been limited in number and geographically concentrated in select locations, making it difficult for startups to gain access, especially if the startups were not from these locations. Crowdfunding has opened up an alternative source of early-stage financing for startups, regardless of their location. Interestingly, crowdfunding platforms do not appear to disfavor startups such as minority-founded startups or startups located outside venture capital (VC) hubs (Cumming & Dai, 2010; Lee & Huang, 2018), which have traditionally been neglected by the incumbent investors (Dern, 2015; Greenberg & Mollick, 2017). The presence of traditionally neglected startups on crowdfunding platforms suggests that crowdfunding may help to rebalance the disproportionate capital distribution in the startup financing industry.

While crowdfunding has begun to play an increasingly important role in the startup finance ecosystem, the extant literature has predominantly focused on its short-run effects— the success of crowdfunding campaigns. Most studies have prioritized identifying factors that determine campaign success or participation behaviors of individual investors (Burtch et al., 2013, 2014; Geva et al., 2019; Greenberg & Mollick, 2017; Hong et al., 2018; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Lin & Viswanathan, 2015; Ryu et al., 2020; Sorenson et al., 2016; Wessel et al., 2017; Younkin & Kuppuswamy, 2018). Although meaningful, these studies provide little insight into how crowdfunding, as an IT-enabled platform, might affect the startup financing industry. Specifically, little is known about how crowdfunding influences startups and traditional investors (i.e., VCs and angels) in the startup financing industry. Startups often need to raise follow-on funding after early financing for survival and growth in the long term (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015). Thus, understanding the broader implications of crowdfunding in the startup financing industry requires an examination of its long-term effects on crowdfunded startups. This study aims to fill this gap by examining whether and how crowdfunding (relative to angel investing) affects subsequent VC investments in the later stages of the startup financing lifecycle. Further, it investigates how the effects vary by type of startup and VC.

These research questions are important and timely, both theoretically and practically. First, understanding how the entry of new IT-enabled platforms impacts traditional industries and their existing players is a fundamental question in the literature (Burtch et al., 2018; Dedrick & Kraemer, 2005; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Zervas et al., 2017). The entry of new IT-enabled players in an industry has broad consequences for organizational strategies and market structures (Lyytinen &

Rose, 2003). While the arrival of crowdfunding can influence the startup financing industry in different ways, we still have a limited understanding of its relationships with other players on the demand and supply sides.

Second, securing early-stage financing affects startups’ chances of accessing subsequent financing for their eventual survival and growth; for example, angel financing has been recognized as the de facto stepping stone to obtaining VC investment (Hellmann et al., 2013). With the advent of crowdfunding, VCs might be lured by startups’ commercial potential, as demonstrated by their successful crowdfunding campaigns. Thus, we expect that crowdfunding success may also affect the chances of obtaining subsequent investments from VCs, albeit through a different route (Roma et al., 2017). Recently, a few studies have observed the effects of crowdfunding on follow-on funding outcomes (Colombo & Shafi, 2016; Drover et al., 2017; Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014; Roma et al., 2018; Roma et al., 2017). These studies provide suggestive evidence on how crowdfunding affects the startup financing industry. However, there is still much to learn about the interrelationship between crowdfunding and the various players in the industry. This study aims to fill this gap in the literature.

We adopt a signaling theory perspective to propose and examine VCs’ interpretations of quality signals from angelfinanced and crowdfunded startups. Signaling theory is a suitable theoretical lens for our research context for three reasons. First, it is well known that VCs rely on quality signals from startups to counter their asymmetric information about startups (Busenitz et al., 2005; Eddleston et al., 2016; Hoenen et al., 2014). Second, angel financing and crowdfunding differ as early funding sources along various dimensions, while both alternatives are costly to attain; thus, they produce substantively different signals about the quality of startups (Stuart et al., 1999). For example, success in crowdfunding signals greater certainty in future market demand based on the “wisdom of crowds” (Roma et al., 2017); conversely, angel financing signals a startup’s access to sophisticated advice from specialized professional networks, which may lead to superior business plans (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015). As investors in later stages, VCs react according to quality signals based on their interpretations of the early funding channels taken by startups (Drover et al., 2017). Given the substantively different nature of the quality signals from the two early financing channels, we examine whether crowdfunding and angel financing would help different types of startups receive follow-on VC investment differently. Finally, it is well known that corporate VCs (CVCs) have different investment objectives and interests from independent VCs (IVCs) (Ma, 2019; Macmillan et al., 2008). Signaling theory offers an appropriate framework for understanding how different types of VCs (i.e., CVCs vs. IVCs) show heterogeneous responses to quality signals from the two early financing channels.

We collected Kickstarter data on technology-related startups from 2011 to 2013, and our sampling strategy yielded a sample of 283 successfully crowdfunded startups. The Crunchbase database was used to construct a comparable sample of 708 angel-financed startups from the same period and to track their financing outcomes until the end of 2016. As the sample of crowdfunded and angel-financed startups was not selected randomly, our analysis had to address the potential bias arising from endogenous sample selection. To this end, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to construct a matched sample that addresses the selection of observable characteristics of startups and their founders. We combined this with a bivariate probit model that incorporated the strategic decision to choose crowdfunding over angel financing. This allowed us to address the possible selection bias that the observable variables did not control.

Our results show that crowdfunded startups are, on average, less likely to receive follow-on VC investments than angel-financed startups, indicating that quality signals from crowdfunding are less effective than those from angel financing. We further observed a heterogeneous effect of startup location and found that startups located outside of startup clusters tend to be more disadvantaged despite their success in crowdfunding, suggesting that the effectiveness of crowdfunding success as a quality signal from startups can be less effective depending on the specific characteristics of the startups. Finally, unlike IVCs comprising the majority of the VC group, CVCs were shown to be more favorable toward crowdfunded startups than angelfinanced startups, suggesting that CVCs respond differently to quality signals from crowdfunding and angel investing because their investment objectives and strategies are different from those of IVCs.

Our study makes several significant contributions to the literature in IS and related disciplines. Being among the first to examine the impact of crowdfunding on post-campaign outcomes (i.e., the subsequent funding of startups in this study), our study complements prior crowdfunding research focusing on campaign outcomes and within-platform market dynamics (Burtch et al., 2014; Geva et al., 2019; Hong et al., 2018; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019). Second, it contributes to the burgeoning literature on the transformative effects of IT-enabled platforms on traditional industries and their incumbents (Burtch et al., 2018; Dedrick & Kraemer, 2005; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003). Previous studies have shown how IT-enabled platforms transform industries, such as accommodations and transportation, by substituting traditional players with ITenabled platforms. We extend this literature by showing that ITenabled crowdfunding can complement a traditional funding channel (i.e., angel investing) and influence downstream players (i.e., VCs). Moreover, we examine some of the nuances of this disruption; specifically, whether crowdfunding could benefit underprivileged startups located outside of startup cluster cities that have been previously neglected by traditional investors. Third, our study contributes to the VC literature by highlighting the different strategic motives and investment strategies of CVCs and IVCs (Kim et al., 2016; Ma, 2019; Macmillan et al., 2008). We posit that crowdfunding sends quality signals that are different from those of angel investing, to which different types of later-stage VCs react differently. Finally, our study highlights that the potential of crowdfunding as a means of democratizing access to finance is rather complicated, providing implications for stakeholders in the startup finance ecosystem.

## Related Work

## IT-Enabled Platforms and Traditional Industries

IT enables the creation of platforms to match buyers and sellers by taking advantage of technology to lower entry costs for sellers and improve the matching of supply and demand in various domains (Van Alstyne et al., 2016), including online markets for software development (Freelancer, Upwork), loans (Prosper, Lending Club), crafts (Etsy), accommodations (Airbnb), rides (Uber, Didi), and startup funding (Kickstarter, Crowdcube). These IT-enabled platforms have disrupted traditional industries and existing players (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Yoo et al., 2010).

Extant studies on platform economics have examined key market design issues for online platforms (Einav et al., 2016; Greenstein & Zhu, 2018; Guerin et al., 2019; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019; Mindel et al., 2018), including the effective matching of numerous heterogeneous buyers and sellers while maintaining low search frictions (Burdett et al., 2001; Dinerstein et al., 2018). Furthermore, there have been a few studies on understanding the broader implications of ITenabled platforms in traditional industries (e.g., how the entry of online platforms leads to changes in market structure and affects incumbent players) (Chang & Sokol, 2020; Zervas et al., 2017). For example, Zervas et al. (2017) found that an increase in Airbnb listings caused a decrease in hotel revenue, and this effect was stronger for lower-end and non-business hotels. Chang and Sokol (2020) found that Airbnb’s entry led to heterogeneous responses from incumbent players: price competition with low-end hotels and quality competition with high-end hotels. In the transportation industry, the entry of ride-hailing platforms has impacted the demand for other modes of transportation (e.g., taxis) to catalyze structural changes in the industry (Cramer & Krueger, 2016; Nie, 2017).

These studies highlight the substitution of traditional incumbents by IT-enabled platforms, albeit with different natures. Our study is closely related to this stream of research but also diverges. Previous studies have focused on ITenabled platforms as direct competitors or substitutes for traditional players. However, our study considers the potential role of online crowdfunding platforms in complementing existing players (i.e., angels) and influencing downstream players (i.e., VCs) in the startup financing industry.

## Crowdfunding and Post-Campaign Outcomes

The extant literature on crowdfunding has predominantly examined the factors influencing individual investors’ funding behaviors and crowdfunding campaign success within crowdfunding platforms (Burtch et al., 2013; Mollick, 2014). Information about the quality of creators and campaigns serves as effective signals for potential (crowd) investors. For example, creators’ social networks (Lin et al., 2014; Mollick, 2014) and educational backgrounds (Ahlers et al., 2015) influence investor behavior and campaign success. Creators engagement within the crowdfunding platform plays a similar role in increasing the likelihood of success (Colombo et al., 2015; Yang & Hahn, 2015). Furthermore, it is well established that peer effects drive the demand for crowdfunding campaigns, indicating the presence of social influence on Individuals’ funding decisions. Kim and Viswanathan (2019) showed that early contributors with expertise distinctly influenced later contributors. The geographical distance between a creator and potential contributors has been found to be essential for explaining social influence (Agrawal et al., 2015b; Lin & Viswanathan, 2015).

While most crowdfunding research has explored the dynamics and interactions within crowdfunding platforms, recent studies have begun to investigate its broader impact on the startup finance ecosystem. For example, Kim and Hann (2019) showed that the usage level of crowdfunding by startups in a region is negatively associated with access to bank lending in that region. Kaminski et al. (2019) demonstrated that the number of successful crowdfunding campaigns is associated with an increase in VC investment in the corresponding product categories. Several startup-level studies have analyzed the effects of crowdfunding on post-campaign outcomes. Da Cruz (2018) showed that crowdfunding can enable startups to commercialize their products by showing that greater crowdfunding success is associated with products’ future availability in online marketplaces. Other studies that have examined the effect of crowdfunding on subsequent VC funding are in line with this study (Colombo & Shafi, 2021; Kaminski et al., 2019; Roma et al., 2018; Thies et al., 2019). For example, Thies et al. (2019) found that successful crowdfunding campaigns are more likely to receive follow-on VC investments. Our study builds on and extends this emerging critical stream of literature in two ways. First, to offer a more holistic picture, we studied the effects of crowdfunding in conjunction with angel investing. Second, for a more nuanced understanding, we highlight that the impact of crowdfunding success on subsequent VC funding might vary depending on the types of startups seeking funding and the types of VCs that invest in these startups. ## Traditional Players in Startup Financing: VC and Angel

Traditionally, small groups of expert investors such as VCs and angels have largely dominated the startup financing industry. VCs are the most desired source of financing for startups with high growth potential; they offer financial resources and expert advice to maximize their expansion (Bertoni et al., 2011). They select startups based on various evaluation criteria using multiple sources of information (Hsu, 2007; Zider, 1998) but consider the expectation of strong financial returns to be the most critical factor. This expectation is the outcome of careful due diligence in which VCs rigorously scrutinize the capability of the founding team, the soundness of the technology, the potential market size for the products/services, and the feasibility of the business plan (Fried & Hisrich, 1994). Furthermore, the VCs themselves bring a reputation effect to their investment decisions, which further promotes the growth of their selected startups. Therefore, association with reputable VCs is highly instrumental for startups (Baum & Silverman, 2004; Tian, 2011). However, inherently high information asymmetry exposes VCs to a high risk of failure (Connelly et al., 2011). Information asymmetry and the strong motivation to pursue financial returns drive VCs to favor (and also disfavor) startups with certain characteristics (Cumming & Dai, 2010; Dern, 2015; Tian, 2011), such as founders’ educational backgrounds and startup experiences (Hall & Hofer, 1993; Hsu, 2007).

There are two types of VCs—CVCs and IVCs. CVCs are established corporations that provide equity investments in startups (Benson & Ziedonis, 2009). They pursue strategic objectives, such as technology acquisition and exposure to innovative products and services, that might challenge their parent corporations’ business models (Dushnitsky & Lenox, 2005; Macmillan et al., 2008). Through this approach, corporations can gain access to new market opportunities and identify prospective acquisition targets (Kim et al., 2016). Conversely, IVCs focus mainly on financial returns (Wadhwa & Kotha, 2006; Weber & Weber, 2011). Although both IVCs and CVCs provide capital to startups, they follow different institutional logics guided by distinctive norms and desired objectives (e.g., financial returns for IVCs vs. strategic outcomes for CVCs).

Angel financing has flourished over the past few decades as an important source of early-stage startup funding (Freear et al., 1994; Kerr et al., 2014). Angel investors are wealthy individuals—often former entrepreneurs—who invest individually rather than as groups, in contrast to VCs that focus on later-stage startups. Most angels have strong investment records as experienced entrepreneurs and investors and spend considerable effort screening potential investment targets (Drover et al., 2017; Kerr et al., 2014). Angels’ entrepreneurial experiences enable them to offer practical advice on strategy and business operations and connect startups with other experts from their networks. Moreover, angel investors and VCs have similar preferences for startup characteristics (Hsu, 2007). Therefore, angel financing has become the de facto stepping stone for VC investments (Hellmann et al., 2013). Startups initially funded by angel investors were 70% more likely to obtain follow-on VC investments (Kerr et al., 2014). Thus, angel investors and VCs complement each other by focusing on different stages and investing in different amounts (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015).

Our study positions crowdfunding as an additional channel, in addition to angel investing, for early-stage funding of startups. As a result, crowdfunding may affect startups chances of receiving subsequent VC funding. While investments from angels and VCs are available for a select few early-stage startups, crowdfunding is a relatively more accessible option for numerous startups because of the openness of platforms. Thus, crowdfunding can complement existing players in the startup financing industry (i.e., angels and VCs) by providing a more accessible funding option for early-stage startups. Crowdfunding may cover a wider scope of the early phase than angel financing does. Crowdfunding typically spans not only the initial development of a prototype as a proof of concept, similar to the scope of angel financing, but also the sourcing of materials, and producing the product in later phases. Nonetheless, with the different yet shared coverage of angel financing and crowdfunding, both early-stage funding sources are important financing alternatives, bridging the gap with subsequent larger-scale financing from later-stage investors, such as VCs.

VCs differ from crowdfunders and angel investors, who generally rely on their own capital for startup funding. VCs tend to invest larger funding amounts and offer value-added services to their portfolio of startups to maximize the portfolio’s value (Fried & Hisrich, 1994). Accordingly, due to the associated costs and risk of investment, VCs predominantly focus on startups at a later stage than crowdfunding and angel investors (Drover et al., 2017). ## Theory and Hypotheses

## Signaling Theory in Startup Financing

We adopt the signaling theory perspective to propose how a startup’s source of early financing—whether angel investing or crowdfunding—produces a more or less effective signal to influence the likelihood of obtaining follow-on VC investments (Colombo, 2021). Signaling theory focuses on reducing the information asymmetry between signalers and receivers (Connelly et al., 2011; Courtney et al., 2017; Spence, 1973, 2002) because signalers have private information required by receivers for decision-making (Spence, 2002; Stiglitz, 2002). The startup financing industry exhibits considerable information asymmetry (Bapna, 2019). Startups typically develop a novel product or service with uncertain market demand, and an objective and proven track record of the startups’ quality and potential is often unavailable (Colombo, 2021). Before more verifiable and objective information becomes available, startups have private information about their inherent quality, which might help attract potential investors (Ahlers et al., 2015; Alsos & Ljunggren, 2017). One possible way to effectively mitigate the information asymmetry between startups and potential investors is to use signals to communicate their quality so that potential investors can draw information from the quality signals (Busenitz et al., 2005; Colombo, 2021).

According to signaling theory, signals corresponding to a signaler’s intent effectively reduce information asymmetry and induce appropriate responses from the receiver (Gulati & Higgins, 2003). Several key factors influence signaling effectiveness, indicating that the signal sent by the signaler draws attention from the intended receiver (Warner et al., 2006). The first is signal credibility (Busenitz et al., 2005), which is indicated by the cost of producing a signal (Connelly et al., 2011). Signals must be credible to convey appropriate quality information, and the cost of producing a signal conveys the credibility of the intended message (Zhang & Wiersema, 2009). It would make less sense for lower-quality startups to produce costly signals, such as acquiring patents and receiving awards (Certo, 2003); conversely, higherquality startups can easily invest the efforts and resources necessary to acquire patents and receive awards. The second is signal fit, the extent to which the signal is correlated with the signaler’s (unobservable) quality (Connelly et al., 2011). Finally, receiver attention influences signaling effectiveness (Gulati & Higgins, 2003). VCs scan the market for startups congruent with their investment interests (Busenitz et al., 2005) but may ignore signals lacking the information they need to assess quality relevant to their investment interests. Therefore, we must consider receivers’ interpretation processes (Perkins & Hendry, 2005) when determining which startup signals are more effective in reducing investors’ uncertainty (Courtney et al., 2017) depending on their investment purposes.

We use signaling theory to develop hypotheses about how early-stage funding sources of startups—angel vs. crowdfunding—might influence their likelihood of securing follow-on VC funding. Here, startups are the signalers, VCs are the receivers, and startups’ successful funding from angels or crowdfunding constitutes an essential component of their quality signal to VCs. The attainment of early-stage funding is indeed a costly signal for startups and should influence VCs’ investment decisions. Signals that have been shown to effectively deliver valuable information to VCs have been identified and their effects tested in the literature. The literature shows that quality signals, such as human capital, social capital, and affiliation with third parties, are often effective in conveying the underlying capability of startups in the startup financing context (Colombo, 2021). Early financing sources may serve as a quality signal, similar to affiliation with third parties (Bapna, 2019). VCs heed the signals if they are aligned with their investment purposes. Next, we formally develop our hypotheses.

## Early Funding Source as Signal: Angel Investing vs. Crowdfunding

Theoretical arguments and empirical evidence support the idea that affiliation with a credible third party can serve as a reliable signal of quality (Colombo, 2021; Connelly et al., 2011) that can help prospective investors distinguish highquality startups from low-quality ones. For example, prior studies have shown that VCs can certify the quality of investing in startups, thereby contributing to their survival and growth (Baum & Silverman, 2004; Tian, 2011). We believe that success in crowdfunding and angel investing can also serve as credible quality signals, helping mitigate the information asymmetry between startups and VCs.

Crowdfunding has emerged as a complementary alternative to traditional angel financing for early-stage startups. It is a more democratic way to raise funds from a loosely linked open community of investors, also called crowdfunders who, with their various financial and nonfinancial reasons for backing crowdfunding campaigns, differ from traditional investors (Gerber et al., 2012; Ryu & Kim, 2016). They are typically less sophisticated and make decisions based on less conventional information (Lin et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2015). Moreover, crowdfunding provides less obvious information about the characteristics of startups and their founders than traditional investors do to assess the quality of potential startups despite their salient and easily accessible information on funding progress. (Burtch et al., 2013; Li & Duan, 2014). Compared with angel investing, which primarily focuses on the early phases of startups to enable them to develop proof-ofconcept prototypes, crowdfunding (especially reward-based) covers a broader scope of the early phases (see Figure 1).

VCs, as later-stage investors, judge startups’ quality and potential based on successful early-stage funding. Regardless of the source, an effective signal contains information corresponding to the key evaluation criteria used by VCs, such as the capability of the founding team, potential market size, and expected financial returns (Baum & Silverman, 2004; Hsu, 2007; Shepherd et al., 2003; Tian, 2011; Ueda, 2004; Zider, 1998). A proven record of early-stage financing, be it angel financing or successful crowdfunding, will be an effective signal if it fits the VCs’ evaluation criteria.

On the one hand, VCs’ interests and evaluation criteria align well with the quality signals from angel financing. First, angels spend considerable time and effort screening potential targets; therefore, their investment decisions certify the quality of startups (Drover et al., 2017; Kerr et al., 2014). Most angels, such as experienced and seasoned investors, have strong investment records. Thus, their investment validates VCs primary interest in ensuring the high quality of the founding team and the potential for high returns and growth (Croce et al., 2018). Second, angels offer access to their experience and networks, which heightens the credibility of the signal (Freear et al., 1994; Kerr et al., 2014). Third, and most importantly, angel investors have preferences similar to those of VCs, and both base their investment decisions on similar startup characteristics, such as the capability of the founding team (Hsu, 2007). Angel investors and VCs complement each other at different stages by investing in different quanta (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015). Startups that combine angel and VC financing have been shown to receive more accumulated funding and have a higher likelihood of being acquired or going public via an initial public offering (IPO) (Croce et al., 2018).

Successful crowdfunding may also lead to follow-on investments by VCs (Roma et al., 2017). Successful crowdfunding campaigns have a positive connection with ex post commercial success (Kim & Viswanathan, 2019). Crowdfunding campaigns generate valuable comments and suggestions from future customers (Hui et al., 2014), which helps create a base of loyal customers who may provide followon financing opportunities (Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014). The reputation of the crowdfunding platform and the number of collective funders, as an indication of future market potential, may influence VCs’ decisions on whether to invest in crowdfunded startups (Drover et al., 2017). Thus, a successful crowdfunding campaign can mitigate the information asymmetry between startups and VCs. At the regional level, Sorenson et al. (2016) showed that an increase in successful crowdfunding campaigns in a region is positively related to an increase in VC investments in the same year.

Nevertheless, VCs may be less attentive to signals from successful crowdfunding than to those from angel investing. This is because the signals used by crowdfunders are incongruent with those used by the VCs. First, crowdfunders are less sophisticated and possess limited information when evaluating a crowdfunding campaign as campaign descriptions usually provide only fragmented information (Gleasure, 2015). They lack the financial incentives to conduct thorough due diligence because they are small-scale investors who are geographically distant from startups (Ahlers et al., 2015). Third, crowdfunders often invest for reasons other than financial returns (Jiang et al., 2021; Ryu & Kim, 2016) and contribute to short-term nonfinancial returns, such as rewards from campaigns. Therefore, we conjecture that success at crowdfunding can signal commercial potential but not the potential for higher financial returns, VCs’ primary consideration.

Considered together, we propose that crowdfunding success helps reduce uncertainty regarding startups’ underlying quality. However, VCs will find the signal from angel investments to be more informative and congruent with their own investment objectives. Thus, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Compared to angel-financed startups, VCs are less likely to provide subsequent funding to crowdfunded startups.

## Heterogeneous Effects by Startup Characteristics

One important factor in determining the effectiveness of signals is the type of signal to which the receivers are attentive (Cohen & Dean, 2005). In startup finance, VCs carefully consider startup characteristics (Baum & Silverman, 2004; Tian, 2011) and focus on and favor startups’ specific characteristics for investment decisions (Cumming & Dai, 2010; Dern, 2015; Tian, 2011). For example, VCs have been shown to hold a strong preference for startups whose founders have attained higher education degrees (Hall & Hofer, 1993) and have prior experience in other startups (Hall & Hofer, 1993).

Interestingly, it has also been shown that VCs are frequently fixated on certain attributes even when others are proven to be more influential and relevant (Zhang & Cueto, 2016). A particular attribute can be mistaken as a critical indicator owing to information asymmetry in the industry and the bounded rationality of VCs (Brush et al., 2018). Many such biases have been examined in the literature (Balachandra, 2020; Buttner & Rosen, 1989; Cumming & Dai, 2010; Jääskeläinen & Maula, 2014; Lee & Huang, 2018; Tinkler et al., 2015; Zacharakis & Shepherd, 2001).

In this study, we consider one such bias: local bias. Physical proximity (or, inversely, distance) is a persistent concern for VCs. Theoretical and empirical studies have shown that VCs tend to make investment decisions based on their geographical proximity to startups (Cumming & Dai, 2010). Such local bias from VCs exists mainly because of the information asymmetry between startups and VCs (Chen et al., 2010). Various factors seem to contribute to VCs’ local bias, including VC reputation, VC network, industry specialization, syndication activities, and status as a lead VC (Cumming & Dai, 2010). In the U.S., startups in states with lower accessibility to VCs are more likely to relocate to states where VCs and other startups are clustered (e.g., California and Massachusetts) (De Prijcker et al., 2017). Moreover, startups that relocate to a startup-cluster state subsequently enjoy a greater likelihood of receiving VC investment than those that do not.

We argue that startup location interacts with the initial funding sources in affecting follow-on VC investments. We previously proposed that the source of early funding, crowdfunding, or angel financing, has signaling value for potential investors, but in different magnitudes. Although crowdfunding success can serve as a quality signal for VCs, the effectiveness of this signal may be less salient than that of angel financing. Moreover, we propose that the signal from crowdfunding, compared to angel financing, could be less effective for startups outside of startup cluster cities for two reasons. First, there is a difference in the signaling costs between crowdfunding and angel financing for startups located in startup cluster cities and those located outside. Given that VCs and angels possess similar investment preferences for target startups, startups disfavored by VCs are also less likely to receive attention from angels (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015; Kerr et al., 2014)—for example, startups located outside startup cluster cities. As such, angel financing can be more difficult for startups in those cities because of less access. Thus, the signal from angel financing is costlier to produce than crowdfunding success for startups not located in startup cluster cities. If such a (disfavored) startup can obtain angel financing, its quality signal will be stronger than that of a crowdfunded startup outside a startup cluster city. Although crowdfunding success is also costly to obtain, it is relatively less costly for startups from such cities to achieve successful crowdfunding, given the lower entry barriers (Antretter et al., 2020; Thies et al., 2018).

Second, compared with those in startup cluster cities, crowdfunded startups outside of these cities may receive less attention from VCs. VCs are more familiar with and confident in evaluating startups with specific (preferred) characteristics (e.g., located in startup clusters). Angel investment based on similar preferences should reinforce the confidence of VCs (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015). The uncertainty perceived by potential VCs will be lower for startups with characteristics that VCs also favor and in which angels have invested.

However, uncertainty remains high for startups that do not exhibit these characteristics.

Although successful crowdfunding provides valuable quality information regarding startups, it remains a new and emerging channel. Based on our arguments in the development of H1, VCs are less likely to have faith in the information given its novelty. Combined with the less preferred characteristic (i.e., located outside startup cluster cities), crowdfunding success may not mitigate VCs’ uncertainty. This may, in turn, decrease the likelihood of obtaining follow-on VC investment, even after overcoming the disadvantage of the less preferred characteristic in the early funding stage. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: VCs are more likely to provide subsequent funding to crowdfunded startups in startup cluster cities compared to crowdfunded startups outside of startup cluster cities.

## Type of VCs as Frame of Interpretation: CVCs vs. IVCs

Receivers’ distinctive needs and institutional logics influence the resonance of signals to them, affecting signal effectiveness (Gulati & Higgins, 2003). In other words, the receivers interpretation processes (Perkins & Hendry, 2005) play an important role in determining which signals are more effective in reducing uncertainty (Courtney et al., 2017). Receivers possess idiosyncratic needs and pay more attention to certain signals or even interpret the same signals differently (Scheaf et al., 2018) depending on their desired outcomes and the exchange context (Highhouse et al., 2007; Tihanyi et al., 2003). Furthermore, the institutional logic underlying the signaling context influences receivers’ attention and consequently signal effectiveness (Scheaf et al., 2018). As institutional logic is a higher-level norm that provides meaning to what is desirable in a specific context, a receiver’s institutional logic provides an idiosyncratic lens that focuses on different aspects of a signal (Thornton, 2002).

CVCs are similar to IVCs in providing capital to startups, but they follow different institutional logics guided by distinctive norms and desired objectives because they are owned and influenced by their parent firms (Wadhwa & Kotha, 2006; Weber & Weber, 2011). They are naturally divergent from IVCs in their raison d’être, organizational structure, compensation structure, and support for portfolio firms (Gompers & Lerner, 2000). Whereas IVCs tend to strictly pursue economic aims with a narrow focus on financial returns, CVCs consider strategic aims to explore, embed, and exploit knowledge. CVCs also play a stronger role in attracting customers, suppliers, and partners and supporting the technological development of startups (Maula et al., 2005).

Therefore, CVCs have different complementary value-added profiles, owing to differences in institutional logic. Consequently, determining which signals are attended to and the extent to which different signals maintain their effectiveness depends on the type of VCs.

CVCs are expected to value crowdfunded startups over angelfinanced ones. First, as discussed, CVCs primarily seek strategic benefits (Dushnitsky & Lenox, 2005) to acquire knowledge and support the parent firm’s strategic objectives (Benson & Ziedonis, 2009; Macmillan et al., 2008). Firms create CVC arms to conduct systematic equity investment in early-stage startups (Ma, 2019) to expose themselves to new markets and technologies and acquire knowledge, which aims to enhance their innovation capacity (Macmillan et al., 2008). Compared to IVCs, whose primary aim is return on capital, the development of new business opportunities is a significant goal for most CVCs. Although CVCs do not intend to lose money, their strategic focus is critical to success (Gompers & Lerner, 2000). For example, on behalf of Google (Alphabet), GV (formerly Google Ventures) invested in dozens of technology startups relevant to existing and new businesses through minority equity stakes. Based on their institutional logic, CVCs will be more interested in crowdfunded startups because of their access to actual customers who provide feedback and suggestions for product improvement (da Cruz, 2018). Successful crowdfunding campaigns verify potential market demand for new products (Roma et al., 2017). Crowdfunding can also provide a window into emerging marketable technologies or products that can be strategically valuable to CVCs’ parent firms. These arguments lead us to expect that CVCs, as late-stage investors, will react more favorably to signals from successful crowdfunding, in contrast to IVCs’ preference for signals from angel investing. Based on these arguments, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: CVCs are more likely to provide subsequent funding to crowdfunded startups compared to angel-financed startups.

## Data and Empirical Analysis

## Empirical Setting

To empirically test our hypotheses on the effects of the two early-stage funding sources, we collected data from multiple sources. First, we used Kickstarter, a leading crowdfunding platform extensively studied in the crowdfunding literature (Kuppuswamy & Bayus, 2017; Mollick & Nanda, 2015), to establish a sample of successfully crowdfunded startups. Crowdfunders on Kickstarter receive tangible rewards, such as copies of developed products, a practice called rewardbased crowdfunding, because funders gain early access to innovative products. Moreover, successful crowdfunding projects on Kickstarter signal future demand in anticipation of an actual market launch (Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014). Kickstarter, and reward-based crowdfunding, in general, have been widely accepted in the literature examining the effectiveness of signals from successful crowdfunding in appealing to stakeholders in later funding stages, including VCs and potential customers (Colombo & Shafi, 2021; Kaminski et al., 2019; Roma et al., 2018; Thies et al., 2020).<sup>2</sup>

Our data includes all successful campaigns initiated in the technology, game, and design categories by startups on Kickstarter from January 2011 to December 2013.<sup>3</sup> Kickstarter covers diverse product categories, such as games, arts, and music, but we limit our sample to the aforementioned three categories, which are more similar to conventional startups (Kaminski et al., 2019; Mollick & Kuppuswamy, 2014; Roma et al., 2017). For example, technology project creators are akin to entrepreneurs in the technology sector who offer innovative products such as smart devices and mobile apps. Thus, crowdfunding is an attractive platform for initiating new projects or fueling ongoing projects. By contrast, many non-technology projects are created by individuals or nonprofit organizations and are less likely to aim for growth and seek follow-on funding.

We focus on successful crowdfunded campaigns initiated in the U.S. We omitted unsuccessful campaigns from our main sample to compare the success of crowdfunded and angelfinanced startups. In most cases, information about startups failing to secure angel funding is unavailable. We also excluded campaigns with funding goal amounts less than US\$50,000, as campaigns with small funding goals are likely to be too modest to seek follow-on financing (Kaminski et al., 2019; Roma et al., 2018). As VCs typically conduct their own market research to identify potential target startups, setting low funding goals makes such campaigns more likely to be successful but, at the time, they are less likely to be of interest to VCs. We also excluded campaigns if extensive background searches on Google and LinkedIn failed to uncover sufficient company information.<sup>4</sup>

To construct a sample of startups receiving early funding from angels, we used Crunchbase, a comprehensive public database of startup funding (Homburg et al., 2014; Kerr et al., 2014).<sup>5</sup> We started with a list of angel-financed startups in the U.S. during the same period, excluding those whose company information was unavailable. Further, we checked the mutual exclusiveness of crowdfunded and angelfinanced startups and excluded seven startups that had both successful crowdfunding and angel financing.<sup>6</sup> This small portion of startups receiving both types of funding indicates that the two funding sources target startups at similar stages. This does not necessarily mean that the two early-stage funding channels compete for funding early-stage startups. Rather, this implies that startups may choose either (or both) funding source(s) but are likely to pursue one source given their constrained resources. Because crowdfunders are different from angel investors in several dimensions, startups likely pursue crowdfunding (over angel investing), given different investment motivations and goals. Therefore, crowdfunding offers an alternative funding source for startups, especially for those that have difficulty accessing angel investments.

Ultimately, our sample included 991 startups (283 crowdfunded and 708 angel financed). We then gathered data on follow-on VC funding events from Crunchbase up to the end of 2016 and complemented it by leveraging social media and company websites for missing or incomplete information.

## Variables

## Dependent Variables

Our dependent variable is related to subsequent VC funding outcomes. We tracked whether a startup received its first round of formal investment from VCs (VC investment). VC investment is an indicator variable taking the value of 1 if the startup received the first round of formal VC funding and 0 otherwise. To identify VC funding, we first included all investments assigned to the VC category on Crunchbase. Subsequently, we manually checked the list of investors for that particular round of investment and ignored that round if the investors included only non-VC firms, such as individuals, angel networks, and incubators. We focused only on the first round of VC investments because also including later stages would have likely confounded our findings, as later stages of VC funding are inevitably affected by the first round of VC funding. In the full sample of 991 startups, 352 startups received subsequent (first-round) VC funding: 280 (of 708 or 39.5%) angel-financed and 72 (of 283 or 25.4%) crowdfunded startups.

We constructed an additional dependent variable to distinguish the response from CVCs from those from IVCs: CVC investment equals 1 if the investor group of a focal startup included at least one CVC and 0 otherwise.<sup>7</sup> Of the 352 startups with follow-on VC investment, 47 startups included at least one CVC in their investor list, and the remaining 305 startups were solely funded by IVCs.

## Independent Variable

The key variable of interest is crowdfunding success, which equals 1 if the startup completed a crowdfunding campaign or 0 if the startup received angel financing.

## Moderator and Control Variables

We constructed an indicator variable to test our hypothesis on the heterogeneous effect of startup type: startup cluster city, denoting whether the startup was located in a startup cluster city—i.e., New York, San Francisco, or Boston (Tian, 2011).

In addition, following prior research, we included a set of control variables related to startup and founder characteristics expected to affect VC funding decisions. First, startup age is important because VCs are likely to invest in young firms that are developing and gaining traction (Zider, 1998). We controlled for startup age at the time of initial funding for both crowdfunded and angel-invested startups. For startup product types, if a startup was related to hardware products, hardware startup equals 1; otherwise, it equals 0. We also included dummy variables for the year in which the startup received its initial funding. As startups founded by single individuals or small teams lack resources and diverse skills (Ruef et al., 2003), we included a variable number of founders for the number of initial founding members. For startup experience, we constructed a count variable indicating the total number of startups the founders founded before the focal startup under analysis (Hsu, 2007). We controlled for founder education level, which may signal capability (Hall & Hofer, 1993), with an indicator variable equal to 1 if any primary founders held postgraduate degrees (e.g., M.S., M.B.A., Ph.D.) and 0 otherwise. The literature suggests that traditional VC investors tend to pay less attention to female entrepreneurs, whereas crowdfunding is relatively more favorable for women (Greenberg & Mollick, 2017). Thus, we also included an indicator variable (female founders), which equals 1 if the focal startup included at least one female founder and 0 otherwise.

VC has been shown to be more accessible to startups in certain industries and regions (Cumming & Dai, 2010; Dern, 2015; Tian, 2011). Accordingly, we added dummy variables for industry class and region to control for unobservable timeinvariant differences across industries and regions. We classified industries according to Crunchbase tags, parsed into six areas:

(1) music, gaming, and media; (2) social media, location, and mobile apps; (3) payment and commerce; (4) web business; (5) underlying technology; and (6) other. We also included dummy variables for startups’ geographic regions based on the classification of the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA):<sup>8</sup> New England, Mideast, Great Lakes, Plains, Southeast, Southwest, Rocky Mountain, and Far West. Table 1 summarizes the operational definitions of all variables. Table 2 provides the descriptive statistics and the correlation matrix.

Panel A of Table 3 presents the descriptive statistics of all variables, comparing crowdfunded and angel-financed startups. Our dependent variable, VC investment, differs significantly between the two groups, indicating that angel-financed startups are, on average, more likely than crowdfunded startups to receive subsequent VC funding, and VC investors tend to prefer investing in angel-financed startups. However, we do not know whether these results would hold after controlling for observable and unobservable startup characteristics. In addition, our results suggest that angel financing tends to prefer non-hardware startups from startup cluster areas that have a greater number of founders. However, no statistical differences were observed regarding female founders, startup experience, or education levels of founders. The significant differences in some variables suggest systematic differences between the two groups.

Furthermore, the selection criteria of angel investors seems more aligned with that of VCs because both angel and VC investors, compared with crowdfunders, prefer non-hardware firms and startups from startup cluster cities (Hellmann & Thiele, 2015). Thus, our analysis needs to account for a potential bias when estimating the causal effects of crowdfunding relative to angel financing on subsequent VC funding.

## Empirical Approach

This study examined the effect of initial funding sources on subsequent VC funding. As discussed above, startups’ initial choice between crowdfunding and angel investing must be accounted for. If not, confounding factors that affect both the choice of the initial funding approach and subsequent VC funding can bias our results. To address this issue, we employed two approaches. First, we leveraged the PSM method to create a matched subsample of startups, where the two groups of startups are more similar in observable characteristics (Dehejia & Wahba, 2002; Engel & Keilbach, 2007). However, even after matching, the two groups may differ in terms of unobservable characteristics. For example, although the social network of founders might affect startups’ choice of the initial funding approach and subsequent VC funding, we did not observe or control for it. To account for unobservables, we employed a bivariate probit modeling approach because both our dependent variable (VC investment) and the key independent variable (crowdfunding success) are binary. Combining PSM with the bivariate probit model is suitable because it allowed us to address selection issues for both observables and unobservables.

## Propensity Score Matching

We used PSM to control for possible differences in observable characteristics between crowdfunded and angel-financed startups. As the values of some covariates are substantially different across the two groups, the analysis results are sensitive to the specification and outliers from each group (Imbens, 2015). By constructing a matched sample of control and treatment groups with similar observed characteristics, PSM helps mitigate the estimation challenges arising from the differences in observable characteristics between the two groups. We set crowdfunded startups as the treatment group and created a matched sample of angel-financed startups to form a control group using propensity scores estimated using a probit model.

Several issues must be resolved when implementing PSM, including the matching method to be applied and the number of control units to match each treatment unit (Dehejia & Wahba, 2002). Matching can be conducted with or without the replacement of the control units. By matching without replacement, when there are few control units similar to the treated units, we may be forced to match treated units to control units that are considerably different in terms of the estimated propensity score, which increases bias. Another complication of matching without replacement is that the results are potentially sensitive to the order in which the treatment units are matched (Caliendo & Kopeinig, 2008). Conversely, matching with replacement minimizes the propensity score distance between the matched control and treatment units, and each treatment unit can be matched to the nearest control unit, even if it implies that a control unit is matched more than once. This is beneficial for reducing bias. Moreover, using more control units generally increases the precision of the estimates. A popular matching method for selecting a set of control units with replacement is the nearest-neighbor method, which selects a preset number of comparison units whose propensity scores are closest to each treated unit in question. Another method is caliper matching, which uses as many comparison units as available within a prespecified radius, allowing for extra data points. However, this may increase the estimation bias.

In our main analysis, we employed k-nearest neighbor matching, which determines a predefined number (k) of the most similar observations in the control group for each treatment observation. We set k = 3, given the relative sizes of the treatment (n = 283) and control (n = 708) groups, resulting in 505 matched samples.<sup>9</sup> Panel B of Table 3 compares the treatment and matched control samples. After matching, the two samples became more balanced, even though the product type (i.e., hardware) still exhibited a statistically significant difference. Thus, we conducted a bivariate analysis with covariates to eliminate any bias that may have remained even after matching.

The estimation results from a univariate probit model, as in Equation (1), could be potentially biased if unobserved characteristics determine both the choice of initial funding source and follow-on financing performance. The potential problem was addressed using a bivariate probit model:

O U T C O M E _ {i} ^ {*} = \delta_ {0} + \delta_ {1} X _ {i} + \delta_ {2} C _ {i} + e _ {i}\tag{5}
$$

$$
C _ {i} ^ {*} = \gamma_ {0} + \gamma_ {1} X _ {i} + \gamma_ {2} Z _ {i} + \mu_ {i}\tag{6}
$$

Equations (5) and (6) represent bivariate probit models that estimate the relationship between the initial funding source and the follow-on financing performance. The error terms $e _ { i }$ and $\mu _ { i }$ is jointly distributed as bivariate normal with means zero, variances one, and correlation $\rho .$ If $\rho$ is significantly different from zero, the two probit models should be estimated simultaneously. If $\rho$ is not significantly different from zero, the two models can be estimated separately. The other variables are defined in Equations (1) and (3). $Z _ { i }$ is a vector used to identify the restrictions.

The first probit model should exploit at least one exogenous variable that is not a part of the second-stage outcome model. To construct one such variable, we identified extremely successful Kickstarter campaigns, known as blockbusters, blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns, which tend to generate a dramatic increase in the number of crowdfunding campaigns on platforms such as Kickstarter (Geva et al., 2019; Wang et al., in press). Such an exogenous shock within a region may affect the selection of angel funding or crowdfunding.<sup>10</sup> However, it should not influence the likelihood that a VC will subsequently fund the startup because a single attractive campaign rather than the region’s entrepreneurial culture and foundation drives large successes in crowdfunding campaigns. To ensure greater confidence that the variable is likely to be exogenous, we excluded the crowdfunding campaign categories of focus in our study: technology, games, and design. We believe that the number of blockbuster projects from non-technology categories, such as art and dance, in a state is unlikely to reflect the VC market in the state because VCs tend to focus on technology projects. The emergence of a blockbuster is somewhat random and does not reflect the entrepreneurial culture of a region, because a project can become a blockbuster for idiosyncratic reasons. Blockbusters are defined as successful crowdfunding campaigns with the top 1% in pledges among the entire population of campaigns. We identified 440 blockbuster campaigns in categories outside of technology, games, and design on Kickstarter, accounting for 1% of over 44,000 successful campaigns. The variable was calculated as the cumulative number of blockbuster campaigns (log-transformed due to skewness) from the same state as the focal startup before it received crowdfunding or angel financing. For example, suppose a startup in Texas obtained crowdfunding in January 2014. In that case, blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns is the log of the total number of blockbuster campaigns initiated in categories not including technology, games, and design on Kickstarter in Texas up to December 2013.

Combining PSM with the bivariate probit model provides the most robust results because it helps control for self-selection issues on observables and unobservables. Our main model is a combined model.

## Alternative Exclusion Restrictions

To further address the self-selection issue, we constructed alternative exclusion restrictions in the selection equation of the bivariate probit model. First, we used fixed time windows (i.e., three- and six-month windows) to calculate the cumulative number of blockbusters within a state in order to construct blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns. This allowed for more precise capture of the salient effect of blockbuster campaigns on startups’ selection of crowdfunding over angel financing. We also constructed an alternative exclusion restriction by using an absolute performance threshold for defining blockbusters: whether the total funding amount raised was over US\$200,000 versus whether it was within the top 1% of projects, a relative performance threshold. This resulted in 91 blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns, i.e., 0.2% of the total non-technology campaigns, during our study period. Finally, we also employed the US\$50,000 criterion to identify our focal sample of crowdfunded startups to examine how the cumulative number of other crowdfunding campaigns that raised over US\$50,000 might influence the selection of initial funding sources for the focal startup. The rationale behind this measure is that the volume of sizable crowdfunding campaigns within close proximity (i.e., within a state) could represent the popularity and salience of crowdfunding, thereby potentially inducing nearby founders to opt for crowdfunding. The results obtained using these alternative exclusion restrictions are qualitatively similar to our main findings (see Table A3). In the selection equations, all alternative exclusive restrictions were significantly associated with crowdfunding selection compared with angel financing.

Arguably, the exclusion restrictions may not be valid and may correlate with the dependent variable VC investment. Thus, we conducted several regressions of VC investment on blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns to examine whether the number of blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns across states is systematically related to VC investment in focal startups. Table D1 shows that, both with and without control variables, blockbuster crowdfunding campaigns at the state level is not significantly associated with VC investment, thus providing additional support for the validity of the exclusion restriction.

## The Control Function Approach

The control function approach (Wooldridge, 2015), often employed with cross-sectional data (Petrin & Train, 2010; Wooldridge, 2010, 2015), can be used as an alternative to address the self-selection issue. For a two-step control function approach, we used the residuals from the first-stage equation in the second-stage model to control startups’ selection decisions. Because our outcome variable is binary, a probit regression model was used. Furthermore, as the secondstage regression involves a nonlinear transformation (i.e., dichotomous dependent variable), the coefficients are not intuitively interpretable. Thus, we report marginal effects, specifically average partial effects, for an easier interpretation of the results (Bartus, 2005). The results of the control function approach are qualitatively similar to those of our main results (Table D2).

## Discussion and Conclusion

Using a signaling theory perspective, we examined whether and how sources of early financing (i.e., crowdfunding vs. angel financing) affect subsequent VC financing. After correcting for potential endogeneity, we found that crowdfunded startups are less likely to receive follow-on VC investments than angel-backed startups. This suggests that startups that choose crowdfunding over angel investing are at a disadvantage when securing subsequent VC funding.

Our investigation of the different types of startups on the grounds of VCs’ preference or avoidance of specific characteristics of startups showed that startups located outside the startup clusters continue to struggle to overcome the disadvantage, even after successful crowdfunding. We also found that CVCs prefer crowdfunded startups because of the strategic benefits associated with crowdfunding success. Overall, the results suggest that crowdfunding plays a nuanced role in interacting with traditional funders and influencing the startup financing industry. Rapidly growing startups require multiple rounds of funding from different sources, either through traditional channels or novel alternative channels, such as crowdfunding. Therefore, the findings show that crowdfunding may potentially help enlarge startups’ opportunities to access financing in the long term.

## Theoretical Implications

Our study makes several significant contributions to the IS and entrepreneurship literature. First, it contributes to the burgeoning literature on crowdfunding by examining its broader impact on post-campaign outcomes outside of crowdfunding platforms. Although many practitioners claim that crowdfunding allows for early access to innovative products, better engagement with potential customers, and a lower cost of capital, scant academic research has conducted rigorous empirical analyses of its broader implications. To date, crowdfunding literature has focused mostly on crowdfunding behaviors and the determinants of crowdfunding success (Burtch et al., 2013; Colombo et al., 2015) but has yet to investigate possible outcomes after crowdfunding campaigns. Recent studies have examined the effect of crowdfunding on subsequent VC funding at different levels (Colombo & Shafi, 2021; Kaminski et al., 2019; Roma et al., 2018; Sorenson et al., 2016; Thies et al., 2019). We add to this stream of literature by examining the relative effects of crowdfunding in conjunction with traditional players in the startup financing industry, such as angels and VCs. While most previous studies have identified significantly positive effects of crowdfunding success on follow-on VC investments (e.g., Sorenson et al. 2016; Roma et al. 2018), our findings suggest that the effects may be relatively smaller than those from angel financing. Moreover, we emphasize that the effect of crowdfunding success on follow-on VC funding can vary depending on the startup’s characteristics. In the current study, we focused on one particular characteristic: the location of startups. The relative disadvantage of crowdfunding compared to angel investing is less salient for startups located in startup hubs, indicating that the broader external impacts of crowdfunding on democratizing access to capital may still be constrained in the startup financing industry. By examining the heterogeneous effects of crowdfunding within the broader context of startup financing, our study complements the extant literature by showing that crowdfunding can serve as a stepping stone for startups to access subsequent funding.

Second, our study adds new knowledge to the literature by applying signaling theory to crowdfunding and startup financing research (Bapna, 2019; Thies et al., 2020). We leverage the logic of signaling theory to offer theoretical arguments and empirical evidence of the relative effectiveness of crowdfunding vis-à-vis angel financing in driving followon VC investments. In our setting, startups are signalers, and their successful funding from angels or crowdfunding constitutes essential signals to VCs, the receivers. VCs respond to signals according to their investment purpose. Our theorizing confirms that quality signals are essential in the startup finance industry, given the inherent information asymmetry, and that success in crowdfunding and angel financing produces distinctive quality signals. Specifically, our study highlights the different responses of CVCs to crowdfunding based on signal receivers’ interpretation processes (Perkins & Hendry, 2005), highlighting the importance of the investment purposes of different types of VCs. We empirically support this contention by showing CVCs’ preferences for crowdfunded startups, thereby contributing to entrepreneurial finance literature (Kim et al., 2016; Ma, 2019; Macmillan et al., 2008). Moreover, little academic attention has been paid to aspects that may be more directly related to the interpretation of signals by prospective investors in the context of startup financing (Colombo, 2021). Our theoretical development and empirical examination of receiver attention, with an emphasis on the distinctiveness of CVCs, help improve our understanding of how signals are processed by different receiver characteristics and their information needs, further developing nuanced insights for signaling theory.

Finally, the findings also add knowledge to the broader literature on how IT-enabled platforms are transforming industries and organizations in existing industries (Burtch et al., 2018; Dedrick & Kraemer, 2005; Lyytinen & Rose, 2003; Zervas et al., 2017). The literature shows that IT-enabled platforms drive organizational transformation in response to disruptive change (Chan et al., 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). Our study adds to the literature on the effects of IT-enabled crowdfunding platforms in the startup financing industry. Previous studies have highlighted the substitution of ITenabled platforms by traditional incumbents in different industries, such as accommodations and transportation. The literature focuses on IT-enabled platforms as direct competitors or substitutes for traditional players and how platforms disrupt traditional incumbents. Our study proposes the potential dynamic role of online crowdfunding platforms in complementing existing players (i.e., angel investors) and influencing downstream players (i.e., VCs) in the startup financing industry. We examine the effects of crowdfunding, in conjunction with angel financing, on the startup finance industry and show how crowdfunding affects the subsequent funding of different types of startups and from different types of VCs. The results show how crowdfunding situates itself among other incumbent players in the startup finance sector and possibly influences the startup finance ecosystem by opening up a new financing channel for startups that helps certify the quality information of startups.

## Managerial Implications

Our study has several practical implications for entrepreneurs and the entrepreneurial financing sector. Notably, we suggest that entrepreneurs should carefully consider the potential outcomes of different initial funding sources. On the one hand, crowdfunding allows entrepreneurs to observe potential market demand and attract early adopters, which is crucial for the startup’s commercial and technical success. Thus, crowdfunding success can serve as a quality signal to later-stage investors, especially CVCs. On the other hand, angel financing may provide professional management consultations and complementary resources. Angel investors help startups connect with the investor community, including IVCs, and support them by leveraging established networks in the entrepreneurial sector. Entrepreneurs should weigh the advantages and disadvantages of these possibilities when selecting early-stage funding sources. For example, if it is critical for a startup to examine the market fit with its product in addition to early-stage financing, crowdfunding could be a better choice. If a startup requires a series of equity financing rounds for rapid growth, specifically from IVCs, the pursuit of angel investing could be a more appropriate choice. As our results indicate, significant differences persist in terms of startups favored by crowdfunding and angel investors, which may limit opportunities and possibilities for entrepreneurs. As such, entrepreneurs must consider the differential opportunities and possibilities of being crowdfunded or angel financed.

Similarly, our study observes that VCs’ preferences for certain types of startups also persist for crowdfunded startups. We confirm that VCs generally favor startups located in startup clusters (Chen et al., 2010). Thus, startups unable to possess such characteristics will have difficulty securing VC funding. Therefore, they might turn to crowdfunding, where they might successfully acquire early-stage funding because crowdfunders typically use different selection criteria. For example, our findings suggest that VC firms are still reluctant to invest in startups located outside startup hubs.

Overall, our findings suggest that the inherent market inefficiency in entrepreneurial finance persists, although crowdfunding has transformed the ecosystem, especially for early-stage funding. Thus, policymakers should consider suitable intervention strategies and incentives to make VCs, especially IVCs, more interested in crowdfunded startups. Although CVCs are favorable to crowdfunded startups, they still account for only a small share of the total VC funding, and their investments are more volatile (Alvarez-Garrido & Dushnitsky, 2016). One possible strategy for improving the chances of crowdfunded startups tapping subsequent VC funding is to encourage expert investors to participate in crowdfunding (Agrawal et al., 2015a; Kim & Viswanathan, 2019). For example, third-party endorsements from reliable entities, such as financial institutions, are appreciated in startup ventures, as they help signal the value and trustworthiness of the business (Massa-Saluzzo & Alegre, 2021). Moreover, their superior social networks will then help crowdfunded startups secure subsequent VC funding (Aggarwal et al., 2012; Aggarwal & Singh, 2013), as these will help strengthen crowdfunded startups’ quality signals with respect to those aspects where angel-financed startups have an advantage.

From a signaling theory perspective, our findings also offer practical implications for startups seeking to improve their chances of subsequent funding. For startups to effectively and successfully deliver their messages, it is essential to know the needs and motivations of the target receivers (Colombo, 2021). In the high-noise environment of the startup financing industry, a comprehensive understanding of receivers is critical to effectively convey the right message and ultimately acquire the required resources. Moreover, with the expansion of crowdfunding platforms as both financing and communication channels, the method of signaling to investors in later stages has changed substantially. The question of what and how to communicate in order to attract later-stage investors’ attention has become more difficult. Thus, regardless of whether startups use new or traditional channels, knowing how to communicate their quality is critical for obtaining subsequent funding.

## Future Research

Our results open new avenues for future research. First, based on our findings on two contingencies influencing signaling effectiveness (i.e., startup characteristics and VC types), future research could further advance our understanding of signal effectiveness by investigating different boundary conditions. In addition to startup locations in our study, some important startup or founder characteristics, such as educational background and startup experiences, could be examined in future studies. Moreover, an examination of the interaction between multiple signals from initial funding sources and other quality information (e.g., patents, third-party endorsements) may be a fruitful direction for future research.

Second, future studies could examine whether the receipt of initial funding from both sources generates greater benefits for startups. This study hypothesizes a mutually exclusive relationship between the two initial funding sources, considering the relatively new appearance of crowdfunding in the startup financing industry during our dataset period. Consequently, there are scant cases of startups being funded by both sources. However, recently, some startups have started to strategically seek both sources by pursuing angel investments before turning to crowdfunding, or vice versa.

Third, future research could choose a different methodological approach to address the weaknesses of observational data. Similar to previous studies on startup financing (Aggarwal et al., 2015; Eckhardt et al., 2006; Tian, 2011), we rely on observational data to examine the funding dynamics. We lack a clean research design, despite our attempts to overcome econometric challenges with rigorous empirical tests such as PSM and the bivariate probit model. Nonetheless, our study design prevented us from clearly identifying the effects of early funding sources and possible mechanisms.

Finally, the research design and sampling procedure could be improved. We analyzed data from a single platform, Kickstarter. Although it is one of the largest reward-based crowdfunding platforms globally, it does not cover all crowdfunding campaigns, raising external validity concerns. Future research could study how the direction and magnitude of the effects differ according to crowdfunding platform type. Future research could examine other types of crowdfunding models, particularly equity-based crowdfunding. We believe that investors in equity-based crowdfunding are more similar to angel investors than those in reward-based crowdfunding because there is a larger proportion of expert investors in equitybased crowdfunding and the primary motivation of these investors is financial. This implies that success in reward-based crowdfunding will inevitably send a more distinct signal from angel investing than success in equity-based crowdfunding. Thus, we suspect that if we were to examine equity-based crowdfunding versus angel investing from a signaling perspective, we might not find a significant difference in their effectiveness in sending a quality signal to potential VCs. Nevertheless, equity-based crowdfunding may differ from angel investing in several other respects as well. Therefore, future research could examine how equity-based crowdfunding, relative to angel financing and reward-based crowdfunding, affects subsequent funding. Furthermore, it would be fruitful to examine the role of other emerging sources of early-stage funding in the startup financing industry such as accelerators and incubators (Cohen & Hochberg, 2014).
