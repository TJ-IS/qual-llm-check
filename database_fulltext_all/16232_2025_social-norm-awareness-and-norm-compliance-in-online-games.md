# SOCIAL NORM AWARENESS AND NORM COMPLIANCE IN ONLINE GAMES<sup>1</sup>

Tzu-Ling Huang Department of Information Management, National Central University Taoyuan City, TAIWAN {tlhuang@cc.ncu.edu.tw}

Gen-Yih Liao Department of Information Management, Chang Gung University, and Division of Pediatric Critical Care Medicine, Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou, TAIWAN {gyliao@mail.cgu.edu.tw}

Alexander S. Dennis Ivy College of Business, Iowa State University Ames, IA, U.S.A. {asdennis@iastate.edu}

Alan R. Dennis Kelley School of Business, Indiana University Bloomington, IN, USA {ardennis@iu.edu}

Ching-I Teng Graduate Institute of Management, Chang Gung University, and Department of Nursing, Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou, TAIWAN {chingit@mail.cgu.edu.tw}

Online games are one of the most popular information systems in the world, with over 2 billion users worldwide. Most users follow the social norms and standards of behavior in online games, but some violate social norms, negatively affecting others’ gameplay experiences and weakening loyalty to the game. The social norms in games and other online environments often differ from the social norms in the real world, so are users who violate norms doing so deliberately or are they unaware of the norms? Our results suggest that many users violate social norms because they do not understand them—about a third of the 1,093 participants in our study reported being unsure of the social norms in an online game they played regularly. Greater experience with a game did not significantly increase social norm awareness. Instead, perceptions of social presence, and learning routines (i.e., community learning and instructional support) influenced social norm awareness, which in turn influenced norm compliance. Thus, a technology’s social affordances, social presence, and learning routines are more important than experience using a technology for helping users become aware of and comply with social norms.

Keywords: Social norm, norm awareness, norm compliance, norm deviation, technology-as-routinecapability, online game, learning, social presence

## Introduction

At the dawn of the internet age, John Chambers, CEO of Cisco Systems, observed that “the internet will change the way we work, live, learn, and play” (Kerravala, 2015). This prediction has come true: Most companies have employees working remotely over the internet, and online shopping, online education, and online games are ubiquitous. It may be argued that the internet has had a greater effect on how we live and play than on how we work and learn because more people play online games (2 billion), shop online (2 billion), and use social media (2 billion) than work online (1 billion) or learn online (half a billion) (Guru99, 2023; Statista, 2021). Online games, ecommerce, and social media are the most widely used information systems in the world, making them an important area for research. Online games have shown their business value to organizations and offer the possibility of large revenue streams to developers (Fang et al., 2019; Mäntymäki & Salo, 2015). Like other information systems, online games need regular, active users, so developers strive to increase user loyalty to increase revenue (Hamari et al., 2020). Much research has examined how to better design online games to increase loyalty and revenue (Guo et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2013; Meng et al., 2021).

Research shows that social norms—commonly held beliefs about what constitutes appropriate behavior (Durkheim, 1898/1982; Lapinski & Rimal, 2005)—have an important role in online games and other online environments (Ivaturi & Chua, 2019; Liao et al., 2020b; Sharma et al., 2021a). Users play games to unwind, socialize, and have fun (Lee et al., 2021), and deviations from social norms (e.g., swearing, ignoring teammates, and cheating—Sharma et al., 2021b; Tan & Chen, 2022) can have major impacts on enjoyment. If users do not have fun playing a game, they can easily stop using it (unlike other technologies whose use is mandated by employers). Norm-deviating behaviors often trigger revenge or imitation using the same norm-deviating behaviors, amplifying the negative effects on the targeted users, others around them, and the community as a whole (Shen et al., 2020). Such toxic behaviors reduce game companies’ revenues (Kordyaka et al., 2020), whereas norm compliance increases users’ team commitment, relational cohesion, and loyalty (Huang et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2020a; Sharma et al., 2021a), important outcomes desired by both users and game developers. Social norm compliance in online games is such an important issue that online games have also been widely used as a context to examine the maladaptive use of technology—behaviors that deviate from accepted social norms (e.g., Shen et al., 2020; Turel et al., 2018). Finally, what happens online does not always stay online, as online social norms and behaviors can spread to real-world behaviors (Cary & Chasteen, 2022). Norm-deviating behaviors such as online sexual harassment (Seo et al., 2022) and online bullying (Livingstone et al., 2014) have become major societal problems that are exacerbated by bad behavior in online games. Of course, not all online social norms are benevolent, with exposure to violent and sexist behavior in games resulting in less empathy for female violence victims (Gabbiadini et al., 2016) and sexist attitudes towards women (Fox & Tang 2014). This potential for online social norms to negatively affect real-world behavior makes understanding social norms in games and other online environments important, as knowing this may help developers promote more beneficial social norms.

Social norms in games and other online environments may differ from social norms in the real world and from game to game. Understanding and complying with social norms in online environments can be difficult, as users may come from different countries and bring their own, potentially different, real-world norms to online environments. When individuals from different cultural backgrounds interact—as is common with games and other online environments—conflicts can arise due to differences in social norms (Jassawalla et al., 2004; Sue et al., 2008). Without an awareness of social norms, users may unintentionally violate them, thus irritating other users and creating a persistent problem that harms loyalty. Likewise, norms may differ from game to game (e.g., it may be unacceptable to kill low-level users’ characters in one game but acceptable in another game), so it is important to understand how social norm awareness and compliance may differ across different game types.

Conventional theory from face-to-face interactions argues that it takes time to learn norms; thus, the more experience a person has in a social situation, the more likely they are to understand its social norms (Ashforth et al., 2007; Peetz & Wilson, 2013; Yu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2023). We theorize that the development of social norms is different in the online world than in the real world because social interactions are mediated by technology. These differences may increase the likelihood that users are unaware of social norms, despite having extensive experience. People learn social norms by interacting with others (Ashforth et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2023), yet the design of online environments may not enable users to feel a strong sense of social presence—i.e., the perception that they are interacting with real people (Hess et al., 2009; Schultze & Brooks, 2019; Yoo & Alavi, 2001)—making others seem less important, or even dehumanized and thus less appropriate to learn norms from. Likewise, users often do not have a strong motivation to educate other users who violate online social norms (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003), because they are unlikely to meet them again, and informing them of a violation may spark conflict and risk revenge. This may lead to routines (Swanson, 2019) that do not promote the development of social norms to the same extent as routines in the real world.

Thus, a lack of social norm awareness (SNA) may be an important factor influencing norm compliance in online environments. Without understanding whether a lack of SNA is a major factor contributing to poor compliance (as well as the factors that influence the development of SNA), researchers cannot develop sound theoretical models to explain norm compliance, and managers and developers cannot develop sound strategies to improve compliance because they do not know whether to focus on improving SNA or on penalizing willful norm violations. Hence, we have two research questions:

1. Is SNA an important factor influencing social norm compliance?

2. To what extent do experience, social presence, and social learning routines influence SNA?

We investigated the theoretical mechanisms influencing SNA and the extent to which SNA explains norm compliance using a research model based on socialization theory (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003; Anderson et al., 1999; Ashforth et al., 2007). This theory describes socialization as a community process transforming members so that they learn what is important in the community (Ashforth et al., 2007) and are motivated to comply with social norms (Anderson et al., 1999). Socialization theory posits that socialization “requires the presence of others” (Ashforth et al., 2007, p. 22). In online environments, the presence of others is less assured; it is conceptualized as social presence, i.e., the extent to which users perceive interactions with avatars as being with real persons (Hess et al., 2009; Schultze & Brooks, 2019; Yoo & Alavi, 2001), which is influenced by social interaction affordances, i.e., the extent to which users feel the environment offers them the opportunity to socialize with others (Lee et al., 2021). When affordances become part of social learning routines, they have the potential to influence outcomes (Swanson, 2019) such as SNA. Thus, we theorize that social norm compliance is influenced by the extent to which social learning routines and social presence influence SNA.

Our study found that SNA is the major factor predicting social norm compliance in online games. The development of SNA is driven by a game’s social interaction affordances that influence both the social learning routines (i.e., community learning, and instructional support) and social presence. Our study makes four contributions. First, it indicates that a lack of SNA is key to explaining why many users fail to comply with social norms in online games. Second, it shows that a social interaction affordance drives social presence and two learning routines (community learning and instructional support), which in turn influence SNA, thus offering actionable means to improve SNA. Third, it extends socialization theory to online environments by considering how online socialization is more complex than its real-world counterpart, due to the presence of technology-mediated others. Fourth, it shows that experience playing games is less important to SNA and norm compliance than learning routines and social presence (experience explained only 1% of the variance in SNA).

In sum, this study contributes to IS research by providing a theoretical model of social norm compliance in technologymediated environments using the technology-as-routinecapability perspective, in which social norm compliance is driven by how the affordances, routines, and perceptions of a technology-influenced SNA. We studied users of one popular IS application (i.e., online games) but believe that our findings also have implications for other online environments where social interactions are mediated by technology. From a practical perspective, our study shows that social norm noncompliance is primarily an issue of lack of norm awareness rather than widespread willful disregard for norms. Since experience playing a game has little effect on SNA, the solution lies in improving the social interaction affordances and learning routines.

## Prior Research and Theory

## Technology-as-Routine-Capability

“While technology is most commonly associated with material things, tools, or artifacts, it is also associated as a concept with routines, defined as patterns of action that provide capabilities” (Swanson, 2019, p. 1007) that enable “the accomplishment of some task” (Swanson, 2019, p. 1009). Routine capabilities are “repeated patterns of action” by which technology features are used (Swanson, 2019). “Routines are seen as integral to technology itself” (Swanson, 2019, p. 1008, original emphasis) because if a technology provides a technical feature but the feature is not routinely used, the feature has little value (Swanson, 2019).

Different technologies offer different technical features (Swanson, 2019) that enable and inhibit user actions (Feldman & Orlikowski, 2011). Affordances are the action possibilities that features offer (Leonardi, 2011; Majchrzak et al., 2016). Affordances are created by the technology’s features, but they are distinct from the features that create them (Karahanna et al., 2018; Majchrzak et al., 2016). A technology objectively either has or does not have a specific feature, while affordances are a user’s subjective understanding of the action possibilities that a feature enables (Karahanna et al., 2018; Leonardi, 2013; Majchrzak et al., 2016). Affordances enable users to achieve their goals (Leonardi, 2011); thus, an affordance means that a user sees the action possibilities enabled by the feature and also has a goal of performing those actions.

Different technologies are designed to enable users to satisfy different goals (Leung & Wei, 2000), so the affordances offered by different technologies can be different. Likewise, different users may have different goals, so different users may perceive the same technology to have different affordances (DeSanctis & Poole, 1994; Leonardi, 2011). For example, a technology may have a text messaging feature that enables users to communicate with their team members. For users who recognize the action possibility of team communication enabled by the feature and have a goal to communicate with their team members, the technology has a team communication affordance. But if a user is not aware of the feature, does not know how to use it, or lacks a goal of using it, then for that user, the technology lacks a team communication affordance.

The affordances of technologies are manifested through routines (Swanson, 2019). Routines are the common patterns of action by which a technology is used (Swanson, 2019), so when affordances become commonly used, they become part of the routines for a technology. When an affordance becomes part of a routine, it has the potential to materially influence outcomes because its routine use changes how users accomplish their goals (Swanson, 2019). When an affordance is present but is not part of routine use, it has little impact (Swanson, 2019).

In summary, developers create technology that contains features. Users perceive affordances when they recognize that features enable them to act in ways that satisfy their goals. Routines emerge when users commonly use an affordance to perform an action, and it is through these routine actions that technology influences outcomes. The relationship between technology and routines is symbiotic in nature, with the usage of technology often acquiring certain routines within communities (e.g., expectations about how quickly emails are answered) so that technologies with similar features (e.g., email) offer different affordances (e.g., archival communication versus instant communication) in different communities. Routines emerge from actions by users and are important sources of stability (Feldman & Pentland, 2003; Swanson, 2019). Routines guide the use of the technology and newcomers acquire an understanding of the technology and how to use it through routines they learn from veteran users (Guo et al., 2021).

The technology-as-routine-capability theory extends our current theoretical understanding of technology by arguing that understanding features and affordances is necessary but not sufficient to understand how technology influences outcomes. Technology exerts its impacts through the capabilities offered by technology-enabled routines that are regularly enacted because features and affordances that are little used have little impact (Swanson, 2019).

This technology-as-routine-capability perspective (Swanson, 2019) fits our research context and our focus on social norms. Social norms are beliefs about appropriate behavior (Lapinski & Rimal, 2005). Routines are both the normal way a technology is used and the way most users believe that the technology should be used (Swanson, 2019). In our context, the game software is the technology that offers features that enable users to take actions (i.e., the affordances), and the routines are contextualized as the normal patterns of affordance use that enable users to learn the norms of a given game.

## Social Norms in the Real World and Online Worlds

Social norms are commonly held beliefs about the appropriate behavior of members within a group (Durkheim, 1898/1982; Lapinski & Rimal, 2005). They are the implicit rules that guide behavior and help individuals understand what behaviors are acceptable or unacceptable (Aarts & Dijksterhuis, 2003). Social norms are typically groupspecific and often linked to members’ pride in their group (Peetz & Wilson, 2013), making social norms important.

Social norms influence various behaviors in the real world and online worlds. Norms in technology-mediated online environments are beliefs about the appropriate ways to behave when using the technology. Social norms can influence members’ desire to make contributions (Tsai & Bagozzi, 2014), alter online community governance (Ivaturi & Chua, 2019), change goals (Liu et al., 2019), and affect information security compliance (Merhi & Midha, 2012).

Social norms have strong impacts in online games. Social norms influence how players behave and judge others behaviors (Dengah et al., 2018). Norm compliance fuels players’ team commitment, relational cohesion, and loyalty to the game (Huang et al., 2022; Liao et al., 2020a; Sharma et al., 2021a). Norms can also lead to game addiction (Gong et al., 2019). Norm deviation can take place in the forms of swearing, ignoring, and sabotaging teammates that hinder victory (Tan & Chen, 2022) and can diminish the fun of everyone in the team. Such deviations can bring both informal and formal sanctions from other users (Bylieva & Nam, 2019).

Online games are social environments that are often different from the real world and may thus have different social norms. Users come from many countries and cultures so they may enter an online community with fewer shared social norms than members in offline communities who live in the same geographic area. Individuals usually learn real-world norms in the groups, organizations, and communities in which they live and work because other individuals with whom they interact have a strong motivation to help them understand the norms (Leonardi et al., 2009). However, online users spend less time together, making it harder for them to learn the norms and creating less motivation for others to help them learn (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003). Thus, even those who spend a lot of time playing online games may not learn the social norms.

As in many other contexts, games and gaming communities have their own norms, which may initially be opaque to novice users (Macabasco, 2018; Wier, 2021). For example, a novice user may assume that they are expected to demonstrate high personal performance, but the norm, in fact, may be to faithfully assume their role (Macabasco, 2018). In this case, their active or overly self-directed actions deviate from the norm and thus upset those they play with. In other words, if users do not understand the social norms, they will be unable to shape their actions to meet others expectations and comply with the social norms.

Past research has examined social norm compliance in online games and found it to be an important antecedent of user loyalty (e.g., Huang et al., 2022). Social norm compliance in games also can increase team commitment (Sharma et al., 2021a), relational cohesion, interdependence, network convergence (Liao et al., 2020a), guild membership (Hsiao & Chiou, 2012), and gift giving (Sharma et al., 2021a). These findings indicate the strong influences of social norm compliance in online games and justify the importance of research exploring what influences it.

We theorize that whether or not users are aware of social norms will strongly affect their compliance with those norms, showing the theoretical importance of understanding SNA and its role in compliance. We are aware of only one study that has examined SNA in online games but this study did not examine the effects of SNA on norm compliance. Goh and Wasko (2012) studied 61 members of a gaming guild (a formal organization within a massively multiplayer online role-playing game) whose members often played together, actively displayed their guild membership, and strongly identified with the guild. They found that users with a higherquality relationship with the guild leader were more likely to be aware of the guild’s norms. Similarly, Hsiao and Chiou (2012) also used a concept called “perceived social norm,” but its measures were focused on norm compliance, including “actions … influenced,” “schedule … changed,” and “behave as … expect me” (Hsiao & Chiou 2012, p. 756).

## Socialization and Social Norm Awareness

Socialization theory was developed in organizational contexts in which newcomers encounter social norms and expectations of normal behaviors held by insiders (Levine & Moreland 1994). Newcomers learn these social norms, either by being told how to act or by observing others and copying their behavior (Anderson et al., 1999; Enns et al., 2003). Socialization is a dynamic process that occurs over time in the presence of social influence (Levine & Moreland, 1994). Therefore, experience from watching others and from direct instruction by others is key to behavioral change (Ke et al., 2012; Levine & Moreland, 1994).

Individuals can learn social norms implicitly by watching others’ behavior and learning how others respond (Hawkins et al., 2019). Wang and Ramiller (2009) call this community learning. Users observe others and learn what behaviors are appropriate and inappropriate. Feedback from observation can lead to further changes as users “replicate and normalize” behaviors so they become routine (Swanson, 2019, p. 1019). Routines are a part of the technology and what the technology enables, as different communities can use it in different ways, leading to different perceptions of the same technology and different outcomes from its use (Swanson, 2019).

Users can also learn social norms explicitly from instruction by others (Ke et al., 2012; Levine & Moreland, 1994). Tan et al. (2021) call this instructional support. Experts provide explicit instruction, indicating the importance of those who spread routines (Swanson, 2019). Such experts (or “veterans”: Anderson et al., 1999) explicitly introduce, envision, promote, and coordinate IT use so that it becomes routine (Pollock & Williams, 2009). Veteran users know the norms and spread them by explicitly teaching new users (Guo et al., 2019).

Both community learning and instructional support are conceptualized as learning routines, as they help users identify the appropriate behaviors. In online games, users can use functions enabled by the game software to learn and to guide others as part of the process of socializing new users (Lee et al., 2021). Users can observe others, and veteran users can offer instructional support to teach new users what they should or should not do during gameplay, with the intent of increasing SNA and compliance with norms. During these socialization processes, users can improve new users’ awareness and understanding of norms and simultaneously increase the stability of the norms and the likelihood of norm compliance.

Social norms emerge from interactions with others (Peetz & Wilson, 2013). This means that social norms are developed and communicated among community members who interact over time; thus, the frequency and duration of interactions with other people influence norm development (Ashforth et al., 2007). In the real world, people interact directly. When socialization occurs in a physically co-located space, newcomers directly learn the norms of their new environment by watching others or being instructed (Ke et al., 2012; Stohl, 1986). Interactions with others are an essential part of the socialization process (Ashforth et al., 2007).

Socialization has been widely studied in various IS contexts. Socialization in online virtual teams is not as straightforward as in face-to-face teams, as the technology and the lack of a physical environment can get in the way of sharing important social information (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003; Morrison, 1993). In the online world, interaction is mediated by technology; therefore, how technologies enable users to interact is central to our theorizing. Not all online environments provide users with the tools necessary to ensure seamless communication. Indeed, the existence and usefulness of such tools, i.e., social interaction affordances, can vary considerably between online environments (Crenshaw & Nardi, 2016). If social affordances are lacking, individuals may have difficulty communicating with others enough to gain awareness of the norms.

That said, well-designed technology can be a boon to socialization. For example, socialization on social networking sites has been shown to help build social capital among corporate users (Dhar & Bose, 2023) and help users engage in social exchange and receive social support (Song et al., 2019). Moreover, socialization theory can explain why the use of social interaction features helps users achieve their goals (James et al., 2019) and motivates gameplay (Merhi, 2016). These applications show that socialization can explain behavioral changes among technology users across contexts.

In online environments, one of the key factors is whether users perceive others to be present when interacting with them. The ability to perceive the presence of others cannot be assumed because this social presence is mediated by the technology (Yoo & Alavi, 2001). The extent to which users perceive interactions with avatars as interactions with real persons—social presence—can vary between different tools and also among different users of the same tool (Hess et al., 2009; Schultze & Brooks, 2019; Yoo & Alavi, 2001). If others are not perceived to be present, newcomers may fail to recognize others as the people they are. This is further complicated by the fact that in online games, some game characters are controlled by artificial intelligence (but not other users); thus, seeing avatars may not always be perceived as the presence of real persons (He, 2015). Hence, the extent to which the game enables users to separate nonplayer characters from avatars controlled by other users is important in the socialization process.

## Theory and Hypotheses

We used socialization theory (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003; Anderson et al., 1999; Ashforth et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2023) to identify the theoretical components in our model to predict social norm awareness: experience accumulation, social interaction affordances, perceptions of others (i.e., social presence), and learning routines (community learning, instructional support). First, this theory argues that newcomers to a group engage in observation and interaction with other members to learn social norms (Enns et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2023). Accordingly, the concept of learning assumes a pivotal role (Zhang et al., 2023), both implicit (community learning) and explicit (instructional support). Second, technology affordances play a central role because the technology mediates interactions among users and enables users to perceive the social presence of other users; technology-mediated interaction is central to facilitating learning in the socialization process (Leidner et al., 2018; Oshri et al., 2007). Social affordances influence the development of routines because affordances are manifested through routines (Swanson, 2019). Third, since these interactions occur over time and users accumulate the experience needed to understand social norms (Yu et al., 2016), the total hours spent playing the game (i.e., gameplay experience) may influence the development of SNA.

Figure 1 illustrates our research model and Table 1 lists the conceptual definitions of the constructs. This model includes the focal construct (SNA) and its associated hypotheses. Our central argument is that a major reason why social norm compliance is a problem in online environments is a lack of SNA. The global nature of online environments such as games draws together users from many different cultures who may not share common social norms and the technology-mediated environment in which they interact influences how social norms develop. Our study contributes to socialization theory (Anderson et al., 1999; Levine & Moreland, 1994) by adding SNA as an explicit factor to be theorized about (and measured) in understanding norm compliance and by including the technology components of routines, social interaction affordances, and social presence. The primary factors influencing SNA are actionable to system developers, as they include technology affordances and technology-as-routine capabilities.

As an example, imagine players of World of Warcraft, a popular game in which players work together in teams or guilds to go on quests and overcome challenges. As players are often not co-located, the technical features include text chat and voice communication, providing players with the opportunity to communicate. When players recognize that these features enable an action possibility of social interaction and have a desire for social interaction, an affordance is created. For example, social interactions are common in World of Warcraft, as players seek tips on how to beat enemies, coordinate strategies to succeed in raids, or get together to chat or have other random acts of fun (Nardi & Harris, 2006). Players can also use the social interaction affordance to observe others’ interactions and directly share norms of behavior with other players (e.g., “If you are playing a healer, you shouldn’t be trying to do damage to the enemy”). The more players that consistently observe others in order to learn or that consistently directly communicate the norms of the game, the greater the community learning routines and instructional support routines, respectively. In each case, it is the presence of features that enable affordances that allow players to develop routines of behavior where they observe others (community learning) or directly hear from others (instructional support) that influence SNA within a game.

It is worth noting that features and affordances are not always consistent between games. Some games may not provide features that enable social interaction and in games that do provide such features, players may not have a goal of social interaction. Both would have consequences on the development of routines to learn norms from other players either implicitly or explicitly.

## Antecedents of Social Norm Awareness and Compliance

Socialization theory posits that socialization occurs over time by experiencing events (Ashforth et al., 2007; Hawkins et al., 2019). It takes time to learn norms (Ashforth et al., 2007; Kuem et al., 2020; Peetz & Wilson, 2013), so the more experience a person has, the more opportunities they will have to experience different events and the more likely they will be to understand social norms (Ashforth et al., 2007). Experience is a function of both the frequency of playing a game (e.g., hours per week) and the duration of playing the game (the years spent playing the game) (Liao et al., 2016). Empirical research shows that social norms often stem from a history of interactions (Peetz & Wilson, 2013), and more experience in a group instills confidence in knowing what to say and how to say it (Kuem et al., 2020) because users know others’ views and expectations. Greater experience also enhances a user’s understanding of “how their expertise is relevant” (Wasko & Faraj, 2005, p. 42). Thus, users who have more experience will be more likely to know what other users expect, i.e., SNA. Hence, we hypothesize:

H1: Experience is positively related to social norm awareness.

Socialization theory posits that socialization occurs through both implicit and explicit learning (Ashforth et al., 2007; Hawkins et al., 2019). Explicit learning refers to receiving instruction from others, while implicit learning refers to learning by observing what others do (Hawkins et al., 2019).

We theorize that the extent to which the routines around technology use encourage implicit and explicit learning will influence SNA.

Community learning is the implicit learning that takes place when users experience or observe what other users usually say and do (Wang & Ramiller, 2009). How users respond to the behaviors of others shows the social norms in the game (Sharma et al., 2021a). Community learning occurs when users “reflect upon their learning and contribute their experiences, observations, and insights to the community’s ongoing discourse” (Wang & Ramiller, 2009, p. 709). Community members can provide knowledge to others (Kuem et al., 2020) by responding to how other users act to show whether a behavior is appropriate or not. Hence, users learn from their experiences interacting with other users (Pham et al., 2023).

Community learning is most effective when it is part of the routines around the use of the game technology. Since gameplay has a strong hedonic nature (i.e., users primarily seek enjoyment when playing games, Li et al., 2015), users may be unlikely to take time away from playing to respond to others’ behaviors, especially inappropriate behaviors. Moreover, users are not forced to join teams or remain in teams with people they dislike, including those who do not know the norms. Further, the vast nature of many online worlds means that it is often unlikely that individual users will be randomly encountered again. Anonymity also inhibits this socialization process (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003), as users may not know one another very well—or even at all. Thus, for many users, it is easier to simply ignore or avoid norm-breaking users, rather than taking time to educate them about social norms. Therefore, it may be more difficult for users who have limited knowledge of social norms to learn norms in the online world as effectively and efficiently as they learn norms in the real world. Nevertheless, they may still play the game frequently, potentially causing a long-term negative impact by annoying other users and deterring them from playing the game. Thus, community learning may not be as frequent as in the real world, leading to a low SNA. Nonetheless, as outlined by socialization theory, community learning has the potential to increase SNA when it is part of a routine. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H2: Community learning is positively related to social norm awareness.

Social norms can also be learned explicitly (Ashforth et al., 2007). Instructional support refers to the extent to which others give instructions or share experiences (Tan et al., 2021; Zwanch & Cribbs, 2021). Clear instructions and the sharing of experiences about appropriate and inappropriate behavior should be helpful for learning norms (Ashforth et al., 2007). Technology can be designed to facilitate learning (Coenen et al., 2018). Learning helps manage conflict among parties (Ravindran et al., 2015) and changes behavior to attain goals (Liu et al., 2019). Instruction in games may come from veteran users who are guild or team leaders. Giving instructions can teach users about social norms. That is, instructional support explicitly provides learning opportunities to other users and familiarizes them with norms. The extent to which this instructional support is part of the routines for the use of technology will influence SNA. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H3: Instructional support is positively related to social norm awareness.

The presence of others is necessary to trigger the socialization process (Ashforth et al., 2007) because social norms are learned by interacting with others, enabling people to understand what behaviors are appropriate and inappropriate (Hawkins et al., 2019). Implicit in this idea, however, is the presumption that when others are present, individuals will recognize them as being present and thus be willing to learn from them. In online contexts such as games, the awareness of social norms is likely to be impaired if a user does not see other users as humans because then their behavior is not perceived to be a relevant guide to how people should behave (Hawkins et al., 2019) but rather a guide to how game elements operate. In online environments, such perceived social presence cannot be assumed. Instead, recognizing the presence of others as real persons in mediated environments depends on social presence (Hess et al., 2009; Schultze & Brooks, 2019; Yoo & Alavi, 2001). Social presence is linked to more open communication (Kim, 2011) and, in turn, improved mutual understanding (Kim, 2011). Thus, a greater perception of social presence could encourage more open communication and therefore increase mutual understanding, including the understanding of in-game social norms. Furthermore, if social presence is high, socialization could be improved, as individuals would perceive other users as viable exemplars, thereby improving norm awareness. Therefore, we propose that social presence is important to socialization in online games: By encouraging users to understand norms, it enhances their awareness of social norms. Hence, we hypothesize:

## H4a: Social presence is positively related to social norm awareness.

The presence of others is also crucial to encouraging compliance (Ashforth et al., 2007) because social norms gain much of their potency through the watching eyes of others. The recognition that others are observing increases prosocial behavior (Bradley et al., 2018) and decreases antisocial behavior (Dear et al., 2019). Social norms can still sway actions in the absence of observation, but other motivations, such as fear of punishment or desire for peers’ esteem, are substantially weakened when others are not around to observe behavior (Andrighetto et al., 2015). This is why the perception of others’ presence can change an individual’s behavior, either to do something or not to do it (Polman et al., 2018; Schüz et al., 2018). Social presence is an individual’s perception that they are interacting with real people (Yoo & Alavi, 2001). Thus, when social presence is high, users will feel that their actions are being observed by other real people. This means that they will be more likely to take steps to ensure that their behavior is consistent with the social norms of their environment in order to gain esteem or avoid punishment. In contrast, when social presence is low, users will feel less like they are being watched and will thus be less constrained by norms. Hence, we hypothesize:

H4b: Social presence is positively related to social norm compliance.

Social interaction affordance refers to the extent to which a game enables users to communicate with other users in various ways, such as working together on in-game activities, casual chatting, and building relationships (Lee et al., 2021). Social affordances are enabled by features that offer users the possibility of communicating with other users (Lee et al., 2021). While many games offer features that enable users to communicate, not all users recognize and understand these features and not all users have a goal to communicate. This is why we use the term social interaction affordance, which captures the need for users to recognize the possibilities offered by technological features and the goal of using them in a certain way (Majchrzak et al., 2013). Users employ social interaction affordances to interact with other users for both game activities and casual interactions. Social interaction increases the perception of social presence (Schultze & Brooks, 2019). Hence, we anticipate that social interaction affordances are important for developing social presence because the interaction with others offered by social affordances enables users to better understand that there is a real person with distinct preferences, attitudes, and behaviors behind each avatar. Social interaction in the same community can instill trust and enhance enjoyment and playfulness, fueling social presence (Hess et al., 2009). Social interaction with others who have a shared history can lead to group cohesion (Dennis & Garfield, 2003), which can also increase social presence (Yoo & Alavi, 2001). Hence, we hypothesize:

H5a: Social interaction affordance is positively related to social presence.

Affordances influence routines because a user must recognize and use an affordance before it can become part of a routine (Swanson, 2019). Thus, routines begin with the recognition that a technology offers an affordance. Once a regular pattern of using an affordance has been established, it becomes a routine (Swanson, 2019). Social interaction is central to community learning because users watch how others respond to their behavior and the behaviors of others (Hawkins et al., 2019; Swanson, 2019). Likewise, social interaction is central to instruction from others (Ke et al., 2012; Levine & Moreland, 1994). Therefore, the recognition that a technology has a social interaction affordance that enables social interaction will influence the extent to which community learning and instructional support become routines. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5b-c: Social interaction affordance is positively related to (b) community learning and (c) instructional support.

## Norm Awareness and Compliance

An awareness of social norms is necessary before an individual can comply with them (Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Literature using socialization theory indicates that knowing the norms is key to behavior (Ke et al. 2012; Levine & Moreland, 1994). If individuals are unaware of the norms, they may struggle to understand and internalize them (Van Maanen & Schein, 1979) and will be unable to comply with them (Yazdanmehr & Wang, 2016). Clear perceptions of others’ expectations and norms strongly motivate individuals to behave consistently with those expectations and norms (Swann & Ely, 1984).

Thus, SNA is essential for compliance but may need to be bolstered by other factors because individuals may neglect norms, despite considering them to be worthwhile (Lee et al., 2011). This can be analogous to the link between receiving knowledge and the associated performance (Ozer & Vogel, 2015); you cannot use knowledge you do not have, but you are not required to use knowledge you do have. Thus, users cannot choose to comply with social norms they are unaware of but can choose to comply (or not) with norms they know. Since users frequently need to rely on each other to overcome gaming challenges to obtain game achievements, complying with the social norms they are aware of is an important part of gameplay (Huang et al., 2022). Thus, high SNA would be expected to motivate users to comply with social norms, while limited SNA would make it difficult for users to comply with them. Hence, we hypothesize:

H6: Social norm awareness is positively related to social norm compliance.

## Method

Participants: Invitations were posted in online forums and on social networking sites. Participants were asked to nominate a recently played online game and answer questions about it. Those who submitted complete responses could join a lottery that awarded each of 50 winners a NT\$200 gift certificate (approximately US\$6.50), a modest incentive, thus minimizing self-selection bias. We tested and found that the first half and the second half of the participant group did not show any significant difference in any of the study variables (p > 0.09), suggesting little nonresponse bias.

We collected two waves of survey data a week apart to provide temporal separation between the variables in the two theoretical stages. We avoided data collection during major festivals and industry-level promotional activities, thus reducing the potential bias due to these events. In Wave 1, we collected 4,259 complete responses. We excluded responses from participants who offered duplicate responses, cited nonexistent games, or indicated not playing online games. We also removed participants who nominated single-player games, games without avatars, or games nominated by less than 5% of respondents. We further removed 60 responses that nominated games in which avatars were not readily visible. In total, 1,093 participants offered valid responses to both waves, and their responses were used for further analyses. About 71% of respondents were male. Most were between 20 and 35 years of age (73%), while 25% were between 36 to 45 years old, and the rest (2%) were over 45 years old. Most (70%) respondents had attended a college or university, and 25% had a graduate degree.

Table 2 lists the five most frequently nominated games, which accounted for about 60% of our sample. Our participants nominated real-time strategy (RTS) games the most (nominated by 446 participants or 41%), with massively multiplayer online role-playing games (MMORPGs) being the second most common game type (295 participants or 27%). RTS and MMORPG accounted for 68% of the responses. The third most common game type was action-adventure (136 participants or 12%). The two most frequently mentioned games accounted for 96% of the RTS games in our sample, while the top two MMORPGs accounted for 58% of the MMORPGs.

Measurement: Most of the measurement items (see Appendix A) came from prior research. The four items measuring community learning were adapted from Wang and Ramiller (2009) and the four items measuring instructional support were adapted from Tan et al. (2021). We conducted a content validity assessment for community learning and instructional support (see Appendix B). The four items measuring social interaction affordance were adapted from Lee et al. (2021). The five items assessing social norm awareness were adapted from Goh and Wasko (2012) and Hu et al. (2016). The commonly used four-item semantic differential measure of social presence developed by Short et al. (1976) has been criticized because it does not capture their conceptualization of social presence as the degree to which a person is perceived as a “real person” (p. 151) (see Kreijns et al. 2022); therefore, we integrated their work with that of Kreijns et al. (2022) to create four items. The three items assessing social norm compliance were adapted from Liao et al. (2020a). Gameplay experience was measured by the total gameplay hours—i.e., by multiplying responses to “How many years is it since you started playing the nominated game?” (gameplay duration) by the responses to “How many hours per week did you spend playing the nominated game?” (gameplay frequency) by 52 (number of weeks in a year). Experience varied widely, so we applied Blom’s (1958) rank normalization; such data transformations are commonly used with this type of data (Ludwig et al., 2014; Majchrzak & Malhotra, 2016).

All items were presented to the participants in Chinese. They were forward-translated from English into Chinese by three native Chinese-speaking scholars who were also experienced in online game research and writing journal articles in English. The Chinese version was then back translated into English by a government-certified translation service firm. One native English speaker assessed the equivalence of the original English version and the back-translated English version and concluded that the versions had no significant differences. Thus, the Chinese version and the original English version were deemed semantically equivalent.

## Results

We first assessed the extent to which the participants reported being aware, unsure, or unaware of the social norms in the game they nominated (see Figure 2). The mean SNA was 4.01 (median = 4.00, SD = 0.59). About 66.1% of the participants said they were aware of the norms (i.e., had a score of 4.00 or above), 0.3% said they were unaware of the norms (i.e., had a score of 2.00 or below), and 33.6% were unsure of the norms (score between 2.00 and 4.00).

The focus of our analysis is on the research model in Figure 1. We tested the hypotheses using LISREL v.8.80 to implement covariance-based structural equation modeling (CBSEM). Direct effects were tested using path coefficients. The structural model fit the data adequately: CFI = 0.94, IFI = 0.94, NNFI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.069 (Bagozzi, 2010). Figure 3 presents the analytical results. Our model explained 46% of the variance in SNA and 60% of the variance in social norm compliance, which are what Cohen (1992) calls “large” effect sizes. The large effect sizes indicate the practical relevance of this model showing the antecedents and consequences of SNA.

Most hypotheses were supported. SNA was affected by community learning (H2), instructional support (H3), and social presence (H4a) but not experience (H1). Social interaction increased social presence (H5a), community learning (H5b), and instruction support (H5c). SNA increased norm compliance (H6), and norm compliance was increased by social presence (H4b).

## Figure 3. Analytical Results

In summary, the results show that SNA plays a critical role in influencing social norm compliance. Contrary to interactions in the real world, greater experience in technology-mediated online environments did not lead to greater SNA. Instead, SNA was influenced more as theorized by the technology-asroutine-capability perspective: learning routines (community learning and instructional support) had the strongest impact on SNA, along with social presence. The social interaction affordance influenced both learning routines and social presence. Our results show that affordances need to be manifested in routines for them to have the greatest impact.

## Differences Among Game Types

Since different types of games may have different social norms, we tested the model with subsamples of different game types. MMORPG and RTS are two major game types that were also well-represented in our sample. MMORPGs have a large game world enabling a huge number of users to play concurrently in the same world. Each user has an avatar, which can move up gaming levels. MMORPGs usually have features enabling communication among users (Lee et al., 2021), and most MMORPGs encourage teamwork (Jin et al., 2017). Socializing is important in MMORPG, and users often form relationships that can be just as strong as relationships in the real world (Yee, 2006). Because MMORPGs offer more opportunities for users to customize their avatars, the avatars are better representations of the users as unique individuals. In contrast, in RTS games, users play at a real-time pace (Elliott et al., 2012; Jang & Byon, 2020). RTS gameplay features real-time competitions or combats, and most users place an emphasis on beating rivals (Eichenbaum et al., 2015; Jang & Byon, 2020). RTS games usually have communication features (Donaldson, 2017), but the need for real-time immediate responses to changing gaming situations focuses communication on in-game information. As a result, in-game socializing may distract users or waste time, potentially causing them to lose a game. For this reason, RTS enables users to turn on/off certain communication channels (Donaldson, 2017). In short, MMORPGs offer more opportunities for in-game socializing (Yee, 2006) while RTS games have an emphasis on real-time competition (Jang & Byon, 2020).

To better understand the effects of game type, we separated the sample into three subsamples: MMORPG, RTS games, and Others (games that were not MMORPG or RTS) and ran separate analyses on the three subsamples. For the MMORPG subsample (295 participants), the results (see Figure 4a) are the same as those for the entire sample (in Figure 3). For the RTS subsample (446 participants), the results were the same except that social presence no longer affected SNA and compliance (i.e., H4a and H4b were not supported; see Figure 4b). For the Others subsample (352 participants), the results were the same except that social presence did not directly affect SNA (i.e., H4a was not supported; see Figure 4c).

The conclusion from these analyses is that the role of the learning routines is stable and consistent across the three sets of game types. In contrast, the role of social presence depends on the game type: social presence has a significant effect for MMORPG games but no effect on SNA for other game types. We theorize that this reflects the greater importance of social interaction in MMORPG games (Yee, 2006).

## Robustness Checks

Importance of SNA: Our model in Figure 3 explains 60% of the variance in social norm compliance. When we omitted the path from SNA to social norm compliance, this percentage was greatly reduced (to 18%). This shows the importance of SNA for social norm compliance.

Rival hypothesis: A rival hypothesis is that experience, community learning, and instructional support may directly affect norm compliance. We tested this by adding experience, community learning, and instructional support as direct effects on compliance in addition to SNA. The revised model did not explain any additional variance and only experience had a significant direct effect on compliance (path coefficient = 0.06, p < 0.05). All hypothesis testing results remained the same as in Figure 3. Hence, we conclude that our findings about SNA are robust to the inclusion or exclusion of the three direct effects.

Importance of experience: Another rival hypothesis is that experience leads to SNA. In a model with only experience influencing SNA (including controls), the R<sup>2</sup> of SNA dropped from 46% (in Figure 3) to 1% (and SNA continued to explain 60% of the variance in compliance). This shows the importance of technology constructs in technologymediated environments.

Role of affordances: Prior to the emergence of the technology-as-routine-capabilities perspective, theory argued that affordances influenced outcomes, so we tested this by removing routines and social presence. In a model with only the social interaction affordance influencing SNA and compliance (including experience and controls), the R<sup>2</sup> for SNA dropped from 46% to 20% and the R<sup>2</sup> for compliance remained essentially unchanged at 61%. When we omitted social presence only and used the affordance and learning routines to predict SNA and compliance (including experience and controls), the R<sup>2</sup> for SNA remained essentially unchanged at 45% (the R<sup>2</sup> for compliance remained unchanged at 60%). This supports the contingent effects of social presence due to game type (see Figure 4) and indicates the value of the technology-as-routinecapabilities perspective.

Decomposing gameplay experience: Gameplay experience was computed by multiplying the number of years spent by the number of hours spent per week by 52 (i.e., 52 weeks in a year). Hence, gameplay experience could be decomposed into gameplay duration (years spent playing the game) and gameplay frequency (hours per week spent playing the game). We conducted this analysis, and the hypothesis testing results remained the same, except that gameplay duration was negatively related to SNA (see Appendix C).

Analysis of control variables: We selected our control variables to be consistent with the literature (Phang et al., 2014; Yoon et al., 2021). We found that gender was related to SNA and social norm compliance. Being male was related to higher norm awareness, while being female was related to higher norm compliance. As a sensitivity test, we removed all the control variables from the model. All hypothesis testing results remained the same and the changes in path coefficients were negligible, (i.e., 0.02), supporting the robustness of our study findings.

Testing of endogeneity: We used Bollen’s (1996) two-stage least squares (2SLS) method for latent variables to address the potential endogeneity concern. When using the 2SLS method, all the hypothesis testing results remained the same.

## Discussion

Our study focused on social norm awareness (SNA), specifically its effects on norm compliance and the factors that influenced its development in online environments such as games. One third of our participants reported that they were unsure of the norms of the game they had selected for the study—one they had recently played. As a result, our results show that SNA had a large effect on compliance; i.e., most of the variance in compliance was due to SNA. Without our study, researchers and game firms might mistakenly believe that users understand norms and that a lack of norm compliance is due to users willfully choosing to disregard the norms. Our study shows that a major part of the norm compliance problem is due to a lack of norm awareness, not willful disregard of norms. Our study has theoretical value, showing that one important theoretical reason for lower norm compliance in online games is low SNA. Our study also has practical value, offering game developers a way to improve compliance—i.e., by improving SNA.

Our study extends socialization theory into online environments where social norm development is technology mediated. Our findings indicate that, unlike the real world, experience does not significantly influence SNA in online games. That is, users with more experience are not more aware of the norms of the games they play. In our study, experience accounted for only 1% of the variance in SNA. Some experience is necessary, but once a modest amount of experience has been gained, additional experience does not further increase SNA.

Instead, we argue that the technology-as-routine-capability perspective better explains SNA and norm compliance in online environments. The primary factors affecting SNA in the online environment we studied were social presence and the routines for technology use (i.e., community learning and instructional support). Community learning means that users implicitly learn norms from others by observing interaction. Instructional support means that users learn norms when other users explicitly share game norms. Both are “patterns of actions,” as defined by the technology-as-routine-capability perspective (Swanson, 2019), whereby the social interaction affordance is manifested into routines and these routines influence outcomes. These findings have theoretical value in that they show that the technology-as-routine-capability perspective is useful and that these two learning routines are the primary drivers of SNA. Our study also has practical value in that it offers game developers a way to improve compliance—i.e., by encouraging implicit and explicit learning routines.

As argued by the technology-as-routine-capability perspective, affordances are manifested in routines—in other words, affordances influence the development of routines. This means that social interaction affordances play an important role in technology-mediated socialization processes, as both the enabler of routines and as a factor that influences user perceptions (i.e., social presence). Most online environments such as games provide communication features, but merely enabling users’ interaction is insufficient. Users need to understand the features and have a goal of social interaction before communication features become social interaction affordances. Users have different goals in different games; thus, different games—even those with similar communication features—can have different social affordances. Social interaction affordances strongly influence routines and social presence, which in turn influence SNA. This may be particularly relevant in online games, as the norms change over time. For example, the knowledge of the optimal way to play a game will evolve as the game matures, and if players are not up to date on a game’s norms, they may face social sanctions (Donaldson, 2017). Our findings have theoretical value in that they show that social interaction affordances enable routines. Our study also has practical value, showing game developers the importance of social interaction affordances.

## Implications for Theory

Contribution to socialization theory: Socialization research has frequently studied how socialization occurs, e.g., how newcomers learn from veterans (Anderson et al., 1999). Yet such socialization processes may be facilitated or constrained by technological factors, given that contemporary interactions between individuals may be technology-mediated (i.e., online environment) or not (i.e., offline environment). Without understanding how technologies both facilitate and impair socialization processes in online environments, we cannot build robust theories to explain social norm compliance and technology use. Our study contributes to socialization theory (Anderson et al., 1999; Levine & Moreland, 1994) by demonstrating the importance of learning routines and illuminating their effects on SNA. Our findings contribute to socialization theory by showing that socialization in online environments is based on routines rather than general experience.

In the socialization theory literature, interactions with others are central to the socialization process (Ashforth et al., 2007; Rollag, 2004). Because of this, socialization may be hampered in online environments, where interactions are mediated by technology (Ahuja & Galvin, 2003). We consider how design choices, in the form of social interaction affordances, can facilitate or inhibit the development of SNA and social norm compliance. Our research shows that social affordances drive perceptions of social presence and the learning routines around technology use that increase SNA. Social affordances can help facilitate community learning and instructional support, promoting socialization through multiple pathways and further underscoring the importance of design choices that facilitate socialization in an online environment.

Moreover, we show how a lack of knowledge of shared norms can inhibit norm compliance in online environments, which was previously not clearly understood. Because of this, it is necessary to first understand the extent to which users are aware of social norms before considering compliance with the norms. Our findings contribute to socialization theory by showing the importance of theorizing about and explicitly including SNA in models of social norm compliance. Our study also reveals the key role of IT design choices, user goals, and technology-as-routine capabilities in online environments. Together, these theoretical changes improve socialization theory’s fit with online environments such as online games.

Contribution to online game research: This study contributes to online game research by (1) showing that SNA is the key driver of norm compliance and (2) proposing and testing five factors that influence SNA. Past game research has shown that norm compliance increases user loyalty (Teng & Chen, 2014). Our study shows that SNA is key to norm compliance. Our findings also offer game developers an alternative way to build user loyalty and competitive advantage by using socialization to promote SNA and compliance. Past research has shown that positive social interaction strongly fuels psychological engagement, thus leading to in-game spending (Cheung et al., 2015; Fang et al., 2019). Therefore, our study offers game developers other ways to increase positive social interaction, engagement, and loyalty, yielding favorable business outcomes (Cheung et al., 2015; Moon et al., 2013). In a supplemental analysis, we also explored the effects of our focal constructs across different game genres, showing that the effects of learning routines on SNA are robust across multiple genres of games while the effects of social presence on SNA and norm compliance are more situational.

Our results extend beyond previous research finding that a user’s relationship with the guild leader influences their awareness of guild norms (Goh & Wasko, 2012) in that this research did not examine norm compliance. Our results show that social affordances, social presence, and the routines around gameplay influence SNA, which further influences norm compliance.

## Limitations

This study was conducted in the online gaming context in Taiwan, which has a mix of Western and Eastern cultures. Although we do not believe that the factors in our model are limited to one culture, there may be unique cultural factors associated with some countries that prove to be limitations. Further research is needed in other cultural settings. We focused only on online games, but our findings may also be generalizable to other online contexts that use similar elements in non-game contexts (Liu et al., 2017).

Individual guilds and communities may have social norms that are different from the social norms of others playing a game. Hence, future studies could examine norm awareness and compliance at different levels. Such an examination would deepen the understanding of what happens when norms at multiple levels differ or even conflict.

Since we used self-reported scores for SNA and compliance, the high levels of SNA and compliance may not be unbiased, and compliance may be dependent on one’s own perception. Future studies could include scores assessed by other users in the same community to measure SNA and norm compliance. Likewise, we did not study ways of enforcing social norms or the effects of noncompliance on the players themselves or on fellow players. Finally, our research did not consider the specific norms in each game. It is possible that some games or some subset of users in certain games may have promoted negative norms (e.g., the strong are expected to kill the weak). Likewise, we did not study the effects of norm compliance. While increasing SNA as a way to increase norm compliance may have beneficial effects, it is also possible that attempts to increase SNA may prove counterproductive if they aid in the spread of harmful norms. Future research might consider toxic gaming behavior (Kordyaka et al., 2020, 2023) and whether increasing SNA could have negative as well as positive effects.

## Implications for Future Research

Antecedents of SNA: One area for future research is the factors that influence the development of SNA. We examined five factors and found that three—namely, community learning, instructional support, and social presence—explained a large amount of its variance (46%). While this is a large effect size, half of the variance remains unexplained; therefore, more research is needed to better understand what other factors influence the development of SNA. Research seeking to understand how SNA develops among newcomers would be especially interesting, as most of the participants in our studies were experienced users. Future research could use a qualitative design to more deeply understand the SNA development process over time. Our participants played a wide variety of online games, which enabled the communication of users’ expectations, norms, and responses. Future research could focus on the specific features of one online game (e.g., League of Legends) to deepen the understanding of how the use of certain game features enables or constrains the development of SNA.

Consequences of SNA: We found that SNA had a significant and meaningful effect on norm compliance. SNA may also have other important effects. More research is needed on the effects of SNA on other important outcomes of online gameplay. Our model examined self-reported norm compliance rather than norm-deviating behaviors reported by others. This is a limitation of the survey method. Future studies could use in-game logs and case studies to capture what behavior actually occurred.

SNA in different games: We found mostly similar effects for different types of games. Different games may have different norms, which should not affect the validity of our model because we assessed the effects of SNA and norm compliance rather than the effects of specific norms. Future studies could examine how the specific norms of different games influence SNA and norm compliance.

SNA in other contexts: We argue that individuals’ unfamiliarity with social norms is influential in online gaming environments. However, individuals in other online contexts (e.g., social media and global virtual teams) may also be unfamiliar with social norms, especially when they first join an online community. We encourage future research to examine how our findings apply in other online contexts and to make the necessary changes to adapt to those contexts, thus extending the theoretical impact of our study.

## Implications for Practice

SNA is important for users’ compliance with in-game social norms. Hence, game developers could design features and processes that directly facilitate the in-game learning of social norms, and gaming communities could strive to improve learning routines. Here, we focus on the four factors that our research showed play a role in SNA.

Enhancing social interaction affordance: Our research shows the central role of social interaction affordances in SNA and social norm compliance because it influenced the other factors driving SNA. Game developers could ensure that their products have features that enable and encourage social interactions. Because social interactions are increased when users play in teams (Pham et al., 2023), developers could encourage team play by designing game functions to better support users to form gaming teams and interact with other users. For example, game interfaces should enable individual users to signal their desire to team up with other users and make it easier to find team members. Interfaces could also simplify team communication.

Enhancing social presence: Social presence is useful in increasing SNA and may, in some cases, improve in-game social norm compliance. Social presence can be increased by changing the appearance of users’ avatars to more strongly signal that there is a real person behind the avatar and that the avatar is not just another element of the game—that is, better distinguishing between the avatars of other users and nonplayer characters. Avatars carrying cultural markers (e.g., dressed in users’ clothing styles) can enhance the perception of social presence to better show that the avatar represents a real person (Aljaroodi et al., 2023). Game developers could also increase the sense of social presence by enabling more vivid interactions (Ledbetter & Meisner, 2021), such as realtime voice and video communication to enhance social presence (Jin et al., 2017). Developers could also increase social presence by linking game interfaces to social media. With this link, users could see each other on social media and be informed of friends’ real-time stories, strongly indicating that the avatar has a real person behind it.

Enhancing community learning: We found that implicit community learning increases SNA, inducing users to comply with in-game social norms. Game developers could build ingame mechanisms that show what behaviors are and are not compliant with in-game social norms by programming the actions of non-player characters to respond positively to behaviors that are compliant with norms and negatively to behaviors that are noncompliant. As game norms are not always positive, it is also important to consider the proactive steps that game developers could take to facilitate the development of positive norms. Developers could take advantage of existing systems designed to recognize user activities (e.g., in-game achievements; Wang & Sun, 2011)— e.g., medals bestowed on well-behaved users with high SNA that reward them for their norm-dissemination behaviors. Since peer effects influence behaviors, such activities could help build a positive learning environment in which beginner users could observe good behaviors and learn from the community (Brunner & Ostermaier, 2019).

Enhancing instructional support: Explicit learning through instructional support increases SNA. Game developers could change in-game tutorials to familiarize new users with in-game social norms or explicitly list norms that are easy for both new and experienced users to follow. Such functions could use a Wikipedia-type model through which all users contribute their knowledge to explicitly identify norms. Game developers could also encourage users to offer instructional support to peer users, which could be gamified by rewarding users, e.g., giving badges. Interestingly, playing prosocial games has been suggested as an amplifier of prosocial behaviors (Gentile et al., 2009). Thus, developers could place prosocial narratives in their game content and reward users by recognizing them or referring them to other users. Leveraging users and their communities can potentially increase the number of prosocial users, thus increasing the actual amount of offered instructional support. Pedagogical gaming narratives could be added to instructions and the initial scenarios that new users encounter in order to better orient beginners toward learning expectations from others and practicing good gaming behaviors. Developers could also implement conversational AI technology (OpenAI, 2023) to create chatting avatars designed to ensure that gaming instructions are delivered in a consistent and efficient manner.

## Conclusion

Our study highlights the central role of SNA in norm compliance, suggesting that one important reason for the lack of compliance with social norms in games and other online environments is that users are unaware of social norms. Interestingly, our findings suggest that users with more experience are not more likely to be aware of the norms in a game they play regularly. Instead, SNA appears to be strongly influenced by social interaction affordances that increase social presence and technology use routines for implicit and explicit learning. Our findings provide new insights for researchers striving to understand social norm compliance in online environments and offer game developers actionable insights to improve social norm compliance.

## Content Validity Tests

We implemented content validity tests for the newly developed concepts, i.e., community learning and instructional support. Specifically, we followed the steps of MacKenzie et al. (2011) to develop the scales. First, we defined each of the study constructs. Second, we developed the items for each construct from past research (i.e., Tan et al. 2021; Wang & Ramiller 2009). Third, we assessed the content validity of the items for each construct following the procedures of Schmitz and Storey (2020) and Lynn (1986). Six scholars who had published papers on online user psychology and behavior rated whether the study items matched the conceptual definitions using a scale of: 4, fully relevant; 3, mostl relevant; 2, somewhat relevant; and 1, irrelevant. The content validity index (CVI) was computed by dividing the number of scholars who gave a score of 3 or 4 by the total number of scholars. Under a binominal distribution, the CVI value should be 0.83 or higher to have a 95% confidence that the majority of scores are significantly toward “relevant” (3 or 4). That is, at least five of the six scholars should give a score of 3 or 4. All constructs met this requirement, i.e., all the CVI scores were 0.83 or 1.00. This range of scores also exceeded 0.70, indicating good agreement (Brown & Hauenstein 2005) and indicating that the items were relevant to their theoretically assumed concepts. These CVI scores are equivalent to kappa values of 0.82 or higher, indicating excellent” content validity (Cicchetti et al. 1981; Polit et al. 2007). We also consulted the scholars comments to make minor modifications to help the items to match the concepts. For example, we changed “peer” to “friend” and added “ingame” and “in the game world” to describe the item scope. We concluded that the items had content validity.

## Analyses Decomposing Gameplay Duration and Gameplay Frequency

As a post hoc analysis, we decomposed gameplay experience into gameplay duration (i.e., years spent playing the game) and gameplay frequency (i.e., hours spent per week playing the game). The analytical results are illustrated in Figure C1. All the testing results other than gameplay experience remained the same, indicating the robustness of the findings of our main study. Gameplay frequency was positively related to in-game social norm awareness, while interestingly, gameplay duration was negatively related to in-game social norm awareness. Gameplay duration was not significantly related to gameplay frequency (r=-.015, p=.62), ruling out the possibility of multicollinearity. It is possible that the reason for the negative correlation may be the changing dynamics of in-game social norms over time, yet we do not wish to overinterpret this particular finding.
