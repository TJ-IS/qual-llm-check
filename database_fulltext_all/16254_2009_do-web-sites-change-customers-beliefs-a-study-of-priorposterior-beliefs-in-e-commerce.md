# Do web sites change customers’ beliefs? A study of prior–posterior beliefs in e-commerce

Fatemeh ‘‘Mariam’’ Zahedi <sup>a,</sup>\*, Jaeki Song <sup>b,1</sup>

<sup>a</sup> Lubar School of Business, University of Wisconsin-Milwaukee, Milwaukee, WI, United States

<sup>b</sup> Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences, Rawls College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Milwaukee, WI, United States

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Posterior beliefs Prior beliefs Web-design elements Perceived presence of web-design elements e-Commerce salient beliefs

## A B S T R A C T

Web sites are the main interface between online merchants and their customers. Despite the consequent importance of web-site design, there is little theoretical foundation that can be used to assess how webdesign factors lead e-customers to revise their earlier beliefs. Our study examined web customers’ salient beliefs and the role of web-design elements in altering customers’ existing beliefs after their exposure to an e-commerce web site. Using salient beliefs and categories of web-design elements identified previously, we developed a conceptual model of belief revisions due to exposure to web-design elements and internal cues. The empirical analysis of the model indicated that web customers were influenced by the external cues from the design factors as well as the internal ones from their prior beliefs. Our findings have implications in designing web sites for e-commerce

## 1. Introduction

Retailers’ web sites have become a major channel for selling and interacting with customers; as a result, several authors have identified elements of web-site design that are important from a user perspective, such as information quality, price information, web-page structure, navigation, security, page-loading speed, available services, and customer risk. The role of web sites can be viewed in two ways: as sources of passive information with little impact on customer attitude or as vendors’ representatives communicating messages that influence visitors’ perceptions and beliefs. Therefore, insight into how a web site’s design impacts potential customers’ belief systems should be valuable to online vendors.

In order understand the impact of web sites, we explored the effect of web-design elements on visiting customers and how these elements change customers’ beliefs. This knowledge is critical for designing web sites that communicate vendors’ messages effectively. Customers visiting a web site have different dispositions and beliefs. Web sites that could positively influence their salient beliefs are more effective in communicating online vendors’ messages and the nature of their services, and thus contributinge to their success (see, for example, [10,16]). Although the inclusion of various web elements has been advocated, there is little known about their effiicacy and how they change customers’ behavior. Our study attempted to fill this gap. Hence, we addressed two research questions:

\- Do web-design elements on B-to-C e-commerce web sites (as external cues) lead web customers to change their beliefs?

\- Are there internal cues that may play a role in web customers post-exposure beliefs?

The answers to these questions could provide insight into the processes by which the interplay of customers’ pre-dispositions and their exposure to web-design elements could influence their beliefs.

## 2. Theoretical background

To investigate the change of customers’ beliefs, we drew on several theories, as summarized in Table 1. These theories fall into three categories. The first includes Bayesian and self-perception theories, which form the argument for the process of belief revision from prior beliefs to posterior beliefs after an exposure to external cues; these theories are the overarching ones in our model conceptualization. The second category consists of theory of planned behavior (TPB) that deals with the impact of salient beliefs on intentions and behavior. The third consists of theories used for the conceptual discussions of specific beliefs, which include expectation–confirmation theory, interpersonal informational influence, self-efficacy, telepresence, basic action theory, media richness theory. The theories in the third category are peripheral and provide local support for specific paths or beliefs.

The Bayesian theory deals with the extent of changes in beliefs after exposure to certain events or information—an individual has prior beliefs that may be modified because of access to new information. This theory is used to explore decision rules and test theories associated with an individual’s beliefs [9]; based on this, one may question how a web site changes customers’ beliefs. We argue that the exposure to the web-design elements may change or strengthen prior beliefs, and the web site’s impacts are reflected in customers’ posterior beliefs (formed after exposure to the site).

Self-perception theory posits that individuals continually revise their perceptions as they collect information about a behavior by observing their own or others’ behaviors. The revised perceptions can affect subsequent behaviors [6].

External cues are observed from the environment. In our experiment, they were the elements on an e-commerce web site. Internal cues are pre-exposure beliefs that exist in web customers’ memory, and are updated by exposure to the external cues [6]. Thus internal cues are directly related to salient customers’ beliefs prior to exposure to a web site. When a web site appears relevant and credible, it impacts the web customer’s beliefs. Thus, customers’ beliefs after exposure to the web site (revised internal cues) are associated with their prior beliefs and the influence of elements on the web site (external cues). This argument forms the theoretical basis for our prior–posterior model.

## 3. Research model

Our model posits that web customers’ posterior beliefs are a function f of their perceptions about the presence of web-design element WD and their prior beliefs PB as:

$$
\mathrm{PosB} _ {i} = f (\mathrm{WD} _ {i}, \mathrm{PB} _ {i})
$$

Constructs representing prior beliefs are defined as salient beliefs held by web customers prior to visiting the site. Posterior beliefs are formulated after exposure to the elements on the site. The perceived presence constructs measure the extent to which customers were aware of the existence of the elements. In order to validate the model, we first had to determine the beliefs and webdesign elements salient to it. This was accomplished in two steps; the details of this were provided in Ref. [23].

## 3.1. Identification of web customers’ beliefs (step 1)

Based on TPB [2] and its synthesis with the interpersonal informational influence approach, we identified web customers’ salient beliefs in three categories: market-related/behavioral, social, and technical/control [5], as summarized in Table 2.

Price beliefs are consumers’ assumptions that they pay a relatively low price and save money. Service beliefs are a combination of perceptions of responsiveness, reliability, and assurance. The salient social beliefs consisted of external normative beliefs. A web customer is interested in what other people know about a product rather than in how they judge his or her purchase behavior. In the informational influence approach, the process involves observing others or asking them about their view of a specific product or service. Technical/control beliefs consisted of self-efficacy and purchase facilitation (perceived availability of resources needed to accomplish the task or the presence or absence of facilitating conditions to help it).

## 3.2. Identification and categorization of web-design elements (step 2)

Web-design elements (WDE) are the features, components, and information used in developing e-commerce sites. This definition excludes their overall design or the process of using them. Developing a conceptual model of the impact of WDE in the revision of beliefs required the identification and categorization of WDE.

The first was accomplished by using an interpretive approach to elements on the sites. Two surveys were administered to establish the importance of each element (on a 10-point continuous scale), as shown in Appendix A.

The second was accomplished in a survey of 101 participants in 2003. In this, respondents categorized the items and rated the importance of each element on a 10-point continuous scale. Following McKinney et al. [14], a cut-off point of 5 was applied to assess importance. Table 3 reports the five categories of WDE. These constituted a synthesis of various categorizations of web elements. For example, Palmer [19] identified five important factors of web site success, while Agarwal and Venkatesh [1] developed an evaluation procedure for web site usability. Other studies suggested that customers are more likely to enjoy web sites that are informative, entertaining, interactive, and orderly [27,28].

The categorization of elements in each category was based on the primary role of each element. However, each element could play a lesser role in other categories. For example, the ‘‘various payment options’’ was judged as being made-for-the-medium content, as it related to order processing, but it may also have a secondary role in the responsive service category as a desirable service feature.

## 3.3. The prior–posterior belief (PPB) model

The specification of important web customers’ beliefs about ecommerce and the identification and categorization of WDE made it possible to conceptualize a model for measuring the impact of WDE. Our prior–posterior belief (PPB) model was developed to analyze web customers’ salient posterior beliefs formed after exposure to an e-commerce web site, see Fig. 1.

## 3.3.1. External cues: perceived presence of web-design categories

Web-design categories form the external cues for formation of beliefs. These cues could promote extrinsic motivations [13,22]. We reasoned that the mere existence of WDE on a web site would not impact web customers’ beliefs—unless customers noticed them, their beliefs would not be affected. We thus defined perceived presence of a web-design element as the fact that web customers noted their existence or absence on a web site. The importance of perceived presence was further supported by the theory of telepresence, defined as ‘‘the mediated perception of an environment’’ [24], giving the user a feeling of being in it or ‘‘the simulated perception of direct experience’’ [8]. Novak et al. [17] also described telepresence as important in navigating commercial web sites. On some web sites, telepresence provides customers with a feeling of being in a real store, where they can ask questions and test products—playing songs on a CD, reading the contents of books, or trying clothes on virtual models that have the customer’s measurements. On other sites with inadequate WDE, telepresence may be quite limited and the site may be seen only as a place for ordering a product.

Telepresence influences a customer’s attitudes and behavior; therefore we hypothesized that web customers should first perceive the presence of WDE to experience some feeling of visiting a real store. This argument is supported by the basic action theory [12], which posits that perception is the antecedent of cognition, which in turn is the antecedent of action. More specifically, we propose that the perceived presence of each category of WDE has a primary impact on its corresponding belief and a possible secondary impact on other beliefs.

Hypothesis 1a. Perceived presence of competitive price webdesign elements positively impacts web customers’ posterior price beliefs.

Hypothesis 1b. Perceived presence of competitive price webdesign elements has secondary impacts on their other salient posterior beliefs (service, normative, self-efficacy, and purchase facilitation).

Hypothesis 2a. Perceived presence of web-design elements for responsive service positively impacts web customers’ posterior service beliefs.

Hypothesis 2b. Perceived presence of web-design elements for responsive service has secondary impacts on other salient posterior beliefs.

Hypothesis 3a. Perceived presence of web-design elements for interpersonal information positively impacts web customers’ posterior normative beliefs.

Hypothesis 3b. Perceived presence of web-design elements for interpersonal information has secondary impacts on other salient posterior beliefs.

Hypothesis 4a. Perceived presence of web-design elements for the ease of use positively impacts web customers’ posterior self-efficacy beliefs.

Hypothesis 4b. Perceived presence of web-design elements for the ease of use has secondary impacts on other salient beliefs.

Hypothesis 5a. Perceived presence of web-design elements for made-for-the-medium content positively impacts web customers’ posterior purchase facilitation beliefs.

Hypothesis 5b. Perceived presence of web-design elements for made-for-the-medium content has secondary impacts on other salient posterior beliefs.

## 3.3.2. Internal cues: prior beliefs

In our model, we proposed that a web customer’s higher levels of prior beliefs positively influenced their posterior beliefs. In this, we drew on the expectation–(dis)confirmation theory, which has three main constructs: expectation, perceived performance, and confirmation (or disconfirmation). In the marketing literature, it was shown that expectation had a positive influence on performance [18]. The application of this theory in e-commerce sites also supported this influence. McKinney et al. found that the expectation of a web site’s information and system quality positively impacted the perceived performance. Applied to our model, we postulated that the performance evaluation of the site (posterior belief in the model) was positively impacted by prior expectations. Furthermore, we postulated that the posterior beliefs were a function of prior beliefs. Hence:

Hypothesis 6. Web customers’ prior price beliefs impact their posterior price beliefs.

Hypothesis 7. Web customers’ prior service beliefs impact thei posterior service beliefs.

Hypothesis 8. Web customers’ prior normative beliefs impact their posterior normative beliefs.

Hypothesis 9. Web customers’ prior self-efficacy beliefs impact their posterior self-efficacy beliefs.

Hypothesis 10. Web customers’ prior purchase facilitation beliefs impact their posterior purchase facilitation beliefs.

## 4. Research method

## 4.1. Scale development and pre-test

Constructs were adopted from publications, operational definitions, and sources of belief constructs, as shown in Table 4.

The perceived presence of WDE was measured after web site exposure. The items measured the respondents’ degree of certainty about the presence or absence of WDE on the web site they visited. The items in the instrument were continuous semantic differentia scales ranging from 0 to 100, with 0 labeled ‘‘not provided for sure,’’ 50 as ‘‘not sure,’’ and 100 as ‘‘provided for sure.’’ The instruments for prior, posterior, and perceived presence of WDE are shown in Appendix B.

## 4.2. Pre-tests: initial assessment of reliability and validity

Four sets of pre-tests were carried out to validate the prior- and posterior-instruments. The first pre-test was performed using item placement and Kappa ratios in two rounds of card sorting. Four different judges were involved in each round. The overall item placement ratios in the second round were 91%, the average Kappa scores was 0.84, and each Kappa score was well above the suggested cut-off point of 0.65.

Further instrument validation was carried out using two pilot tests and the items were modified to make the prior belief instrument to match it more closely with the instrument for posterior beliefs. The items in the instrument for perceived presence of WDE were based on the salient WDE. The instruments were pilot-tested with another 29 participants and the final instruments are shown in Appendix B.

## 4.3. Experimental design

Data collection was via a controlled lab experiment similar to that reported by Song and Zahedi. The experiments had a full factorial design involving five categories of WDE and required the creation of 32 (2<sup>5</sup>) different e-commerce web sites as treatments for implementing the experimental design. All sites were designed for selling a Palm Organizer as its product.

In each web site, a category of WDE could be either present or absent. Therefore, the designed presence involved the existence of the WDE of a category. The perceived presence for each category was the perception of participants of the presence of these categories after their exposure to the site. The purpose was therefore to create treatment effects of categories under a controlled environment.

One important design question in this study was: How can we measure prior beliefs about WDE when web users have not yet seen the web site? There were two possible approaches. One was to assume that the participants know their own prior- and posteriorbeliefs about WDE and to collect both sets of data (prior and posterior) simultaneously after visiting the target web site. This could be done with or without manipulating prior expectations. The second approach was to use pre- and post treatment methods. In this approach, data should be collected twice: once before participants visited the web site and the other after. Thus, elements on the web site constituted the treatments whose efficacy was being tested. Since participants would answer questions about their prior beliefs before visiting the web site, their prior beliefs about a generic web site are termed ‘‘should’’ beliefs. We adopted this approach; indeed, Podsakoff et al. recommended collecting past and present beliefs at two different times to counter the problems of common method variance [20].

Web site stimuli for posterior beliefs were constructed web sites. Prior- and posterior-beliefs were collected for web-design elements of a generic web site in which web-design elements were sole treatments and their efficacy were tested without confounding effects, such as vendor reputation or web customers’ familiarity with the web site that could influence perceptions of web-design elements.

## 4.4. Data collection

The instruments were converted into web pages and data was collected online. Participants’ answers were entered by moving a Java-based ruler to the desired rating. The ruler displayed the exact number (in the interval 0–100) as the mouse was dragged on the ruler.

The lab experiments were carried out in three steps. The first involved collecting data on participants’ profiles and prior beliefs. In the second, participants were asked to interact carefully with their randomly assigned web site as a web customer with intention to buy the Palm Organizer. In the third step, immediately after the interaction with the web site, the participants filled out the instrument for perceived presence of WDE and posterior beliefs. To increase the intensity of participants’ interest in their assigned web site, a similar Palm Organizer was displayed at the session with the stated purpose that it would be raffled in a lottery drawing at the end of the data collection process. Furthermore, the participants received a small extra course credit for their participation. The experimental sessions lasted about 30–35 min.

Data collection was carried out simultaneously at two universities (one in the Midwestern US and one in the South). The profiles instrument contained questions about age and gender as well as 10 items measuring the participants’ perception of their own capabilities in using the web and shopping online. The itemby-item mean difference t-test of all profile items indicated no difference between the participant profiles in the two data sets.

## 5. Data analysis

## 5.1. Perceived presence of web-design elements

The nature of certainty about the presence or absence of WDE indicated that we should expect each item to have a tri-modal or W shape with two high ends (representing certainty about the presence or absence of the element) and one smaller middle spike for those who were unsure about the presence of the element. All items in perceived presence had such a shape with some variations in the spikes, except for four of the items: shipping charges (pd-CP3), information on how to contact sales representatives (pd-RS11), detailed product descriptions (pd-MC13), and product delivery dates (pd-MC14). These were often falsely perceived as present, hence they were removed from the analysis. Table C1 in Appendix C contains the final list of items used in the analysis. Note that the impact of an element on beliefs could be measured only when it was cognitively processed and its presence was generally perceived correctly. The exploratory factor analysis of the perceived presence of 31 items is reported in Table C1 of Appendix C. Except for two items in the fifth category, all factor loadings were high and little cross-loading was observed.

Convergent and discriminant validity was assessed through an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) looking for possible problem items loading on the unintended factors and having high crossloadings. EFA for the prior- and posterior-constructs are reported in Tables C2 and C3 of Appendix C. Both the high values of factor loadings and the absence of cross-loadings above 0.40 supported the convergent and discriminant validity of these constructs. Furthermore, the high pairwise item correlations with the general question for the construct provided further evidence for convergent validity of the constructs.

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the measurement model in SEM is a strong statistical method for validating theorydriven scales. We conducted CFA to assess the convergent validity of the latent constructs in the PPB model using Mplus. The distributions of perceived presence items were not normal. Thus the ordinary maximum likelihood estimation was not appropriate for this part of our analysis. We used the maximum likelihood estimate with robust standard errors and a mean-adjusted Chisquared method instead. This adjusts for the non-normality of the data. The t-statistics for the factor loadings were quite high, providing strong support for the convergent validity of the belief constructs. Various fit indices for the measurement model are shown in Table 7. They were at or better than the recommended thresholds except for NFI and GFI, indicating a good fit between the data and the measurement model.

## 5.3. Model estimation

We tested the model using SEM and Mplus. The fit indices indicated a good fit. Normed chi-square was 2.27, well below the recommended threshold of 3. The RMSEA requires lower than lower than 0.05 for an excellent fit. Our model had an RMSEA of 0.045, indicating an excellent fit. SRMR was 0.085, below the recommended threshold of 0.10. The estimated model is shown in Fig. 2.

R<sup>2</sup> results showed that the PPB model had a strong prediction power for web-consumers’ posterior beliefs. The only exception was in self-efficacy, where $\hat { R ^ { 2 } }$ was relatively lower.

Although most belief-related studies emphasized the robustness of self-efficacy, the low $R ^ { 2 }$ for the estimation of posterior selfefficacy beliefs may indicate that the popularity of Internet use has created such strong specific internal cues about self-efficacy that common WDE may now be less effective in changing web customers’ belief of their self-efficacy.

In the estimated PPB model, the perceived presence of each web design category strongly influenced the posterior beliefs to which it was primarily associated. For example, perceived presence of competitive-price elements influenced price beliefs with a t-statistic of 27.3 and standardized coefficient value of 0.71. A similar pattern of strong results emerged for the impact of responsive service, interpersonal information, ease of use, and made-for-the-medium content WDE categories on posterior service, normative, self-efficacy, and purchase facilitation beliefs. Thus, the estimated model strongly supported Hypotheses 1a–5a for the primary impact of web-design categories on web customers’ beliefs revisions and the main tenet of the model was strongly supported.

The impacts of prior beliefs on posterior beliefs were postulated in Hypotheses 6–10. Prior price beliefs did not have significant impact on the posterior price belief. Hence, Hypothesis 6 was not supported. The other four prior beliefs had weaker coefficients and t-values than those of WDE.

We also carried out a post hoc analysis of the model using the actual (as opposed to perceived) presence of the five webdesign categories. The fit indices continued to be good in this model. Furthermore, the significance of links was similar to that of Fig. 2, except for the link between perceived presence for ease of use and posterior self-efficacy beliefs. In a second post hoc analysis, we examined the possible mediating role of perceived presence of web-design element from prior to posterior beliefs (prior beliefs ! perceived presence ! posterior beliefs). We did not find any significant links from prior beliefs to perceived presence of WDE.

## 6. Discussion

The test of the PPB model provided evidence that the perception regarding WDE in e-commerce web sites strongly influenced web customers’ salient posterior beliefs. It also demonstrated that the role of prior beliefs was relatively weaker in the formation of posterior beliefs. The results showed the critical role of WDE in changing web customers’ beliefs as they visit web sites.

Focusing on specific beliefs, the posterior price beliefs were entirely formed by the WDE for competitive price. This indicated that the way price information is presented on a web site is of critical importance in customers’ beliefs about price competitiveness of the online vendor, which in turns indicates the need for a careful study of how various ways of presenting price information may be effective in different contexts. An unexpected result was the insignificant role of prior price beliefs. The result indicates that online vendors have plenty of opportunities to convince their visiting customers that they offer competitive prices by using the appropriate WDE.

The posterior service beliefs were four times more influenced by responsive service than prior service beliefs (0.56 vs. 0.12). Similarly, the path coefficient for perceived presence of informational influence was five times that of prior normative beliefs (0.71 vs. 0.14). This supported the vital role of the WDE in altering a web customer’s views of other people’s opinions.

The category of ease of use was an exception. It had a relatively weaker influence in the test of the PPB model and no impact in the test of PPB with actual presence of web-design categories. The posterior purchase facilitation beliefs were impacted twice as much by the WDE for made-for-the-medium content than they were by the prior belief (0.38 vs. 0.18). This category involved information about products and shipping costs. It seems that web customers with Internet experience have already formed their beliefs about purchase facilitation. Web designers need to take innovative measures to influence purchase facilitation beliefs, such as virtual examination of products or video clips about their use.

The secondary impacts of DWE categories provided additional insight. Most WDE categories had small secondary impact path coefficients, indicating that the WDE categories had a relatively well-defined role in changing web customers’ beliefs.

The overall process of testing the PPB model shows the feasibility of testing the efficacy of WDE on e-commerce web sites in a laboratory setting. This is an important finding in that one can focus a certain belief as the primary target for the design of a new web-design technology and measure its efficacy in a laboratory setting prior to its deployment. Such laboratory testing could provide online vendors and providers of web services with a tool for prioritizing the investment in various technologies used on ecommerce web sites. Such an analysis could help vendors reduce the risk of introducing a new web-design technology and avoid the cost associated with a failed or premature attempt. At the same time, web customers could benefit from such an analysis by being exposed to WDE that are most helpful to their shopping experience.

## 7. Conclusions, contributions, limitations

Our tests showed that important posterior e-commerce beliefs were strongly influenced by exposure to WDE categories and by web customers’ prior beliefs. Also, while these prior beliefs play a role in post-exposure beliefs, exposure to various categories of web elements are much more important in forming customers’ postexposure beliefs.

Our study scientifically examined how web sites could influence visitors’ beliefs and provided a relatively comprehensive list of web-design elements: a valuable check-list for site designers. The relative importance of these WDE categories makes it possible to determine their role and the deployment of new WDE as they become technologically and economically feasible. Online vendors considering the introduction of a new technology could carry out formal cost–benefit analyses of its influence on customers’ post-exposure beliefs.

Our work also made a number of contributions to e-commerce research. First, we identified the list of salient beliefs in ecommerce that are influenced by WDE; thus our work made it possible to study web sites based on their important building blocks. Second, it contributed to the methodology of studying the role of web site components in influencing web customers; it provides a scientific approach for testing the efficacy of web sites. Third, our model provides a bridge connecting web-site design with theories of social psychology and decision analysis, laying a foundation for a stronger theoretical approach to the study of e-commerce sites. Fourth, the findings confirm the importance of web customers’ internal cues (prior beliefs) in responding to elements of web sites. This helps explain factors in the personalization and customization of web sites.

Our study had some limitations due to the use of student subjects in our experiments; however, they had similar profiles to those of typical web shoppers but did not include novice or older shoppers.
