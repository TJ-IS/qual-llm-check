# A hybrid performance measure system for e-business investments in high-tech manufacturing: An empirical study

Ing-Long Wu <sup>\*</sup>, Jian-Liang Chen

Department of Information Management, National Chung Cheng University, 168 University Road, Ming-Hsiung, Chia-Yi, Taiwan

Received 23 December 2004; received in revised form 28 June 2005; accepted 13 August 2005 Available online 14 February 2006

## Abstract

For over a decade, empirical studies on the organizational performance of IT investment have been far from conclusive, and one major issue that has led to an ongoing debate is whether inadequate methods are applied in measuring IT values. Traditional measures have primarily been financial: return on investment and return on sales. Researchers have suggested a need to use other measures, although there has been little agreement on which precisely to use. Moreover, empirical studies showed limitations in using only a single organization-level measure, whereas a more complete assessment could involve measures from several levels. In addition, benefits from IT investments are normally realized over time. This study used an integrative assessment framework with a three-level structure of organizational hierarchy: corporate strategies, manufacturing decisions, and operational activities, along with a time-lag effect. Different levels of performance measures were examined over different time periods. The framework was verified by survey data. Our results indicated that time tag had positive impact on the performance measures of corporate strategies and that they were significantly correlated with operational activities. <sup>#</sup> 2005 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved

Keywords: Productivity paradox; e-Business; IT investment; Performance measure

## 1. Introduction

Today, it is still necessary to consider traditional manufacturing processes and relationships with suppliers and customers, enhancing them by using information technology (IT) to aid collaboration with trading partners in real time. The first wave of Internet procurement focused primarily on automation of internal workflow. The next phase must offer applications that provide decision support as well as reporting and analysis tools. The online marketplace will aid both buyers and suppliers by serving as a single point for their interaction, eliminating the constraints inherent in connecting trading partners [29]. However, the transformation from the traditional to e-business in manufacturing requires large investments in IT.

For over a decade, empirical studies have also attempted to quantify the benefits realized from IT investments. Although, much of this has shown positive relationships between IT investment and economic performance (e.g., profits and productivity), the results, especially those related to the contribution of IT to productivity, have been mixed, finding little or no improvement in productivity despite massive investment [15,8,17]. One possible reason for this is inadequate methodology in researching IT value. The measures used to evaluate an organization’s performance have historically been financial ones, such as the monetary value of sales and profits or percentage return on investment. These financial measures are regularly classified as internal rather than external (e.g., market share and growth). In fact, they are mostly defined in terms of more concrete business activities within organizations and can be considered as operational measures. For manufacturing activities, the objectives traditionally have been to minimize cost, increase labor efficiency, and improve machine utilization [49].

Perhaps the most serious issue in measuring organizational performance resulting from IT has been that its payoff has been considered in isolation, separate from other organizational activities. Similarly, there have been suggestions that different performance measures should be used at different levels of the organizational hierarchy [34]. Although many authors have agreed on the need to use more non-financial measures, there has been little agreement on which ones. We decided to study the strategic potential of IT in implementing a firm’s business strategy to achieve competitive advantage, particularly in the current major e-business streams of supply chain management, enterprise resource planning, and customer relationship management [14]. This has not been discussed in earlier research on organizational performance measures. Also, defining performance measures in manufacturing should be linked to organizational strategy, as reflected by the manufacturing decisions [51,52,39]. Although different authors have selected different sets of manufacturing decisions, the more common ones include efficiency, dependability, quality, and flexibility [30,2]. Because an organization is a complex system, when one factor is changed, meaningful evaluation may need to go beyond immediate, isolated outcomes, to consider long-term change. More specifically, the values of e-business investments may be realized over an extended period of time, in particular in dealing with non-financial measures [10].

Therefore, we investigated a hybrid performance measure system based on three levels of the organizational hierarchy, with corporate strategies, functional decisions, and operational activities, and a time-lag effect for e-business investment in manufacturing industries. Furthermore, because high-tech industries are more likely to have this kind of experience, firms from high-tech industries were good subjects for empirical examination in the framework.

## 2. Literature review

## 2.1. Traditional performance measures and limitations

Because of the strong emphasis on financial reporting, internal performance measurement systems of companies were normally based on management accounting systems. Thus, productivity has been considered the primary indicator of a technology’s contribution [53]. Although the so-called productivity paradox was originally defined at the economy level, most MIS researchers have addressed the productivity question at the firm level [16]. Several have tried to produce hard evidence of productivity gain as a result of IT investments. Studies have often led support to IT productivity paradox [12]. Many researchers have discussed the limitations of traditional performance measures, and the most commonly cited ones are that [38]:

1. As they are based on traditional management accounting systems, labor is the major cost driver and others are de-emphasized by grouping them in one category—overhead. However, today the typical labor cost seldom exceeds 12%, while overhead is 50–55% of manufacturing cost. Thus, labor is no longer a significant portion.

2. New technology investment may not have immediate impact and can take several years to show results. These lags may be of 2–3 years duration, because users may require learning and adjustment before becoming proficient in the new technology.

3. Benefits of aggregated IT investments are often redistributed within or across the organization and, as a result, improvements may not be reflected in the IT table of accounts. More specifically, traditional financial reports are inflexible, since they have a predetermined format. Thus, performance measures used in one department may not be relevant for others.

4. Cost reduction has always been considered an effective weapon to achieve competitive advantage. However, customers’ demands have changed and low cost is no longer the most important factor in most markets. Skinner [48] argued that a manufacturing firm should instead concentrate on quality, reliable delivery, short lead times, customer service, product innovation, flexible capacity, and efficient capital deployment.

## 2.2. Emerging performance measures for corporate and functional decisions

After reviewing and assessing the research literature, it appeared that the shortfall of IT productivity was as much due to deficiencies in measurement as to mismanagement by developers and users of IT. Rapid innovation has made IT-intensive industries particularly susceptible to the problems associated with measuring quality changes and valuing new products. Increased variety, improved timeliness of delivery, and personalized customer service are benefits that are poorly represented in productivity statistics; these are all likely to be enhanced by IT [11].

Barua et al. [5] examined the effect of IT on intermediate-level variables, such as capacity utilization, inventory turnover, relative quality, and new products. They related these intermediate variables to final performance variables, such as market share and ROA. Lee [27] also found that IT could affect many intermediate variables and thus make an impact on profit. Thus, the relationship between IT and productivity is indirect and complex. Moreover, there are limitations of assessing IT impact with only an organization-level approach or any single measure, such as ROI [43]. Because an organization is a complex system, when one factor is changed, meaningful assessment may need to go beyond immediate, isolated outcomes, to encompass long-term system changes or strategic impacts as well. Thus, longitudinal studies for IT evaluations may be required.

Much recent work focused on the performance of strategic alignment with IT investment [7,13,33]. However, the studies recommended that measures used in the department and business systems be made much more concrete; e.g., the percentage of good parts as a quality measure or the number of complaints as a measure of customer satisfaction. Particularly for manufacturing, manufacturing decisions/strategies and performance measures were linked together in the way of Skinner’s [47] work in operations strategy or later studies [1]. He laid the groundwork for improvements in performance measures through his criticism that the connection between manufacturing and corporate success is rarely seen as more than the achievement of high efficiency and low cost.

Following this, Wheelwright went on to suggest using the same concept but extending it to four manufacturing strategies for measures linked to organizational performance: efficiency, dependability, quality, and flexibility. Dependability consideration involves the company’s ability to meet delivery dates at promised prices. Quality measures how well a product or service meets customer’s needs. Flexibility is the ability to react to market change: introducing new products and changing production volumes. Noble [37] also indicated that better performing competitors should build manufacturing capabilities in a sequential and cumulative manner: quality, dependability, delivery, efficiency, flexibility, and innovation. Other researchers stressed the necessity for prioritizing manufacturing capabilities, suggesting that performance measures be tied to organizational strategy according to ‘‘competitive priorities’’ [20,22]. In addition, business strategies like differentiation strategy are the major drivers of future functional performance [44].

## 2.3. Identification of corporate strategies

Corporate strategies must predict future development, take the lead in business process thinking, and draft plans to meet future challenges. Methods of developing strategic opportunities include: analysis of the value chain [40], the strategic target/strategic thrust matrix [54,55], critical success factors [42], the customer resource life cycle [23], and McFarlan’s framework [32]. However, the adoption of most of these methods has not been widely reported in the literature (except the value chain and strategic thrust/strategic target matrix [6]). The latter analyzes external environment for developing proper strategies for certain targets, as indicated in Fig. 1. This includes three targets (suppliers, customers, and competitors) and five thrusts (competitive strategies: cost, innovation, growth, alliance, and differentiation). For instance, the cell in the first row and column indicates a cost strategy adopted for a firm toward its suppliers. This, therefore, is used to analyze the determination of corporate strategy for a firm [56].

## 2.4. Integrated performance measurement framework

The complexity of managing an organization today requires managers to view performance in several areas simultaneously. Thus, reliance on one instrument could lead to sub-optimization [35]; the effect of IT on organizational performance can be best identified through a web of intermediate-level contributions. In particular, for manufacturing activities, improvement of the linkage between corporate strategies and manufacturing decisions may have a major role in measuring overall business performance [41]. Accordingly, research has suggested several performance measurement frameworks that provide an overall view of performance and guard against sub-optimization.

The strategic measurement analysis and reporting technique (SMART) was developed by Wang Laboratories, Inc. [28]. It can be represented by the four-level pyramid of objects and measures of Fig. 2. At the top, management assigns a corporate portfolio role to each business unit and distributes resources. At the second, market and financial performance measures are defined by each business unit. At the third, more concrete objectives or measures are defined for each business operating system. At the fourth, the department and work centers are evaluated by specific criteria, such as quality, delivery, process time, and cost. Thus, the lower level performance measures are the keys to achieving higher level results to ensure successful implementation of the strategy. However, the performance measures, such as financial measures defined for measuring higher level performances, are inconsistent with that of other literature.

The balanced scorecard allows managers to look at a business from four perspectives (customers, innovation and learning, financial, and internal business) and links their goals and performance measures, as indicated in Fig. 3 [24–26,4]. It provides answers to four basic questions. How do customers see us? Can we continue to improve and create value? How do we look to shareholders? At what must we excel? The balanced scorecard attempts to integrate the four important performance perspectives in a simple and easy-to-use management report. Its main weakness is that it is primarily designed for senior managers by providing them an overall view of performance [19]. Moreover, the performance measures cyclically influence each other; e.g., the interaction between financial and internal business perspectives and thus financial and customer perspectives.

## 3. Theoretical framework

In using non-financial measures for proving productivity of IT in organizations, there has been little agreement on measures to use. Our study developed a hybrid performance measure system based on Anthony’s organizational hierarchy [3], with time-lag effects for manufacturing industries (see Fig. 4). Four hypotheses were proposed to verify the issues. Two dealt with time-lag effects on the performance and two with the relationships between the performance measures of corporate strategies and other organizational levels. The performance measures of manufacturing decisions were based on four evaluation criteria: efficiency, dependability, quality, and flexibility. The traditional financial measures, such as return on investment, return on sales, sales by total assets, and sales by employees, were used for the performance measures of operational activities.

Since our study was intended to evaluate the performance measures of e-business investments in high-tech manufacturing industries, a survey from a large sample of firms in these industries was required. Moreover, we also wished to investigate the lag effects of e-business investments and longitudinal studies are difficult to conduct in large sample survey, being more suitable for case studies [50]. To overcome these problems, the research method was carefully designed as follows:

1. The sample firms were required to have a certain number of years experience in e-business investments.

2. Respondents were requested to recollect the performance of their e-business investments in recent years and rate the similarities and differences of these years consistently.

3. Because literature has suggested that time lags over 3 years may provide meaningful results for organizational performance measures [9,36], we designed the study to discriminate performance measures over a 3-year time period, manufacturing decisions across a 2-year time period, and operational activities over a regular 1-year time period.

Hypothesis 1. For suppliers (H1a), customers (H1b), and competitors (H1c), time lag has positive impact on corporate strategic performance.

Hypothesis 2. Time lag has positive impact on manufacturing strategic performance.

Hypothesis 3. For suppliers (H3a), customers (H3b), and competitors (H3c), there is a significant relationship between performances of corporate strategies over a 3-year time period and manufacturing strategies over a 2-year time period.

Hypothesis 4. For suppliers (H4a), customers (H4b), and competitors (H4c), there is a significant relationship between performances of corporate strategies over a 3-year time period and operational activities over a regular 1-year time period.

## 4. Research design

A large sample survey from the high-tech manufacturing firms was designed to collect empirical data.

## 4.1. Instrumentation

## 4.1.1. Basic information

This part was used to collect basic information about the organizational characteristics (type of industry, annual revenue, and number of employees), as well as respondent characteristics (experience, education, age, and position).

## 4.1.2. Performance measures of corporate strategies

This part was divided into two sub-sections. The first was designed to self-identify firm strategies for most favorable competition in their industry. The strategic thrust/strategic target technique was used for this purpose and the definitions of cost, innovation, growth, alliance, and differentiation strategies toward the three targets were also given to help respondents self-identify their firm strategies. The second was designed to self gauge firm’s performance in achieving their goals for suppliers, customers, and competitors over periods of 1, 2, and 3 years. Respondents, therefore, were asked to recollect the performance against the firm’s strategies.

## 4.1.3. Performance measures of manufacturing strategies

This was adapted from Wheelwright and Noble’s studies, including the four evaluation criteria. Similarly, time lag was considered for the potential impact on the performance of manufacturing strategies, and respondents were asked to recollect their experience of the performance levels over the past 1–2 years.

## 4.1.4. Performance measures of operational activities

This was mainly the traditional productivity measure adapted from Mahmood and Mann’s study [31]. It contained six items. In general, the level of operational performance was evaluated for a regular 1-year time period.

## 4.2. Sample organizations and respondents

The qualified firms for our study had to be large IT investments for several years. Thus, it was assumed that larger, high-tech, manufacturing firms would be more likely to have the right experience for our survey. Our sample was thus from the high-tech manufacturing industries; 932 firms were selected from the 2002 listing of high-tech firms published by the Taiwan Stock Exchange Corporation. Furthermore, the content of this study involves understanding the role of IT on supporting the achievements of corporate strategies and manufacturing decisions. CIOs were assumed to be the persons likely to know the activities while manufacturing executives were generally responsible for production activities. Thus, CIOs are selected as primary respondents and manufacturing executives as major associate respondents to them in our survey; this was assumed to reduce response bias of a single informant.

Questionnaires were sent to either CIOs or manufacturing executives of the sample firms. In order to improve the survey return, telephone calls or letters were used to follow up after 3 weeks. A total of 154 firms replied, with 14 incomplete responses being deleted and thus resulting in a total sample of 140 firms, a 15% response rate. This low response rate raises concern about non-response bias. A test was conducted for non-response bias using two subsamples: early and late respondents. These were correlated on their annual revenue and working experience. The results indicated that there was no significant systematic non-response bias. Table 1 summarizes some of the demographics of the respondents. The characteristics are reasonably consistent with the known profiles of the sample respondents. This increases the external validity of the questionnaire.

## 4.3. Scale validation

Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to analyze scale validation. The literature suggests that, for a good model fit, Chi-square/degrees of freedom $( \chi ^ { 2 } /$ d.f.) should be less than 3, adjusted goodness-of-fit index (AGFI) should be larger then 0.8, goodness-of-fit index (GFI), normed fit index (NFI), and non-normed fit index (NNFI) should all be greater than 0.9, and root mean square error (RMSE) should be less than 0.10 [21,45]. Convergent validity can be assessed by three criteria. Factor loading (l) for an item to be at least 0.7 and significant, construct reliability to be a minimum of 0.8, and average variance extracted (AVE) for a construct to be larger than 0.5 [18]. Finally, discriminant validity can be assessed by the measure that AVE for a construct should be larger than the squared correlation between the construct and others.

For the questionnaire of corporate strategic performance, a single multiple-choice item, there was no need to consider scale reliability and validity. For the questionnaires of manufacturing strategic and operational activity performances, the indices for the measurement model indicated a poor fit, with GFI (0.85) and AGFI (0.77) below the acceptable levels. The results further indicated that two items from efficiency, dependability, and quality, as well as one item from flexibility had item factor loadings lower than 0.7. The literature suggested that model fit could be improved by re-specifying the model without these items [46]. The re-evaluation results showed a good fit, with $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d . f . }$ (0.96), AGFI (0.88), GFI (0.92), NFI (0.96), NNFI (0.98), and RMSE (0.02). The results of convergent and discriminant validities for the four constructs of manufacturing strategic performance measure and the one construct of operational activity performance measure are shown in Table 2. These all indicated a high degree of reliability and validity.

## 5. Analysis and findings

## 5.1. Lag effect on corporate and functional strategic performances

Time-lag effect on corporate strategic performance from e-business investments was examined in terms of the three strategic targets (suppliers, customers, and competitors). The proposed relationship structure indicated time period and choice of corporate strategy as two independent variables, and corporate strategic performance as one dependent variable. Moreover, time periods with three levels (1, 2, and 3 years) and choice of corporate strategy with five levels (cost, innovation, growth, alliance, and differentiation) were of nonmetric attributes, and corporate strategic performance was a metric attribute. Therefore, two-way ANOVA was used to analyze their relationship.

For suppliers, results are shown in Table 3. The effect of time lag had positive impact on corporate strategic performance. Therefore, Hypothesis 1a was accepted at the 0.01 level. In addition, the choice of corporate strategies did not have positive impact on corporate strategic performance. For customers, time lag significantly affected corporate strategic performance, as indicated in Table 4. Therefore, Hypothesis 1b was accepted at the 0.01 level. In addition, the choice of corporate strategies had a significant impact on corporate strategic performance. For competitors, the results showed that both time lag and choice of corporate strategies significantly affected corporate strategic performance, as indicated in Table 5. Therefore, Hypothesis 1c was accepted at the 0.05 level.

Except for efficiency, three strategies are significant for time-lag effect. Apparently, although efficiency criteria has been considered one of the major manufacturing decisions, it is probably an immediate measure of IT payoff rather than strategic measure, and thus time lag will not be an influential role. ## 5.2. Relationships between performances of corporate strategic and other levels

The relationships between performances of corporate strategies over a 3-year time period and manufacturing strategies over a 2-year time period were first verified with respect to the three targets. Both independent and dependent variables were of metric attributes; canonical correlation approach was therefore used to analyze their relationship. For suppliers, there are four functions for this relationship structure and one of them was significant. Canonical correlation analyses for customers and competitor also indicated that one reached significance. Only these three significant functions are shown in Table 8. Hypotheses 3a–c were all accepted.

While time lag was considered in the IT payoffs of the functional and corporate strategies (in this case, 2 years versus 3 years), there was an apparent influential relationship between them. This may explain the argument in the literature that lower level performances in organizational hierarchy influenced achievement of higher level performances. Moreover, the relative effects of four manufacturing strategic performances on corporate strategic performances toward the three targets were examined by canonical cross-loadings of the predictor canonical variate, as shown in Table 9. A variable was considered important when canonical cross-loading was greater than 0.3. The results indicated that four manufacturing strategic performances were all important determinants of corporate strategic performances toward the three targets. The implication in practice is that senior management in high-tech industries should attempt to improve all four manufacturing strategic performances rather than a part of them in order to optimally achieve corporate strategic performance.

The relationships between performances of corporate strategies over a 3-year time period and operational activities over a regular 1-year time period were then verified for the three targets. Their relationship structure can be described in terms of operational activity performance as one independent variable and five corporate strategic performances as five dependent variables. Thus, the items for measuring operational activity performance were mainly defined to assess operational productivity and converged to one construct. Both independent and dependent variables were of metric attributes, and a canonical correlation technique was used to analyze their relationship. There is one canonical function with significance for this relationship structure for the three targets, as indicated in Table 10. Therefore, Hypotheses 4a–c were all accepted. Operational activity performance over a regular 1-year time period and corporate strategic performances over a 3-year time period indicated a significant explainable relationship. Furthermore, canonical cross-loadings of the predictor canonical variate for the three targets were all greater than 0.5. Both testing results indicated that while lag effect was properly considered in the IT payoff of corporate strategies, operational productivity played an intermediate role in influencing corporate strategic performance.

In summary, if IT values are assessed in terms of the operational productivity combined with the performances of the other organizational levels over extended time periods, the contribution of IT to productivity will be positive. In general, the isolated view from the other organizational measures on examining IT contribution shows the findings similar to those in the literature. However, as the results are put together and examined as a whole, overall operational productivity is above average operational productivity. This may be explained from the domain of this study in that high-tech industries are characterized by short product life cycle and product/process innovation and therefore, the feedbacks from e-business investments are expected to be realized in a short time period.

## 6. Suggestions and conclusions

For over a decade, empirical studies have examined the impact of IT investments on various performance measures. However, the results from firm-level studies have been mixed in showing any relationship between IT investments and productivity. We proposed a hybrid performance measure system in terms of an organizational hierarchical structure associated with time-lag effects. This framework was successfully validated, using empirical data.

The implication for practitioners is that the value of IT is realized basically through a chain of relationship within the organizational hierarchy. As a result, an integration of the performance measures in terms of the structure of organizational hierarchy is capable of avoiding sub-optimization in certain organizational levels and effectively reflects the contributions of operational activities and manufacturing decisions to the final goal of corporate strategies. Also, adequate time lags are needed for measuring the performances of different organizational levels in order to examine and prove the contribution of IT to productivity.

Future research could be founded on this work. This framework can be applied in discussing the performance measures of the specific e-business investments, such as customer relationship management (CRM), supply chain management (SCM), knowledge management (KM), and so on. This would provide insight to the specific decisions of IT investments by senior management. Next, this research is attributed to survey-based study with considering time-lag effect and alternatively, case studies with longitudinal experience will be the other way to more clearly examine the performances.

Finally, although this study has produced some interesting results, it may still have some limitations. The first is the generalizability of the findings to other industries, such as service industries and traditional manufacturing. Second, this study required respondents to recollect their performance of e-business investments over past years. A potential problem may therefore occur for respondents clearly and correctly answer the questionnaires (possibly by their mis-statement of or knowledge about the real results over a certain period of time); thus, the data might have biases. However, the responding sample was well representative of the sample frame.
