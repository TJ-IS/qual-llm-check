# Does “Being There” Matter? The Impact of Web-Based and Virtual World’s Shopping Experiences on Consumer Purchase Attitudes

## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: e-Shopping Virtual worlds Social Presence Telepresence Trust Enjoyment Consumer attitude

## A B S T R A C T

The use of virtual worlds as an emerging technology is already having a significant impact on business to consumer commerce and on corporate Internet retailing strategies. This research investigates consumer behavior in e-commerce retail environments, particularly with respect to consumer attitudes toward using virtual world’s shopping compared to web-based, online shopping for real-world items. We investigate two forms of presence, perceived social presence and telepresence, and their efect on attitudes toward e-commerce, attitudes that ultimately influence the consumer's intention to shop using a particular e-commerce environment. Key im plications of this research for researchers and practitioners are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

The only compelling reason for businesses and organizations to invest in virtual worlds is if these environments can be shown to add business value to the enterprise. Virtual worlds are fundamentally immersive because of the higher levels of perceptual engagement and the capability of richer social interactions [1]. A large perceived benefit of using virtual worlds is the ability to interact and collaborate online [2,3]. Several areas of electronic collaboration and mediation, including virtual world’s team work [4–6] and virtual world’s educational endeavors [7–10], have demonstrated significant value to the organizations that have adopted virtual world's technologies. Further, it has been demonstrated that organizational learning is facilitated by various forms of interpersonal “play” made possible by the digital representations of people, objects, and processes in virtual worlds [11]. One significant trend in Internet consumer behavior relates to the initial stage adoption and difusion of virtual world’s environments for e-commerce. Examples of this type of virtual world’s real commerce include Deutsche Post World Net, which ofers virtual cards that are subsequently delivered as real postcards, and a US electronics retailer that uses its flagship store in Second Life, the most frequented virtual world’s ecommerce environment open to the public, to sell real-life items that are subsequently shipped to the user's home [12]. Virtual reality environments provide further functionality and interactivity, and ofer remedies to existing limitations of web-based e-commerce transactions, such as the lack of face-to-face interaction and direct product contact [13,14]. This higher level of engagement and interactivity in virtual worlds is a form of intense involvement often referred to as “flow” [15], which has been shown to lead to positive outcomes for virtual world’s visitors, including satisfaction and loyalty.

Users who participate in virtual world’s buy and sell virtual products, virtual goods, virtual properties, and other virtual items, including trading into and out of virtual world’s currencies per se [16]. More and more retailers are experimenting with three-dimensional store web presences to increase the retailer's visibility and sales, both online and ofline [17]. While some firms were successful in setting up an e-commerce endeavor in a virtual world [18], many were not able to attract customers to their stores in early virtual world’s eforts [19]. Moreover, other studies have indicated the dificulty of conveying the value of real-life brands in virtual worlds, and particularly in Second Life [20]. However, at least one recent study has shown the utility of “cyber-mavens,” highly knowledgeable consumers who disseminate generalized marketplace information, to promote the trials of new products in virtual worlds [21]. In one study of avatars' physical activities in Second Life, it was found that avatars in commercial transaction regions, for the buying and selling of virtual goods, were more physically active than avatars in help-supporting regions, such as for seeking and ofering help [22].

Even in light of the infancy of the eforts, the rapid development of virtual world’s technology and virtual reality experiences for the end user have led to the revenues of virtual worlds reaching \$6 billion in 2012 [23]. However, in spite of the fast growth of investment, revenue, and user numbers, unique challenges to sustained economic activities in virtual worlds, such as the selling of goods and services, have been identified, including how to retain customers, and the diferential rates of adopting virtual world’s technology in non-Western Nations, such as China, potentially a huge market [24].

As the popularity and technological advancement of virtual world and virtual reality technologies grows, companies are taking further advantage of the e-shopping channels as possibilities for e-commerce. Virtual reality technologies are those that work to immerse the user in a virtual world, very similar to the aim of virtual world's technology, yet incorporating a more pronounced sensory experience using specialized hardware to enhance a user's feeling of actually “being there” in the virtual world [25,26]. Current examples include Facebook's Oculus, Samsung's Gear VR, and Google's Cardboard. Virtual worlds aford the potential of richer, more robust consumer interactivity with products and store environments, compared to web-based e-commerce sites. De Vries, Jager, Tijssen, and Zandstra [27] reported that higher interactivity promoted ownership feelings and product valuations in a 3D online grocery shopping experiment. Krasonikolakis, Vrechopoulos, and Pouloudi [36] compared consumers' store selection criteria in online versus virtual worlds (Second Life) environments. They reported that “Core Store Features” and “Security and Privacy” are the most important store selection factors in virtual worlds and sales amounts are predicted by the frequency of visiting and time spent within virtual world’s stores.

While virtual reality is not a broadly difused technology, it is very well-positioned for investment as a shopping channel. Goldman Sachs estimates that in a base case of virtual reality adoption for the retail sector alone, virtual reality revenues in 2020 are to be \$500 M US with 9.5 M users, growing to \$1.6B US in revenue with 32 M users in 2025, a 300% increase during that period [28]. Virtual worlds, a stable tech nology with significant adoption [29], share many of the characteristics of virtual reality that when studied can give insight into consumer 3D ecommerce attitudes. Given the huge potential of virtual worlds and virtual reality as e-commerce retailing environments ofering unique experiences compared to web-based environments, 3D e-commerce environments are expected to be important business platforms [12,30,31].

Shopping in virtual world’s environments for real items that consumers buy for themselves is becoming more popular among consumers [12]. Here, the possibility of virtually experiencing products before purchase is likely to lead to more favorable attitudes and higher pur chase intentions due to higher object interactivity [32,33]. An early study on advertising efects on consumer behavior found substantial evidence that when users feel a “sense of being” in a mediated environment, they are more likely to be persuaded to purchase or use products presented by the mediated environment [34]. Research into brand crossovers from virtual world’s environments to real life shows that real-life brands that establish a virtual presence are more likely to be remembered in the real world [35]. There is evidence of a continued uptake of virtual world’s shopping in the future, as virtual world’s shopping environments come to exploit what consumers value most in e-commerce and in shopping in general: enhanced engagement, both with products and services [36] and with other shoppers [37]. More specifically, customers seek engagement through the provision of information on related products and services and through increased communication capability about the products and services ofered [38]. Accordingly, it becomes essential that IS researchers gain a thorough understanding of virtual world’s consumer behaviors beyond what we have already established pertaining to web-based e-commerce sites and inform practitioners with clear guidelines on how to design compelling virtual world’s shopping environments [36,39]. Hassouneh and Brengman [40] note that retailing in virtual worlds is considered an evolution of the traditional web store, with concomitant advantages, such as the realism and interactivity, that ofers an improved shopping experience for the customer. To this end, they compiled a comprehensive typology of virtual store “atmospherics,” or virtual store buying environmental characteristics, that produce emotional efects in the buyer to enhance his/her purchase probability. Chen, Liao, Chen, and Liu [41] demonstrated the importance of social interactions in recommending virtual goods for purchase. Jung and Pawlowski [42] examined the goals that users seek through virtual consumption, reporting that amusement, decorative activity, self-expression, and socialization are the predominant goals driving virtual consumption in Second Life. Mäntymäki and Salo [43] researched why teens spend real money in virtual worlds, reporting that benefits of a premium user account, decoration, status, and boosted enjoyment of the user experience to be the most common reasons.

The literature on website-based e-commerce related to online shopping attitude is robust, with many notable studies demonstrating the impact that various antecedents have on an e-shopper's attitude and behavioral intention [44]. However, these studies do not incorporate the additional factors that might impact e-shopping adoption in virtual worlds, or in other e-shopping environments that leverage having a shopper feel physically present in the shopping environment virtually, such as virtual reality environments. The key diference between webbased and virtual world-based e-commerce rests in the concept of presence, specifically social presence and telepresence. The term “telepresence” was first introduced by Marvin Minsky [45], as the degree to which a user feels “transported” via a virtual “tele-porting system.” In more recent years, telepresence has been used as a term descriptive of the sense of being “transported” by a new technology. With respect to virtual worlds, this construct describes the sense of actually being present within the virtual world's interactive environment and is often modeled as an antecedent variable of other components of the user's experience in that environment [46].

E-commerce web sites incorporate perceived social presence, where the shopping site conveys a feeling of human contact, sociability, and sensitivity in the attempt to approximate the in-store consumer experience [47]. Alternatively, virtual world’s shopping incorporates perceived social presence in addition to telepresence, where the environment induces the disappearance of mediation, where both the medium (the software-based environment) and the surrounding physical environment disappear from the user's phenomenal awareness [48]. Our primary research objective is to better understand underlying consumer behavior in virtual world’s retail environments, particularly with respect to consumer attitudes and acceptance of virtual world’s shopping, contrasted with web-based online shopping. Although the use of 3D web design has been examined in the e-commerce context [49] as a proxy for enhanced user experience in 3D and immersing a consumer in the environment with augmented reality technology [50], this is among the first studies to directly compare web-based e-commerce environments and virtual world’s e-commerce environments and the antecedents to examine user attitudes toward e-commerce involving real-world’s items, as opposed to virtual world’s items [51].

The primary theoretical contribution of this study is the addition of telepresence as an antecedent to online shopping attitude when in vestigating the potential of virtual world’s shopping channel adoption or any potential 3D e-commerce shopping channel where telepresence might be a significant factor, including virtual reality shopping chan nels. Papagiannidis, Pantano, See-To, Dennis and Bourlakis [50] conducted an experiment comparing users' perceptions of a 2D online clothing store with an enhanced 3D immersive virtual store. They modeled a “simulated experience” construct as a surrogate for telepresence, finding that the simulated experience in the 3D environment is more conducive of engagement and enjoyment, in turn, leading to greater purchase intention. The dimension of presence known as telepresence is the key diferentiating factor between 2D web-based ecommerce shopping site experiences and 3D internet e-commerce experiences, inclusive of virtual worlds and virtual reality. Without this additional antecedent incorporated into theoretical models of online shopping attitude, current models do not provide suficient insight into the factors that influence shopping attitudes in these new channels. The development of this model directly addresses the impact of presence, both perceived social presence and telepresence, on trust, enjoyment, and perceived usefulness, antecedents of users' intention to purchase in an e-commerce environment, within diferent e-commerce environments. By incorporating telepresence into the nomological net of ecommerce antecedents, virtual world’s e-commerce environments can be compared to web-based e-commerce shopping environments, giving critical insights into how virtual world’s technology can change retail organizations, online retail sales strategies, and the e-commerce en vironments themselves [52], and how this technology will afect individual consumer behavior [53]. Additionally, the model we present will provide researchers investigating these 3D shopping channels a theoretical basis for further investigations.

Finally, using this model to compare web-based and virtual world’s e-commerce environments provides practitioners relevant insights into what aspects must be considered with developing a virtual world’s store presence that difer from the web-based store presence that an organization already has developed, following on the work of Dionisio et al [54] and Hassouneh and Brengman [51]. The results of this study give companies and designers some insight into how best to position their virtual world’s presence in light of the significant investments that are currently being made in virtual world’s environments and virtual reality technologies, where shopping is a central activity [55].

## 2. Theoretical Foundations

## 2.1. Perceived Social Presence and Telepresence

The sensation of immersion, such as that encountered in virtual worlds, has been previously confirmed as an antecedent condition to both social presence and telepresence [56]. Broadly, presence is defined as the sensation that a manifestly mediated experience is not perceived as mediated, with virtual objects experienced as actual objects [31,48,57]. While presence has other characterizations in research in diferent academic fields, Ijsselsteijn et al. [58] collapsed the multi dimensionality of presence from six to two constructs: social and phy sical presence, with the intersection of the two called co-presence. In information systems research. both telepresence and social presence have been often studied with respect to their importance to systems adoption [31]. With this research's focus on contrasting web-based shopping environments versus virtual world’s shopping environments influence consumers' attitudes toward e-commerce, we focus on two key factors that would be likely to have the most impact within our research context: perceived social presence and telepresence.

“Flow” is a form of intense involvement directly related to the in teractivity of virtual worlds [15]. Flow is an afect-based response to intense involvement that promotes high psychological engagement and often leads to positive outcomes for virtual world’s visitors, including learning, satisfaction, and loyalty [15]. The “presence” aspect of flow relevant to this research is the concept of telepresence, given that this work compares web-based e-commerce environments to those in virtual worlds. Virtual world’s environments have the added aspect of presence – telepresence – that web-based e-commerce systems do not have. The overall efect fostered by telepresence in a virtual world's store is to engender the sense of actually being present in the store, which, in turn, elevates the importance of the efects of physical presence, and potentially social presence as well [34,57,59]. Additionally, telepresence is the extent to which one feels present in the mediated environment rather than in the immediate physical environment [59–61]. A virtual world’s communication medium increases the interactivity and vividness of user interaction as compared to a web-based medium by ofering a greater number of movements in the virtual world’s space, a sense of contact or touch, and sounds that stimulate those in the real environment [50]. Therefore, an e-commerce shopper will likely have an enhanced sense of telepresence in the virtual world’s shopping environment. Telepresence is fostered by a greater degree by the virtual world’s e-commerce sites compared to web-based e-commerce sites and, thus, is an integral part of our research model. Comparing the efects of the web-based e-commerce shopping attitudes model to those of the virtual world’s e-commerce shopping attitudes model will pro vide insight into which aspects of the e-commerce environment enhance adoption, focusing on the aspects of a virtual world’s environment found as a significant enhancement over the web-based ecommerce model.

Social presence theory regards social presence as a quality inherent in a communication medium, widely varying among these media [62], with social presence defined as the extent to which a medium allows users to experience others as being psychologically present [48]. The theory of social presence posits that a medium's social efects are principally caused by the degree of social presence that it afords to its users [62]. The increased social presence of a medium leads to a more focused perception of the person with whom one is interacting, by conveying a feeling of human contact, sociability, and sensitivity [47,63,64]. By adopting this perspective of perceived social presence, we can add our findings to those of other studies of web-based and virtual world’s-based e-commerce environments [65–68] in investigating the impact of perceived social presence on the commonly studied antecedents to consumers' attitudes toward e-commerce and how its impact difers between the two environments.

## 3. Research Model and Hypotheses

Prior research into antecedents to e-commerce and to virtual world’s purchasing behavioral intentions is vast and often presents conflicting findings. Bleize and Antheunis [69] performed a meta-analysis of factors in the literature influencing purchasing intent in virtual worlds and reported that perceived enjoyment, social influence, customization, and ease of use are the important factors, as well as beliefs and attitudes. Mäntymäki and Salo [70] builds on the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model to examine the influence of perceived usefulness, perceived enjoyment, social presence, and perceived ease of use (among other variables) on purchasing intent in social virtual worlds. Ahmad and Abdulkarim [71] performed an experimental study investigating the factors that impact the intention to use, or not use, Second Life. They report that a positive “flow” experience with virtual world’s positively influences attitudes toward virtual worlds, which, in turn, influences intention to use virtual worlds. What is notable about the Ahmad and Abdulkarim [71] model is the integration of several prominent theoretical streams, including TAM, Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) Embodied Social Presence theory, Flow theory, and Jungian personality theory. Cox, Guzman, Cromer, and Bagui [72] and Cox [73] both examined the efects of virtual world’s skills, attitudes, and subjective norms on intentions to purchase in virtual worlds, drawing from both TRA and Flow for theoretical underpinnings. Piyathasanan et al. [74] examined social presence, social capital, flow experience, and situational involvement as key drivers of the virtual world’s experience, finding that these variables positively influence perceptions of both economic and social value and, in turn, loyalty, in both the real and virtual worlds.

Ingham et al. [44] performed a meta-analytic review on studies of eshopping acceptance and found that while there were varied findings across the studies, there was an improved model over TAM to describe e-commerce success, which they termed TAM with attitude. The TAM with attitude model [44] is an adaptation of the TRA to the field of IS. While extensive studies have confirmed the robustness of TAM, several studies have also pointed out limitations of the model. Attempts to extend TAM to address its limitations have primarily taken one of four approaches, including examining antecedents and moderators of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (new exogenous mechanisms) [75]. We look to extend the TAM with attitude model by looking at how the individual-level contextual factor of technology attributes impacts this baseline behavioral intention model to create this new model, a multi-level investigation approach suggested by Venkatesh et al. [75] as a future research direction for enriching acceptance and usage models. Thus, we add the antecedents of perceived social presence and telepresence to the factors of perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment to investigate the impact on user's attitude toward e-shopping comparing two difering technology contexts (web-based e-commerce sites and virtual world’s e-commerce sites). It is particularly germane to investigate how the levels of per ceived social presence and telepresence impact the overall shopping experience in both web-based and virtual world’s e-commerce environments [14,76], where shoppers desire socially rich and function ally convenient experiences within the context of e-commerce user adoption models. Including telepresence as an antecedent for e-commerce shopping attitudes allows investigation into the potential impacts of a virtual world’s e-commerce shopping environment on eshopping attitudes in contrast to a web-based e-commerce environment.

## 3.1. Attitude as endogenous variable

In this investigation, attitude is chosen as the dependent variable rather than behavior intention, which the Ingham et al. [44] model uses. Three reasons prompted this model modification. First, this in vestigation utilizes a controlled experimental design with directly in dicated e-shopping sites to evaluate. Participants were told exactly which sites to purchase from. As such, asking respondents to indicate their behavioral intention, such as their likelihood to purchase from or return to a mandated shopping site, might not be appropriate or realistic, particularly with respect to the virtual world’s shopping site [37]. Second, behavioral intention (for example, to purchase) is most often used as a surrogate for actual behavior (to purchase). In this study, all respondents did actually engage in purchasing behavior; thus, cap turing “behavioral intention” (to purchase) would be redundant and unnecessary. Rather, it is more realistic to solicit perceptions of atti tude, as a predisposition to influence shopping behavior in other in stances. Third, attitude is particularly relevant to the consumer decision-making context. In the domain of consumer online shopping adoption, several researchers have found positive and significant impacts of attitude on intention [77–79,80]. Additionally, attitude has been shown to correlate well with behavioral intention when adoption is voluntary [81], as it would be in e-shopping. These reasons support the usage of attitude as the endogenous variable rather than behavioral intention.

## 3.2. Risk

Risk was not included in our model for reasons similar to those underpinning the exclusion of behavioral intention. The experimental design directing the participants specifically where to shop makes any measure of risk problematic to interpret. Rather, we follow the lead of studies investigating the link between trust and risk, which find trust is an antecedent of risk: The greater the trust in the e-shop, the less the perceived risk (see Pavlou)[82]. Thus, we did not include risk in our antecedent model and rely on future investigations to assess risk. For a comprehensive discussion of research on trust in virtual worlds, please see Zhang et al. [83]. Our research model is presented in Fig. 1 below.

## 3.3. Impact of Perceived Social Presence on Perceived Usefulness

Extensive research has been conducted on social presence and its impact on online shopping [67,84–86]. Both web-based e-commerce environments and virtual reality environments employ social presence to entice shoppers to their sites. In this context, perceived social presence reflects giving the consumer a sense of warmth. Because users make a psychological connection between this human warmth using a communication medium and the medium's usefulness across a range of communication tasks [87,88], we expect that perceived social presence will be positively related to perceived usefulness. Prior studies have investigated and established the relationship between perceived social presence and perceived usefulness in the web-based e-commerce environment [66,89,90]. With respect to the efect of telepresence on perceived usefulness, there are conflicting findings. Increasing levels of telepresence have shown to have a positive efect on perceived usefulness in a web-based environment [91], while in a social network context, it was found to be not significant [92]. By engendering the state of flow, the virtual world’s environment would be expected to have an increased level of perceived social presence compared to a webbased e-commerce environment. Thus, we hypothesize:

H1a. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a web-based ecommerce environment.

H1b. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a virtual world’s e-commerce environment.

H1c. The impact of perceived social presence on perceived usefulness will be greater in the virtual world’s environment than in the web-based environment.

## 3.4. Impact of Telepresence on Perceived Usefulness

The efect of telepresence on perceived usefulness mirrors that of the efect of perceived social presence on perceived usefulness, with conflicting findings in the literature [91,92]. In our model, we expect the more conservative finding that telepresence will not be significant in the web-based environment. When investigating the virtual world’s environment, we expect that increased levels of telepresence and, thus, the greater levels of perceived social presence (H1b), would have a greater impact on perceived usefulness in a virtual world’s environment over a web-based environment. However, this relationship might also be impacted by spatial memory confusion [93], where interfaces with higher dimensions are less eficient for the user.

H2a. Increasing levels of telepresence will have no impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a web-based e-commerce environment.

H2b. Increasing levels of telepresence will have a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a virtual world’s ecommerce environment.

H2c. The impact of telepresence on perceived usefulness will be greater in the virtual world’s environment than in the web-based environment.

## 3.5. Impact of TAM Constructs on Attitudes toward Online Shopping

TAM studies in the context of web-based e-commerce have consistently shown that perceived ease of use influences perceived usefulness, which, in turn, afects user attitudes and intentions [44]. This theory has also been shown to apply in the broader context of virtual world’s adoption [94]. To contrast attitudes toward a web-based ecommerce environment with those toward a virtual world’s e-com merce store, including these TAM constructs, elucidates the impact of the perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of the e-commerce environment on a user's attitude toward each shopping environment. From a theoretical perspective, we see no support for the dimensionality of the environment having a significant diferentiating impact on the TAM constructs. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3a. Increasing levels of perceived ease of use will have a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a web-based e commerce environment.

H3b. Increasing levels of perceived ease of use will have a positive impact on the perceived usefulness of online shopping in a virtual world’s environment.

H3c. The impact of perceived ease of use on perceived usefulness will show no significant diference between the web-based environment and the virtual world’s environment.

H4a. Increasing levels of perceived usefulness will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a web-based e-commerce environment.

H4b. Increasing levels of perceived usefulness will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a virtual world’s environment.

H4c. The impact of perceived usefulness on attitudes toward online shopping will show no significant diference between the web-based environment and the virtual world’s environment.

## 3.6. Impact of Perceived Social Presence on Trust

Gefen and Straub [95] in their investigation of eServices confirmed that perceived social presence was a necessary condition for the development of trust. The greater the sense of social presence, the harder it is to act in an untrustworthy manner. Similarly, Srivastava and Chandra [96] examined and confirmed the influences of social presence on institutional trust-building factors that, in turn, promote consumer trust. As a result, a site with increased levels of perceived social presence was likely to facilitate deeper trust among the consumers [85,97]. One might anticipate that the telepresence aspect of a virtual world’s ecommerce environment would elicit feelings of being more personal to a shopper than a web-based site, and therefore, consumers would be expected to feel more certain about these vendors and have more confidence in their online transactions with these vendors. Yet, factors of web site quality and vendor reputation that build consumer trust in a web-based environment might not be suficient to overcome the negative perceptions that users often have about the safety of the virtual world’s environment [98]. Thus, we hypothesize:

H5a. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on trust in online shopping in a web-based e-commerce environment.

H5b. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on trust in online shopping in a virtual world’s e-commerce environment.

H5c. The impact of perceived social presence on trust will show no significant diference in the virtual world’s environment and in the webbased environment.

## 3.7. Impact of Trust on Attitudes toward Online Shopping

Subsequently, consumer trust in a company's website has been shown to positively afect a consumer's attitude toward the company and ultimately the behavioral intention to buy from that firm [67,99,100]. When investigating the virtual world’s environment, we expect that increased levels of telepresence, and thus the greater levels of perceived social presence (H1b), would have a greater impact on trust in virtual world’s environments than in web-based environments. Thus, we hypothesize:

H6a. Increasing levels of trust will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a web-based environment.

H6b. Increasing levels of trust will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a virtual world’s environment.

H6c. The impact of trust on attitudes toward online shopping will be greater in a virtual world’s environment than in a web-based environment.

## 3.8. Impact of Perceived Social Presence on Enjoyment

One of the most significant impacts of social presence is enjoyment [57,101]. When users feel an e-commerce site is warm and inviting, they will enjoy the shopping experience more than shopping at a site with lower levels of perceived social presence [102,103]. When enjoyment is investigated as a key component of flow, several studies have shown that increased perceived social presence has a positive impact on enjoyment [65,67]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H7a. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on the enjoyment in online shopping in a web-based environment.

H7b. Increasing levels of perceived social presence will have a positive impact on the enjoyment in online shopping in a virtual world’s environment.

H7c. The impact of perceived social presence on enjoyment will be greater in the virtual world's environment than in the web-based environment.

## 3.9. Impact of Enjoyment on Attitudes toward Online Shopping

Enjoyment is particularly relevant to this study with respect to the nature of the e-commerce environment’s impact on whether or not the user has a positive attitude toward shopping in that particular environment. As a result, these studies among others [for example, [77,80]] have indicated that enjoyment has a positive impact on consumer attitudes toward e-commerce websites, while Yim et al. [104] show that enjoyment has a positive impact on consumer attitudes in a virtual world’s environment. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H8a. Increasing levels of enjoyment will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a web-based environment.

H8b. Increasing levels of enjoyment will have a positive impact on attitudes toward online shopping in a virtual world's environment.

H8c. The impact of enjoyment on attitudes toward online shopping will be greater in the virtual world’s environment than in the web-based environment.

## 3.10. Impact of Telepresence on Trust

The impact of telepresence on trust is hypothesized to difer between the two environments. In a web-based environment, we would not expect telepresence to have a significant impact on trust, based on our hypothesis that telepresence is not engendered in a web-based en vironment [34]. In a virtual world’s environment, we would expect that telepresence would have a significant impact on trust [105]. Prior research has found that physical telepresence had a significant impact on trust and attitude with respect to e-commerce [56,106]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H9a. Increasing levels of telepresence will have no impact on trust in online shopping in a web-based environment.

H9b. Increasing levels of telepresence will have a positive impact on trust in online shopping in a virtual world’s environment.

H9c. The impact of telepresence on trust in online shopping will be greater in the virtual world’s environment than in the web-based environment.

## 3.11. Impact of Telepresence on Enjoyment

When shopping in a virtual world, consumers who experience a heightened sense of telepresence are so immersed in the environment that they have little attention to concentrate elsewhere. Results of empirical studies show significant positive efects of increased vividness and interactivity on the development of a sense of telepresence [107,108]. These consumers are less likely to sense the separation between themselves and the virtual environment and, thus, reach a state of enjoyment. These consumers are consequently more likely to be satisfied with the virtual world’s e-commerce experience [109]. This suggests that shoppers in a virtual reality environment, with the increased level of telepresence, might have a higher level of satisfaction than with a web-based, e-commerce environment. Recent studies demonstrated this impact of telepresence on enjoyment, when enjoyment was studied as an aspect of flow [65,110]. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H10a. Increasing levels of telepresence will have no impact on enjoyment in online shopping in a web-based environment.

H10b. Increasing levels of telepresence will have a positive impact on enjoyment in online shopping in a virtual world's environment.

H10c. The impact of telepresence on enjoyment in online shopping will be greater in the virtual world's environment than in the web-based environment.

## 4. Research Methods

## 4.1. Research participants

The hypothesized relationships presented in Fig. 1 are empirically examined through a survey of 237 online shoppers, each of whom made a purchase at a virtual world’s clothing store (RFrye) in Second Life, and who subsequently made an additional purchase at a web-based ecommerce clothing store (Land’s End). This provided a total of 474 observations using a convenience sampling approach. Ninety-two respondents were identified as female (38.8%), and 145 were identified as male (61.2%). The individuals generally spent more than 10 h a week on the Internet (71.3%), although not a significant amount of this time in a virtual world’s environment, with 57.0% responding that they spent between 0 and 5 h in-world. ## 4.2. Experimental procedure

Clothing retail sites were chosen for this study for their familiarity in terms of items available for purchase and the clear nature of purchasing a good online. Online shoppers with less experience in virtual worlds were given instructions on how to create an avatar and navigate the environment, after which they followed the same survey procedure as the more experienced virtual world’s users. Appendix A presents the survey questions, which correspond to the measurement items for each latent construct in the research model and the literature source for each item. The wording of each item was modified such that the phrase “Second Life vendor site” or “Internet e-commerce site,” as appropriate, was substituted for the term “website” in the original survey items. With the exception of these substitutions, the wording of this survey items is virtually identical to the wording of the original studies' survey items.

Each participant was provided with a gift certificate in Linden dollars to purchase any clothing apparel of their choice at Rfrye. After purchasing their item(s) of choice at Rfrye, they were directed to an online survey that solicited their responses to the measurement item questions for the constructs specified in Appendix A. They responded to each individual survey item using a 7-point Likert Scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Next, each participant was provided a second gift certificate in US dollars to purchase an item of clothing on the Land's End e-commerce site. After purchasing their item(s) at Land's End, they were again directed to the relevant survey regarding their shopping perceptions. When they successfully completed the second survey, they were given an additional gift certificate to spend at any retailer as a reward for finishing the experiment. At the end of the respondent's participation, each had completed two identical surveys assessing their shopping perceptions: one reflecting their experience with the virtual world’s (RFrye) store and the second reflecting their experience with the e-commerce clothing (Land's End) site. Each participant also responded to demographic questions regarding their gender, age, and relative usage of virtual worlds and the Internet. Participants were also asked, in free-form text boxes, to state their reasons for shopping on the Internet. A summary of these responses is given in Table 1.

## 6. The Measurement Model

Table 2 presents the factor loadings for the measurement model based on the web-based e-commerce environment and on the virtual world's observations. All of the factor loadings were statistically sig. nificant at $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ . Each measurement item loaded on its respective latent constructs from a lower bound of 0.80 to an upper bound of 0.96. Reliability results for the two measurement models are presented in Table 3. The data indicate that the measures are robust in terms of their internal consistency reliability as indexed by the composite reliability. The composite reliabilities of the diferent measures range from 0.88 to 0.97, which exceed the recommended threshold value of 0.70 [116]. In addition, consistent with the guidelines of Fornell and Larcker [117], the average variance extracted (AVE) for each measure well exceed 0.50. Specifically, the AVEs range from 0.64 to 0.90. Table 4 reports the results of testing the discriminant validity of the two measurement scales. The elements in the matrix diagonals, representing the square roots of the AVEs, are greater in all cases than the of-diagonal elements in their corresponding row and column, supporting the discriminant validity of our scales. We do note, however, that the cross-construct intercorrelation between enjoyment and attitude in the web-based environment is 0.78 and between trust and attitude in the virtual world’s model is 0.85.

We tested convergent validity using SmartPLS by extracting the factor and cross loadings of all indicator items to their respective latent constructs. These results, presented in Tables 5a and 5b, indicate that all items loaded on their respective construct from a lower bound of 0.80 to an upper bound of 0.95, and more highly on their respective construct than on any other. The loadings and cross loadings of the items in the constructs and the highly significant T-statistic for each individual item loading (at $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ both confirm the convergent validity of these indicators as representing distinct latent constructs.

## 6.1. The Structural Model

Figs. 2 and 3 depict the structural model results for the web-based environment model and the virtual world’s environment model, showing the beta values of the path coeficients, their significance levels, and $\mathbb { R } ^ { 2 }$ values. Appendix B gives the descriptive statistics for both structural models. Viewing the results exclusively from the web-based environment data (Fig. 2), it can be noted that all path coeficients are positive and significant, except for the three paths emanating from telepresence to perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment. This is evidence that telepresence has less of an impact (or rather, no impact) on the downstream latent constructs when purchasing on a website. This is consistent with prior literature on antecedents to web-based ecommerce shopping attitudes.

In the structural model for the virtual world’s environment, perceived social presence did exhibit positive influences on perceived usefulness (beta = 0.12, p < 0.05, hypothesis $\operatorname { H } _ { 1 } ) _ { ; }$ , trust (beta = 0.30, $\mathsf { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ , hypothesis H ), and enjoyment $( \mathrm { b e t a } = 0 . 3 9 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 0 1$ hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 7 } ) .$ . Interestingly, telepresence did not exhibit significant direct positive influences on perceived usefulness (beta = -0.05, hypothesis $\operatorname { H } _ { 2 } ) .$ . However, telepresence did have a positive direct influence on both trust (beta = 0.13, $\mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 5 ,$ , hypothesis $\operatorname { H } _ { 9 } )$ and enjoyment (beta = 0.28, p < 0.001, hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 0 } )$ . Perceived social presence and telepresence together accounted for 30.9% of the explained variance $( \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 } )$ in enjoyment and 13.9% of the explained variance (R<sup>2</sup>) in trust. Also, as expected, perceived ease of use had a strong positive impact on perceived usefulness (beta = 0.70, $\mathsf { p } < 0 . 0 0 1$ , hypothesis $\operatorname { H } _ { 3 } )$ . Finally, perceived usefulness (beta = 0.16, $\mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 1$ , hypothesis $^ { \mathrm { H _ { 4 } ) , } }$ , trust (beta = 0.65, $\mathsf { p } \ < \ 0 . 0 0 1$ , hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 6 } ) ,$ , and enjoyment $( \mathrm { b e t a } = 0 . 1 9 , \mathrm { ~ p ~ < ~ } 0 . 0 0 1$ , hypothesis $\mathrm { H } _ { 8 } )$ each exhibited positive influences on attitude, as expected. The direct influences of perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment, together with the indirect influences of perceived ease of use, perceived social presence, and telepresence, accounted for 77.9% of the explained variance $( \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 } )$ in attitude.

## 7. Discussion

The findings demonstrate that the nomological networks that apply to web-based e-commerce and virtual world’s e-commerce are similar, with the primary distinction between the two being the construct of telepresence having an impact in the virtual world's environment and not being experienced by the shopper in the web-based environment. Greater levels of trust and enjoyment fostered improved attitudes toward shopping in both the web-based and virtual world’s environments (Figs. 2 and 3), consistent with other studies which showed that interaction richness with the product and the ability to seek advice from another entity on the product had a positive impact on consumers attitudes toward e-commerce [121]. Additionally, this study is consistent with prior studies that have shown that e-commerce environments that mitigate perceived uncertainty (e.g., reduce perceived risk and enhance trust) lead to greater adoption [36,120]. The impact of trust on attitudes toward e-commerce shopping is greater in the virtual world’s environment than in the web-based environment, reinforcing the enhanced importance of instilling trust in the virtual world’s environment, as well as the actual storefront in which the consumer will be shopping.

This study provides empirical evidence to validate Ingham et al's [44] TAM with attitude model and supports the inclusion of factors related to presence as having an efect on attitudes toward online shopping. The findings were revealing with respect to the added efect of telepresence on perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment in the virtual world’s environment. While the structural model path loadings in the virtual world’s shopping environment (Fig. 3) indicated that telepresence did not have significant direct efects on perceived usefulness, there was clear evidence that the efects of telepresence on trust and enjoyment were mediated by perceived social presence (Fig. 4). In this sample of electronic shoppers, increased levels of perceived social presence lead to increased levels of perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment, but an increased sense of telepresence also indirectly affected two of these constructs, as mediated by perceived social presence.

## 7.1. Implications for Research

The most significant theoretical contribution of this study is extending established models of attitudes toward virtual world’s e-commerce in contrast to web-based e-commerce shopping experiences, with the base model of antecedents of attitudes toward e-commerce being the meta-analysis of Ingham et al [44]. Animesh et al. [65] established the impact of environmental stimuli on a consumer's virtual experience, and this study shows that perceived usefulness, trust, and enjoyment are mediators of virtual experience on attitudes, which are known to have a direct influence on intention to purchase. Additionally, the concept and impact of “flow” is confirmed to be composed of two independent and distinct constructs in virtual world’s e-commerce: telepresence and enjoyment, supporting the findings of Nah et al. [110]. Flow has been studied in web-based e-commerce settings and found to be nonsignificant [122]; yet, when flow is studied in a virtual world’s ecommerce setting, flow is found to have a significant impact on user attitudes and intention to purchase [110]. Our study corroborates these findings. Thus, the e-commerce experience in a web-based environment versus a virtual world's environment varies only with the addition of telepresence.

Applying Baron and Kenny's [123] rubric to the data from this study indicates that perceived social presence completely mediates the efect of telepresence on trust and enjoyment in a virtual world’s environment, but not on perceived usefulness. Specifically, telepresence has direct and significant efects on both trust (beta = 0.13, p < 0.05) and on enjoyment (beta = 0.28, p < 0.001) when the efects of perceived social presence on these two variables are controlled. However, the introduction of perceived social presence as a mediator renders the direct efect of telepresence on trust as nonsignificant (H9 is not supported). Increased levels of telepresence (for example, in virtual worlds) do positively impact the trust of buyers and the enjoyment they experience, which, in turn, can positively impact buyers' attitudes. Schultze and Leahy [124] found evidence of the avatar-self relationship being integrated in virtual worlds, where the avatar acts as a “virtual me” and is invisible, an important aspect of engaging virtual world’s ecommerce consumers in shopping for real products for themselves. In terms of research, this (mediated) finding underscores the importance of continuing to investigate the impact of telepresence on behaviors of shoppers of virtual world, in spite of the lack of direct efects of telepresence on perceived usefulness reported in Fig. 3.

Notably, these findings do not indicate that this increased perceived social presence alone accounts for the shoppers' attitude toward virtual world’s e-commerce. Countering the positive efect of increased per ceived social presence is a negative impact on perceived usefulness when using the virtual world’s e-commerce site. Although the negative impact of the virtual world’s environment was not statistically sig nificant over the web-based environment, virtual world’s shoppers did not find the environment to be easier to use than the web-based ecommerce venue. In the open-ended questions asked at the end of our survey, novice virtual world’s environment respondents expressed frustration in using the interface, potentially indicating that shoppers find this virtual world’s environment to be more dificult to use than a web-based e-commerce site when shopping online. The impact of increasing telepresence mitigating the positive impact of perceived social presence on enjoyment is the result of the consumer experiencing dif ficulties using the virtual world's environment, an important finding for researchers and practitioners alike.

## 7.2. Implications for Practice

The primary contribution of this research to practitioners is to provide insight into consumer experiences in virtual world’s environments to IS strategists responsible for B2C e-commerce channels. Our findings provide guidance to e-commerce shopping channel strategists and designers with information on what is important to consider when building a 3D shopping channel presence, whether that is in virtual worlds, augmented reality, or virtual reality. We find that there are additional, diferent factors that impact a user's e-commerce shopping attitude in a virtual world’s' shopping channel when compared to that in a 2D, web-based e-commerce shopping channel.

We find that enjoyment has a much more significant impact on web based e-commerce attitudes than in virtual world's e-commerce attitudes, the result of telepresence having an impact on enjoyment in the virtual world's model and not the web-based e-commerce model. Thus, practitioners focusing on 3D shopping channel experiences do not necessarily need to focus on enjoyment as specifically as they do in 2D web-based e-commerce stores. The challenge for retailers in virtual world’s environments is that while shoppers find the telepresence aspect of virtual worlds do not directly impact perceived usefulness, increased telepresence mitigates the increase in perceived social presence toward increased enjoyment of the experience. While a virtual world’s environment can bring shoppers closer to the in-store experience through increased perceived social presence and indirectly through telepresence, the dificulties of using a virtual world's environment impacted the shopping experience negatively.

Looking at the findings from the virtual model alone, greater telepresence leads to greater enjoyment and trust in the shopping experience, which is not so in the web-based environment. This is a signal to practitioners that investments in 3D shopping channel experiences are likely to pay of in terms of increased positive e-commerce shopper attitudes in those channels. Adding the feeling of “being there” increases enjoyment and trust in the platform. In addition, when comparing the two models of e-commerce shopper attitudes, we find that for virtual world’s shopping channel, greater trust leads to a more positive attitude toward e-shopping, more so than in a web-based shopping channel. This indicates to practitioners that resources need to be focused on experiences that will engender trust in shoppers. This has historically been a problem for virtual worlds, such as Second Life, and it could persist in virtual reality shopping environments if attention is not paid to the issue.

In addition to providing insights into virtual world's e-commerce, the findings of this research could also be applied more generally to virtual reality e-commerce environments, another e-commerce channel where telepresence is a significant aspect of presence. North and North [125] discuss how two types of virtual reality, traditional virtual reality environments and immersive visualization environments, have dimen sions of telepresence. As virtual reality becomes more important as a driver of commerce revenues for real products, these findings become increasingly significant for practitioners, as the findings apply to any 3D e-commerce shopping experience where telepresence is a significant factor in the model.

## 7.3. Limitations

A sequencing efect could be a potential weakness in this design, with all the respondents having completed the Rfrye survey, then the Land's End survey. The need for this progression was to be able to perform a tutorial with those who were less familiar with the virtual world’s environment and lacked the basic skills to maneuver in the site and subsequently make informed survey responses on their shopping experience. For sequence efects to have an impact on responses, there must be a primacy efect, a recency efect, or serial efects present [126]. The primacy efect does not exist in this study, as each re spondent filled out the environment's survey, either Internet e-commerce site or virtual world’s site, after each shopping experience. There was little potential for remembering the first environment better to impact scores, as the respondents were asked about each environment individually after the task set. This same logic holds for the recency efect, as there was no diference in remembering the most recent environment better. Serial efects could have an impact, yet respondents were never asked about the other environment during the completion of the survey for the current environment, leaving no explicit scenario in the survey where there could be comparison. Thus, in this study, sequence efects are unlikely to be significant.

One limitation of the study is that we used only one virtual world’s site and one e-commerce retailer in this research. Consequently, generalizability is limited. Further research using several diferent web sites and virtual world’s stores should be pursued to gain additional insight into these phenomena. Another limitation involves the fact that task order was not randomized in the study. Respondents went to the web-based environment first, then the virtual world’s environment to answer the survey. While the user's tendency to feel more comfortable on later tasks and less comfortable on earlier tasks might be an issue, the potential for a lurking efect is lessened as the experience level in the web-based environment (the first task set) for respondents was much higher than the experience level in the virtual world’s environ ment (the second task set). This order efect threat can be mitigated by applying counterbalancing in future studies.

## 8. Conclusion

The continued investigation of virtual world’s e-commerce is critica to understand the factors that underlie consumer behavior in these emerging retail environments and to better understand consumer attitudes toward shopping in virtual worlds. This study finds that the increased sense of telepresence experienced by virtual world’s shoppers in comparison to a web-based e-commerce environment has a positive efect on their perceived social presence. As virtual worlds gain more popularity and present more retailing opportunities, the utilization of this so-called “mixed reality,” where consumers shop in a virtual world’s environment to buy real-world products for their person, may provide advantages aforded by the intersection of virtual world and real-world characteristics. Specifically, the use of mixed reality may serve to drive consumer behavior toward virtual world’s e-commerce because of the levels of enhanced perceived social presence and subsequently enjoyment and trust provided by increased levels of telepresence.

As consumers gain familiarity with mixed reality e-commerce through virtual world’s shopping experiences, the sense of complexity and lack of utility of virtual world’s shopping environments may diminish, and consumers may engage more frequently in this type of shopping behavior. Companies that participate in the early adopter stage of this technological advance will be best positioned to take advantage of the presumed acceleration of virtual world’s e-commerce activity that is sure to follow when consumer acceptance and legal issues catch up with the technological opportunities inherent in this enhanced form of e-commerce.
