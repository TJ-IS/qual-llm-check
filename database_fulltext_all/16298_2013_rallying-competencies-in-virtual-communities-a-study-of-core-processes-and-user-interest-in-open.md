# Rallying competencies in virtual communities: A study of core processes and user interest in open source software projects

## a b s t r a c t

Managing work practices has become one of the key challenges facing virtual communities. This paper seeks to broaden the research on virtual communities by applying the theory of competency rallying (TCR) in a longitudinal manner to examine the impact of task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, task assignment, task execution and task management on user interest in open source software (OSS) projects de<sup>fi</sup>ned by popularity and communication. The aim of this paper is: (1) re-conceptualizing the concept of user interest and broadening our understanding of user interest by incorporating users' post-usage behaviors, (2) examining the effect of various tasks involved in an OSS project's key processes of defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement on user interest. The current study found that project popularity is positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by task assignment, task execution and task management. Additionally, user communication was found to be positively impacted by task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and task execution. The data collected from 1178 OSS projects in a longitudinal manner (at 3 time points over a period of 16 months) con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the expectations from TCR and also demonstrated that over time the effects that development of competencies and managing short term cooperative work have on project success might increase, while the impact of identifying market needs and marshalling competencies may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change.

## 1. Introduction

Open source software (OSS) has been widely adopted by commercial and government organizations because it has been known as a high-quality, cheap, and reliable alternative to proprietary software (Paulson, Succi, & Eberlein, 2004). About 60% of the largest North American <sup>fi</sup>rms have planned to adopt OSS products including for their crucial needs (Schadler, 2004). The widespread adoption of OSS (e.g. Linux, Apache, and Mozilla) has created much interest among researchers as well as practitioners.

Every OSS project forms a Virtual Community (VC) in which the main participants involved are the developers who actually do the programming job. An OSS project is a VC in which the team is formed “just-in-time and without regard to distance and shared context (Faraj & Sproull, 2000, P. 1564)”. One of the main characteristics of OSS teams that distinguish them from many other virtual communities is that they have a voluntary community style of governance instead of an organizational hierarchy (Jensen & Scacchi, 2005). This characteristic imposes different practices of structuring and functioning compared with other sort of virtual communities (Crowston & Scozzi, 2008). Thus, potential findings of this research will have implications for virtual communities with voluntary governance style, in general, and OSS teams, in particular.

In spite of increasing adoption of open source software, many OSS projects still fail in the early stages of development (Chengalur-Smith & Sidorova, 2003). According to Krishnamurthy (2002), 63% of OSS projects on Sourceforge.net, the world's largest OSS host, experience failure. One key reason to this failure is that a large majority of OSS projects cannot attract the interest of the developer community (Stewart & Gosain, 2006a). User interest is of critical importance for OSS projects because unlike commercial proprietary software development (Hendry, 2008), OSS projects are not always driven by direct pro<sup>fi</sup>ts and do not offer developers monetary incentives (Bragge & Merisalo-Rantanen, 2009), instead relying on input from volunteer developers/users to further their development (Colazo & Fang, 2009). The success of OSS projects has become a critical topic with the growing dependence of private and government organizations on OSS products (Sen, 2007).

Various factors have been reported in prior studies as antecedents to user interest in OSS project. For instance, certain decisions that project managers have to make before launching the project can in<sup>fl</sup>uence success. Examples of this include decisions on project licensing (Subramaniam, Sen, & Nelson, 2009), sponsorship (Stewart, Ammeter, & Maruping, 2006), project audience and project topic (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002). Our study, on the other hand, focuses on a different trait: project process. The main research question underlying this study is: “What is the role of various tasks involved in the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement processes of OSS projects on attracting user interest?”

The selection of the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and the enhancement processes was for three main reasons. First, after an OSS project reaches a certain stage, the main thing that leads to the release of a new version of the software is incorporating new features into the software and <sup>fi</sup>xing its defects (Midha et al., 2010). Second, effective defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and enhancement processes are tied to users' perceptions of the project quality, activity and value (Mockus & Weiss, 2008) that current literature implies will affect the project success (Venkatesh et al., 2003). Third, these two processes are within the control of OSS project team so understanding their impacts on project success might be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant for OSS practitioners. The process view in this paper actually means that this work is looking at OSS development as an ongoing process of adding new codes and functionalities into the software and removing its bugs. The data analysis, however, applies a traditional variance analysis to examine the relationships proposed (as opposed to taking a process approach to data analysis).

Our study contributes to the previous studies by: (1) re-conceptualizing the concept of user interest in OSS projects and broadening our understanding of user interest by incorporating users' post-usage behaviors; (2) examining the impact of various tasks involved in an OSS project's key processes of defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement (i.e. task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, task assignment, task execution and task management) on user interest in OSS projects de<sup>fi</sup>ned by popularity and communication; (3) applying the recently developed theory of competency rallying to postulate its relationships. More importantly, for the <sup>fi</sup>rst time in the literature, this paper examines the relationships proposed in TCR over time; and (4) studying a considerable number of OSS projects from different software categories over a 16 month period of time (in 3 time points) instead of conducting a cross-sectional study.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews the OSS literature on user interest, the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and enhancement processes. Research model and theoretical justi<sup>fi</sup>cations for the relationships proposed are presented in Section 3. The research design is presented in Section 4. Data analysis and results are presented in Section 5. Discussion of the results and the implications for research and practice is presented in Section 6. Section 7 shows concluding remarks and research limitations.

## 2. Research background

This section consists of three sub-sections. Firstly, user and developer are de<sup>fi</sup>ned. Next, literature on user interest in OSS projects is reviewed followed by a review of the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and the enhancement processes of OSS projects.

## 2.1. User vs. developer

In an OSS project, different actors are involved, including core-developers, co-developers and users (Gacek, Lawire, & Arief, 2002). Core developers are project team members who are registered on the project pro<sup>fi</sup>le as developers and undertake programming work for the project. Co-developers or peripheral developers are a loose group of developers interested in contributing to the project, but they are not registered on the project pro<sup>fi</sup>le as developers. They contribute to the project regularly or irregularly in different ways, such as reviewing source code, reporting defects or new features, <sup>fi</sup>xing defects or implementing features (Mockus, Fielding, & Herbsleb, 2002). Users, on the other hand, are those who have downloaded the project and potentially use it, but are not involved in the programming job (Gacek et al., 2002). This study regards core- and co-developers as project “developers”, and others as project “users”. Therefore, when we talk about “user interest”, we actually mean the interest shown by users of the project (excluding its core- and co-developers).

As mentioned above, unlike a typical closed-source software (CSS) application which is developed by a core development team, different roles are involved in the development of a typical OSS application (i.e. core developers, co-developers, and active users). Therefore, development team of a typical OSS project incorporates three roles of core developers, co-developers, and active users. Hence, whenever the term ‘project team’ is used in this paper, we actually mean a broad team consisting of all the three actors of core developers, co-developers, and active users.

## 2.2. User interest

User interest and involvement has been found as a key competence for organizations (Lettl, 2007) as well as projects (Gales & Mansour-Cole, 1995). ‘User interest’ in an OSS project is the ability of the project to attract the interest of community users to the project software (Stewart et al., 2006; Subramaniam et al., 2009). The extent to which an OSS project is able to attract community interest to the project software (Barcellini, Détienne, & Burkhardt, 2008) is a key success factor for OSS projects (Subramaniam et al., 2009).

In the case of OSS, data on the traditional success factors of software projects like being on time and budget, and satisfying speci<sup>fi</sup>cations is not easy to collect, whereas it is much easier to gather data on success factors such as user and developer interest due to the openness of the development environment. As mentioned previously, the development of OSS software is contingent on volunteer developers and voluntary contributions by users. Thus, the existing studies on OSS success mainly focus on user interest in OSS project to evaluate the success of OSS projects.

In the past decade, researchers have examined user interest in open-source software. However, the focus of these studies has been on a narrow aspect of user interest (typically, the number of downloads). This study proposes a re<sup>fl</sup>exive pause about the notion of user interest itself. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the paper encourages broadening our understanding of user interest by incorporating further behaviors. In this paper, we study user interest in an OSS project from two aspects: the popularity of the project in the user community as well as user communication about the project. Project popularity is related to the user interest in getting information about the project and potentially downloading the software. The selection of project popularity is in line with typical OSS research on user interest. This research also suggests communication with others about an OSS project as a post hoc usage behavior which shows user interest in that particular OSS. One could argue that a user who communicates with others on project forums has probably downloaded the software, and as a result of using the software s/he has decided to communicate with others primarily to respond to a personal need of his/hers regarding the software. This is in line with prior research on IT acceptance where Schwarz and Chin (2007) called for incorporating post-hoc usage behaviors (Thong, Hong, & Tam, 2006) to operationalize IT acceptance. Although the literature on IT acceptance is quite rich (Kim, Chan, & Chan, 2007), this gap has not been <sup>fi</sup>lled.

## 2.3. Defect-fixing and enhancement processes of OSS projects

A defect is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as any deviation from prede<sup>fi</sup>ned quality properties (Laitenberger & DeBaud, 2000). In the defect-fixing process. defects observed in the software are handled and resolved to improve the quality of the software. In OSS projects, this process is normally handled through a defect tracking system. The defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing process has been studied by few OSS researchers. Herbsleb and Mockus (2003) found that the progress in <sup>fi</sup>xing defects reported in<sup>fl</sup>uences the positive outcomes in OSS projects. Mockus et al. (2002) conducted two case studies of Mozilla and Apache projects and quanti<sup>fi</sup>ed some concepts such as defect density and problem resolution interval for OSS projects. Stewart and Gosain (2006b) showed that the percentage of defect reports completed impacts perceived effectiveness of OSS projects.

Enhancement is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a process to augment a software product with features not originally incorporated (Notkin & Griswold, 1988). Enhancement process, “adding features” to the software (Crowston et al., 2006), aims the software project to quickly identify and develop new features to keep them competitive. In OSS literature, some researchers have taken enhancement process into account. For instance, through a multiple case study Garousi (2009a, 2009b) concluded that in the same period of time the quantity of defects resolved exceeds the quantity of feature requests implemented. ## 3. Research model

Theory of competency rallying (TCR) was <sup>fi</sup>rst introduced by Katzy and Crowston (2000). Crowston and Scozzi (2002) then developed TCR and exempli<sup>fi</sup>ed it in the context of OSS projects as a virtual organization. TCR introduces four steps which are important for the success of projects undertaken in a virtual organization: (1) identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of market needs; (2) marshalling of competencies; and (3) development of competencies; and (4) management of a short-term co-operative effort. These four capabilities are all necessary for the success of a project undertaken in a virtual organization. In essence, TCR states that: <sup>fi</sup>rst, a virtual organization should identify the needs of its consumers; second, the organization should recognize and bring together its employees with the skills required to meet the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need; third, the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed employees should address the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need (as a result they will develop their skills and competencies); fourthly, addressing the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need creates a short-term cooperative work that should be well-managed by the organization.

Ahuja and Carley (2006) introduce three main characteristics for virtual organizations: existing shared interest among members, geographical distribution, and employing information and communication technologies (ICT) for coordination. OSS projects can be attributed as virtual organizations (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002) because: OSS community members share a common interest i.e. the software under development; additionally, a typical OSS project is geographically distributed and Internet is used as a communication medium for coordination purposes (Raymond, 1999). Given that, the TCR is applicable in OSS environment. This research employs the TCR to explain how competency rallying for the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and the enhancement processes of OSS projects might lead to the OSS project success in terms of attracting user interest. This is in response to Katzy and Crowston (2008) call on empirical studies with large sample size to test the relationships proposed in TCR.

This paper relates project success in attracting user interest to the outcome of TCR (i.e. success). Moreover, following the discussion in Section 2.2, we relate the capabilities required for the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and enhancement processes of OSS projects to the TCR capabilities. In what follows, we will develop our hypotheses and support them by TCR. Fig. 2 shows the research model underlying this paper.

## 3.1. The impact of task identification on user interest

The <sup>fi</sup>rst capability in TCR refers to the ability of identifying a market need that could be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled by the virtual organization. A key capability of any OSS project is thus the ability to identify and utilize consumers' needs. This ability might be held by the players in the OSS project's development community (i.e. active users, and co- and core-developers). According to Crowston and Scozzi (2002), identifying and ful<sup>fi</sup>lling such needs might lead to attracting users' attention and contribution.

According to Midha et al. (2010), after OSS projects reach a certain stage of development what leads to a new release of the software is mainly incorporating new features into the software and <sup>fi</sup>xing the software defects. Thus, the main needs of users of a particular OSS project are the identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of the features that have not been originally incorporated into the software and the resolution of the defects existing in the current version of the software. A defect report shows some quality needs and a feature report presents some functionality needs that might be held by the software community. Thus, we relate task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation (i.e. identifying new defects and features) to the <sup>fi</sup>rst competency in TCR, identi<sup>fi</sup>cation of needs, and measure it by the number of features suggested and the number of defects reported in a particular OSS project. Building upon TCR, identifying a broader range of software defects and features by an OSS project is a key capability for the OSS project to succeed. On the basis of the foregoing, we formulate the following hypothesis:

H1. The more an OSS project can identify new defects and features, the higher the user interest in the OSS project (i.e., (a) the higher the project popularity; and (b) the higher the user communication about the project).

## 3.2. The impact of task assignment on user interest

The second capability in TCR is called competency marshalling, and relates to the ability of the virtual organization to recognize and bring together actors with the competencies required to meet an identi<sup>fi</sup>ed market need. This capability is de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Faraj and Sproull (2000) as “knowing where expertise is located, knowing where expertise is needed, and bringing needed expertise to bear” (p. 1554). The capability for competency marshalling shows the extent to which participants in a particular OSS project know where the expertise resides (Bollinger et al., 1999). According to Crowston and Scozzi (2002). marshalling of competencies contributes to project positive outcomes.

If a reported defect is decided to be resolved (or a suggested feature is decided to be implemented), someone assigns the defect/feature to a developer(s) to resolve/implement. This needs comprehension of the assigner on who possesses the expertise required to <sup>fi</sup>x a defect (or implement a feature). Therefore, similar to the <sup>fi</sup>rst competency, we relate task assignment (i.e. assigning new defects and features) to the second competency in TCR, marshalling of competencies, and measure it by the number of features suggested and the number of features and defects that are assigned to developers(s) to be ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. Borrowing from the TCR, assigning a larger amount of the defect reports and the feature suggestions can be considered as a critical capability of OSS projects to succeed. Accordingly, we formulate the following hypothesis:

H2. The more an OSS project can assign the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed defects and features, the higher the user interest in the OSS project (i.e., (a) the higher the project popularity; and (b) the higher the user communication about the project).

## 3.3. The impact of task execution on user interest

The third capability in TCR shows the ability of the virtual organization for ongoing development and re<sup>fi</sup>nement of individual actors' competencies. Competencies can be viewed as residing in the skills and expertise of individuals and groups within the organization. Thus, developing and re<sup>fi</sup>ning the skills and expertise of talented developers in an OSS project is of critical signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. This can happen through addressing the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed market need by the developers.

If it is decided that a reported defect is to be resolved, a developer(s) <sup>fi</sup>xes the defect (implements the feature). By <sup>fi</sup>xing more and more defects and implementing more and more features, a given developer can develop his/her competencies in the course of real-practice, “… [Project developers can] autonomously develop their competencies in the course of their on-going ‘real world’ activities and sharpen them in the course of an OSS project” (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002, p.5). Specially if the assignee is the developer who is in charge of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c faulty software module, s/he can realize what mistakes has been made during development of the module. Similarly, if the assignee implements a feature which is related to the software module that s/he is in charge of, s/he can learn what software features s/he has been missing and how to implement such a feature. As a result, s/he can develop her/his programming skills through the learning that occurs after <sup>fi</sup>xing more defects (or implementing more features).

Hence, we relate task execution (i.e. executing new defects and features) to the third competency in TCR, development of competencies, and measure it by the number of features implemented and the number of defects resolved. Based on the suggestions from TCR, a broader range of <sup>fi</sup>xed defects and implemented features demonstrates another capability to achieve success in an OSS environment. On this basis, we formulate the following hypothesis:

H3. The more an OSS project can execute the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed defects and features, the higher the user interest in the OSS project (i.e., (a) the higher the project popularity; and (b) the higher the user communication about the project).

## 3.4. The impact of task management on user interest

The fourth capability in TCR is the ability of the virtual organization to manage a short-term co-operative work. Each identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need triggers a process that involves different participants. The ability of the participants to work together and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently ful<sup>fi</sup>ll the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed market need is called managing short-term cooperative work. For a virtual organization to succeed, it is imperative to manage the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed need to be addressed.

Each defect/feature reported on the defect/feature tracking system of a particular OSS project can be attributed as a short-term cooperative work because different people (e.g. submitter, assigner, assignees, <sup>fi</sup>xers, and etc.) are involved in it. Timely addressing of the defect/feature reports is an indication of the successful management of the short-term cooperative work. Hence, we relate the speed of task completion (i.e. the speed of executing new defects and features) to the fourth competency in TCR, managing short-term cooperative work, and measure it by average time to <sup>fi</sup>x a defect (and to implement a feature). One limitation here is that there could be many measures other than completion speed as indicators of completion speed (e.g. quality of the output, customer satisfaction etc.). However, we consider this limitation acceptable since except completion speed other possible indicators are very subjective, while completion speed can be measured consistently across all sampled projects. Plus, completion speed, to a large extent, demonstrates that the particular task has been well-managed. Moreover, given the large number of sampled projects, the inclusion of quality aspects was beyond our limited time and resources.

Drawing on the TCR, speedy defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and enhancement show the better management of short-term cooperative work as a critical capability of OSS project to succeed. On the basis of the foregoing, we formulate the following hypothesis 4:

H4. The faster an OSS project can complete the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed defects and features, the higher the user interest in the OSS project (i.e., (a) the higher the project popularity; and (b) the higher the user communication about the project).

## 3.5. The impact of popularity on communication

Software falls within products/services category that shows “direct network effect” (Subramaniam et al., 2009). A direct network effect refers to growing value that a consumer receives from using a product as the number of product consumers rises (Shy, 1996). In OSS context, a boost in the number of users can lead to more communication between them, more knowledge and experience sharing among them, and more collaboration in terms of helping each other to solve potential problems with software.

H5. Projects with a higher popularity are more likely to attract higher user communication about the projects.

It was just postulated that project popularity can impact user communication about the project in the same period. However, it is quite possible that user communication, in turn, impacts project popularity in the next period of time. The reason for having such a lagging effect is that it takes some time for users from the moment they download an OSS until the moment they start communicating about the project. Although herein we focused on the impact of project popularity on user communication, it should be noted that more user communication may, in turn, increase project popularity in the following period. Even though, this second relationship is very likely to exist, it is not within the scope of this study (and requires further data collection). This opens a new research opportunity for future researchers.

## 4.1. Sampling

Since 1996 there have been a number of web portals launched that are dedicated to serving open source projects (Capiluppi, Lago, & Morisio, 2003). Sourceforge.net, Freshmeat.net, OSDir.com, and BerliOS.com are examples of such portals. These portals provide a wide range of tools for development, defect <sup>fi</sup>xing, enhancement, software release, communication and coordination and so on. The setting chosen for this research is the largest OSS repository, Sourceforge.net. As of May 2011, Sourceforge had 260,000 registered open source software projects, and it also has more than 2.7 million registered members (Source: www.sourceforge.net).

Sourceforge divides OSS projects into various categories such as communication, database, desktop, education, and text editor. In order to increase the generalizability of the results, we took our sample from a number of different categories. Given the available time and resources, collecting data from projects in all categories was not feasible; hence, we focused on 3 categories: Communication, Software Development, and Scientific/Engineering. In the current study, Scientific/Engineering on the one hand, and Communication on the other, were chosen because they show different extremes in terms of the range of users. For example, Scientific/Engineering software is generally used by engineers and scientists for a relatively narrow set of engineering and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c purposes, whereas Communication software programs might be used by a broader range of users (anyone, including engineers and scientists) for a wide range of purposes. The category Software Development sits somewhere between these two extremes (Stewart et al., 2006). Projects in this category are typically programming tools for developers. Given the range of use and users of projects in these categories, selecting from them should allow wider generalization of the results.

In order to narrow our sample, we then imposed some restrictions as below: We excluded projects that had not had any <sup>fi</sup>le releases within the last 2 years to discard inactive projects; We excluded projects whose development status was planning, pre-alpha, or alpha; We excluded projects not in Beta or Stable status, and also projects that had not had any <sup>fi</sup>le releases; We excluded projects whose development status was mature because they are normally mature in terms of defects and hence have less activity; There were a number of projects whose development status was beta or stable at the <sup>fi</sup>rst round of data collection, but reached their mature status at the second round, we also excluded these projects; We only focused on those projects that had had at least 5 records in their defect- and feature- tracking systems; There were a number of projects that disappeared at the second round of data collection, we also excluded such projects; We excluded commercial projects as well as projects sponsored by organizations and/or foundations. It can be realized whether a project is commercial or sponsored by browsing their website. We also used further sampling criteria as below:

Projects may use Sourceforge in different ways. For example, some projects use Sourceforge for downloads, but not for issue tracking, or use an external Forum package. Moreover, many OSS projects do not use the tracking systems Sourceforge offers for defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement, they prefer to use their own tracking system located on their external website. For the purpose of this study, we focused on those projects that solely used Sourceforge for download, communication, defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and functionality-enhancement, and excluded the rest.

The total number of projects that satis<sup>fi</sup>ed our sampling criteria was 1178. Table 2 shows some demographic information on the sample.

## 4.4. Validity and reliability

Validity indicates how well an empirical measure <sup>fi</sup>ts with the conceptual de<sup>fi</sup>nition (Neuman 2006). Usually there is a gap between conceptual constructs and empirical measures because constructs are typically abstract ideas, while measures are concrete observations (Neuman 2006). To achieve measurement validity, we considered the de<sup>fi</sup>nition of the construct and then sought to <sup>fi</sup>nd a measure with high validity that covers the de<sup>fi</sup>nition.

In this research, we used Sourceforge archival data to attain the intended data from the sampled projects. Taking such an approach (i.e. using archival data for empirical research) can offer several methodological advantages to researchers. Taking advantage of archival data could help researchers mitigate, “if not overcome, threats to internal validity like experimenter bias [p.34]” (Shultz, Hoffman, & Reiter-Palmon, 2005). Using archival data could also help researchers take larger samples and conduct more powerful statistical analyses (Shultz et al., 2005). There are also some disadvantages of using archival data; for example, the appropriateness of the data may be questionable (Shultz et al., 2005).

Data accuracy has been reported to be a potential validity concern about OSS project data stored on Sourceforge, because certain project data are self-reported (e.g. development status in this research) by project managers (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002). However, I believe that using OSS project data from Sourceforge is valid for my proposed research because, according to Tirole and Lerner (2002), OSS project leaders seek to attract new developers, users and sponsors, so it is less likely for them to make “the project appear to be something other than what it really is [p. 33]”, since it would not be a positive signal to new developers, users and sponsors.

## 5.6. Longitudinal results

After all hypotheses were examined for both time periods, a post-hoc analysis was conducted to <sup>fi</sup>nd out which relationship has signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changed over time. We used multi-group analysis to undertake this comparison. In order to do that, we compared the model for period 1 with the model for period 2. To perform this analysis, we treated the unconstrained model as the baseline model and compared that with the constrained models to see any change in the relationships proposed over the two periods. It should be noted that this part of the analysis has an exploratory nature. Thus, we are only able to report the <sup>fi</sup>ndings from this exploration and cannot con<sup>fi</sup>rm them.

We started the analysis by taking note of chi-square and degree of freedom for the baseline model (the unconstrained model). We then began making constrained models by imposing equality constraints on each relationship proposed (e.g. path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for H1a in period 1 = path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for H1a in period 2), one at a time, and took note of the chi-square for each constrained model. The change in chi-square fo each relationship was then computed by subtracting the chi-square of the unconstrained model from the chi-square of the constrained model. If this change is higher than 2.71 (change in degree of freedom is 1 because each time I only impose one equality constraint), the change in the parameter estimate for the hypothesis under study is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.1 level. In order for the change in path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient for a given relationship to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.05 level, change in chi-square should be at least 3.84. Moreover, the minimum value for the change in chi-square to be signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.01 level is 6.64.

Out of all 9 hypotheses examined, there were only 2 hypotheses whose parameter estimates have signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly changed from period 1 to period 2 (H3a and H4a). The change in the chi-square for the relationship between task execution and project popularity (H3a) was 6.1 and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.05 level. Moreover, the change in the chi-square for the relationship between task management and project popularity was 2.74 (it was signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at 0.1 level). This indicates that as the project becomes older the impact of task execution and task management on user interest remains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and even becomes stronger.

This indicates that as the project becomes older the impact of task execution and task management on user interest remains signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and even becomes stronger. A potential reason is that as a project grows old (within its beta or stable development phases), the users become more reliant on the product of the project, and, therefore, a higher responsiveness from the project regarding <sup>fi</sup>xing defects and implementing features is more appreciated by them.

The implication of our result for TCR is that over time the effects that development of competencies and managing short term cooperative work have on project success might increase, while the impact of identifying market needs and marshalling competencies may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change.

## 6. Discussions

This paper sought to re-conceptualize the concept of user interest and broadened our understanding of it by incorporating users' post-usage behaviors. Collecting data from 1178 OSS projects which use Sourceforge tools almost exclusively, this paper examined the impact of task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, task assignment, task execution and task management on a multi-dimensional variable of user interest (including project popularity and user communication). The <sup>fi</sup>ndings showed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of task assignment, task execution and task management on project popularity. In addition, task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and task execution were found to positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence user communication.

The <sup>fi</sup>ndings of this paper have two general lessons for voluntarily governed virtual communities, one in terms of team design structure, and the other in terms of operation. With respect to team structure, team members need to know each other's skills and expertise so that they can assign the de<sup>fi</sup>ned tasks to the right person, therefore producing a better result potentially within a shorter period of time. With regard to the team operation, it is important that team members respond to the needs of the VC's customers effectively and reasonably quick. The lesson that the longitudinal conclusions of this paper has for virtual teams is that as the project grows old, development of competencies and managing short-term cooperative work become even more important for the success of the project.

In light of the insights from this study, we would like to pose several implications to raise OSS scholars' awareness. Firstly, prior research has considered limited indicators to measure user interest in OSS projects (typically, the number of downloads). In response to Schwarz and Chin (2007)'s call on incorporating post-hoc usage behaviors to operationalize IT acceptance, this paper contributes to broadening our understanding of user interest by incorporating measures that show further user behaviors. This paper used two dimensions to represent user interest in an OSS project: (a) project popularity measured by the number of project downloads and the number of project hits that shows an initial interest of user community in an OSS project; and (b) user communication about a project as a post hoc usage behavior in response to a personal need of a user which shows user interest in that particular OSS.

Secondly, in response to Katzy and Crowston (2008)'s call on applying TCR using a large sample size, our study applies the current theory of competency rallying to investigate user interest in OSS projects. TCR extends our theoretical understanding of why rallying competencies for the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and functionality-enhancement processes might lead to attracting a higher user interest in OSS projects. The results of this study con<sup>fi</sup>rmed the expectations from TCR that 4 sets of TCR competencies impact project success. More importantly, this paper has examined the relationships proposed in TCR over time. Our results showed that over time the impacts that development of competencies and managing short term cooperative work have on project success might increase, whereas the impact of identifying market needs and marshalling competencies may not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly change.

Thirdly, when examining the drivers of user interest in OSS projects, prior research have mostly examined project characteristics such as project licensing (Stewart et al., 2006; Subramaniam et al., 2009), sponsorship (Stewart et al., 2006), operating system (Subramaniam et al., 2009), project audience and project topic (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002), and reputation of participants (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002). This research, on the other hand, takes into account the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and functionality-enhancement processes.

Finally. this paper is one of the first studies that have studied the potential relationships between the two indicators of user interest in OSS project (i.e., project popularity, and user communication). As shown in Table 4, our study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that project popularity has a signi<sup>fi</sup>cant positive impact on user communication. This shows the key role of network effects on the success of OSS projects. Apache web server is a good example of network effect's impact on the success of OSS project. One of the key factors that led to the success of Apache is its large user base that offers much network bene<sup>fi</sup>ts.

Our study has several recommendations for OSS projects' managers and developers as well as corporations interested in adopting open source software. Firstly, OSS project managers should be aware of the fact that simply attracting a high download rate might not guarantee that their project will be successful in other respects. Project popularity and user communication about the project are two dimensions introduced in this paper as indicators of success in an OSS environment. The factors proposed in this work to measure user interest provide organizational users who want to evaluate and compare OSS projects with a criterion on which to base their analysis. These factors can be used to generate and compare overall user interest for different projects. This is also of value for OSS project managers because it enables them to gauge the overall interest of user community in their projects.

Secondly, the current study found that project popularity is positively in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by task assignment, task execution and task speed. Among these drivers, task execution was found to have the strongest predictive value to explain project popularity. Our results also indicated that task speed is a stronger predictor of popularity than task assignment. This paper also demonstrated that user communication is positively impacted by task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and task execution. Task identi<sup>fi</sup>cation, however, was found to have a stronger predictive value than task execution. Overall, project processes of defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and functionality-enhancement were found to have a greater impact on project popularity than user communication. These conclusions inform OSS project managers in how much emphasis to put on each factor, depending on whether popularity is more important for them or user communication. In particular, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings stress the superior role of project processes of defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and functionality-enhancement in impacting project popularity.

Finally, this paper enhances our understanding of the implications of project popularity for OSS project success in terms of attracting user communication. OSS projects that have a higher download rate and web hits are more likely to attract community users to participate in their project discussions. Thus, the number of downloads and a project web hits can help OSS project managers measure the popularity of their projects and as a result enable them to predict user participations in their project discussions.

## 7. Conclusions

The learning from the application of TCR in this study con<sup>fi</sup>rms that in order for OSS projects to be able to attract a higher user interest they need to: (1) constantly identify their users' quality and functionality needs. OSS projects that effectively identify their user community's quality and functionality needs are more likely to have intensive communication happening in their project's forums; (2) they also need to know who possess the expertise required to address a particular defect or feature and effectively assign the defects and the features submitted to appropriate developers. Structural equation modeling of this research showed that effectively assignment of the submitted defects and features contributes to project popularity; (3) projects should also develop their individual developer's competencies through effectively addressing the submitted defects and features. The results of this study showed that OSS projects that effectively <sup>fi</sup>x the defects and implement the features are more likely to have a higher popularity and user communication; and (4) the projects need to operate their defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and enhancement processes in a timely manner. The data analysis of this paper demonstrated that timely addressing the defect and feature requests can contribute to higher project popularity.

This paper has employed a quantitative approach to examine the relationships proposed in TCR over time. Herein, we call future research to conduct qualitative studies in order to investigate the way through which OSS projects integrate, develop and organize their competencies in order to attain success.

The non-signi<sup>fi</sup>cant moderating role of project category on the proposed relationships demonstrated that the results of this study have no significant differences across the 3 categories from which we sampled projects (i.e. communication, software development and scienti<sup>fi</sup>c/engineering). This means that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings could be generalized across these 3 project categories. Even though the selection of projects from 3 categories that show different extremes in terms of range of use and range of users makes us see no reason that the <sup>fi</sup>ndings cannot be generalized across all project categories, our ability to generalize the <sup>fi</sup>ndings is limited to the categories from which we sampled projects.

We acknowledge that there are some limitations on the use of Sourceforge data (Crowston & Scozzi, 2002) to test our hypotheses, including: (i) Use of the number of defects/features assigned as measures of marshalling competencies. Sometimes a defect or feature is assigned after it is actually completed just to get credit for a developer, or some defects/features are self-assigned, or some defects/features are completed by someone but for some reason the assignee has not been indicated in the tracking system; (ii) Use of the number of features closed. Even though Sourceforge provides various statuses including Open, Close, Deleted and Postponed for projects to use when updating a feature request, some projects simply allocate a “Closed” status to a feature that they meant to delete; (iii) Sometimes, developers simply close a defect or feature request without addressing it. The reason might be that it doesn't interest them, or they don't have enough knowledge to address it, or simply because it is time consuming. To test our hypotheses, we recognized available variables that offer an indication of each construct, but acknowledge that these measures are not perfect due to the above-mentioned limitations.

As mentioned before, Sourceforge is not the only OSS hosting sites. Github, for example, is another OSS host that has recently surpassed Sourceforge in terms of total number of code commits (Finley, 2011). During the period of January to May 2011, Github was found to have 1,153,059 commits, whereas Sourceforge had 624,989 (Finley, 2011). Given this, it should be stated that the results of this paper cannot be generalized to the OSS projects hosted on OSS hosting websites other than Sourceforge.

Every OSS project has limited resources in terms of developers' time and effort, thus project developers are not normally able to address all the requested defects and features. That is where defect/feature selection and prioritization come to the play and show its signi<sup>fi</sup>cance. Therefore, taking task prioritization into account is important when studying the defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement processes of OSS projects. This research initially sought to address this, however, we noticed that the majority of the OSS projects hosted on Sourceforge fail to properly adopt the feature that Sourceforge offers to them to manage their task prioritization. On the other hand, excluding those projects which have not used this feature would have left us with a handful of projects which would not be enough for the purpose and statistical analysis of this study. We acknowledge this as one of the limitations of this paper, and call for future qualitative research that conducts multiple case studies and deeply study few OSS projects taking into account of task prioritization for their defect-<sup>fi</sup>xing and feature-enhancement.
