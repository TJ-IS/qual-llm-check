# Organizational adoption of supply chain management system: A multi-theoretic investigation

Qing Cao ⁎, Qiwei Gan, Mark A. Thompson

Area of Information Systems and Quantitative Sciences, Rawls College of Business, Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX 79409-2101, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 18 May 2012 Received in revised form 11 October 2012 Accepted 9 February 2013 Available online 27 February 2013

Keywords: Supply chain management Network externality Task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t Adoption Business process

## a b s t r a c t

Supply chain management (SCM) systems have many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to <sup>fi</sup>rms, including minimizing the bullwhip effect, maximizing the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of activities, reducing inventories, lowering cycle times, and achieving an acceptable level of quality. The key to realizing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts is the sharing of information among members of supply chain network via SCM systems. We survey more than 200 <sup>fi</sup>rms and the results show that <sup>fi</sup>rms tend to adopt SCM systems if they <sup>fi</sup>t their major business processes and that there is a network externality to adopting such systems. Moreover, the aforementioned two forces interact with each other as the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SCM system will be ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the supply chain network as the number of system users increases. Likewise, the problems of SCM system caused by the mis<sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and major business processes will be exacerbated as the number of users in the network increases.

## 1. Introduction

Supply chain management (SCM) has drawn increasing attention from business researchers since the 1980s [42]. Supply chain is a network of organizations involved in moving a product or service from suppliers to customers [39]. As such, organizations in the network exchange information to coordinate the production and distribution of goods and services. The prosperity of each organization in the network is connected and interdependent. In fact, the competitive advantage and value of individual organization in the network are embedded in the resources and capabilities of both the individual organization and the whole supply chain network [32,41]. Unfortunately, information <sup>fl</sup>ow in the network may also be problematic. Many supply chain networks have seen incomplete, inaccurate, distorted, and/or delayed information that in turn leads to poor and uncoordinated decisions and results in bloated inventories or stock-outs [52,60], long product life cycles, system-wide inef<sup>fi</sup>ciencies, and the bullwhip effect [35,54]. To tackle these problems and improve the performance of the whole network, effective SCM is needed.

Many <sup>fi</sup>rms have been using IT-based SCM systems to manage their supply chain network. A SCM system is a key enabler of effective SCM [12], which provides information and information processing capability to support the strategy, operations, and decision-making functions for the <sup>fi</sup>rm's supply chain network [56]. The bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of such SCM systems include minimizing the bullwhip effect, maximizing the ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency of activities, reducing inventories, lowering cycle times, and achieving an acceptable level of quality [37]. Although SCM system functionalities vary across <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., advance planning and scheduling, capacity planning, e-procurement, order management, distribution resource planning), all SCM systems have the capabilities of timely interorganizational information sharing and exchange. However, <sup>fi</sup>rms in a supply chain network are often independent economic entities with different organizational objectives. Each <sup>fi</sup>rm makes its own decision to optimize its own interest rather than that of the whole supply chain network. SCM system is indeed an enterprise-wide information system and the adoption and use of it are determined by individual <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. The more bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from the SCM system to the individual <sup>fi</sup>rms as well as to the whole supply chain network will be realized when more <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt and use SCM systems. This phenomenon is referred to as positive network externality [26]. However, does this mean when some <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt SCM system, other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network need to follow suit to adopt SCM system leading to improved performance of the supply chain network? The fact that SCM systems are heralded with so many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for <sup>fi</sup>rms, the consequences of SCM system adoptions can be unpredictable [64] as many SCM system design and implementations have failed over recent years [56]. Thus, this uncertainty impedes <sup>fi</sup>rms from adopting or inef<sup>fi</sup>cient implementation of SCM systems [30].

Most studies on organizational adoption of SCM systems draw upon institutional theories, socio-political theories, and organizational theories [2,40,65]. While these existing studies provide insights on the factors affecting the <sup>fi</sup>rms' decisions on adopting different types of information systems, they do not examine the supply chain network along with the <sup>fi</sup>t between major business processes and the functionalities of the SCM systems. However, the major business processes involved in SCM differ among the <sup>fi</sup>rms within the supply chain network. For example, a retailer's major business processes may be the sales of product and inventory management, while a manufacturer's major business processes may be production planning or procurement management. Likewise, the functionalities and characteristics of SCM systems of different <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network may be different resulting in a mis<sup>fi</sup>t between the major business processes and characteristics of SCM system. In the literature of technology adoption, the mis<sup>fi</sup>t of technology characteristics and <sup>fi</sup>rms' business processes is attributed to the inadequate adoption of the technology [13,21,58]. However, these studies did not consider the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and the major business processes of the <sup>fi</sup>rm and its effect on the adoption of SCM systems.

This paper addresses the gaps in current theory related to the adoption of SCM system and how SCM systems may be inadequate even though it has many bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network [30]. We pay particular attention to the characteristics of SCM system and the major business processes involved in SCM practice by incorporating network externality into task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t (TTF) theory. TTF explains the <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology characteristics and its effect on the performance of the task [19]. The combination of network externality and TTF is not additive, but one where we consider the interactions of the underlying factors between the two theories. In our framework, the network externality moderates the relationships between TTF and the adoption of SCM. In other words, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SCM system when it <sup>fi</sup>ts the major business processes will be ampli<sup>fi</sup>ed in the supply chain network as the number of SCM system users in the network increases. In the same token, the problems of SCM system caused by the mis<sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and major business processes will be exacerbated as the number of users in the network increases.

This paper makes two key contributions to the literature. First, it considers the heterogeneity of the major business processes involved in SCM of <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network and of the characteristics of SCM systems. Both <sup>fi</sup>rms and SCM systems are different in the supply chain network, even though the <sup>fi</sup>rms are dependent on each other and SCM systems are of the same type of IT innovation with common characteristics. The organizational perception of the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and business processes of the <sup>fi</sup>rm differs due to this heterogeneity and hence the adoption decisions differ accordingly. Second, it reveals the interaction between network externality and the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM systems and business processes of <sup>fi</sup>rms regarding the adoption of SCM systems. As such, this paper <sup>fi</sup>lls a void in the existing literature by contributing to the decision-making of organizations in adopting SCM system. It suggests that <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network should not follow suit blindly when others adopt SCM system; nevertheless, they need to examine if the SCM system <sup>fi</sup>ts their major business processes.

The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we review the theoretical background on SCM system adoption to develop our hypotheses. Then, we describe our empirical research methodology including sample description, measures, instrument validation, and statistical model. Finally, results are presented and discussed along with limitations of the research and possibilities for future research.

## 2. Theoretical background and hypothesis development

SCM is de<sup>fi</sup>ned in this paper as “the integration of key business processes from end user through original supplier that provides products, services, and information that add value for customers and other stakeholders” (Global Supply Chain Forum as reported by Lambert et al. [33, p. 1]). SCM system is a key enabler of effective SCM and has been used in many <sup>fi</sup>rms, which is an enterprise-wide information system providing information and information processing capability to integrate SCM within or across organizational boundaries [38,56]. Many studies report the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SCM systems to the <sup>fi</sup>rm [8,59]. For example, Dehning et al. [12] <sup>fi</sup>nd that “SCM systems increase gross margin, inventory turnover, market share, and return on sales, and reduce selling, general, and administrative expenses” (p. 806). Hendricks et al. [24] <sup>fi</sup>nd that the adopters of SCM systems have (on average) positive stock returns and improved pro<sup>fi</sup>tability. In addition, SCM systems are also found to improve <sup>fl</sup>exibility, lower transaction cost and enable effective customer services [49].

SCM systems vary in different <sup>fi</sup>rms with many functionalities ranging from operational functions (e.g., barcoding) to tactical functions (e.g., warehouse management) to strategic functions (e.g., collaborative planning forecasting and replenishment) [2]. However, SCM systems have a common characteristic: the capability of exchanging and sharing information between (among) other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network. This network characteristic of SCM systems is rarely mentioned in the existing studies on the adoption of SCM system. Many studies focus on the individual <sup>fi</sup>rm in the supply chain network. For example, Ke et al. [30] investigate how power, trust and institutional pressure interact and affect the adoption SCM systems. Liu et al. [40] also look at institutional pressures on the adoption of SCM system while exploring more about three dimensions of institutional pressures and moderating effects of organizational culture. In addition, these studies failed to incorporate the subtle nature that different <sup>fi</sup>rms are using SCM systems of different functionalities and thus the adoption decisions may differ. We examine the relationship between both network characteristic of SCM systems and the heterogeneity of the business processes of <sup>fi</sup>rms and functionalities of SCM system, and the adoption of SCM system through our research hypotheses in the following sections.

## 2.1. Network externality and SCM system adoption

Network externality or network effect refers to the situation that the value of an innovation depends on the number of previous adopters of that innovation [26,27]. Telephone is an example of a network externality in that as the number of people who install telephones increases, the value of the telephone increases. The network effect can either be a direct or indirect network effect. Direct network effect refers to the value increases as the number of adopters increases; whereas, an indirect network effect refers to the value of innovation increases when there is a greater availability of its complementary goods [4,15]. For example, the value of a computer increases as more computer software becomes available. Other (technology) examples that illustrate network effects include, for example, instant messaging [63], computer networks [23], electronic data interchange [9], electronic billing system [1], RFID [29,62], and interorganizational systems [51,67]. These technologies have a common characteristic that they used over a network. “Technologies that are subject to network externalities have unique characteristics and traditional explanations of innovation diffusion do not accommodate those characteristics” [28].

In supply chain studies, Setia et al. [53] investigate the network adoption of SCM systems on SCM agility and <sup>fi</sup>nd that there will be greater SCM agility as more <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network adopt SCM systems. While they only investigated direct network effects with respect to SCM agility, they did not examine the relationship between the adoption of SCM system and the network effect. In fact, as discussed earlier, SCM system has the capabilities of timely and ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently sharing and exchanging information with other <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network. The shared information is the key for <sup>fi</sup>rms to make informed decisions regarding the management of the supply chain. For example, if a retailer has a SCM system with order management functions that processes order information in real time, then the real time order information may be very valuable for the manufacturer. However, if the manufacturer does not have a SCM system that can receive and process real time order information, then the value of retailer's SCM system is con<sup>fi</sup>ned to only the retailer. On the other hand, if both <sup>fi</sup>rms have the SCM system, real time order information can be shared by both <sup>fi</sup>rms expanding the value of the

SCM systems to both <sup>fi</sup>rms. Similarly, if a supplier in their network also has a SCM system that receives and processes real time order information, the values of SCM systems are increased even more. In other words, the more <sup>fi</sup>rms using SCM systems, the more likely a <sup>fi</sup>rm in their network will adopt SCM system, which would suggest the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1a. The higher level of perceived direct network effect is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

In addition, when more <sup>fi</sup>rms are using SCM system, the costs of software applications, middleware, and hardware for the SCM system should decline as the size of the market increases due to economies of scale, competition and technological development. Due to these indirect network effects, <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to adopt SCM systems. For example, when more <sup>fi</sup>rms use RFID technology in their supply chain network, the cost of RFID chips will decline due to economies of scale resulting in <sup>fi</sup>rms being more likely to adopt SCM systems that can process RFID information. Therefore, we develop the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1b. The higher level of perceived indirect network effect is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

## 2.2. Task-technology fit and SCM system adoption

TTF postulates that if the technology <sup>fi</sup>ts the requirement of the task, it will improve the performance of the task [19,21]. In other words, a technology will be used well if, and only if, the functions of the technology can support the needs of the task [20]. The <sup>fi</sup>t is attributed to both task characteristics and technology characteristics. In Goodhue and Thompson [21], tasks are “broadly de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the actions carried out by individuals in turning inputs into outputs” (p. 216) when they examine the individual performance. TTF has been extended to examine group and organizational performance. For example, TTF has also been used to explain user adoption of technology by combining such IS models like Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [14]. TTF has been used to explain the adoption of various technologies including group decision support system [13], high speed data services [47], technology-mediated distance education [46], mobile commerce [36], mobile banking [66], mobile location systems [25], to name a few. With its broad use. TTF has the potential to explain technology adoption in this context.

Furthermore, TTF has also been used in a variety of supply chain studies. Setia et al. [53] investigate the TTF effect on supply chain agility and <sup>fi</sup>nd that better <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and SCM task resulted in greater supply chain agility; however, they do not examine the relationship between the <sup>fi</sup>t and the adoption of SCM system. In organizations, tasks can be referred to as a business process, which is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a set of activities or tasks to produce a speci<sup>fi</sup>c output [11]. For the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the supply chain network, their major business processes regarding the management of supply chain include advance planning and scheduling, capacity planning, e-procurement, order management, distribution resource planning, to name a few. One of the keys to these business processes is the information and information processing capabilities shared among <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network that are enabled by SCM systems. How well SCM systems meet the requirements of these business processes impacts <sup>fi</sup>rm performance. The adoption of SCM system can be explained by the intention of the <sup>fi</sup>rm to improve performance through the <sup>fi</sup>t between business processes and SCM system. Better <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and major business processes of <sup>fi</sup>rms has positive impacts on <sup>fi</sup>rm performance and thus <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to adopt SCM systems.

Goodhue and Thompson [21] designed detailed measures of TTF along eight broad dimensions: data quality, locatability, timeliness, usability, ease of use, authorization, compatibility, and system reliability. These dimensions measure the degree of <sup>fi</sup>t between task and technology. In the current study, each dimension measures how SCM systems meet the requirements of major business processes regarding the management of the supply chain. For example, if the order data provided by the SCM system is up to date (real time in some instances) as required by inventory management of the <sup>fi</sup>rm, the quality of data is high and it facilitates the inventory management of the <sup>fi</sup>rm. In other words, the SCM system <sup>fi</sup>ts well with the business process of inventory management and <sup>fi</sup>rm performance is enhanced. Therefore, the <sup>fi</sup>rm is more likely to adopt the SCM system if it enables good data quality. The rationale also applies to other dimensions. Goodhue and Thompson used a number of dimensions in a series of studies based on different contexts [19–21]. In this study, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that several dimensions of TTF (data quality, locatability, timeliness, and usability) re<sup>fl</sup>ect a speci<sup>fi</sup>c aspect (information quality) of the SCM system. Information quality is proposed as a distinct construct of information system research [43]. As such, we combine data quality, locatability, and usability into information quality, which captures a unique characteristic of the SCM system. Our analyses of different dimensions of the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and the major business processes of the <sup>fi</sup>rm provide the framework for the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2a. The higher level of perceived overall SCM information quality is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

Hypothesis 2b. The higher level of perceived ease of use of SCM system is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

Hypothesis 2c. The higher level of perceived SCM system reliability is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

Hypothesis 2d. The higher level of perceived authorization of SCM information quality is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

Hypothesis 2e. The higher level of perceived compatibility of SCM information quality is positively associated with the adoption of SCM system.

## 2.3. Interaction between TTF and network effects

Network effect and the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and the major business processes of the <sup>fi</sup>rm are not two independent forces driving the adoption of SCM system. The two forces interact with each other. Network effect moderates the relationship between TTF dimensions and the adoption of SCM system, which serves as an ampli<sup>fi</sup>er. In other words, the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and the major business processes of the <sup>fi</sup>rm will be increased if more <sup>fi</sup>rms are using SCM system. Therefore, if a <sup>fi</sup>rm believes there is a good <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and business processes and also see many adopters of SCM system in its network, they will be more likely to adopt the SCM system. For example, if the data quality of the SCM system is high, up to date and accurate data will be shared among <sup>fi</sup>rms in the network. The value from sharing this up to date and accurate data will increase for the individual <sup>fi</sup>rm and the whole network as more <sup>fi</sup>rms use the SCM system to process data for decision making. On the other hand, the problems caused by mis<sup>fi</sup>t will be exacerbated in the network as more <sup>fi</sup>rms use the SCM system [34]. For example, incomplete or inaccurate order data from the SCM system will cause serious information distortion as more <sup>fi</sup>rms use the SCM system. Therefore, a <sup>fi</sup>rm will be less likely to adopt the SCM system. The same applies to the indirect network effect on the relationship between TTF dimensions and the adoption of SCM system, suggesting the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3a. The higher level of perceived direct network effect moderates the relationships between TTF dimensions and the adoption of SCM system.

Hypothesis 3b. The higher level of perceived indirect network effect moderates the relationships between TTF dimensions and the adoption of SCM system.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sample

The organization is the unit of analysis in this study and, as such, the subjects are IT decision makers within the organization such as the CIO, CTO, or the Director of IT. An initial sample of <sup>fi</sup>rms for inclusion in this study was randomly selected from the 2007 North American Industry Classi<sup>fi</sup>cation System (NAICS) Manual. A qualifying factor in their selection was their intensive use of their supply chain as a critical success factor in their operations. An additional qualifying factor was that a <sup>fi</sup>rm had to utilize SCM to support supply chain activities and each was a part of a supply chain. A total of 1000 questionnaires were distributed in a single mailing in the US. Out of 246 responses, 207 were usable, resulting in an actual response rate of 20.7%. The unusable surveys were those that did not contain suf<sup>fi</sup>cient data for further analysis. These response rates are not unusual when the unit of analysis is the <sup>fi</sup>rm and the questionnaire involves extensive organizational level questions [22].

To examine possible non-response bias, the companies that responded were compared with non-responding companies. Comparison of the distributions of company size (i.e., the number of employees) showed no statistically signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences at the p b 0.10 level [17]. Descriptive statistics for our sample appear in Table 1.

## 3.2. Questionnaire construction and operationalization of constructs

The TTF construct measures are adapted from scales used in Goodhue and Thompson [21]. There are eight dimensions in task-technology <sup>fi</sup>t including data quality, locatability, timeliness, usability, ease of use, authorization, compatibility and system reliability. Since data quality, locatability, timeliness, and usability re-<sup>fl</sup>ect the information quality, we combine them into one single construct. The validity and reliability checks on questionnaire items also support our combination of these four dimensions as they have a high convergent validity, a high reliability, and are clearly distinguished from other dimensions (Table 3). The perceived value of a network has been explored in various studies [18,28] and perceptual measures have been developed [48,67] of which several were used [55]. A total of 21 items measure the constructs in our proposed SCM adoption model. Control variables in our study include <sup>fi</sup>rm size, department size, and industry.

## 3.3. Instrument validation

In this study, overall instrument validity is assessed by evaluating the results of content validity, criterion-related validity, convergent validity, construct validity, and reliability tests [5,57]. The survey questionnaire used in this study was based on innovation and organizational adoption literature, as well as, enterprise systems research. These topics cover each of the major content areas of this study. As we noted earlier, the preliminary questionnaires were sent to and examined by a panel of IT experts in supply chain organizations. The questionnaire was then modi<sup>fi</sup>ed based on the input from the panel of experts. Content validity was thus established by de<sup>fi</sup>ning the topic of concern, describing items to be scaled, developing the scales to be used, and using a panel of experts to judge the quality of the instrument [10].

Criterion-related validity is the degree to which the survey instrument correlates with one or more criteria. The expected cross validity index (ECVI) is one measure for criterion-related validity [31]. The ECVI values of all constructs (largest being 0.90) in this research are well below the value of 1 that has been described as “adequate”. This study employs con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis (CFA) in LISREL to test the unidimensionality of the constructs because CFA is deemed to be a better technique for assessing unidimensionality than exploratory factor analysis (EFA) [3,45]. These CFA loading results were in the moderate-to-high range (0.71–0.90). Moreover, t-values for scale items ranged from 6.29 to 15.41, exceeding the 2.0 rule of thumb. As a result, all loadings for scale items in Table 3 were signi<sup>fi</sup>cant (p b 0.01).

Table 4 shows the summary of the CFA measures of the conceptual model. The RMSEA (0.058), λ<sup>2</sup> (signi<sup>fi</sup>cant at level of 0.01), and λ<sup>2</sup>/df (2.34) values meet the requirements for good <sup>fi</sup>t. All GFI, AGFI, CFI, and IFI values also exceeded the minimum cutoff value of 0.90. The Critical N of 83 was lower than the sample size indicating that the conceptual model is a good <sup>fi</sup>t. All constructs and scale items used in this study thus meet the requirements for adequate validity.

## 3.4. Scale reliability

Cronbach's alphas were calculated for all constructs and dimensions in the conceptual model [16,61]. Cronbach's alpha is the most widely used method of reliability assessment in information systems research [7]. The Cronbach's alpha values in Table 3 that all exceed the suggested value of 0.70 were generally considered adequate for assessing reliability in empirical research [44]. The scale items used in this research can thus be considered reliable.

## 3.5. The structural model

Structural equation modeling (SEM) allows us to assess the network effect (direct and indirect) on SCM system adoption (H1) and the effects of TTF on SCM system adoption (H2). In each case, our statistical results support our hypothesized positive relationships. Coef<sup>fi</sup>cients and p-values are reported in Table 5.

To estimate the effects of interacting variables, we employed a two-step estimation approach [50]. The <sup>fi</sup>rst step of the approach involves calculating the loading and error variance for the single indicator of the latent product using measurement model parameter estimates. The second step <sup>fi</sup>xes the loading and error variances at their calculated values in the structural model. For example in our study, to test the interaction of information quality with direct network externalities, we <sup>fi</sup>rst centered the three variables, (1) information quality of SCM system, (2) direct network externalities, and (3) the intent to adopt by replacing their raw scores with deviation scores. In the second step, we added up scores of each latent variable to form a latent product. Then, the single indicator of the latent product (interaction) was formed by multiplying the summated scores for both bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of SCM system and direct network externalities.

## 5. Implications

This paper investigates organizational adoption of SCM system and reveals that network externality has a positive relationship with <sup>fi</sup>rms' intention to adopt SCM systems, and the <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM system and the major business processes of <sup>fi</sup>rms also has a positive relationship with the <sup>fi</sup>rm's intent to adopt SCM system. In addition, network externality moderates the relationship between the <sup>fi</sup>t and <sup>fi</sup>rms' intent to adopt SCM system. Among <sup>fi</sup>ve TTF dimensions, there is evidence that the relationships of three dimensions (i.e., information quality, ease of use, and system reliability) with intention to adopt are moderated by network externality.

These <sup>fi</sup>ndings have both theoretical and practical implications. First, existing studies do not consider network externality when examining adoption of SCM system. While this paper reveals the importance of direct and indirect network externalities in the adoption of SCM systems, it also acknowledges the variations of business processes of <sup>fi</sup>rms and functionalities of SCM systems. These variations may lead to different degrees of <sup>fi</sup>t between SCM systems and business processes, which may be one reason why <sup>fi</sup>rms have not adopted SCM even while observing other <sup>fi</sup>rms' adoption (or use) of SCM system. In fact, <sup>fi</sup>rms should not follow suit simply because other <sup>fi</sup>rms in their supply chain network have adopted SCM systems. Firms need to pay particular attention to whether the SCM system <sup>fi</sup>ts their business processes. If the SCM system <sup>fi</sup>ts its business processes well, the value of the SCM system will increase as more <sup>fi</sup>rms adopt and use the system. On the other hand, if the SCM system does not <sup>fi</sup>t the business processes, the mis<sup>fi</sup>t will be deteriorated through the network. This mis<sup>fi</sup>t may explain why many SCM system implementations have failed.

This multi-theoretic examination increases our understanding of SCM system adoption. As Burgess et al. [6] pointed out “for the <sup>fi</sup>eld of SCM, the extent to which theories have been developed appears to be slight” (p. 711) and “if theory development was more evenly spread, then one would expect to see … multi-theoretic approaches used” (p. 717), we strive to develop theories in SCM <sup>fi</sup>eld by incorporating a multi-theoretic perspective in this study, namely TTF and network externality, to the adoption of SCM systems.

## 6. Limitations and future research

One limitation of this study is that it is based on a cross-sectional design. Data were collected from diverse business types across various industries. However, the cross-sectional design is limited and does not eliminate all the external factors in obtaining industry-speci<sup>fi</sup>c information. Although the response rate of our survey is acceptable, increasing the sample size could add to power and contribution of the study. Therefore, we suggest that future research continues to explore both individual categories of industries as well as a broader collection of industries within this research framework.

Another limitation is that all the measuring instruments used in this research were based on manager's perceptions. While this is a valid operational process for measuring various constructs, all questionnaire surveys are limited by the truthfulness of the respondents. However, the validation and reliability analyses undertaken in this study provided some level of assurance of the instrument's ability to capture useful measures.

This study extends prior research on diffusion of innovation by providing a framework to identify appropriate theories for researching various types of innovations. While the framework can be a useful guide for identifying relevant adoption research, it is principally focused on software and IS studies. Since the table is a presentation of theories, we believe that its applicability to operations management and other subject areas is not a major limitation. In that regard, our empirical study in SCM systems adoption has sought to illustrate its general usefulness in operations management. We also suggest combining network externalities with other theories (e.g., Technology Organization and Environment framework) that may empower other models in the research domain to produce interesting and useful results for various types of innovations.
