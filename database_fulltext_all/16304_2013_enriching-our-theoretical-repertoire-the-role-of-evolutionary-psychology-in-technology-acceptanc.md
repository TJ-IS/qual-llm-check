# Enriching our theoretical repertoire: the role of evolutionary psychology in technology acceptance

Chon Abraham<sup>1</sup>, Marie-Claude Boudreau<sup>2</sup>, Iris Junglas<sup>3</sup> and Richard Watson<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Mason School of Business, College of William & Mary, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, U.S.A.; <sup>3</sup>Accenture Research Institute for High Performance, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Marie-Claude Boudreau, MIS Department, Terry College of Business, University of Georgia, 314 Brooks Hall, Athens, GA 30602, U.S.A. Tel.: þ 1 7 06 583 0887; E-mail: mcboudre@terry.uga.edu

## Abstract

Information systems (IS) research has drawn heavily on social and cognitive psychology to explain technology adoption. Indeed, the many variations of the technology acceptance model all share these same theoretical foundations. Focusing exclusively on the socio-cognitive lens can lead to overlooking enhanced explanations of technology acceptance, such that new theoretical perspectives may be warranted. In this qualitative grounded theory study, we discovered how the lens of evolutionary psychology, as embodied in the Four-Drive model, was helpful in understanding technology acceptance across three organizational sites. We contend that evolutionary psychology is an important addition to the theoretical repertoire of IS researchers, and propose including ‘evolved psychological mechanisms’ within traditional models of technology acceptance.

## Introduction

Humans are evolved, social, and cognitive beings who have long sought technologies that extend their capabilities to process information and support their work practices (Abraham et al., 2009). In the information systems (IS) field, we have primarily based explanations of human behavior towards technology on tenets from social and cognitive psychology, specifically, the seminal Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) and its extension, the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) (Ajzen, 1991). These theories define decision-making as a social-cognitive process, which is the lens that a lot of IS researchers have used to explain technology acceptance. This approach has resulted in many powerful technology acceptance models, including the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology model (Venkatesh et al., 2003), which explains as much as 70% of the variance in technology acceptance.

Without under-estimating the power of such models, the field of social and cognitive psychology has called for a more in-depth understanding about the formulation of individual reactions to behaviors (Ajzen, 1991; Kenrick et al., 2006). Social cognitive theorists (e.g., Ajzen, 1991) assert that there is imperfect knowledge about the origins of cognitive and social constructs, like attitudes or social norms, and encourage research to understand why intentions do not always lead to expected behavior. Focusing only on cognition and ignoring the role of human nature can lead to omitting fascinating, and potentially integrating, explanations (Kaplan, 1992). Accordingly, many researchers recognize the need for additional work to develop a coherent, and potentially more complete, model to explain human behavior (Krantz, 1987; Greenwald et al., 2002; Trope, 2004).

In this context, and as we discovered through this research, evolutionary psychology has the potential to provide an alternative theoretical lens for understanding the influence of human nature on behavior (Cosmides et al., 1992; Buss, 2004; Kock, 2009). Evolutionary psychology posits that evolved psychological mechanisms are innate, encoded skill sets that became embedded in the brains of our ancestors. These mechanisms promote the necessary behavioral responses that ensured survival and reproduction, such as gaining status, forming social coalitions, protecting oneself, selecting a mate, appeasing curiosity, recognizing danger, and the need for communication (i.e., language) (Cosmides et al., 1992; Buss, 1995, 1996; Pinker, 1997). Evolved psychological mechanisms guide the formulation of perceptions, problem resolutions, and adaptation to local environments as part of ‘human nature’ (Cosmides et al., 1992; Buss, 1995, 1996; Pinker, 1997; Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). When combined with a socio-cognitive lens, evolutionary psychology (and its associated evolved psychological mechanisms) promises to help better understand IS-related phenomena (Kock, 2009). Given the vast interest in technology acceptance among IS researchers and the limitations of the traditional technology acceptance models (Bagozzi, 2007), this research led us to propose the use of evolutionary psychology as a complementary lens to investigate technology acceptance.

This paper is structured as follows. First, we provide an overview of technology acceptance models and their foundations in social and cognitive psychology, and explain our quest for the discovery of novel facts regarding technology acceptance. Next, we describe the methodological details of the qualitative grounded theory approach that we adopted. Also, given that it was during the analysis of our data that we considered evolutionary psychology, it is at that point of the paper that we further explain this stream of literature and also present a particular model (i.e., the Four-Drive model) that helped us make sense of the studied phenomenon. This is then followed by our findings, which we frame according to the aforementioned Four-Drive model. Lastly, we discuss our findings and propose augmenting technology acceptance models with the inclusion of evolved psychological mechanisms. We conclude by emphasizing the usefulness of evolutionary psychology and its possible manifestations in other areas of research, along with discussing the value of our findings for practitioners.

## Background: the limits of technology acceptance models

Theories that explain technology acceptance and adoption decisions have a longstanding tradition in the IS field. In fact, most models can be traced back to one seminal theory that originated from social and cognitive psychology: the TRA (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Over the years, the TRA has been a springboard for a succession of technology acceptance theories. Some of these are considered extensions, such as the TPB (Ajzen, 1991) and its variations (Taylor & Todd, 1995b); others incorporate base constructs from TRA, such as the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) (Davis, 1989), the Motivational Model (Davis et al., 1992), the Model of PC Utilization (Thompson et al., 1991), Innovation Diffusion Theory (Moore & Benbasat, 1991), and the Social Cognitive Theory (Compeau & Higgins, 1995) – to name a few. Lately, the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology model has emerged as an integrative model (Venkatesh et al., 2003).

Technology acceptance models share the same theoretical skeleton, as illustrated in Figure 1. All models implicitly assume that human behavior relative to technology is the result of social (e.g., social influence) and cognitive processes (e.g., performance and effort expectancy). We acknowledge the distinction between cognition and affect, as demonstrated in psychology (Bodur et al., 2000) and as surfaced in later technology acceptance models. For the simplicity of argument, we subsume affect as part of cognition.

TRA, for example, proposes that an individual’s reaction to using a technology derives from cognitive processes that entail an appraisal of the technology with regards to certain outcomes (e.g., job performance), and the perceived social pressure of using this technology (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975). Researchers have applied versions of the preceding generic structure across domains to amass knowledge about how individuals behave in certain situations (Madden et al., 1992). These phenomenon-oriented explanations, as some critics call them (Buss, 1995), led researchers to include a wider range of factors in their models. For example, extensions to the TPB (Fishbein & Ajzen, 1975) include belief saliency, past behavior and habit, self-efficacy, moral norms, selfidentity, affect (Conner & Armitage, 1998), and selfconstrual (Park & Levine, 1999). Proposed extensions for the TAM (Davis, 1989) include factors such as prior experience (Taylor & Todd, 1995a), innovation characteristics (Agarwal & Prasad, 1997), social influence and cognitive instrumental processes (Venkatesh & Davis,

Relying on cognition as the underlying tenet of an individual’s reaction to technology implicitly postulates some assumptions. First, it posits a certain level of technology determinism (Kock, 2004; Bagozzi, 2007), that is, an individual reacts to technology and technology related factors only, and few non-technology-related factors are considered to have an impact. Second. it assumes that individual cognitive processing skills (which typically equate to ‘rational’ decision making) determine adoption (Beaudry & Pinsonneault, 2005), and any ‘irrational’ behavior is by default not explainable (Cosmides & Tooby, 1994; Tooby & Cosmides, 2007). In addition to these restrictive assumptions, the current technology adoption models may be too limited in what they include. Prominent IS scholars have encouraged research that investigates alternative theoretical perspectives when studying technology acceptance (Venkatesh et al., 2007), with a plea for broadening the field’s understanding of acceptance toward a wider constellation of behavioral responses and their psychologica counterparts (Schwarz & Chin, 2007). Along those lines, it has been argued that reflection on the future of TAM is long overdue (Hirscheim, 2007), and that it is debatable whether extending TAM to accommodate new phenomena might be reflective of a ‘degenerative’ rather than a ‘progressive’ research program (Silva, 2007). In a pro gressive research program, theory leads to the discovery of hitherto unknown novel facts, whereas in a degenerating research program, theories are fabricated to accommodate known facts (Lakotas, 1978). In other words, TAM may be a theoretical anchor encouraging research of what is 'true' to the detriment of what is 'new' (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). The move towards a progressive research program on technology acceptance, one that would encourage the discovery of novel facts. requires us to focus on alternative theoretical perspectives to those that dominate today’s research (Hirscheim, 2007). Like others before us, we posit that we ought to expand our repertoire of factors impacting individual behaviors during technology acceptance; solely relying on social and cognitive processes may be overly restrictive and potentially the source of fundamental misunderstanding of the human condition (Dixon & Massey, 1969). A new perspective that provides greater explanatory power is needed (Bagozzi, 2007).

Accordingly, the objective of our study was to uncover novel factors contributing to the acceptance of technology by individuals, above and beyond those suggested by the social and cognitive lenses. To achieve this objective, we needed to select a specific technology acceptance phenomenon and to design research that would ultimately help us either develop a new theoretical account of such a phenomenon (Martin & Turner, 1986) or elaborate on an already existing one (Strauss & Corbin, 1994; Suddaby, 2006).

## Research approach

## Phenomenon

We elected to focus on technology acceptance and use in a non-business related context, that is, healthcare. Such setting has indeed been suggested as a fertile ground to uncover new concepts (Benbasat & Barki, 2007). We also choose to study the adoption and use of mobile information communication technologies (MICT) as this was a fairly novel technology that was at the early stage of adoption by healthcare professionals within hospitals (Moore, 1996). Thus, the phenomenon we investigated was MICT acceptance and use in hospital settings.

## Research methodology

We framed our research within the post-positivist paradigm tradition (Guba & Lincoln, 1994). We used an inductive approach, and accordingly, did not decide on theories to test or to incorporate prior to our data collection and analysis. Rather, relevant theories were investigated as data were analyzed. Our approach was similar to the one adopted in other inductive qualitative inquiries (e.g., Barrett & Walsham, 1999; Boudreau & Robey, 2005) in that we entered the field with some knowledge of potentially relevant literature (mainly, the social-cognitive factors related to technology acceptance and use). As we progressed in the analysis of our data, we then considered additional literature to help in the understanding and framing of the phenomenon.

A grounded theory research methodology was applied (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1990, 1997; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). More specifically, the ‘Straussian’ version of grounded theory was used (Locke, 2001). This version allows for the consideration of literature, along with personal and professional experiences, to guide data analysis (Suddaby, 2006; Strauss & Corbin, 1994). This version aligned with our intention to either create a new theoretical account or to elaborate on already existing theories regarding technology acceptance and use.

## Sites selection

Two criteria guided our sites selection. First, the sites had to provide the necessary technological infrastructure encouraging nurses to employ MICT to support patient care. Second, the nurses’ work environments had to vary, so that we could discern if this variety led to various technology acceptance behaviors. A project manager at a leading MICT vendor for the healthcare industry suggested hospitals that had recently adopted MICT. Within these hospitals we selected three sites (Emergency Department (ED), Post Anesthesia Care, and Regular Acute Care Floor), which provided enough variation in the nurses’ work environments.

The ED was part of a non-profit 528-bed hospital located in the southeast United States. Patient triage was ED’s main task, and the MICT was used to standardize the documentation process for the nurses' initial assessments. ED’s triage nurses were initially motivated to adopt MICT because they needed quick access to patients’ medical history, so as to promptly provide accurate treatment. Prior to the implementation of the MICT, nurses used a combination of manually written checklists, manual data entry, and information access via a tethered computer, away from the patient’s location.

The Post Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU) was part of a smaller (124-bed), non-profit hospital also located in the southeast United States. PACU nurses cared for unconscious patients following general surgery. They dealt with patients until they regained consciousness, as they were then transported to other units in the hospital. The MICT enabled electronic charting and decreased the need for narrative documentation of the events regarding the patient. PACU nurses were initially motivated to adopt MICT because of the potential to decrease laborious manual documentation, to decrease errors, and to improve patient safety. Prior to the implementation of the MICT, nurses manually charted and entered data on a tethered computer at the nurses’ station.

Finally, a Regular Floor Unit (RF) from a 478-bed veteran hospital, also located in the southeast United States, constituted our third site. At that site, nurses recorded patient medication administration. The MICT was implemented to document every instance of patient medication and to validate that the correct medication was delivered to the intended patient. Prior to the implementation of the MICT, the basic process for medication administration entailed nurses printing a list of validated prescriptions from an IS located at the nurses’ central station, physically collecting as many medications as they were able to handle, manually indicating the time of delivery to each patient, and then returning to the tethered computer to update the electronic medical record.

At all three sites, the MICT had been part of the technological infrastructure for at least three months. The MICT was mobile (in the sense of movable) and provided ubiquitous wireless access to an IS within the boundaries of the unit. More specifically, at each site, the MICT consisted of a mobile workstation with one encased lightweight computer, supplemented by a wireless local area network operated on the 802.11b standard. The workstations were equipped with a laptop size screen and a full keyboard.

## Data collection

Data collection began in November 2002 and ended in July 2004; the first author visited each site during three different periods. The data collection process was guided by theoretical sampling, that is, the concepts that emerged from data analysis greatly influenced the choice of respondents, observations, and questions (Corbin & Strauss, 2008). For example, the emerging theme of ‘selfpreservation’ (which we will discuss in the data analysis section) prompted us in seeking to talk with nurses who had witnessed litigious situations, and to compare and contrast their acceptance of the MICT with other nurses who had not encountered such situations.

Techniques for data collection included interviews, direct observations, and document review. We conducted interviews with 35 individuals. Most were nurses, as it was them who ultimately decided to accept (or not accept) the MICT. To a lesser extent, we also focused on the managers and unit directors in order to get a sense of the expectations set by higher organizational levels. Interviews were semi-structured around questions designed to elicit respondents’ personal reactions to the technology and experiences (or expectations) with the MICT. All interviews were audio-recorded and later transcribed. They averaged 45 min for managerial personnel (i.e., directors and nurse managers) and 20 min for nurses. The initial interview questions are available in Appendix A.

We used direct observation to assess actual usage behavior so as to minimize self-report bias. There were 35 instances of direct observations of individual nurses across sites. These instances included observation of the nurses and the registration personnel while at work. Observations were interspersed between interviews to provide a holistic picture of the nurses’ task environment and to inspire new or revised interview questions (again, in the spirit of theoretical sampling). For example, after observing that nurses were repeatedly using the MICT in the hallways in one site (as opposed to at the bedside), we decided to add an open-ended question to ask why they sometimes used the system at locations other than the point of care (i.e., the intended location).

We solicited and reviewed systems documentation prior to interviews at each site to gain insights into intended usage and technical characteristics. We also asked to review systems documentation that interviewees referenced in conversations, or that we observed them using while working. We reviewed over 400 pages of written materials, which included project proposal descriptions, system requirement documents, end-user manuals, and training manuals.

Given the multiple sources underlying our data collection efforts, along with the three time periods during which we collected data, we were able to triangulate our sources and to focus on acceptance behaviors that exhibited permanency rather than transiency. Data collection (which occurred iteratively with data analysis, described next) was considered completed when theoretical saturation was obtained, that is, when no new data (from the interviews, observations, or documents) informed the emergent codes, dimensions, and themes, and when all of these were supported by ample data (Corbin & Strauss, 2008).

We summarize, in Appendix B, our data collection techniques and timelines.

## Data analysis

We started coding shortly after the beginning of our data collection efforts. We elected to use the coding paradigm typical of grounded theory research (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Accordingly, the analysis of data included three major types of coding – open, axial, and selective – with each type being at a higher, more abstract level of data analysis than the preceding one. We used Atlas.ti<sup>s</sup>, a tool to support the coding of qualitative data, to facilitate this process.

Open coding Open coding entails fracturing the data by describing concepts in the data that may define a significant occurrence, or incident, about the phenomenon. Through this type of coding, we created 39 codes related to 410 textual segments, obtained from the 35 interviews, 35 observations, and 400 þ pages of project documentation. As an example of a coded textual segment, we highlighted a portion of a nurse’s interview (i.e., ‘they can lose trust in you’) and coded this text segment as ‘trust building’. A sample of the textual segments we open coded appears in Appendix C. Our general impression, after the open coding stage, was that the phenomenon of interest, technology acceptance, was not always explained by the traditional antecedents mentioned in social (e.g., social influence) and cognitive psychology (performance and effort expectancy).

Axial coding Axial coding requires comparison of the codes to classify them under common themes, thus entailing the creation of hierarchical classifications. Although we initially followed Strauss & Corbin’s (1990) coding paradigm (i.e., causal conditions, phenomenon, context, intervening conditions, strategies, consequences), we, like Urquhart (2001), did not find this scheme particularly apt to our data, and used it instead to draw preliminary distinctions among our codes. In this process, we related and combined codes to form themes representing sources of influence towards MICT acceptance; these themes, for the most part, fell under the ‘causal conditions’ category of Strauss and Corbin’s paradigm. For example, we created a relationship between the codes of ‘trust building’ and ‘decreasing anxieties’ and later combined them to form the theme ‘establishing relationship’. Five themes, among the ones created, were most richly supported by the data and strongly represented key considerations in the nurses’ decision to accept the MICT to support their work practices. These considerations (and associated themes) included: the establishment of a good relationship with the patient (theme: ‘establishing relationship’), the gathering of information concerning the patient (theme: ‘information gathering’), the assurance of the patient’s safety (theme: ‘safety assurance’), the preservation of the nurse’s reputation as a competent worker (theme: ‘selfpresentation’), and finally, the preservation of the nurse’s job (theme: ‘self-preservation’). We also used memoing to keep track of our emerging analysis and questions related to the development of the themes and underlying codes. As suggested by Corbin & Strauss (2008) in their third edition of 'Basics of Qualitative Research'. a seminal grounded theory textbook, we opted for a relatively fluid scheme of memoing rather than the more structured one suggested in their previous editions. We present each of the five key themes, along with their underlying codes and initial related memos, in Appendix D.

Except for ‘self-preservation’, which construct was previously used by Beaudry & Pinsonneault (2005), these themes were not directly considered by the traditional social and cognitive literature related to technology acceptance. Therefore, we looked into alternative theoretical perspectives to make sense of our emerging analysis. Specifically, we first considered behavioral psychology, which suggests that behaviors are acquired through conditioning. Upon further examination, however, this lens did not seem to support the codes and themes that we had uncovered (e.g., ‘trust building’, like many other codes, was not a behavior that nurses had learned through conditioning). We then turned to a branch of psychology about which we had, at the time, limited knowledge – evolutionary psychology – and found it to have potential relevance to what we were observing. Indeed, evolutionary psychology’s main premise suggests that human behavior can be explained by appeal to internal psychological mechanisms developed through an evolutionary process (Buss, 2009); nurses, in other words, may be predisposed to behave in a certain way under certain situational cues. This insight resonated with what was emerging from our analysis and we further considered the premises of evolutionary psychology.

A useful lens– evolutionary psychology: Although theories are usually presented in the front-end of a paper (i.e., in the Literature section), given that the consideration of evolutionary psychology became relevant to us during (as opposed to before) the conduct of our research, we introduce this literature in this section of the paper. This is done in the spirit of faithfulness to the research process. Evolutionary psychology is a frame for explaining how human nature impacts human behavior. It is based on tenets from sociology, evolutionary biology, anthropology, cognitive science, and the neuroscience (Cosmides & Tooby, 2000). Evolutionary psychology assumes that hominid behavior is influenced by a set of evolved psychological mechanisms – sometimes also referred to as domain-specific programs (Cosmides & Tooby, 2000). These evolved psychological mechanisms arose as a response to repeated encounters with adaptive problems that constituted reproductive opportunities or challenges (Buss, 1995, 1996; Cosmides et al., 1992; Pinker, 1997). Adaptive problems included forming social coalitions, gaining and maintaining status, protecting oneself and valued others from threats, finding and retaining mates, or caring for family members (Buss, 1996; Pinker, 1997).

For each of these domains, mechanisms formed that did not necessarily develop in coherence with each other. Instead, each domain-specific program (as the name implies) developed in response to a particular adaptive problem and is thus functionally specialized (Cosmides & Tooby, 1994; Pinker, 1997; Tooby & Cosmides, 2007). Consequently, evolved psychological mechanisms compete on some dimensions. For example, the tendency to exhibit jealousy in mating situations counters the need to form coalitions that ensure survival and reproduction. The behavioral manifestation of any given mechanism is not fixed, but depends heavily on the environment and the cues that activate it (Kaplan, 1992). Nonetheless, we are prone to exhibit certain patterns of behavior when faced with situations that closely resemble the ones that shaped our evolved psychological mechanisms (Buss, 2004).

For tens of thousands of years, humankind was exposed to relatively unvarying or slowly varying environmental conditions (Pinker, 1997). Evolved psychological mechanisms had sufficient buffer time to adapt to the environmental demands – until recently. Indeed, over the most recent few hundred years, the environment has changed dramatically through human intervention (Hass et al., 2000). In fact, humans now determine the environment to a greater extent than the other way round. We therefore have a tendency to overlook the impact of evolved psychological mechanisms, as they are camouflaged by daily lives very remote from survival on the African Savannah 100,000 years ago (Hass et al., 2000). Evolutionary psychology posits that human nature and environment are two inseparable forces, which influence each other throughout an individual’s life (Ruth, 1993). Moreover, it does not negate the importance of cognition, but rather recognizes it as a necessary ingredient in the calibration of evolved psychological mechanisms (Cosmides & Tooby, 2000).

Selective coding It is at the last step of the coding process, that is, during selective coding, that we uncovered specific psychological mechanisms which allowed us to integrate our analysis and to portray a coherent conceptualization of the phenomenon. This integrative procedure is the substantive force in theory building or explaining phenomena (Klein & Myers, 1999; Urquhart, 2001). In that stage of the coding, we realized that the five major themes resulting from axial coding were consistent with one classification of evolved psychological mechanisms that had been suggested by contemporary academicians and applied to organizational setting: the Four-Drive model (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). Such a model, we recognized, was a powerful frame to make sense of our findings. An overview of this model is now presented. Again, for the reason mentioned in the preceding section, we introduce the Four-Drive model at this point in the paper.

The Four-Drive model: The Four-Drive model (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002) classifies evolved psychological mechanisms into four drives: the drive to acquire, the drive to bond, the drive to defend, and the drive to learn. The Four-Drive model represents a parsimonious description of human nature based on evolutionary forces. The model contends that these drives, though evolved to solve ancient adaptive problems, can influence modern human behavior as well.

The drive to acquire is a categorization of some evolved psychological mechanisms to seek status, take control, retain objects and personal experiences that humans value (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). Humankind has been driven to acquire goods that are either material, such as food, clothing, and shelter, or positional, such as social acknowledgement and recognition (Cosmides et al., 1992; Pinker, 1997). The likelihood of survival was greater for those who were more apt at acquiring material goods, since doing so elevated their social status, made them (appear) more capable of caring and providing for others, and thus increased their chances of reproductive success. As a consequence, these individuals had to continue acquiring objects (Cosmides et al., 1992) because their social status and power were based on the continued well-being of their acquired dependents and goods (Wilson, 2000).

The drive to bond represents an evolved psychological mechanism targeted at forming social relationships and develop mutual caring commitments with other humans (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). Our ancestors engaged in bonding activities to strengthen group cohesion on the inside and form coalitions against the outside. The premise is that those who bonded well had a relative advantage over those who did not. After all, establishing and maintaining groups of individuals bonded by mutual caring relationships improved the odds of surviving environmental threats (Cosmides et al., 1992; Levy, 2004). Bonding and its associated aspects, such as trust, empathy, compassion, loyalty, respect, partnership, and alliance, also manifests itself in behavioral outcomes that include altruism and establishment of moral codes regarding social relationships (Trivers, 1971; Wieselquist et al., 1999; Rusbult & Van Lange, 2003; Van Vugt & Van Lange, 2006). Interestingly, many of the strongest reactions, both positive and negative, are linked to belongingness and engagement in a mutually caring relationship (Baumeister & Leary, 1995).

The drive to defend is a deep-rooted categorization of evolved psychological mechanisms that make us defend ourselves and our valued accomplishments whenever we perceive them to be endangered. At the individual level, the drive to defend is activated by perceived threats to one’s person, valued objects, status, or beliefs (Hirschhorn, 1988). At the collective or organizational level, the drive to defend triggers when individuals perceive a threat either to the bonds with others in their group or the collectively shared resources, or as a deviation from socially accepted norms deemed disloyal (Hirschhorn, 1988). The human mind is preconditioned to respond to a variety of threats, and the reaction escalates as the severity of the threat heightens (Buss, 2006).

Last, the drive to learn is a categorization of evolved psychological mechanisms that impel humans to collect information, assess the needs of a situation, examine their environment, and make observations about explanatory ideas and theories to appease curiosity and make sound judgments (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). This mechanism encourages individuals to seek out information to resolve problems associated with fulfilling fundamental needs (Kaplan, 1992). Individuals seek to learn in order to decrease their uncertainty, bring about closure to a problem that challenges well-being, satisfies their curiosity, or make situations more consistent with what is perceived as a ‘normal’ behavior (Hackman & Kaplan, 1974; Kaplan, 1992; Kurzban, 2006).

The Four-Drive model contends that these drives, shaped by evolution to solve problems humans faced tens of thousands of years ago, remain influential today; they been implicitly reported by scholars for centuries, and are central to a unified understanding of human behavior (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). It also contends that all environmental inputs are evaluated in terms of their potential to satisfy or threaten each of the four drives. Thus, we would expect the drives to influence IS acceptance and use – just as they influence our everyday life. We framed the themes we had uncovered according to the Four-Drive model, as there was a clear mapping between themes and drives (see Table 1). We also structured the findings, presented next, according to this model.

## Findings

As we illustrate in this section, psychological mechanisms (i.e., drives) played a role in influencing the nurses’ acceptance and use of the MICT. Indeed, even though we were dealing with different sites and instances of MICT, the nurses’ statements indicated that reasons for accepting to incorporate the MICT into their work had, in some instances, more to do with evolutionary psychology (i.e., underlying drives) than with socio (i.e. social influence) and cognitive psychology (i.e., performance and effort expectancy). In the following, we present our results, highlighting the influence of each of the four drives.

## Drive to bond

As explained earlier, the drive to bond refers to our need for social relationships with others. In this research, the drive to bond was frequently evoked by nurses, as they explained the importance of connecting with their patients, and sometimes, with their patients’ families and friends. In the ED site, for example, triage nurses indicated that, as patients entered their unit, they needed to reassure them that they would be taken care of, along with establishing mutual trust and decreasing their anxiety. This, in turn, enabled a better dialogue between nurses and patients. The ability to assess patients at their location via the MICT decreased triage time, giving nurses a greater opportunity to address patients’ immediate needs, and thus increasing their level of trust:

It’s so hectic in here that sometimes y having the wireless systems y lets us get to treating the patient faster y They [patients] get triaged quicker and when they get back to a bed quicker y they do trust you more because they are getting back here [into a bed] quicker y so they are easier to deal with. (ED nurse)

Once trust was established, it was easier for nurses to comfort patients. Although the RF unit did not exhibit the same level of chaos as found in the ED, the drive to bond was also important, and similarly promoted acceptance of MICT. Indeed, by using the technology, RF nurses could increase the level of comfort provided to their patients:

To me, the patients seem more comfortable with us using the computer to give them the medications. They ask questions about what they are getting and you can show them what the doctors ordered. It calms their nerves by showing them this in the system and then showing them how the pharmacist validated it. And then I say, ‘I look at it a third time before I give it to you’. (RF nurse)

We also observed that nurses did not only have to reassure their patients, but also their families and friends:

During a medication administration episode, the attending nurse was repeatedly questioned by the patient’s visitors concerning what was being given and how effective this medication had been for the patient’s pain. The nurse was able to immediately answer the questions by accessing the relevant information via the wireless cart equipped with technologies supporting ubiquitous IS access that she brought into the patient’s room. They seemed reassured that they were being given medication in accordance to the physician’s prescription y (Field notes from observation at RF)

At PACU, where patients spent more of their time anesthetized, the drive to bond did not initially appear salient. However, we found out that this drive became of utmost importance once the patients gained consciousness, as the nurses needed to soothe patients’ anxiety about their condition. Our field notes read:

As patients began to regain consciousness, the nurses stroked the patients reassuringly and spoke to them in soft tones in an effort to keep the patients calm during their recovery process. Only after calming the patients did the nurses revert back to documenting using MICT. (Field notes from observation at PACU)

A PACU nurse manager noted that she frequently observed nurses making favorable comments about the MICT, because it gave them more time to directly care for patients’ physical needs and decrease the patients worries as they regained consciousness. The nurses’ ability to remain more focused on patients, with the help of the MICT, led to a decrease in charting time, thus allowing nurses to spend more time with their patients:

I say, on average, it [MICT] cuts down on about fifteen minutes, which gives them more time to see after other patients or spend more time with the patient if they have complications like pain or nausea following the surgery y My staff wants to tend to the patient and anything that will help them do that is welcomed. So this was the major reason why they wanted to use the system because they could have time doing other things for the patient rather than writing forever. (PACU nurse manager)

In sum, in all three sites, the drive to bond was an important one to satisfy, and was an underlying reason supporting the use of MICT in the nurses’ work practices.

## Drive to defend

In the context of our study, we conceptualized the drive to defend as the need for the nurses to protect themselves from liabilities. As a result, nurses documented all aspects of the interactions between them, the doctors, and the patients, very thoroughly. Whereas both PACU and RF nurses acknowledged this need, the ED nurses did not. From our understanding, this was most likely because triage was designed to be a quick assessment of what might have been causing a patient’s ailments. Even though physicians and acute care nurses used this assessment as a starting point to make their diagnosis in ED, it was, ultimately, the medical personnel performing the medical interventions who were accountable for what happened to the patient (not the triage nurses). Therefore, the drive to defend, in this specific site, was not salient and thus did not influence the nurses in accepting (or rejecting) the MICT.

At PACU and RF, where nurses’ responsibilities were more prominent, this drive was very salient. Many of them indicated that an important benefit from using the MICT was the increased protection in case they ever faced a lawsuit. In the past, some of PACU nurses had been involved in a deposition, which had sensitized the entire unit to the importance of such matters. The nurses perceived the MICT as offering some safeguard. A nurse manager explained:

It keeps us from making mistakes and to protect ourselves y with the manual chart sometimes things get forgotten or you can’t even read some of it. (PACU nurse manager)

The nurses were thus inclined to accept the MICT because its use reassured them that they were following prescribed medication procedures. Specifically, they appreciated that the technology gave them the ability to log administration of medications and have automatic alerts when individual patients needed medications, thus reducing reliance on manual charts that were sometimes illegible. Moreover, the technology was a means to access information authorizing the physician’s prescription with detailed regimen instructions. and to validate information from the pharmacists about drug interactions or potential allergic reactions. Essentially, the MICT was considered as a means to reduce error, thus allowing the nurses to protect the patients and themselves. An RF nurse elaborated:

[The MICT] helps me to keep better track of everything I do for a med delivery y It’s all right there in the system and it keeps me from making mistakes or overlooking something in the record y like this one time I went to give a med that I knew was right. I scanned it and I got an alert not to administer it to the patient y I found out that it was because the med had expired in the system in the last hour since I gave it y The doc had submitted a newer prescription in the system. I can’t really say if I would have caught that as easily with the old way [before MICT]. This whole thing [i.e., implementation of the MICT] was to make things safer for patients but I feel like it protects me too. (RF nurse)

At RF, reducing errors was not only a way for the nurses to protect themselves against potential lawsuits, but also a way to maintain good standing in regard to their supervisors. An RF nurse told us:

When something goes wrong like a patient has an adverse med reaction, the nurse is the first one they [i.e., management] come to figure out what happened y they look at what I gave and when and compare it to what the prescription says y (RF nurse)

Overall, the MICT enabled nurses to retain a log of the who, when, where, and why of their interventions; it enabled the information to be readily accessible for authorized personnel; and it enabled the nurses to validate their actions, thus reducing potential errors caused by memory lapses or poor documentation. These outcomes were perceived as major advantages of the technology, as it protected the nurses from potential liabilities and reprimands from their supervisors.

## Drive to learn

The drive to learn impelled nurses to collect information about patients and their ailments. This drive was salient at all three sites, and promoted the acceptance of the MICT. ED nurses, for example, felt that the MICT allowed them to learn and validate known and unknown conditions about their patients, thus decreasing the time for triage. The nurses accessed historical medical records to find out about conditions that patients would not (or could not) divulge and to validate the information they did provide. An ED nurse commented:

We have the ability to look up previous medical information like allergies and medication effectiveness. The patient may come in by rescue and was found unconscious at home and nobody really knows anything about them y or they have HIV or TB and don’t want to tell you y [Before MICT] it would take us hours to look up the information with the paper charts y With the system y you can put information right in as you work and it’s right there accessible for anvbody to use. which is the nice thing. (ED nurse)

Additionally, the hectic nature of the ED often precluded the triage nurses to directly communicate with the medical personnel providing follow-up care. Because the MICT allowed the triage nurses to record everything as they worked, this documentation was accessible in real time by physicians and acute care nurses from the other units where the patients would eventually be sent.

At PACU, because patients were anesthetized for the majority of their stay, they could obviously not answer questions about their current state; the nurses could not validate patients’ identity, status, or condition verbally, as was possible across the other sites. The MICT afforded access to what was deemed to be the most reliable source of patient information: the electronic chart. PACU nurses thus were motivated to accept the MICT because this was their major source of information regarding patients. A nurse discussed the importance of accessing patients’ information via the system:

All we have [referring to information about the patient] is the chart that comes with them from the operating room and whatever the nurse who brings them in tells us. We enter in all that into our system when they come in. We update as we monitor. The patient can’t really tell you anything. (PACU nurse)

With regard to the nurses and managerial personnel from the RF site, they emphasized the importance of corroborating the patients’ description of their ailments and medications with the detailed medical history of interventions available from the MICT. These nurses also found it useful to be able to obtain, retain, and access needed patient information while they worked, as opposed to sending messages via phone, email, or fax and waiting for responses from other medical personnel – or worst, locating and drudging through written, often cryptic, medical records. One of them commented:

[With MICT], we have a good record of every med given to the patient since they have been in the [healthcare] system. [y] you can check prior orders and you have all the information you need about the patient right there at your fingertips [y]You know who ordered what, when it was validated and who did it. (RF nurse)

In sum, the need to gather information about patients was very important across sites. Having access to the MICT decreased the time to locate, validate, and document crucial medical history information. The MICT thus allowed nurses to satisfy their drive to learn about their patients.

## Drive to acquire

The drive to acquire, in the context of this study, was not related to material artifacts, but rather to social acknowledgement. Specifically, the drive to acquire was concerned with the pursuit of status. The influence of the drive was most telling in the ED and RF units. At ED, triage nurses indicated that presenting themselves as competent nurses truly interested in assessing the needs of the patient helped them to legitimize their role. They noted that, prior to the MICT, they had to manually document the triage process using a checklist. Given the chaos typical of an emergency room, it was not uncommon for a nurse to ask the same questions to a patient more than once. The nurses considered the triage process, unsupported by MICT, as cumbersome; their perception was that because of the process’ inefficiency, patients regarded them as lacking professionalism. This negative perception occasionally upset patients, and at times, caused belligerent behavior; such behavior, the nurses further construed, was less likely to occur when patients were dealing with physicians (rather than them), or when nurses could rely on the MICT. A nurse described the remarkable change in patients’ perception after her unit began to use the MICT:

Between running here and there to pick up records or answering calls, you get distracted y forget stuff and have to keep running back to the patient y scribbling stuff down on paper or whatever. They [the patients] get impatient with you and think it’s because of you being all haywire that they haven’t seen the doc yet y Now [with the MICT], I don’t appear like this is my first day on the job y I think the patients regard me better y I mean it’s kind of like the white coat effect; a certain amount of respect automatically comes with the coat y it may be that having the [MICT] shows the patient we are serious. (ED nurse)

The MICT thus enabled the ED nurses to streamline the triage process. They had access to existing records, charted more comprehensively and with less chances of missing pertinent information, and therefore avoided repeating or asking additional questions of the patient. This streamlining allowed patients to be escorted to their bed faster, which made them less irritable and generally more respectful of the nurses.

The need for greater acknowledgment of the nurses’ professional status was also observed in the RF unit. Patients were occasionally uncooperative and hesitant to trust the nurses. For example, patients would sometimes ask nurses to confirm that the physician said it was okay for them to receive a given medication before agreeing to take it. Having access to the MICT was a way for nurses to substantiate their request for medication intake, and thus to be perceived as professionals and to gain respect.

As to PACU, given the unconscious state of the patient, the drive to acquire status was not as salient as in the other sites, at least not as it related to the interactions with the patients. But at ED and RF, however, this drive was certainly present and constituted a driving force behind the nurses accepting to use the MICT.

## Discussion

At the outset of our study, evolutionary psychology was not a part of our collective theoretical repertoire. However, as academics in the quest of a greater understanding of the phenomenon under study, we came to realize, during our data analysis, that the Four-Drive model based on evolutionary psychology was insightful in explaining occurrences in our findings that did not seem to adequately align with traditional technology acceptance constructs. This progression in thought enabled us to theorize about the role of human nature in technology acceptance, emphasizing the importance of evolutionary psychology. In the following sections, we discuss the theoretical development emerging from our study, suggest potential areas of research that could leverage our theoretical insights, consider the impact of our findings on practice, and briefly acknowledge the controversy surrounding the evolutionary psychology theoretical perspective.

## Theoretical development

As we initially speculated, by limiting our understanding of technology acceptance to a social cognitive perspective, we would have neglected a keener explanation of MICT acceptance. Indeed, if we had tested technology acceptance within this same healthcare context (via a survey instrument measuring typical social and cognitive constructs), we would have likely found good empirical support, but would have contributed to a ‘degenerative’ research program bemoaned by some of our most prominent IS scholars (Benbasat & Barki, 2007; Hirscheim, 2007; Silva, 2007). By designing a research that allowed us to inductively discover the ‘new’ (Benbasat & Barki, 2007) in an already saturated research area, we responded to the call for alternative theoretical perspectives (in this case, evolutionary psychology) to better understand technology acceptance.

Kock (2009) contends that ‘evolutionary psychology holds great promise as a basis for information systems theorizing’ (p. 401). In line with his belief, our research points to the need for consideration of evolutionary psychology in IS research, and more specifically, for its role in technology acceptance. As we saw, MICT acceptance, within the hospitals studied, was primarily explained in terms of deeply embedded drives that were important for the nurses to address: the drives to bond, to defend, to learn, and to acquire. The drives were not always salient (e.g., the drive to defend at ED), but when they were, they seemed to be as important (if not more so) as other considerations (such as social influence and effort expectancy) in explaining MICT acceptance. The saliency of the drives does not necessarily constitute inevitable behavior. Rather, a behavioral manifestation depends heavily on the environment and the cues that activate it. We therefore might not see the influence of a drive until we encounter certain tasks to be performed in specific environments that amplify their effects. For example, in PACU, the drive to bond was not salient until the patient became conscious. Only then did we observe activities of nurses interacting with patients to establish mutual trust, promote comfort, and reduce patient anxiety. Similarly, in ED, the drive to defend was not salient to the nurses, because other medical professionals were present and carried greater responsibility towards the patient’s care. Thus, depending on the environment. a drive may be more or less salient and thus may influence human behavior to greater or lesser extent.

Evolutionary psychologists argue that treating evolved psychological mechanisms and cognitive-social behavior independently is not useful and inhibits researchers from fully understanding the complexity of human psychology (Simpson & Kenrick, 1997; Brewer & Caporael, 2006; Schaller et $a l . ,$ 2006; Tooby & Cosmides, 2007). Indeed, even from an evolutionary perspective, cognition still has its place (Cosmides & Tooby, 2000). Cognition may be viewed as a short-term, phenomenon-driven activity that is necessary to analyze, prepare, and adjust evolved psychological mechanisms for long-term survival and reproductive benefits (Kenrick et al., 2006). The evolutionary perspective does not disclaim the influence of cognition. It proposes that, depending on the situation, some underlying evolved psychological mechanisms might also be activated (Kenrick et al., 2006). These mechanisms may support (or oppose) cognition depending on circumstances.

Accordingly, we propose a ‘theoretical elaboration’ of the generic technology acceptance model, on that combines both cognitive and evolved factors. As suggested by Vaughan (1992, cited in Strauss & Corbin, 1994), theory elaboration consists of ‘taking off from extant theories and developing them further in conjunction with qualitative case analysis’ (p. 282). More specifically, we suggest that evolved psychological mechanisms (categorized as the four human drives) should be included in traditional technology acceptance models. It not clear how, exactly, evolved psychological mechanisms ultimately impact behaviors. Maybe they directly impact constructs of individual reactions and/or intentions; alternatively (or, additionally), they may moderate the relationship between individual reactions and intentions. What is clear is that, in certain situations, these mechanisms appear to be of fundamental importance to the decision to accept technology. We thus propose that by broadening our theoretical repertoire by including tenets of evolutionary psychology, we are able to better explain the forces at work. In this context, evolutionary psychology should be viewed as a complementing (rather than competing) lens that augments social and cognitive models. The ‘elaborated’ model of technology acceptance is represented in Figure 2 and we provide criteria to evaluate our resulting model and research process in Appendix E.

Many scholars are not yet sensitized to thinking about evolved psychological mechanisms. As Emerson noted, ‘people only see what they are prepared to see’ (Emerson & Emerson, 1904). Thus, if evolutionary psychology is not part of an individual’s theoretical repertoire along with other lenses used for understanding human behavior, it is unlikely that one will see its possible explanatory power. In the course of this research, given our immersion in the field and repeated interactions with the nurses, we increased our theoretical sensitivity (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) about how underlying, deeply rooted drives have an influence on human actions. Only after much data analysis did we become open to the influential role of evolutionary psychology, as instantiated by the drive model, on technology acceptance. We hope that by adding this lens to the IS theoretical repertoire, other researchers will also be sensitized to the potential role that evolutionary psychology can play in their investigations. A logical follow-up to our proposal to include evolutionary psychology in our research is to reflect on the contexts and possible areas where this could be the most germane.

## Potential areas of research

As demonstrated in our study, the tasks at hand greatly influenced the saliency of certain evolved psychological mechanisms. Indeed, even across the same healthcare setting, the tasks performed by the nurses varied, and so did the influence of the drives across particular sites. This does not decrease the value of the evolutionary psychology lens, but it does emphasize that the saliency of an evolved psychological mechanism is, to some extent, context and task dependent. However, as evolved psychological mechanisms are inherent to all human beings, they will be influential in a variety of settings.

For example, keeping within the healthcare context, the shift towards telemedicine (Hanson et al., 2009) changes the relationship between healthcare professionals and their patients, thus potentially affecting the drive to bond and its impact on technology acceptance. Also, the trend in health consumerism (Ball & Lillis, 2001), where patients have greater involvement in their own healthcare decisions (often made possible by technology), may make the drive to defend much more salient, in that healthcare professionals are faced with patients who have greater ability to detect (and thus act upon) medical errors. Moreover, telemedicine and consumerism are developments that have led researchers to consider technology acceptance not only from the healthcare professionals’ perspective, but also from the patients’ (Calabretta, 2002), thus potentially requiring the consideration of drives emanating from both healthcare professionals and patients, and the possible interaction between drives. That could be the case, for example, when healthcare professionals’ drive to acquire (status) conflicts with the patients’ drive to learn (about their health situation), which is consistent with research that has shown that health professionals fear for diminishing status in relation to more informed patients (Malone et al., 2004; McMullan, 2006).

In organizational settings, Lawrence & Nohria (2002) themselves have suggested considering the drives across individuals and groups to explain behavior in business organizations. They have done so, in two empirical studies, about employee engagement, satisfaction, commitment, and intention to quit. These studies provided insights into what actions managers can take to satisfy their employees’ drives so as to promote intended behaviors (Nohria et al. 2008). Lawrence & Nohria (2002) argue that ‘individuals and social institutions will enjoy adaptive advantages (i.e., advantages in meeting changing business environmental demands) to the extent that they are able to fulfill all four basic human drives’ (p. 264). Accordingly, by providing an organization with the appropriate tools, technologies, or opportunities supportive of its employees’ drives, the organization is in a stronger position to influence desired behaviors (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002).

The inclusion of the drives could in fact be relevant to many more phenomena within a business setting, some specifically involving IS. The drive to acquire, for example, could be pertinent in studies looking into the impacts associated with an employee’s status (e.g., the financial influence of a CEO’s status, Graffin et al., 2008). It could also be relevant when considering organizational legitimacy, which stream of research has considered firms’ reputation and quest for prestige (Deephouse & Carter, 2005). In the realm of IS, the drive to acquire status may be important to understand volunteer contributions within open source communities (Lakhani & von Hippel, 2003) or knowledge sharing within virtual communities (Chiu et al., 2006).

The drive to bond reflects on the motivation for individuals to select affiliations with other individuals or institutions with regard to employment or organizational alignment (Pinker, 1997; Putnam, 2000; Wilson, 2004). Bonding allows individuals to establish relationships with colleagues or customers and to align themselves better with the business organization. Bonding is said to manifest itself in form of moral codes regarding social relations (Sundie et al., 2006) and eventually in the form of culture (Norenzayan et al., 2006). Within IS, the drive to bond is likely to have relevance to research on the motivation behind social networking (DiMicco et al., 2008), as well as it could help explain the slow embrace of telecommuting (Gainey et al., 1999), given its potential to isolate workers, both socially and professionally.

The drive to defend has manifested itself in business contexts where individuals exhibit behaviors to defend their actions, positions, and valued artifacts for which they are responsible. Individuals engage in these behaviors in hopes of sustaining their relationship with the referent groups and organizations when the relationships are deemed worth preserving (Lawrence & Nohria, 2002). An organization will defend itself in the same manner and will expel those who pose threats, abandon, or reform strategies that do not promote its survival (Ashforth & Lee, 1990). IS wise, some of our key foundations technologies (like the Internet, started as ARPANET in 1969 by the Department of Defense to ensure secure communications during a war) were propelled by the drive to defend. Research dealing with the role of technology in securing corporate data (Dhillon & Backhouse, 2000) or consumers’ expectations regarding data privacy (Foxman & Kilcoyne, 1993) could also benefit from considering the drive to defend in the conceptualization of their research.

Finally, manifestations of the drive to learn come in different forms (e.g., competence (White, 1967), growth (Maslow, 1954), achievement (McClelland, 1961) or efficacy (Bandura, 1977)). The drive to learn is relevant to a series of experimental research studies resulting in Information-Gap Theory (Lowenstein, 1994). Essentially, a gap is generated when an individual encounters an external observation that is perceived as inconsistent with what is currently known and poses an unpleasant sensation that should be eradicated (in order to reduce cognitive dissonance (Festinger, 1957)). Thereafter, the individual seeks insights to reconcile the new observation by collecting new information and thereby appeasing the drive to learn. Specifically in IS, we suggest that the study of information overload caused by the use and misuse of information technology (Eppler & Mengis, 2004) may be an area where the drive to learn would be particularly salient. Human’s addiction to information, supported through the deployment of ubiquitous technologies such as smart phones and PDAs, can indeed be considered a major cause of information overload and other ‘information pathologies’ (Bawden & Robinson, 2009), thus making the drive to learn about one’s environment very central to this type of research.

In summary, the influence of evolved psychological mechanisms on human behaviors, such as those suggested by the Four-Drive model, is very relevant beyond technology acceptance in the healthcare context. The areas of research highlighted above do not constitute an exhaustive list, but rather indicate that evolutionary psychology, and more specifically the human drives, may be pertinent to research across a vast array of contexts and tasks.

## Potential impacts on practice

Given the role of evolutional psychology in the context of technology acceptance, we suggest for IS designers and managers to not only be mindful of traditional social and cognitive factors (i.e., social influence, performance expectancy, and effort expectancy) when developing or rolling out new systems, but to also consider an evolutionary psychology lens (such as the Four-Drive model) to better facilitate an individual’s needs and promote intended use. For example, to address the drive to bond, IS should be designed to lessen obtrusiveness and interference with natural human interactions, such as eye-to-eye contact. In the context of MICTs, one could consider the use of virtual displays instead of physical screens that are less inhibiting and can be more engaging to mediate communications between involved parties. In the case of the drive to learn, IS should not only support a high quality level of information (i.e., it should support information that is most current, cross-linked across different platforms, and available in real-time at the point of work), but could also provide additional information that supplements the primary data exchange to enhance wellbeing, such as with educational or awareness information. Access to additional information could also work towards satisfying the drive to acquire, by elevating the perceived status and competency of the technology user. To appease the drive to defend, systems could be designed to proactively warn to ward off error. The level of incidence detection could be raised by using intelligent management systems or comprehensive situational monitoring systems that take into account situational variables. The general practical implication of understanding the influence of the drives in human behavior is that designing IS to satisfy, rather than impede, the drives of the users (or parties involved when the system is used) enhances the likelihood of promoting desired behaviors, such as adoption of technology, and favorable follow-on outcomes such as user (and patron) satisfaction and economic return.

## Controversy

Although only few theories are without detractors, evolutionary psychology has received much heated opposition because of its relation to sociobiology and its assumption that human nature plays a role in individual and collective human behavior (Buller, 2005). Critics have questioned not only the political and ethical implications that they perceived evolutionary psychology suggests, but also its scientific nature. We would like to acknowledge, however, that a recent special issue dedicated to the attribution of evolutionary concepts appearing in the American Psychologist, an authoritative source for the advancement of psychology, includes compelling evidence that the collective intellect of the most influential scholars in psychology has dramatically shifted from criticism of evolutionary concepts to acceptance of its seminal importance. For example, whereas in a 1974 American Psychologist article, renowned psychologist Ebel condemns the application of evolutionary concepts in psychology as ‘useless y for understanding of behavioral phenomena’ (Ebel, 1974, p. 491), in 2009, leading scholars recognize, demonstrate, and celebrate how evolutionary principles over the past 200 years has become ‘an important part of psychology’ (Dewsbury, 2009, p. 67) and are essential in a comprehensive explanation of functions of the mind and their influence on behavior (Burghardt, 2009; Buss, 2009; Dewsbury, 2009; Hess & Thibault, 2009).

Although a thorough coverage of the controversy is outside of scope of this paper (and has been done elsewhere: see Segerstra˚le, 2000; Alcock, 2001; and Confer et al., 2010), one charge against this theory is worth discussing here: the undue adaptationism (Gould, 1981; Lewotin et al., 1984). Essentially, this criticism suggests that evolutionary psychology may see adaptive explanations when other possible explanations (e.g., culturally or socially based) could as well have been hypothesized. Although we were careful not to force data into our theoretical frame (questioning, e.g., if the nurses’ desire for establishing relationships with their patients was not socially influenced by other people’s expectations), and that we were very transparent as to how evolutionary psychology mechanisms allowed for a greater understanding of the studied phenomenon, it remains that our theoretical elaboration of the generic technology acceptance model calls for further testing (e.g., how have these drives varied over time?) and development (e.g., how do they influence attitudes?). In other words, while we believe that we have support to suggest the expansion of our theoretical repertoire by including evolutionary mechanisms, we also hope that our claim will stimulate additional IS research to consider (i.e., further develop and test) the evolutionary psychology perspective.

## Conclusion

One of the important goals of IS research is to understand how and why people use IS. Often, we focus on new technologies because their successful implementation can have important benefits. Sometimes, we are fortunate to recognize that a particular site makes salient a factor that may always have been present, but not discerned as relevant to technology acceptance. Our research reveals that evolved psychological mechanisms, specifically in the form of human drives, affect technology acceptance. ## Evaluating grounded theory

Most qualitative researchers are not comfortable using the terms ‘validity’ and ‘reliability’ for the assessment of their work – these terms carry too many quantitative implications (Leininger, 1994; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Moreover, it is important to select assessment criteria that are specifically appropriate for a given qualitative methodology (Corbin & Strauss, 2008) – there is no set of criteria that fits all of them. In this spirit, we choose to use Glaser & Strauss’s (1967) recommendations to assess our work. Accordingly, the criteria of credibility and applicability are discussed below.

<table><tr><td>Criteria</td><td>Assessment</td></tr><tr><td>Credibility– is there sufficient detail and description so that readers feel that they were vicariously in the field?</td><td>While remaining within the scope expected from a journal article, we tried to provide sufficient details about the nurses&#x27; acceptance of MICT within all three sites. We shared some of their comments along with some of our observations. A subset of these is reported in the Finding section and in Appendix C.</td></tr><tr><td>Credibility– is there sufficient evidence on how the data were gathered and how the analysis was conducted?</td><td>In the Research Approach section, we provide details as to how the three sites were selected (i.e., from a list obtained from a MICT vendor, and according to given criteria) and the respondents chosen (mainly via theoretical sampling). Triangulation (by way of different data collection techniques and time periods) provided a means to validate the collected data. The data analysis efforts are also described in the Research Approach section, that is, all three types of coding (open, axial, and selective) are detailed.</td></tr><tr><td>Applicability– is the theory readily understandable by laymen and professionals?</td><td>At the completion of our research, we presented our results at the American Nursing Informatics Association (ANIA), comprising an audience of over 250 frontline nurses, nursing informatics personnel, and managers who either were using or were engaged in implementations of MICTs. We solicited responses and obtained feedback from ANIA members stating that our findings resonated with their interpretations of the important reasons underlying MICT acceptance. We believe MIS researchers will understand our &#x27;elaborated&#x27; model of technology acceptance model (as shown in Figure 2), because it is based on a very familiar model. Our elaboration emphasizes the importance on including an additional lens to explain acceptance behavior. We also discuss the relevance of our findings for practitioners.</td></tr><tr><td>Applicability– is the theory sufficiently general to be applicable to diverse situations and populations?</td><td>Our research shows that the task at hand greatly influences the saliency of certain drives. If a given drive is not threatened, then it is unlikely that it will become salient. As evolved psychological mechanisms are inherent to all human beings, we are certain that other contexts will also promote the saliency of the drives, particularly when a change occurs that threatens such drives.</td></tr><tr><td>Applicability– does the theory provide the user with sufficient control to bring about change in situations?</td><td>We believe it does. The &#x27;elaborated&#x27; model suggests that focusing exclusively on social and cognitive considerations to promote acceptance of technology is likely to be incomplete. It is also important to make sure that fundamental drives are not threatened, and if they are, to articulate how the technology can appease the drives. We discuss the impact of our research on practitioners in the later part of the paper, emphasizing how IS designers should consider the Four-Drives model to increase user acceptance and use of technology.</td></tr></table>
