## Abstract

Firms in IT industries are under constant competitive pressure from incumbent firms and startups to offer innovative products and services. This study explores the effect of mergers and acquisitions (M&As) on firms’ product differentiation in IT industries. The unique characteristics of IT industries, namely hypercompetition, distributed innovation, modularity of products, and the tacit nature of knowledge, make M&As an attractive mechanism to enable IT firms to stay competitive. Drawing on product differentiation theory, we hypothesize a positive effect of M&As on product differentiation and suggest that this relationship is more potent in firms with higher in-house innovation, an exploitative innovation orientation, and under high levels of industry turbulence. We test our hypotheses using M&A transactions from 1996 to 2016 and a unique 10-K textual analysis-based measure of product differentiation. Using the difference-in-differences identification strategy, the following findings are supported: (1) M&As lead to higher product differentiation, (2) in-house innovation capability and exploitation-oriented innovation strategy positively moderate the positive effect of M&As. These results, which are robust to endogeneities and other robustness tests, contribute to theory and managerial practice by shedding light on the role of M&As as an essential mechanism for product innovation and the complementary role of in-house innovation capability and strategy for firms that intend to take advantage of M&As for creating a competitive edge through differentiating their products and services from competitors. These findings have important managerial value, providing an alternative narrative to the current view on M&As in IT industries as a means of absorbing and dismantling the competition.

Keywords: Merger & Acquisition, Product Differentiation, In-House Innovation, Exploitation Innovation Strategy, Industry Turbulence, Difference-In-Differences, Propensity Score Matching

## 1 Introduction

IT industries are fast-growing and hypercompetitive, requiring IT firms to constantly differentiate their products from their incumbent competitors while deterring new entry threats (Feng et al., 2018; Pan et al.,

2018). This competitive reality contributes to the prominence of mergers and acquisitions (M&As) in IT industries, with high-technology M&As accounting for \$214 billion of the \$3.5 trillion M&A deals in all areas in 2014 (Kupor, 2015). On the one hand, IT firms turn to the broader technology ecosystem and acquire innovative firms to differentiate their products and services from their competitors (Wang & Hui, 2017). For example, Google has differentiated its products and services from its competitors through acquisitions of office tools, artificial intelligence, and cloud computing companies (Alcantara et al., 2021). On the other hand, large IT firms have been under scrutiny for using M&As as a mechanism to deter new entry threats by acquiring potential competitors and dismantling them rather than absorbing their resources and capabilities to differentiate their products from their competitors (Hayes, 2021; Alcantara et al., 2021). For example, Facebook’s 2012 acquisition of Instagram is known to be motivated by deterring Instagram’s competitive threat and extending Facebook’s market power rather than differentiating Facebook’s products and services (Perez, 2020a). Despite the prevalence of M&As in IT industries and the diversity of motivations behind such M&As, our understanding of the effectiveness of M&As in IT firms’ product differentiation and its underlying mechanisms remains vastly limited.

Two streams of IS research on M&As (see summaries in Table 1) and product differentiation (see summaries in Table 2) are yet to provide a theoretical link between the two essential constructs and shed light on the underlying mechanisms, which can help a firm use M&As to differentiate their products and services from their competitors. On the one hand, IS literature exploring M&As has remained focused on studying the effect of the underlying IT resources and capabilities on the outcome of the M&A event (Gao & Iyer, 2006; Mehta & Hirschheim, 2007; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011) and the effect of customer or product-oriented motives and firm characteristics on the M&A decision (Banker et al., 2011; Benitez et al., 2018; Chang & Cho, 2017). However, the specific mechanisms through which M&As provide a competitive edge to IT firms remain unclear. On the other hand, IS literature on product differentiation has studied mechanisms such as platform membership (Foerderer et al., 2018), investments in corporate venture capital (Kim et al., 2016), in-house IT and R&D investments (Kleis et al., 2012), participating in open innovation alliances (Han et al., 2012), and forming strategic alliances (Rahmati et al., 2021). However, M&As are fundamentally different from in-house investments and interfirm mechanisms such as strategic alliances, open innovation, and joint ventures (Hagedoorn & Duysters, 2002; K. Li et al., 2019). Hence the results of these studies are not directly extendable to the context of M&As. Therefore, while product differentiation has been shown to be an essential component of firms’ competitive advantage (Zott & Amit, 2008), and despite the prevalence of M&As as a mechanism for product differentiation in IT industries, to the best of our knowledge, there has been no empirical IS research examining the relationship between the two constructs and the potential mechanisms that might drive that.

This study investigates the link between M&As and product differentiation in IT industries. Drawing on research on hypercompetition (Lee et al., 2010), distributed innovation (Nambisan & Sawhney, 2011), modularity of products (Yoo et al., 2010), and the tacit nature of knowledge in IT industries (Gans et al., 2008), we argue that the hypercompetitive and turbulent nature of IT industries increases the need for the rapid exploration, acquisition, and assimilation of external know-how while limiting the challenges caused by the complex nature of knowledge involved. At the same time, the abundance of technology startups at different levels of maturity, inherent product modularity, and distributed innovations in IT industries increase the value of M&As as an innovative strategy, enabling inhouse innovations to enhance firms’ ability to assimilate the acquired knowledge and know-how. We therefore posit that in IT industries: (1) M&As improve the acquirer’s product differentiation, and (2) the firm’s innovation output, exploitative innovation, and industry turbulence positively moderate the effect of M&As on product differentiation.

To test our hypotheses, we leveraged the records of historical M&A transactions and a database of product differentiation based on textual analyses of US public IT firms’ SEC filings. Our cross-sectional sample includes IT firms engaged in significant M&A(s) between 1996 and 2016. To address the selection bias and associated endogeneity concern, we built our primary data set using a matched sample of treatment firm-years and control firm-years within Hoberg & Phillips (2016)’s TNIC industries based on a propensity score calculated using factors likely to impact the M&A decision in the first place. Using a Difference-in-differences (DiD) identification strategy with year- and firm-fixed effects controlled for on a propensity score-matched sample, we conducted a rigorous estimation of the impact of M&As on acquirers’ product differentiation for robust causal inference and tested the moderation effects described above.

Our empirical results indicate that M&As lead to higher product differentiation for IT firms. Moreover, in-house innovation and an exploitative innovation focus complement M&As in enhancing firms’ product differentiation. However, industry turbulence does not impact facilitating differentiation of firms’ products from their competitors through acquiring external innovation. Building on the extant literature on product innovation in IT industries (Foerderer et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2021; Kleis et al., 2012), our paper helps to create an empirical link between M&As, in-house innovation, and product differentiation. In line with more recent literature on product innovation in IT industries (Kim et al., 2021), we are among the first studies to directly examine product differentiation as the ultimate goal of innovation rather than other related firm outcomes such as innovation or returns on R&D. Furthermore, our paper builds on existing literature on interfirm mechanisms of product innovation in IT industries (Han et al., 2012; Rahmati et al., 2021), being among the first papers to explore M&As as a mechanism for taking advantage of external innovation for differentiating products and services from complement firms’ in-house innovation for investigating, identifying, and absorbing external knowledge, identifying, and absorbing external knowledge, in IT industries.

## 2 Theoretical Background and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1 M&As and Product Differentiation in IT Industries

M&As have received limited attention in IS research as a mechanism for product differentiation. Few IS studies have considered M&As as a mechanism for enhancing the innovative outcome of the acquirers (see Table 1). Instead, the IS literature on M&As has mainly focused on the antecedents of M&As in IT industries (Benitez et al., 2018) or the performance benefits of M&As at the deal level (Gao & Iyer, 2006; Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011), rather than their product innovation outcomes at the firm level. Only Banker et al. (2011) have explored the effect of diversification on the decision to innovate through M&As or R&D investments for IT firms. Their findings show that diversified IT firms are more likely to innovate through acquiring new entrants rather than spending on in-house R&D. While their findings reveal M&As as a mechanism used by IT firms for product differentiation, there is a lack of empirical evidence demonstrating whether M&As lead to product differentiation in IT industries.

The IS literature on innovation has focused almost exclusively on other mechanisms (see Table 2). For example, one stream of IS research has extensively studied IT-enabled in-house product and service innovations (Kleis et al., 2012; Srivastava & Shainesh, 2015; Tarafdar & Tanriverdi, 2018), while another has examined the impact of different forms of interfirm partnerships on IT firms’ innovative output (Foerderer et al., 2018; Han et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2016; Rahmati et al., 2021). The mechanisms studied by this literature serve fundamentally different from M&As. While the three mechanisms—namely M&As, interfirm partnerships, and in-house innovation—are complements, M&As allow firms to internalize the acquired external resources and capabilities. In contrast, interfirm partnerships (i.e., strategic alliances, open innovation alliances, and joint ventures) make firms dependent on their partners to access external resources. In-house innovation does not include access to external innovation (K. Li et al., 2019). Therefore, the findings of these studies are not directly extendible to M&As, and we have yet to understand the effect of M&As on product differentiation.

Further, while product differentiation is a critical product market strategy and a source of competitive advantage (Zott & Amit, 2008), IS studies do not provide empirical evidence on the factors affecting firms’ product differentiation and instead focus on firms’ innovative products, using measures such as firms’ patenting output (Kleis et al., 2012), product updates (Foerderer et al., 2018), and returns on R&D (Rahmati et al., 2021). Our study addresses this gap in our understanding of product differentiation in IT industries. Product differentiation, or the extent to which the firm’s products or services are distinct from its competitors, is a core construct in industrial organizations and indicates demand-side benefits (Kim et al., 2021). However, to the best of our knowledge, other than Kim et al.’s (2021) study, there has not been any IS study that focuses on this vital construct as a performance measure of IT firms.

In this paper, we draw on four characteristics of IT industries to address the need for a better understanding of the effect of M&As on product differentiation in these industries. First, IT firms operate in a hypercompetitive environment (Lee et al., 2010; Rai & Tang, 2014; Tanriverdi et al., 2010). The payoff of occupying specific competitive positions is constantly changing in this environment. Firms must continuously reinvent their products and services to distinguish themselves from their rivals and occupy emerging competitive peaks in this highly competitive landscape (D’Aveni et al., 2010; Ilinitch et al., 1996). Identifying and tapping into external sources of innovation is essential for IT firms to enhance their internal innovation and remain competitive in a dynamic and hypercompetitive environment (Chung et al., 2019). Second, IT product and service innovation is distributed rather than bounded by the firm’s boundaries (Lakhani & Panetta, 2007; Nambisan, Lyytinen et al., 2017). M&As represent a vital instrument through which firms can adjust their organizational boundaries relative to their technological limitations and protect their competitive positioning by absorbing external innovative products and services formed around thei core capabilities (Rothaermel & Alexandre, 2009). Third, IT products and services are modular, allowing for innovative products to be created by combining their existing products with those gained from other firms and industries (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2018). Fourth, digital knowledge is both tacit (Gans et al., 2008) and complex (Rahmati et al., 2021). The complex nature of digital knowledge makes it difficult to transfer between firms through strategic partnerships as it accumulates in social structures and is rarely codifiable. The tacit nature of digital knowledge limits the ability of intellectual property rights protections to protect the focal firm’s knowledge base and creates opportunities for strategic behavior that rewards firms engaging in strategic partnerships.

## 2.2 Hypotheses Development

We posit that M&As in IT industries lead to higher product differentiation. IT firms can use M&As to differentiate their products from competitors by taking advantage of IT industries’ distributed, combinatorial, and complex nature of innovation. First, M&As allow IT firms to expand their boundaries to include external innovation around their products and services, enhancing product differentiation. Innovation around IT products and services is distributed within a network of firms, extending beyond the firm and industrial boundaries (Yoo et al., 2010). The IT products offered by a firm can thus become the core of innovative products and services provided by other firms (Chesbrough, 2003). IT firms must constantly reassess their boundaries to repel competition from external innovations based on their products and services (Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018). In this environment, M&As are a crucial strategic action through which IT firms can achieve product differentiation (Dewan & Ren, 2011; Parker et al., 2017). M&As allow firms to expand their boundaries to include distributed innovation around their existing products, internalizing new products without competition for control inherent in different forms of partnerships while limiting the cost and other risks associated with in-house development or joint ventures (Dewan & Ren, 2011).

Second, M&As allow IT firms to differentiate their products by combining them with complementary products and services. IT products and services are intrinsically modular, allowing IT firms to develop and commercialize new products by combining existing and complementary modules (Baldwin & Woodard, 2009). IT firms are thus well-positioned to integrate their products and services with the heterogeneous offerings of other firms as different building blocks of the product or service concept (Yoo, 2012; Yoo et al., 2010). Furthermore, the contemporary reality of pervasive computing, particularly the digitization of traditionally nondigital products in different industries, has created new potential markets for IT firms to combine their products and services with those of other sectors (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2018; Satyanarayanan, 2001). During the past decade, IT firms have used M&As to generate value by acquiring firms from seemingly unrelated industries and recombining their capabilities with the seemingly unrelated capabilities of firms in those industries (Iansiti & Lakhani, 2018). There are numerous examples of such M&As by IT firms (see, for example, Amazon’s acquisition of Whole Foods and Google’s acquisition of Nest, among others), through which they have been able to differentiate their suite of products and services from those of their rivals by acquiring target firms from other industries and creating innovative combinations between their resource base and these newly acquired resources.

Moreover, the modularity of IT products allows firms to maintain a decentralized innovation model, with limited structural integration and high autonomy for the target firm post-M&A. The M&A literature shows the prevalence of M&As as a mechanism for product innovation in decentralized organizational settings, where innovation is distributed between firms business units (Arora et al., 2014), and post-merger structural integration has been shown to harm the ability of the acquirers to leverage the innovative capabilities of the target firms (Puranam & Srikanth, 2007). Hence, IT firms are well-positioned to reap the innovative outcomes of M&As by taking advantage of the modularity of their products and maintaining a decentralized structure. There are multiple examples of such settings in IT industries. The acquirer supports its target firms as autonomous business units and offers its products as a set of product modules created by the various target firms. Facebook’s limited integration with Instagram and WhatsApp and Microsoft’s limited integration with GitHub and LinkedIn, where both companies have taken advantage of the synergies between in-house and acquired product modules, are examples of this phenomenon. Third, M&As allow IT firms to differentiate their product offerings by introducing new products or enhancing their existing products and services, limiting the need to deal with the complexities of developing and commercializing new IT products. Since IT products are sophisticated, offering them requires complex social structures that accumulate the knowledge and know-how necessary for their development, commercialization, and maintenance over time (Rahmati et al., 2021). The complexity of IT products creates a natural barrier for firms seeking to diversify their products and services by simply imitating their competitors’ successful product features (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). There are many examples of such failed attempts in IT industries. For example, Twitter has recently introduced a “fleet” mimicking the “story” feature of its rival Instagram, but with only minimal success (Perez, 2020b). Instagram itself has had a not very successful attempt to add the “reel” feature, imitating the “for you” feature of TikTok, its main competitor (Chen & Lorenz, 2020). The complexity of IT products, combined with their modularity, has made M&As a more successful mechanism than internal development for IT firms attempting to differentiate their products from their competitors. The sophisticated features and complex social structures required for IT products highlight the general advantage of M&As over internal development, especially regarding the associated costs and risks (Singh & Montgomery, 1987). Hence, we argue that:

## H1: M&As have a positive effect on product differentiation for firms in IT industries.

Building on our earlier arguments regarding the prevalence of M&As as a mechanism for achieving product differentiation for IT firms, we posit that the acquirer’s in-house innovation capability has a positive moderating effect on the effect of M&As on product differentiation. M&As help the acquirer better identify relevant targets and translate the targets’ knowledge base into innovative products and services. First, the acquirer’s in-house innovation output helps them to identify relevant external sources of knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1989); the M&A literature posits that the acquirer’s patenting output is a determinant of an M&A event, as firms with both high and diminishing patenting output utilize M&As to enhance their innovative output (Bena & Li, 2014; Valentini, 2012). A high level of in-house innovative activity increases the firms’ sensitivity to opportunities for acquiring external innovation (Rothaermel & Alexandre, 2009) and helps the firm value the external knowledge gained from the acquisition target more highly (Zahra & George, 2002).

Second, the acquirer’s in-house innovation helps them take full advantage of the innovative potential of external sources of knowledge (Cohen & Levinthal, 1989). Focal firms’ stocks of in-house innovation have been shown to have a positive moderating effect on the effect of their M&As and their post-M&A innovative performance (Fernald et al., 2017). If the acquirer has a relatively larger knowledge base, it will more successfully assimilate the acquired knowledge and benefit from greater subsequent innovation output (Ahuja & Katila, 2001). A firm’s internal innovation intensity affects the success of the new products resulting from external knowledge acquisition (Cassiman & Veugelers, 2006). M&As thus provide more opportunities for radical innovation for the acquirers with high levels of in-house innovation (Zhou & Li, 2012). The IS literature shows that innovative IT firms are better positioned to engage in radical innovation through M&As as an extensive knowledge base improves IT firms’ sensing and experimentation routines, enhancing the quality of their search for external knowledge, their ability to assess its business value, and their success in integrating it with their knowledge base (Carlo et al., 2012). IT firms with large innovative output are more successful in exploring valuable bodies of external knowledge and extracting economic value from acquired knowledge resources (Chung et al., 2019). Therefore, we hypothesize that:

## H2: The acquirer’s in-house innovation capability has a positive moderating effect on the positive effect of M&As on product differentiation for firms in IT industries.

Firms with exploitative innovation focus on enhancing existing products and services for their customers (Benner & Tushman, 2003). Exploitative innovation positively moderates the effect of M&As on product differentiation. M&As serve as a complement to an exploitative innovation orientation. Exploratory innovation is challenging as it requires firms’ success in a high-risk process of discovery and experimentation (Katila & Ahuja, 2002). IT knowledge is globally distributed, hindering IT firms’ ability to develop new products and services through exploratory innovation (Saldanha et al., 2020). Organizations must pursue both exploitative and explorative innovation, enabling them to develop new products and services, differentiate them from their competitors, and continuously enhance and improve those products simultaneously (Gibson & Birkinshaw, 2004). IT firms with exploitative innovation orientation can use M&As to compensate for lower levels of explorative innovation and use the targets’ innovation base and products and services to differentiate themselves from their competitors. At the same time, their exploitative innovation orientation enables them to develop the acquired products and services further. Therefore, we argue that:

H3: The acquirer’s exploitative innovation orientation has a positive moderating effect on the positive effect of M&As on product differentiation for firms in IT industries.

Industry turbulence is a condition characterized by a high volume of changes in an industry’s technologies, customer preferences, and demand or supply of products and inputs of production (Dess & Beard, 1984). In such an environment, products and services become obsolete, and firms must constantly differentiate their products from their competitors (Sørensen & Stuart, 2000). Existing research shows the importance of innovation, particularly exploratory innovations, for the continued viability of firms’ products and services in such an environment (Jansen et al., 2006; Levinthal & March 1993). We argue that industry turbulence has a positive moderating effect on the effect of M&As on product differentiation. High industry turbulence motivates firms to take advantage of M&As for product differentiation and helps them do so. First, in an environment where existing products and services quickly become obsolete, firms must provide a timelier response to the changes in their competitive environment (Xue et al., 2011). At the same time, such turbulence increases the uncertainty associated with mechanisms such as explorative innovation, through which firms respond to a rapidly changing competitive environment (Folta, 1998). Therefore, industry turbulence increases the effectiveness of M&As as a faster and less risky mechanism through which firms can differentiate their products and services from their competitors. Second, industry turbulence is associated with more start-ups and a higher likelihood of start-ups exiting (Hannan et al., 1995; Huyghebaert & Van de Gucht, 2004). The availability of many innovative products at their early stages and the ease of acquiring firms offering those products in a turbulent environment provide incumbents with more opportunities and options, differentiating their products from their competitors using M&As. Therefore, we argue that:

H4: The acquirer’s industry turbulence has a positive moderating effect on the positive effect of M&As on product differentiation for firms in IT industries.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Data and Sampling

To empirically test our hypotheses, we collected data from multiple sources. M&A transaction data was obtained from the SDC Platinum US M&A Database. We began with all M&A deals (whose acquirers are US publicly traded firms) recorded in the SDC database from 1996 to 2016 that could be matched with Compustat using the SDC DealNumber-GVKEY mapping developed by Ewens et al. (2019). We further selected transactions: (1) whose status was “completed,” (2) that were not stock repurchases, or (3) where the percentage of shares acquired was 100%.

We also limited our sample to IT industries, i.e., the acquirer had to be in one of the IT industries defined by Kim et al. (2016). This process resulted in 13,953 M&A transactions whose value transaction was reported. Values of the transaction were used to decide the significance of the M&A to the acquirer. Product differentiation data was derived from Hoberg and Phillips’s (2016) Text-Based Network Industry Classification (TNIC) library, which measures the degree to which the focal firm’s product portfolio differs from its competitors. Measures of variables are discussed in the following subsections.

## 3.1.1 Independent Variable

Unlike most M&A studies in the literature, the unit of analysis here is at the firm-year level such that, instead of examining each M&A at the transaction level, our independent variable is a dummy coding of a given firm-year indicating whether an IT firm has engaged in significant M&A(s) in that year. Using the M&A transactions identified, we coded the firm-years in our sample to either 1 if the focal firm i had made significant M&A(s) at year ?? or 0 otherwise. When selecting treatment firm-years, we required the transaction value of any M&A deal completed in a given year t to be at least 10% of the acquirer’s average market capitalization at t-1, t, and t+1 for this year to be considered as a year when the focal acquirer engaged in significant M&A(s). If so, this firm-year was considered a treatment firm-year. In addition, to minimize the confounding effect, we only considered firms as treatment firm candidates if they had not engaged in any significant M&A in the [t-3, t+3] window around the intervention year t. After applying the above criteria, we retained 752 qualifying treatment firm-years.

## 3.1.2 Dependent Variable

The traditional methods for measuring a firm’s product differentiation rely on static industry classifications, such as Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) or the North American Industry Classification System (NAICS) codes (Kim et al., 2021). For instance, Y. Li et al. (2019) considered the variations in the products of different firms within each industry. More precisely, they obtained the gross margin of firms in each fourdigit SIC industry code and divided the standard deviation of the gross margin by the average as a proxy of product differentiation by following Liu et al. (2013). Banker et al. (2022) defined a firm pursuing product differentiation based on the firm’s characteristics (e.g., the proportion of R&D expenses to sales and the number of employees divided by sales). More precisely, they ranked the firms by the values of the characteristics within two-digit SIC industry codes. However, the measures based on static industry classification have a limitation in that firms are grouped assuming that a product market is fixed. To state it differently, the measures cannot reflect the changes in the product market over time (Hoberg & Philips, 2016).

However, our paper focuses on the IT industries, which are highly competitive and rapidly changing. Thus, product differentiation should be measured by considering the dynamic nature of IT industries. In other words, the measures set by the SIC may not fully reflect IT firms’ dynamic strategies to differentiate their product from those of competitors. In contrast to the static industry classification, the TNIC is suited for fastmoving and competitive industries (Kim et al., 2016) because it is updated annually based on words in the firm’s 10-K product descriptions (Hoberg & Philips, 2016). More precisely, Hoberg and Phillips (2016) applied text parsing algorithms to the business descriptions in the public firms’ SEC 10-K annual filings, which are required by law to describe the significant products they offer to the market accurately. For each product description section, they parsed for each firm-year, a vector of product keywords (after removing common and top words in the description) that is analogous to the patent technology-based space in Jaffe (1986). For each pair of firms in Compustat, a firm-by-firm pairwise similarity score was then computed by calculating the cosine similarity score of the two vectors representing the product space of the two firms in that year. This process yielded a product similarity score for any two firms i and j that is a real number within the interval [0,1] and describes how similar the words used by firms i and j are. After calculating the product market similarity score for every possible pair of firms, Hoberg and Phillips (2016) constructed a TNIC based on the pairwise similarity scores. The TNIC records firms with pairwise similarity scores above a threshold selected based on the coarseness of the three-digit SIC classification data.

As a result, the TNIC facilitates capturing the dynamic nature of IT industries that stems from the entry of new firms or the introduction of new products. Therefore, we believe that Hoberg and Phillips’s average similarity score, which is based on the TNIC, is more appropriate for our study than other measures for product differentiation since it accounts for the time-varying nature of IT industries. In particular, we measured the product differentiation of a focal firm by reverse-coding the average product similarity score between the focal firm and all other firms in its firm-by-firm pairs in the TNIC database. We denoted this reverse-coded average product similarity score as pd\_avgps—a higher pd\_avgps value indicates that the focal firm has a higher product differentiation level than its peers in the TNIC industry.

## 3.1.3 Control Variable

Although a matching procedure was applied to control for firm heterogeneities, it was still necessary to include a set of control variables in our analyses. First, the four variables used in the matching process (see Section 3.2 for details)—firm size, return on assets (ROA), Tobin’s q, and cash at year t-1 were controlled for. Other innovation modes that might impact the outcome of product differentiation, including the number of strategic alliance partners, joint venture partners, and R&D alliances, were also considered. Furthermore, we controlled for the firm’s diversification and market share, as a more diversified firm with a larger market share might be more likely to perform M&As and may have different product and service offerings compared to its industry competitors. Finally, we controlled for industry concentration, as a low industry concentration might be associated with different products and service offerings by industry players and a large number of M&As at the same time. Table 3 provides detailed descriptions of all the variables used in this study and Table 4 summarizes their statistics. We present the correlation matrix in Table 5 and examine the correlation between the independent variables using the variance inflation factor (VIF) test shown in Table 6. According to the VIF test, there are unlikely to be any multicollinearity problems in our models.

## 3.2 Identification Strategy for Causal Inference

To test our hypotheses, we would ideally compare the product differentiation of a firm that engaged in significant M&A(s) (i.e., y(1)—the potential outcome for the treatment) and a firm that did not engage in M&A(s) (i.e., y(0)—the potential outcome for the control) within the same firm. However, it is not possible to observe both outcomes because of the fundamental problem of causal inference (Rubin, 2006); for any firm, we can only observe either y(1) or y(0). For example, if a firm has experienced one or more M&As, then only the product differentiation under the treatment can be observed. The product differentiation under the control (that experienced no M&As) would be missing.

Moreover, the fact that the treatment of engaging in significant M&As is endogenous creates challenges when comparing the product differentiation (or the performance in general) of M&As. Specifically, simple comparisons of product differentiation between firms that engaged in M&As and firms that did not are statistically problematic because factors that might impact the M&A decision could also impact the outcome variable of the product differentiation. Thus, it is not easy to conclusively prove that an M&A did indeed result in a higher level of product differentiation. In this context, the central question is how to select a more reliable control group (i.e., a firm that did not engage in M&As). The validity of the estimation hinges on the choice of control units. To address this problem, we used the following procedure to select potential control firm-years to match with our 752 treatment firm-years: for every firm-year when there was significant M&A activity (as defined in Section 3.1.1), we found a group of comparable control firm-years with a similar product portfolio within the focal acquirer’s TNIC industry in the same year, as defined by Hoberg and Philips (2016). For example, if Firm A engaged in a significant M&A in 2005, we checked the TNIC database for all firm pairs containing Firm A in 2005 and kept al of its “peers” deemed to be in the same TNIC industry as potential control firm candidates. We set a threshold for a pairwise product similarity score of 0.05 to decide whether to include a firm as a control candidate because that was the median value in the TNIC database. Similar to the restriction imposed when selecting treatment firmyears, we required any control firm-year to not have any M&A in the [t-3, t+3] window in order to be considered a control firm candidate. This process yielded 5,520 potential control firm-years that we ran propensity score matching (PSM) on, along with their treatment counterparts. Before running PSM, we further excluded firm-years that lacked any pre- or post-intervention observation of our dependent variable of interest (i.e., product differentiation, see Section 3.1.2 for details), resulting in our final cross-sectional sample with 655 treatment firm-years and 4,251 control firm-years.

Next, we applied the propensity score matching methodology (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983; Imbens & Wooldridge, 2009) following prior studies (Rishika & Ramaprasad, 2019). We used the psmatch2 command in Stata (kernel method) to conduct propensity score matching on our treatment and control firm-year candidates. Pre-intervention covariates that might impact the treatment status (i.e., whether the focal firm had any significant M&A) were used in this matching procedure. More precisely, we matched on the focal firm’s size, ROA, Tobin’s q, cash, level of diversification, market share in its industry, its industry concentration, the number of strategic alliances, joint ventures, and the R&D agreements it had, all at t-1. Every treatment firm-year had a weight of 1, and a control firm-year might have been matched to multiple treatment firm-years depending on its proximity to its treatment counterparts in terms of matching covariates. For each cross-sectional firm-year retained, we built a panel data set around the intervention year t by including observations from up to t-3 to up to t+3. Notably, we adapted previous literature discussing the effects of M&As in selecting a three-year window (e.g., Rahman & Lambkin, 2015; Bianconi & Tan, 2019). In the long run, M&A effects may disappear or be contaminated due to environmental uncertainty. All the panel data sets were combined to build our primary panel data set for DID panel regression analyses. As a robustness check, we also constructed an alternative sample after propensity score matching on only the focal firm’s size, ROA, Tobin’s q, and cash, and found consistent results.

## 4.3 Robustness Checks

## 4.3.1 Common Trend Assumption

The appropriateness of the DiD approach relies on the common trend assumption. When this assumption is not satisfied, DiD yields a biased estimate because any changes unrelated to the event—in this case, the M&A, will be omitted, thus obscuring the full impact of the event. We therefore tested the common trend assumption using the didq command in Stata.<sup>1</sup> This test enabled us to determine whether our samples satisfied the common trend assumption when using the DiD model with several pre-intervention periods in the data. Following Mora and Reggio (2015), since three pre-intervention periods were included in our data set, the test for the common trend was a test for the joint equivalence of parallel-1, parallel-2, and parallel-3, where parallel-q indicates the q years prior to the event of an M&A; for example, parallel-2 signifies 2 years prior to the intervention.<sup>2</sup> Table 8 reports the results of the two tests for the equivalence of the parallel assumptions. The first test assessed the equivalence of parallel-1 and parallel-2 in line $q = 2$ . The second test assessed the equivalence of parallel-2 and parallel-3 in line $q = 3 .$ . At a significance level of 0.05, we failed to reject the null hypotheses $( p = 0 . 2 6 2 \mathrm { i n } q = 2 ; p =$ 0.267 in $q = 3 ) ;$ ; the ??-values are reported in brackets. This indicated that the standard DiD approach was indeed appropriate for this study because we could not reject the common trend assumption and equal treatment effects of M&As. This means that the matching method successfully eliminated the systematic difference between the treatment and control groups.

## 4.3.2 Alternative Matching Method: Coarsened Exact Matching

Despite the popularity of PSM as a matching method, PSM has its limitations—it does not guarantee the reduction of systematic differences (i.e., imbalance) across treatment and control groups after matching (King & Nielson, 2019). Notably, since the goal of matching methods is to balance the covariates’ distributions across treatment and control groups, it is vital to check whether the covariates’ distributions across the two groups are balanced in the matched sample. Thus, we employed the alternative matching method, i.e., coarsened exact matching (CEM), and compared the multivariate imbalance measures $L _ { 1 }$ after PSM and CEM. The $L _ { 1 }$ measure, proposed by Iacus et al. (2011), shows the extent of the overlap of the multivariate distributions between treatment and control groups. A smaller value indicates greater overlap, and thus indicates greater balance between the two groups.

We found that the matched samples after applying PSM and CEM provided similar $L _ { 1 }$ measures as shown in Table 9. Specifically, the raw data had a multivariate imbalance measure of 0.997, and after PSM and CEM, it was reduced to 0.463 and 0.435, respectively. In addition, Figure 2 shows the propensity score distributions before and after PSM. We found that the propensity scores after PSM were well-balanced across the treatment and control groups.

As a robustness check, we reported the main results based on the matched sample using CEM in Table 10. After applying CEM, we found broadly consistent results for all our hypotheses. For instance, the effect of the event of M&A(s) on product differentiation is statistically significant and positive $( \delta = 0 . 0 0 2 , p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). The moderating effect of the in-house innovation capability and exploitation strategy, respectively, on the relationship between significant M&A activity and product differentiation is statistically significant and positive $( \delta =$ 0.045 $p = 0 . 0 4 7 ; \delta = 0 . 0 0 4 , p = 0 . 0 0 4$ respectively). Consistent with the main results, H4 is not supported $( \delta = 0 . 0 0 2 , p = 0 . 6 3 3 )$ .

## 4.3.4 Accounting for Unobserved Time-Variant Effects

We also tested all models using interactive fixed effects (IFE) to account for unobserved time-variant effects. A key issue in causal evaluation is that the treatment and outcomes may be associated with each other, owing to the presence of unobservables. It should be acknowledged that, when using our M&A data, unobserved factors characterizing firms may be time-variant confounders (i.e., interactions between time- and unit-specific effects; time-variant constructs) because the underlying cycles of the economic activities of firms are likely to be multiple. In this case, IFE models are aimed precisely at allowing the set of unobserved heterogeneity terms that are controlled for to have a moderately large dimension (Bai, 2009). To state it differently, a primary objective of this analysis is to control, through statistical adjustment, the potential biasing effects of unobserved confounders.

Furthermore, the estimated IFE approach extends the conventional DiD model by accounting for unobserved confounders of outcomes that vary with time. If unobserved confounders are assumed to be constant in time, then the conventional DiD model provides unbiased estimates by differencing out the confounders; the DiD specification relies on the assumption that the average value of the control firms provides a good counterfactual for the average of the treatment firms. In practice, however, these confounders may vary with time, especially when researchers have more than two time periods of panel data. It is known that the DiD approach produces biased estimates in the presence of unobserved timevariant confounders between the two groups because the above DiD assumption may not hold (Xu, 2017; Arkhangelsky et al., 2021). However, the IFE approach allows unobserved confounders to vary in time. Thus, we used the IFE approach as a robustness check. To identify the causal effect of M&As on product differentiation, we estimated IFE regression of the following form by following Garthwaite et al. (2014):

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \delta_ {i t} + \delta \mathrm{T} _ {\mathrm{i}} \times \mathrm{A} _ {\mathrm{t}} + e _ {i t},
$$

where the firm-fixed effects $( \alpha _ { i } ) .$ , year-fixed effects $( \gamma _ { t } )$ , their interaction $( \delta _ { i t }$ ; time-variant unobserved effects), and an error term $( e _ { i t } )$ are assumed to be uncorrelated with the other unobserved determinants of the outcome variable. In this setting, we believe that the time-variant unobserved factor causes the systematic difference between the treatment and control groups and the violation of the parallel assumption in the DiD model. The results from the IFE model are broadly consistent with the hybrid approach that combines the matching method and DiD model. It is important to note that these findings are another indication that the matching method was successful in this study. As shown in Table 12, the results are consistent with those of the main models.

## 4.3.6 Alternative Time Windows for Sample Selection

As mentioned earlier, we constructed our sample with three-year windows to avoid any confounding effects due to environmental uncertainty. For robustness checks, we also tested the hypotheses with the samples constructed based on four- and five-year windows and found that the effects were statistically insignificant. The results may be due to the fact that the effect of M&As disappears in the long run, consistent with the findings from previous literature (Rahman & Lambkin, 2015; Bianconi & Tan, 2019).

## 5 Post Hoc Analysis

To better understand the role of M&As on product differentiation related to other innovation strategies, we conducted a post hoc analysis to uncover the effects of in-house innovation (i.e., patent stock) and interfirm relationships (i.e., strategic alliance and joint venture) on product differentiation. We used the sample of firms from the periods not affected by M&As to exclude the possibility of increasing product differentiation after an M&A occured. Control variables included firm size, ROA, cash, Tobin’s q,

R&D agreements, market share, industry concentration, and diversification. The model specification is as follows:

$$
y _ {i t} = \alpha_ {i} + \gamma_ {t} + \delta S _ {i t} + X _ {i t} \lambda + e _ {i t},
$$

where $y _ { i t }$ indicates product differentiation, $\alpha _ { i }$ and $\gamma _ { t }$ represent firm- and time-fixed effects, respectively, $S _ { i t }$ represents innovation strategies (i.e., patent stock, strategic alliance, and joint venture), $X _ { i t }$ indicates the set of control variables, and $e _ { i t }$ is an error term. The coefficient of $S _ { i t } ~ \left( \mathrm { i . e . , } ~ \delta \right)$ captures the effects of innovation strategies on product differentiation for firm ?? . As shown in Table 15, we found that other innovation strategies did not have any significant relationship with product differentiation.

Even though it would be interesting to examine the relative effectiveness of M&As in driving product differentiation compared to other innovation strategies, they are not comparable since M&As are events whereas others are not. Thus, we instead focused on whether M&As and other innovation strategies have synergy effects to increase product differentiation. Notably, from the results for H2, we confirmed that in-house innovation is positively related to the relationship between M&As and product differentiation.

## 6 Discussion

M&As serve as a critical and popular means to acquire knowledge, human capital, products, and innovations from external resources through the expansion of firm boundaries. This approach is desirable for IT firms because of the nature of these industries. Drawing on product differentiation theory, we argue that product differentiation is one of the most important strategic options to improve competitiveness, enabling firms in IT industries to survive and grow. We further posit that the characteristics of IT industries such as hypercompetition, distributed innovation, the modularity of products, and the tacit nature of knowledge in these industries make M&As a vital choice that firms should consider if they want to achieve product differentiation from their competitors in a timely and efficient manner. Our empirical analysis reveals the positive effect of the acquisition of external firms on improving product differentiation in the IT bidding firms and a positive moderation effect of in-house innovation capability and exploitation strategy on that relationship. IT firms with good innovation performance internally and an exploitation innovation orientation tend to be better at leveraging newly acquired resources to differentiate themselves from their competitors in the product market.

This paper contributes to the IT innovation literature in four specific ways. First, the findings of this study support our contention that product differentiation is a key performance indicator for IT companies. Traditionally, the IS literature has used firms’ patenting performance to measure firms’ innovation output (Kleis et al., 2012; Xue et al., 2012), product innovation (Foerderer et al., 2018), R&D intensity and returns to R&D as proxies of firms’ product innovation (Rahmati et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2012), and marketoriented measures as a gauge to assess how these innovations are valued by the market (Han et al., 2012). In line with the recent IT innovation literature (Kim et al., 2021), our study focuses directly on product differentiation as a source of competitive advantage in IT industries. Our novel theoretical and empirical approach in exploring product differentiation creates essential opportunities for future research, as it highlights the need for future research in this area to address product differentiation in IT industries in an explicit manner. For example, future studies can explore the specific mechanisms that mediate the effect of M&As on product differentiation.

For example, is the effect mediated by enhancing existing product offerings through absorbing the targets’ intellectual property and organizational capabilities or by diversifying product lines using newly acquired products and services?

Second, our study is the first to explore the potentially crucial role of M&As in IT firms’ innovation. To date, the IT literature on M&As has, by and large, focused on the financial performance of IT industries following M&As by looking at factors that affect the integration of the newly acquired resources (Benitez et al., 2018) or the complementary factors likely to enhance their value (Tanriverdi & Uysal, 2011). As a result, the IS literature on IT innovation provides only minimal insights regarding M&As as an essential mechanism for absorbing and commercializing external innovation. Hence, while this literature explores the effect of firms’ different modes of innovation, such as platforms (Foerderer et al., 2018), the role of venture capital (Kim et al., 2016), in-house innovation (Kleis et al., 2012), and strategic alliances (Han et al., 2012), little attention has been paid to the reasons why M&As are potentially so compelling. Our study contributes to the IS literature, as it joins these two streams of IS research and explores how M&As by IT firms can enhance product innovation. Our results show that the acquired knowledge, human capital, and products and innovations gained via M&As serve as “resources” for bidding firms that will enable them to gain competitive advantages in the product market. Our findings suggest that M&As are an effective way for a bidding firm to increase product differentiation from their competitors, an important factor for success.

Third, our results suggest that internal innovation and orientation output complement acquired knowledge and know-how. This is an important finding, as it supports recent reports in the IT innovation literature investigating the interaction between internal and external innovation (Rahmati et al., 2021). Our findings suggest that acquirers who invest in and commit to internal research and development can better leverage their newly acquired resources. Further, our results show that M&As have complementarities with an exploitative innovation strategy, hence helping firms explore external innovations to achieve ambidexterity. Our results thus point to the broader role of in-house R&D investments in IT industries, showing that such investments can help firms assimilate and commercialize external R&D. Future studies can build on this contribution by exploring how internal innovation can enhance the value of acquired resources. For example, is the moderating effect of inhouse innovation on M&As shaped by the activities of innovative incumbent IT firms to undermine competition from smaller start-ups, implying that firms that are better at dismantling their competition will be better at commercializing their internal innovation into new products and services? Or are we observing the effect of internal knowledge and know-how on the ability of IT firms to assimilate and commercialize external knowledge and know-how? Also, our findings suggest that having an exploitation-oriented innovation strategy complements M&As for product differentiation.

Finally, our results contribute to the discussion of IS literature on the effect of environmental turbulence on the value of resource combinations (Chung et al., 2019; Havakhor et al., 2019; Mithas et al., 2013; Xue et al., 2011). Building on and extending the IS literature exploring the link between innovation output and orientation, industrial environments, and firms’ outcomes (Chung et al., 2019), our hypotheses demonstrate the importance of M&As in turbulent environments. However, we failed to conclude that M&As might have higher effectiveness as a mechanism for increasing firms’ product differentiation in industries with a high rate of change in terms of their players, customer demands, and technologies.

Our study will also prove to be valuable to practitioners. First, our contributions have practical value, as they relate to the current debates surrounding the competitive environment in IT industries. While market regulators’ concerns regarding the way that IT firms use M&As to kill their competition have been growing during the past decade, our results highlight the role of M&As as a vital source of innovation in IT industries. We show that while mega-cap IT firms might be using M&As to acquire and dismantle the start-ups that could potentially become major competitors to their products and services, taking a broader view, within IT industries, M&As are used to complement in-house innovation for fueling product innovation. This highlights the importance of our results from a public policy perspective. Second, M&A decisions are not always made rationally; therefore, our study provides helpful information regarding the managerial implications of M&A activity and can help IT firm executives make better, more confident decisions on M&As, especially when undertaken to improve their firm’s product market performance. Third, the moderation effect of internal R&D also has important implications for the IT firm managers who must determine the optimal levels of resource allocations for internal versus external innovation initiatives, especially those close to the firm’s boundary.

This study is not without limitations and provides many opportunities for future research to fill in the gaps in our understanding. The focus of our theoretical development and empirical analysis in this paper is to create a link between M&As and product differentiation in IT industries. However, the IS literature contains extensive discussions of multiple potentially competing mechanisms, such as internal innovation, platform innovation, open innovation, and strategic alliances, all of which can help firms compete in IT industries by creating advantages in terms of their products and services, relative to their competitors. Our study is limited in its focus and does not include any comparisons with these competing mechanisms. Although comparisons of the relative impacts of M&As and strategic alliances or other forms of external innovation have been widely reported in the strategic management literature (Hagedoorn & Duysters, 2002), the IS literature has yet to shine a spotlight on this area to explore how these mechanisms interact with each other. Further, while product innovation and differentiation are closely related concepts, a direct relationship between the two cannot be assumed, as product differentiation can result from factors other than innovation, i.e., marketing or organizational differences. However, we were unable to directly address and explore the link between the two constructs due to a lack of data on product innovation (e.g., product updates, new product developments) for the firms in our sample.

Moreover, firms may still face some risks after M&As, implying that M&As do not always provide opportunities for innovation. One of the risks that the literature on M&As has discussed is cultural risk, which occurs when an acquirer and a targeting firm exhibit cultural differences. Notably, cultural differences are considered one of the important factors in determining performance after an M&A (Teerikangas & Very, 2006). For example, Sarala (2010) examined antecedents of intergroup conflicts after merging an acquirer and a target firm, finding that cultural differences and acculturation at an organizational level are vital to explaining the conflict. However, the effect of cultural differences remains controversial. Bauer and Matzler (2014) showed that M&As with a high level of culture overlap could result in a low degree of formal integration because they tend to rely on informal communication. In addition to cultural risks, organizational characteristics can introduce risks that prevent the creation of synergy. Steigenberger (2017) showed that employee resistance could also lower the possibility of realizing synergy between firms.

In the IS literature, it has been argued that the effectiveness of M&As is premised on the extent of IS integration, defined as the process of alignment after M&As (Sarrazin & West, 2011; Henningsson & Kettinger, 2017). However, because of the need for an alignment process (i.e., coordination), acquirers may be exposed to negative consequences from the integration. Henningsson and Kettinger (2017) provided a taxonomy for possible deficiencies in IS integration. The deficiencies stem from operational inefficiencies, temporary disturbance due to the merging process, unrealized benefits, employee resistance to using new IS, delays in realizing synergy, and excessive resource use.

Accordingly, M&As may not necessarily result in positive outcomes, especially in terms of innovation. Zollo and Singh (2004) showed that acquisition could hinder innovativeness during the process of coordination, especially when the replacement of the top management team in the target firm is simultaneously undertaken. In addition, Ahuja and Katila (2001) found that innovation capacity after M&As depends on whether technology is an important acquirer asset. They showed that for nontechnological firms, M&As do not have a positive impact on innovation. Meanwhile, Wu et al. (2023) found that even if an acquirer and a target firm have some common technologies, the power imbalance between firms may weaken the effect of their technological similarities on innovation. Taken together, acquirers may experience several risks after M&As, but the extent of such risks may be less than that of in-house innovation and interfirm partnerships. In certain circumstances, M&As may fail to provide opportunities for innovation.

## About the Authors

Kangkang Qi is an assistant professor of information systems management in the Department of Business Analytics & Information Systems at the Raymond J. Harbert College of Business of Auburn University. His research interests include IT management, entrepreneurship, healthcare IT, social media, the economics of information systems, and business analytics. His work has appeared in the Journal of the Association for Information Systems and in the proceedings of several leading conference proceedings, such as the Workshop on Information Systems and Economics (WISE) and the INFORMS Conference on Information Systems and Technology (CIST). Dr. Qi obtained his PhD in business administration (IT management) from Michigan State University and his master’s degree in information systems and operations management from the University of Florida; he completed his bachelor of science degree in computer science in China.

Sumin Han is an assistant professor in the Department of Business Analytics & Information Systems at the Raymond J. Harbert College of Business, Auburn University. She earned her PhD in business analytics from the University of Tennessee at Knoxville. Her current research focuses on dynamic panel data analysis, joint models, consumer behavior, and international business research. Her work has appeared in peer-reviewed journals such as the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, the Journal of Banking and Finance, the Journal of Business Research, the Public Administration Review, and the European Journal of Marketing.

Yeasung Jeong is an assistant professor in the Information Systems and Business Analytics Department at the State University of New York (SUNY), Albany. Her research interests are in business analytics, including statistical modeling for causal inference, optimization, and learning algorithms, IT governance change, and IT adoption. She has had publications in Machine Learning with Applications and Physica A: Statistical Mechanics and its Applications.

Pouya Rhamati is a transfer pricing manager at Deloitte Tax, LLP, where he specializes in intangible property transactions in multinational enterprises. Prior to joining Deloitte, he was an assistant professor of management information systems in the Terry College of Business, University of Georgia. His research, which focuses on codeveloping digital products in complex economic and organizational environments, employs empirical analyses to identify and quantify the drivers of firms’ success in developing digital products and services. His work has appeared in journals such as MIS Quarterly and Journal of the Association for Information Systems. He completed his PhD at the University of Illinois at Chicago.

Anjana Susarla is the Omura-Saxena Professor of Responsible AI at the Eli Broad College of Business at Michigan State University. She earned an undergraduate degree in mechanical engineering from the Indian Institute of Technology, Chennai; a graduate degree in business administration from the Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta; and a Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Texas at Austin. Her research interests include the economics of information systems, social media analytics, and the economics of artificial intelligence. Her work has appeared in several academic journals, such as Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Information Systems Research, and in the proceedings of leading peer-reviewed conferences, such as the Academy of Management Conference, Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining, International Conference in Information Systems, and International Conference in Learning Representations. She has served or serves on the editorial boards of Information Systems Research and MIS Quarterly. Her research has been funded by prestigious organizations such as the National Institute of Health (NIH).

Vallabh “Samba” Sambamurthy is the Albert O. Nicholas Dean of the Wisconsin School of Business at the University of Wisconsin. He is a leading expert on how businesses compete in the digital economy. He has published his work in several leading journals and served on the editorial boards of premier academic journals, including two terms as editor-in-chief of Information Systems Research, and has held leadership roles in professional associations. Sambamurthy has garnered several recognitions for contributions to teaching, research, outreach, and mentoring. The Association of Information Systems honored him with the LEO Lifetime Achievement Award. He was selected as a distinguished fellow of the Information Systems Society at INFORMS and received the William Beal Distinguished Faculty Award—the highest honor of Michigan State University. Sambamurthy received his PhD from the University of Minnesota, his MBA from the Indian Institute of Management, and his BE from the National Institute of Technology (Tiruchirappalli, India).
