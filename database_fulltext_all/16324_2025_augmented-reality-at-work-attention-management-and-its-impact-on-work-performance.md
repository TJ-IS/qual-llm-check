# AUGMENTED REALITY AT WORK: ATTENTION MANAGEMENT AND ITS IMPACT ON WORK PERFORMANCE<sup>1</sup>

Runge Zhu Department of Management Science, School of Management Science and Engineering, Central University of Finance and Economics, Beijing, CHINA {zhurg@cufe.edu.cn}

Cheng Yi Department of Management Science and Engineering, School of Economics and Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, CHINA {yich@sem.tsinghua.edu.cn}

Ting Li Department of Technology and Operations Management, Rotterdam School of Management, Erasmus University, Rotterdam, THE NETHERLANDS {tli@rsm.nl}

Augmented reality (AR) is rapidly emerging as a transformative display technology, blending computergenerated content with the real-world environment in real time. Using divided attention theory, this study investigates how different information delivery channels (i.e., AR vs. mobile phone) and the nature of information (i.e., dependence on specific physical context and complexity) affect work performance. A field experiment in the aircraft maintenance context demonstrates that the effect on work performance of providing information via AR vs. a mobile phone is mediated by work attentiveness. The findings reveal that the effectiveness of AR is particularly pronounced when information is highly dependent on the specific physical context but diminishes when information complexity is high. This research deepens our understanding of how presenting information directly in front of users’ eyes (i.e., via AR) affects their attention management and work performance. The findings have significant implications for firms in terms of how to leverage AR to enhance work performance in industrial settings.

Keywords: Augmented reality, divided attention, field experiment, information complexity, information dependency, work attentiveness, work performance

## Introduction

In many industrial settings, workers are required to maintain a high level of focus and ensure a high degree of performance. For example, aircraft maintenance mechanics must perform precise procedures when inspecting aircraft to ensure flight safety (see Figure 1). These tasks often require following critical maintenance instructions provided via paper or digital guides, forcing workers to frequently shift their attention between physical operations and instructional materials. This constant attention switching can disrupt focus, hinder information processing, and negatively affect performance. Hence, firms are actively seeking more seamless methods for delivering information (Galluch et al., 2015; Puranik et al., 2020; Weinert et al., 2022).

In recent years, firms have started to explore augmented reality (AR) technology for delivering real-time information during inspection and maintenance tasks (Overby, 2019; Li et al., 2022). Unlike paper documents or traditional digital devices, AR presents information in proximity to physical objects via see-through screens (Lee et al., 2019; 2021).<sup>2</sup>

This allows users to maintain their central vision on physical tasks while processing information on the see-through screen, which may fall outside their direct line of sight, in what is known as peripheral vision (Chaturvedi et al., 2019). For example, at DHL, the world’s leading logistics company, warehouse workers wear AR glasses that display real-time picking information, such as product location and quantity, adjacent to the products they are locating, sorting, and moving.<sup>3</sup> However, the effectiveness of AR compared to traditional digital devices in facilitating work remains unknown. Given AR’s unique capability to overlay information near physical objects and our limited understanding of how people process concurrent physical and virtual information, research is needed to evaluate AR’s performance relative to conventional digital channels. These findings can also help firms make informed decisions about their choice of information delivery systems.

This study examines how AR affects users’ work performance compared to traditional mobile channels, particularly the mobile phone, and explores the underlying mechanisms. We define work performance as the extent to which workers act according to standards (Campbell & Wiernik, 2015). Prior research has demonstrated AR’s advantages over paper-based instructions in operation acuity (Elmi-Terander et al., 2020), productivity (Wuttke et al., 2022), and on-site problem solving (Sara et al., 2022). These studies have focused primarily on AR’s ability to deliver real-time, multimodal information, which enhances search efficiency and information richness. However, they have not isolated the effects of information display location—a feature unique to AR—from the nature of information content typically conveyed through digital channels. Hence, there remains limited theoretical understanding of the benefits and limitations of AR’s distinctive capability to display information in spatial context near physical objects. By comparing AR with the mobile phone—another digital channel that also delivers accurate real-time information but differs in display location—this study aims to clearly delineate the effect of information display location on users’ information processing and task performance. This focus also has practical significance, as mobile phones remain the predominant tool in industrial workplaces during digital transformation (Saarikko et al., 2020) and will continue to be used alongside AR in the coming decade.

We draw upon research on divided attention (Kahneman, 1973) and dual-task interference (Pashler, 1994) to understand the effects of AR. These streams of literature suggest that when people switch their attention between multiple tasks, their performance declines due to competition for attentional resources. In the current context, we expect that using a mobile phone will require users to shift their attention between virtual information and physical tasks, while AR will be able to leverage users’ peripheral vision to draw attention more seamlessly, potentially leading to enhanced task performance (Chaturvedi et al., 2019; Mayer, 2002). Research on dual-task interference further suggests that the complexity and relationship of tasks will determine the extent to which they compete for attention (Pashler, 1994; Jenkins et al., 2016). Accordingly, we also examine how the effect of AR varies depending on the types of information delivered: the complexity of virtual information, defined as the number of information components (Wood, 1986), and the dependency of virtual information on the immediate physical environment, defined as the relevance of information to the specific physical context in which it is used (Steffen et al., 2019; Smith and Vela, 2001). While information complexity directly affects users’ cognitive processing load, dependency determines the need for users to process information in relation to the immediate physical task. Together, these factors allow us to further understand how different information channels influence attention switching behavior and the subsequent impacts.

We thus ask the following research questions: (1) How does AR, in comparison a with mobile phone, affect users’ work performance, and why? (2) How does this effect vary based on the types of information presented? We partnered with a major airline in China and conducted a field experiment in the context of aircraft maintenance. At the time of the study, the company was undergoing digital transformation, exploring how to employ digital technologies to guide and assist aircraft maintenance mechanics. Airplane maintenance involves a series of complex procedures, with safety inspection during transit being one of the most frequent tasks. Specifically, a maintenance mechanic must perform a comprehensive aircraft inspection after every landing to guarantee the safety of the subsequent flight. In our study, this inspection task comprised over 50 steps. Despite their training, mechanics still need to follow the step-by-step instructional information (traditionally in the form of a paper brochure) during inspection procedures to ensure flight safety. We worked closely with the IT department and experienced engineers to incorporate this information, along with record-keeping functions, into AR and mobile phones. We also designed varying levels of information complexity and dependency for specific procedures based on the paper instructions.

For over three months (from September to December 2021), we worked with the aircraft maintenance team and collected extensive data on how mechanics used digital tools to perform transit inspection tasks. Specifically, the AR glasses recorded first-person perspective videos, showcasing the visual inspection patterns, while a research assistant filmed thirdperson perspective videos, capturing the mechanics’ actions during inspections. Using a combination of manual coding and machine learning techniques for video analysis, we examined whether the visual inspection patterns and the actions performed adhered to the standards. This approach provided a relatively accurate measure of performance (Puranik et al., 2020). We also distributed questionnaires after each inspection task.

Our data analysis revealed compelling insights into the use of AR vs. mobile phones for guiding workers through tasks. We found that the effect of information delivery methods (AR vs. mobile phone) on work performance was mediated by work attentiveness. Specifically, the benefits of AR were more pronounced when information was highly dependent on the immediate physical context, demonstrating its effectiveness in context-heavy environments. However, when the information became more complex, the positive impact of AR significantly diminished, highlighting users’ limitations in processing complex information via peripheral attention. Interestingly, we also found that although information was provided only for selected task steps, the attention-enhancing effects of AR carried over to the two following steps, which had no direct instructions. These findings remained robust across various validations, including alternative measurements of the dependent variables and subsample analyses.

Our work contributes to the literature in several ways. First, while prior research on AR has mainly focused on information content characteristics, our study advances the theoretical understanding of AR’s spatial display capability. By applying divided attention theory, we reveal how AR, compared to a mobile phone, reduces attention switching to improve performance and identify boundary conditions of information dependency and complexity. These insights create new pathways for IS and HCI researchers to investigate the business value of AR and optimize information design. Second, our investigation extends divided attention theory beyond traditional contexts by exploring the relationship between AR use, attention management, and work performance. Grounded in the tenets of divided attention, our research provides initial evidence that using central and peripheral attention can reduce switching costs and facilitate work, and highlights the constraints of peripheral information processing. This raises new questions about how different spatial display configurations might shape cognitive resource allocation. Practically, our work provides empirical evidence for potential adopters regarding the effectiveness of AR in delivering context-specific information. However, it also cautions that AR display design should carefully consider both information complexity and its relationship to the physical context.

## Literature Review

## Related Research on Augmented Reality

AR technology takes different forms and has been increasingly applied in contexts such as e-commerce, medical treatment, and the industrial workplace (e.g., Morimoto et al., 2022; Uhm et al., 2022). One stream of research has focused on mobile AR. For example, in ecommerce, consumers can view virtual products, such as furniture, with their real physical environments as backgrounds via mobile cameras. In this case, users’ main task is to evaluate the virtual products on the mobile screen. Research has shown that an immersive product experience enhances users’ product understanding and evaluation (e.g., Rauschnabel et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2022).

In other contexts, such as industrial training and maintenance, users need to focus on their physical tasks while virtual information to facilitate the tasks is delivered via hands-free AR glasses or headsets. This is the focal scenario of the current study. In recent years, researchers have paid increasing attention to the adoption and effectiveness of AR in this context (John et al., 2022; Schein & Rauschnabel, 2023; Steffen et al., 2019; Windhausen et al., 2024). Most studies have found that using AR to provide information can enhance work productivity (Vanneste et al., 2020; Windhausen et al., 2024; Wuttke et al., 2022). For example, Wuttke et al. (2022) showed that using AR increased workers’ productivity by 43.8% compared with using paper-based instructions due to the provision of realtime instructions. Vanneste et al. (2020) revealed that AR reduced error rates in training compared to oral or paper instructions by providing more vivid guidance. However, several studies have identified potential negative impacts of AR on users’ attention to physical stimuli and work efficiency (Drouot et al., 2021; Schein & Rauschnabel, 2023), suggesting that AR-displayed information may be distracting. Overall, prior studies have not separated the effects of information display location, which is unique to AR, from information characteristics common to digital channels. There remains a lack of comprehensive understanding about whether and when information provision via AR versus traditional digital channels can facilitate work.

A broader review of the literature on human-computer interaction (HCI) and cognitive science also failed to fully answer this question. Studies have explored the potential of using AR to direct attention in a physical space (Biocca et al., 2007; Lystbæk et al., 2024) and the design of simple near-eye information (Alves et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2021). These studies underscore the importance of considering AR’s unique capability—positioning virtual information in close proximity to physical objects—in information design. Nonetheless, they fall short of providing a theoretical understanding of how information display via AR shapes users’ attention management and information processing. A complete review of the AR literature is summarized in Appendix A.

To understand how users process virtual information (via AR or other channels) while conducting a physical task, we need a fundamental understanding of human attention and information processing. Hence, we next review theories of attention and dual-task processing.

## Theoretical Foundation and Related HCI Research

Divided attention theory describes attention as finite mental resources that can be allocated across different activities (Kahneman, 1973). Users often need to shift their focus of attention and alternate between different stimuli (Parasuraman, 1998). For example, individuals may need to alternately learn (e.g., read a recipe) and perform (e.g., cook). However, switching attention between tasks incurs mental costs and often reduces performance because the brain requires time and effort to adjust to each new task (Elchlepp et al., 2017). Specifically, when switching between related tasks, such as reading a recipe on a tablet and cooking, users must remember their progress and retain information from the first task. As they transition to the next task, their brain must backtrack, recall where it left off in this task, and then apply the new information from the first task. This attention-shifting process relies heavily on short-term memory, which is responsible for receiving and processing information over brief periods (Cowan, 1993). A key limitation of short-term memory is its limited capacity, which increases the likelihood of information loss during attention switching (Elchlepp et al., 2017). Indeed, modern workplaces are plagued with high levels of attention switching, which can negatively impact productivity.

A highly related stream of literature focuses on dual-task interference (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018; Pashler, 1994). This stream of literature specifically examines how the nature of the task affects the allocation of the user’s mental capacity, thereby influencing the extent of attention switching and thus interference between tasks (Jenkins et al., 2016). Two important task characteristics stand out. First, the complexity of tasks, which reflects the degree of intricacy involved in completing a task, affects the interference between tasks (Pashler, 1994). When tasks become more complex, attention switching is more likely to occur, and task performance will likely suffer (Wickens & Seidler, 1997). Second, the relationship between the tasks also affects the level of interference. Specifically, when processing two congruent tasks, which share common elements and use similar cognitive processes or context, users’ need to reorient mental resources is lower (Barsalou, 1982; Hazeltine et al., 2002). Users are thus more likely to handle both tasks simultaneously, leading to enhanced performance (Navon & Miller, 2002).

Recent research has highlighted the potential of leveraging peripheral attention in dual-task contexts. Peripheral attention enables an individual to process spatially close information concurrently with a main task. This capability can reduce central attention switching, thereby alleviating task interference. Specifically, the human field of vision can be classified into central vision (i.e., around 10 degrees to the right and left horizontally as well as up and down vertically from the center of the visual field) and peripheral vision (i.e., outside of the central field) (Chaturvedi et al., 2019). Central visual attention can process a large amount of detail but can only be devoted to one particular piece of information at a time. Peripheral vision has lower information processing acuity but a large detecting field; as such, it can perceive global information such as environmental cues (Carlson et al., 2006; Caves et al., 2018). Hence, an activity can be performed in peripheral attention when another activity is being performed simultaneously in central attention (Bakker et al., 2015). For example, when driving a car, a driver’s central attention is allocated to the road ahead, but her peripheral attention can perceive information from the windows, rear-view mirrors, and the instrument panel. In this case, peripheral visual information serves as an “external memory” that can be easily accessed simultaneously when another main task is performed. This helps free resources of short-term memory and increases the performance of multitasking (Luck & Vogel, 1997; O’Regan, 1994).

HCI research has extensively studied how users manage their attention across multiple information sources. Extant work has mainly focused on managing central attention, suggesting that spatial continuity—placing related digital content in close proximity—is a key information design principle for desktop interfaces to reduce attention switching costs (e.g., Rashid et al., 2012; Wickens, 2008). Recent HCI studies have started to explore how new technologies can leverage users’ peripheral attention in scenarios requiring physical-virtual context switching, which often necessitates a broader field of view (Colley et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2021; Vater et al., 2022). For example, in designing a driving simulator, Kunze et al. (2019) compared a traditional display on an instrument cluster with a peripheral display. They showed that the latter display afforded users higher flexibility in receiving instructions through peripheral attention while focusing central attention on the road, leading to increased driving performance.

Overall, the divided attention literature suggests that attention shifts between tasks often induce switching costs but peripheral attention can process information in parallel with central attention, potentially mitigating these costs. The dual-task processing literature further suggests that the nature of tasks significantly influences users’ attention switching and information processing behaviors. In the context of the current study, when users check information on mobile phones in the workspace, they often need to shift their central attention from their work environment to their phone screens, incurring switching costs. However, AR technology has the potential to deliver information in users peripheral vision (Nenna et al., 2021). Further research is needed to understand how and when AR display differs from traditional screens in affecting users’ attention process and work performance.

## Hypotheses Development

This study investigates how AR, compared to a mobile phone, affects work performance and how this effect varies when different types of information are delivered. We first hypothesize the effects of AR on work performance and the mediating role of work attentiveness. Then, we explore two moderating factors, that is, information complexity and information dependency on the specific physical context. The overall research model is shown in Figure 2.

## The Effect of the Information Channel on Work Performance Mediated by Attentiveness

We first propose that delivering information via AR (vs. mobile phone) will lead to higher work performance, mainly because AR minimizes users’ attention loss. Specifically, we argue that the effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) on performance is largely mediated by work attentiveness, that is, a state of being concentrated on physical work or work-related information (Rodell & Judge, 2009).

The method of information delivery strongly influences the worker’s level of attentiveness. When information is presented on a mobile phone, which is often spatially separated from the physical objects on which users are working, users need to shift their central attention between the task at hand and the virtual information. Specifically, users must disengage from the physical task (e.g., assembling IKEA furniture pieces) and store key information in short-term memory, move their attention to a new object (e.g., instructions on a mobile phone screen), and start processing new information. Then, they will have to refocus their attention on the previous object (i.e., the furniture), retrace their prior steps, and mentally simulate and map the new information (i.e., instructions) to the physical task at hand. This process of disengaging and reallocating attention often demands significant cognitive effort (Barrouillet et al., 2004; Posner et al., 1980). As a result, users will have fewer cognitive resources available to control their attention focus and become more susceptible to distractions. Dynamic and detailed information can be lost due to strained short-term memory resources.

With AR, information is displayed beside the physical object on which users are working, often within users’ peripheral vision (i.e., approximately 10 to 30 degrees in both horizontal and vertical directions from the center of attention). According to research on divided attention, visual elements at the periphery can be processed in parallel with those that are the focus of central attention (Treisman & Gormican, 1988). Therefore, users can process information on AR without consciously switching their central attention away from the physical task at hand. Since information in peripheral vision serves as an “external memory” that can be accessed easily (O’Regan, 1994), users’ short-term memory resources are less likely to be strained, and they will have resources needed to understand and internalize the information. In addition, when users want to process the virtual information in more detail, the spatial proximity of the information within their peripheral vision also enables efficient visual anchoring and thus swift attention shifts at relatively low cost (Juola, 2016). As attention switching is minimized, users are better able to maintain sustained attention to the main task and less likely to be distracted.

Overall, with AR, users’ work concentration can be better preserved, which enables them to focus on understanding the information and integrating the information into the physical task. When users are more concentrated, they are more likely to have sufficient cognitive resources to process and internalize the information, closely following the information needed to perform the physical task. Task performance is likely to increase accordingly. On the contrary, when users are easily distracted, they will struggle to maintain consistent thinking and cognitive engagement in processing and applying the needed information. As a result, their understanding of the information and their work performance will suffer (Oberauer, 2019; Shah et al., 2021).

Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Workers using AR (vs. mobile phone) for instructions will achieve higher work performance, with work attentiveness mediating this effect.

## The Moderating Effect: Information Complexity and Dependency

Based on the dual-task processing literature, we further propose that the type of information provided—information complexity and information dependency—will moderate how information channels affect work performance. As established in H1, AR enhances work performance through increased attentiveness by reducing attention switching costs. This benefit likely depends on the amount of attention switching the task requires between virtual information and the physical context. Further, attentiveness improves performance through better information integration, suggesting that the relationship between attentiveness and performance varies according to how much information must be integrated with the physical context. Accordingly, we propose two moderating effects: information complexity moderates the relationship between the information channel and attentiveness by determining attention switching demands, while information dependency moderates the impact of attentiveness on performance by determining the integration requirements. We detail these moderating hypotheses below.

We first consider the role of information complexity (Wood, 1986). Complexity can be determined by various factors, such as the number of task components, the relationships between the components, and the dynamic state of the task (Xu et al., 2014). The number of components is the most fundamental aspect of complexity, as it captures the amount of information that must be processed and managed (Wood, 1986). It is thus directly related to the demands placed on information processing and memory storage (Naylor & Dickinson, 1969). Hence, in this study, information complexity refers to the number of information components that need to be processed.

We argue that information complexity will influence how information channels affect users’ attention switching behavior and hence their work attentiveness. While AR facilitates parallel processing of information and physical context via an integral use of central and peripheral vision, this advantage may diminish as information complexity increases. This limitation arises because peripheral vision, which has fewer visual processing cells than central vision, operates with reduced precision (Rosenholtz et al., 2012). When peripheral vision receives excessive input, these limited cells can become overwhelmed (Vater et al., 2022). Consequently, when the AR display presents increasingly complex information in users’ peripheral visual field, users cannot accurately perceive details through peripheral vision alone. Instead, they must repeatedly move their eyes, shifting their central focus from the physical work ahead to the AR display for adequate information processing. This frequent switching between the physical context and the virtual information display prevents parallel processing, compromising sustained focus on the task and increasing the susceptibility to distraction. Accordingly, as information complexity rises, the positive effect of AR in maintaining work attentiveness—and consequently task performance— becomes less prominent. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Information complexity will moderate the indirect effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) on work performance through work attentiveness, with higher information complexity weakening the impact of AR on work attentiveness.

Another information type that may moderate the effect of information channels on work performance is the dependency of information on the physical context, which captures the virtual-physical task relationship. It is defined as the degree to which the virtual information is related to users’ specific physical context (Barsalou, 1982; Steffen et al., 2019). For highly context-dependent information, users will have to rely on specific environmental cues to interpret it (Barsalou, 1982; Smith & Vela, 2001). For example, in industrial workplaces, information on how to repair specific parts that users are working on is of high dependency. Conversely, reminders that are not associated with any specific physical objects can be considered generally relevant to the physical task but of low dependency.<sup>4</sup>

We expect that information dependency will positively moderate the effect of attentiveness on work performance. When information is closely associated with physical objects, such as specific components that need to be examined, the need to integrate the information with the physical context increases. Thus, the benefits of work attentiveness— particularly in processing and integrating information—will be even more prominent. Specifically, in cases of high information dependency, users must rely on specific environmental cues to comprehend the information, making seamless processing of information with real-world context more critical than in low-dependency cases. In other words, highly context-dependent information is cognitively congruent with the physical context and is thus most beneficial when users can process it simultaneously (Barsalou, 1982; Hazeltine et al., 2002). Accordingly, maintaining consistent attentiveness to their work is crucial for users to smoothly integrate the information with the physical task, thereby improving task performance. Since AR can help users focus their attention on the information and the physical context better than a mobile phone, users provided with AR can more effectively integrate highly context-dependent information with the work context and can better follow instructions while working. Consequently, the effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) on improving performance will be more prominent in cases of high dependency.

However, when the information delivered is less dependent on the specific working context, the need to process the information and the task context together is less prominent (Stavrakas & Gergatsoulis, 2002). In such cases, users do not have to spend a lot of resources connecting the virtual information with the physical work since they are less related by nature. In such cases, the issue of poor information processing and integration due to lower attentiveness is thus less evident. Accordingly, the advantage of AR in facilitating work performance is less pronounced. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H3: Information dependency will moderate the indirect effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) on work performance through work attentiveness, with higher information dependency strengthening the impact of work attentiveness on work performance.

## Methodology

## Empirical Setting

We collaborated with a major Chinese airline and conducted a randomized field experiment at a major airport in central China. We tested our hypotheses with high external validity in the context of aircraft maintenance. Aircraft maintenance requires a robust regimen of scheduled servicing, inspection, testing, and repair activities for every aircraft in service. Its purpose is to keep the aircraft in a state of airworthiness and to be safe for the crew, passengers, and anyone in its service area. This study focused on one of the most frequent aircraft maintenance tasks, the transit safety inspection, which is a visual inspection task to assess the pre-flight condition of an aircraft and its components. As the first line of defense for safety-related issues, visual inspections must be accurate and efficient in terms of reporting defects, manufacturing errors, or component fatigue.

In our research, the focal task was the visual inspection of Boeing 737-800 aircraft—the most widely used narrow-body aircraft in the world. Specifically, every time the aircraft lands, a mechanic must perform a thorough aircraft inspection, which typically requires 53 steps in a 30-minute period (see Figure 3a). In each step, the mechanic stands in the suggested position and visually inspects relevant aircraft components for obvious or potential damage. For example, in the 20th step, the mechanic inspects the front area of the left engine, which includes four key components: the nose cowl, spinner, fan blades, and screws (see Figure 3b). In the past, mechanics inspected the aircraft holding a thick paper document with instructions and illustrations for each step (referred to as a “job card”). At the time of this research, the airline was transitioning to paperless operations. In particular, it was rolling out an AR job card, which was also associated with a mobile application. Information regarding the sequence of steps and operational instructions about each step—which mechanics are required to follow—can be delivered via AR glasses or a mobile phone.

## Experimental Design

Manipulation of the information channel: We used GLXSS SE AR glasses, produced by the AR solution provider LLVISION. Figure 4 shows an image of the AR glasses (without lenses) and a worker wearing the glasses and a mobile phone. These AR glasses, which have won several industry awards and have been widely covered in the media, have several features that make them attractive for industrial applications. First, the glasses can display virtual information via prism projection technology 1.5 meters from a user’s eyes with a scope of around 22 inches diagonally. The information presented is located just outside of a user’s focal line of sight, which means that it does not block the user’s central vision but is always available at a glance within peripheral vision (i.e., a range of 10 to 30 degrees from the center of attention). Second, the glasses are equipped with a high-resolution camera that is capable of recording outward. What a mechanic sees during an inspection process is recorded as a video, which we refer to as a “first-person perspective video.” The video serves as a record of the mechanic’s work process and can be used later to analyze attention and performance. Third, the glasses weigh only 33.4g and are thus almost as light as regular glasses. This lightweight design is crucial for industrial workplaces. Because users need to wear the glasses for long periods of work, any design factors that may cause discomfort could lead to distraction and should thus be avoided. In addition, with the detachable optical lenses provided, nearsighted or farsighted users can attach lenses that work best for them.

In our experiment, all mechanics wore AR glasses that were connected to a 6.5-inch touch-screen mobile phone secured to one arm with a strap (i.e., the worker’s non-dominant arm, which was the left for all mechanics in our experiment). In both the AR and mobile phone conditions, AR glasses were used to record first-person perspective video for later analysis. During the visual inspection task, the title of each step— indicating the area being inspected (e.g., the front area of the left engine)—was displayed on both the AR glasses and mobile phone. The instructional information about each step was shown either on AR glasses or a mobile phone, depending on the mechanic’s assigned condition (described below). Mechanics proceeded to the next step by swiping the screen of the phone. Following the regulations of China’s Aviation Administration, visual inspection tasks require confirmation at each step. In our setting, swiping to the next step on the mobile phone indicated step completion, and all mechanics were required to swipe immediately after completing each step. This swiping interaction was consistent across both AR and mobile phone conditions, enabling rigorous comparison between the two conditions focusing on information delivery.

To investigate the effect of information delivery via AR versus the mobile phone, we manipulated the delivery channel of real-time instructional information for nine selected steps (out of 53; see Figure 3a). A pre-experiment questionnaire revealed that mechanics often overlook details or make mistakes during these steps. Indeed, these nine steps covered more than 80% of the previous mistakes identified in this visual inspection task. In the mobile phone condition, the instructional information for these steps was displayed only on the mobile phone when users began the particular step. The step title and instructional information were displayed on the same screen, but with a 2-second delay between the appearance of the title and the instructional information (see Figure 5a). The purpose was to better guide users’ attention to different information— the current location and the components to be inspected. Our pilot test indicated that a 2-second interval was sufficient for users to clearly read the title (i.e., the current location). The direction of the screen could be adjusted to landscape or portrait view.

In the AR condition, real-time instructional information was displayed only on the AR glasses (see Figure 5b). Similar to the mobile phone condition, the instructional information was displayed after the step title was presented for two seconds.

We used two separated screens for the step title and instructional information to ensure optimal readability of information on AR. The instructions were the same across the two conditions.

Manipulation of information type: To investigate the moderating role of information type, we manipulated the instructional information at the abovementioned nine steps in two dimensions: the level of information dependency on the specific physical context and the level of information complexity. We collaborated with senior mechanics and engineers to ensure the validity of information in every condition. Specifically, low-dependency information was manipulated as a textual instruction to remind mechanics to perform a thorough inspection (e.g., “please inspect carefully”; see Table 1, Type I). This information was relevant for the inspection task in general, but it did not relate to any specific inspection area users were working on. Highdependency information included a textual instruction specific to the area being inspected, with the key components in this area identified in an image. For example, in the left engine inspection step, a piece of high-dependency information asked mechanics to check the fan blades in this area (see Table 1, Type II).

To test our hypotheses, we conducted a 2 (information channel: AR vs. mobile phone) × 3 (information type: Type I vs. Type II vs. Type III) mixed-design field experiment. The information channel was manipulated as a betweensubjects factor, and the information type was manipulated within-subjects. Specifically, every subject conducted the experimental task three times (as described in the following section). In each task, they wore the experimental devices and received one of the three types of information. Each subject was assigned to the same information channel condition for all three tasks (i.e., each subject consistently received instructional information from either the mobile phone or the AR glasses throughout the three tasks). For each task, the information type was consistent across all nine steps (e.g., for the Type I condition, the information for all nine steps was of low dependency and low complexity). To control for potential order effects, we randomized the sequence of the three types of information for the subjects across the three tasks. We also recorded the order assigned to each subject and included it as a control variable.

## Experimental Procedure

In the experiment’s preparation stage, we first invited a professional optometrist to perform eye tests for each subject, checking for myopia, hyperopia, astigmatism, and pupil distance. We did so to design customized AR lenses for each subject. We issued a pre-experimental questionnaire to collect subjects’ demographic information. We also collected their feedback regarding the steps where they were prone to make mistakes (e.g., performing non-standard actions, neglecting key components). Doing so helped us reconfirm the selection of the nine key steps to provide instructional information.

Then, we conducted a two-stage training session comprising group training and on-site training in order to familiarize the subjects with the operation of the AR glasses and mobile phone. In the group training, an adviser from the firm’s IT department guided the subjects step by step in their use of the AR glasses and the mobile phone. After that, each subject tried out the AR glasses and the mobile phone and practiced for 20 to 30 minutes, going through all nine steps of the visual inspection task. In the on-site training stage, which lasted two weeks, subjects wore the AR glasses for an on-site visual inspection. An experimental assistant accompanied each subject throughout the inspection process and answered any questions a subject had about the equipment.

The main experiment was conducted in three rounds, from November 21 to December 15, 2021, with each round lasting eight days. During each round, every subject conducted the experimental task once, wearing the AR glasses and a mobile phone.<sup>5</sup> Before each task, we assisted subjects in wearing and using the devices. During the task, the subjects’ AR glasses recorded the first-person perspective video. The research assistant observed and recorded the entire inspection process (i.e., a third-persion perspective video).<sup>6</sup> The videos were used for subsequent analysis of the subjects’ work attentiveness and performance. After completion of each visual inspection task, the subjects completed a questionnaire that measured perceived information dependency, perceived information complexity, and cognitive load, among other things. The experiment procedure is presented in Figure 6.

## Data and Measurement

A total of 61 mechanics were recruited to participate in our experiment, with 31 subjects assigned to the AR condition and 30 to the mobile phone condition. For each subject, we collected the first-person perspective videos recorded by the AR glasses, the third-person perspective video recorded by the research assistants, and the subjects’ responses to the questionnaires. Table 2 presents a summary of the dependent variables, definitions, and data sources.

Attentiveness is typically assessed by visual attention spent on a focal task (e.g., Kaewkaisorn et al., 2024). In this research, we measured work attentiveness as the proportion of attentive work duration, calculated by dividing the time spent inspecting the aircraft components and processing information at each step (i.e., attentive work duration) by the total duration of that step. Two coders were recruited to code the first-person perspective videos. They first recorded the total duration of each instructional step, starting when workers swiped to the current step on the mobile phone and ending when they swiped to the next step (see Appendix C for details). The durations were coded in seconds, with the average duration per step being 31.2 seconds. The attentive work duration was then determined by excluding any time the subject was not focused on the aircraft or the instructional information from the total duration of this step.

Work performance was measured in two dimensions: visual performance and action performance. We define performance as the extent to which workers acted according to standards (Campbell & Wiernik, 2015). This is because our experimental task involved visual inspection, with few immediate feedback indicators to reflect actual outcomes. For example, the rate of failing to detect faulty or malfunctioning aircraft components is typically low, and attributing specific aircraft malfunctions to such failures is also challenging. Hence, the best way to assess the performance of inspection workers, according to current practice, is by evaluating the consistency of their inspection behavior with the specified requirements (i.e., instructions). Therefore, workers’ visual inspection of certain components for a specified duration and adherence to certain inspection action standards serve as direct indicators of their performance. Specifically, we measured visual performance as the number of key components that a subject inspected for a specified duration. Within the nine steps for which instructional information was provided, seven key components were identified based on the maintenance manual (i.e., parts that are often damaged or missing, such as the engine grid, the wheel well, and the rear side of the landing gear). A standard visual inspection process should thus pay sufficient attention to these components.

We used YOLO (“you only look once”), a well-performing machine learning algorithm, to identify the key components from each subject’s first-person perspective video (see Appendix D for details) (Wu et al., 2021). Specifically, when individuals inspect an object, they typically place the focal object within their central vision, which spans approximately 10 degrees from the center of attention in both horizontal and vertical directions (Strasburger et al., 2011). In this study, the recorded first-person perspective video had an 80-degree horizontal and 40-degree vertical field of view. We designated the central vision area as the central 20 horizontal degrees and 15 vertical degrees of the video frame (illustrated in Figure 7, the area within the red frame).<sup>7</sup> We then identified the number of key components that appeared in this area during the inspection.

In particular, according to the maintenance manual, a highquality visual inspection should ensure that each key component is inspected for more than two seconds. We thus extracted five frames per second and treated every 10 successive frames (i.e., two seconds) of a video as an analysis unit.<sup>8</sup> If the central coordinate point of the identified component was located in the central vision area in at least 10 consecutive frames, we coded the visual inspection of this component as 1, which meant that the component was properly inspected (i.e., at least for two seconds), and 0 otherwise (see Appendix D for pseudocode). We performed this analysis for each of the seven key components and tallied the number of properly inspected components at each relevant step. In particular, five out of the nine steps contained at least one of the key components (see Appendix B). The sum of the properly inspected key components served as an indicator for the visual dimension of inspection performance.

The second measure of work performance is action performance, that is, the level of standardization in inspection actions. While visual performance provides a direct measure of the quality of inspection, in some steps, the key components may not be accurately detected by analyzing the first-person perspective videos due to poor lighting conditions (e.g., under the aircraft wings). Thus, we also analyzed the third-person perspective videos to detect whether a mechanic conducted inspections with required actions, especially at the steps where a mere standing posture was not enough for a close inspection. In particular, squatting is an important action that mechanics are required to do in many steps. Some areas, such as the engine bottom guard and the lower landing gear, usually cannot be examined well in a standing position. Among the nine steps, mechanics are required to squat in six steps (see Appendix B). Two research assistants were recruited to code the third-person perspective videos independently—the average interrater reliability score was 0.83. In a step that required squatting, if a subject performed the required actions, the event of squatting in the particular step was coded as 1, and 0 otherwise.

## Regression Analysis

To clearly reveal the impacts of AR (vs. mobile phone) on user attentiveness and performance for each step, we constructed a dataset at the user-step level, which contained 1,647 observations from 61 subjects (i.e., three tasks per user, with each task containing nine steps with manipulations). The hypotheses were tested using a two-way fixed effects model. The full model (Equation 1) includes work performance as the dependent variable and information channel (AR) as the independent variable, coded as 1 for AR and 0 for mobile phone. The moderators included information complexity and information dependency, both coded as 0 (low) or 1 (high).

$$
\begin{array}{l} \text { Performance } = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} A R + \beta_ {2} \text { Attentiveness } + \beta_ {3} \text { Complexity } \\ + \beta_ {4} A R \times \text { Complexity } + \beta_ {5} \text { Dependency } + \beta_ {6} \text { Attentiveness } \times \\ \text { Dependency } + \mu + \gamma + \epsilon , \end{array} \tag {1}
$$

where $\beta _ { I }$ and $\beta _ { 2 }$ represent the estimated coefficients of the information channel and work attentiveness, respectively. $\beta _ { 3 }$ represents the coefficient of information complexity, while $\beta _ { 4 }$ captures its interaction with the information channel. $\beta _ { 5 }$ represents the coefficient of information dependency, while $\beta _ { 6 }$ captures its interaction with attentiveness. μ captures the individual-level fixed effect, accounting for unobserved characteristics specific to each subject that might influence work performance, and γ captures the step-level fixed effect, accounting for the impacts of step sequence or the inherent properties of particular steps. The error term ϵ captures the unexplained variability at the user-step level. We measured work performance via the visual and action performance metrics explained above. As these two metrics are count variables, we employed negative binomial regression models.

We also modeled attentiveness as a function of the information channel. Since attentiveness is a probability variable, we used a fractional logit model.

We adopted a stepwise approach, first including the independent variable and the mediating variable, and then including the moderators and the interaction terms in the models. The results on visual performance are presented in Table 4, and the results on action performance are presented in Table 5. ## Robustness Checks and Additional Analysis

To bolster the validity of our findings, we applied the bootstrapping method for hypothesis testing and performed robustness checks using alternative measures for the key variables. Additional analyses were also conducted to provide further evidence for our theorization.

## Bootstrapping Analysis

We adopted PROCESS models as an alternative approach to test moderated mediation hypotheses (Hayes, 2015, 2018). The tests were conducted with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and 5,000 bootstrap resamples. In line with Hayes (2018), the choice of PROCESS models was guided by theory and prediction. To provide a baseline estimate of the indirect effect of the information channel on performance (i.e., H1), we used PROCESS Model 4 (see Figure 11), which excluded moderating variables. For the moderated mediation effects, we adopted PROCESS Model 7 and Model 14 (see Figure 11) to separately test the two moderators (i.e., H2 and H3, respectively). These models produced an index of moderated mediation, which was a direct indicator of the statistical significance of the target moderated mediation effect (Hayes, 2015, 2018). In addition, we included individual characteristics—tenure, AR familiarity, IT efficacy, workload, and the order of receiving the three types of information—as covariates in the subsequent analysis.

Table 6 summarizes the results of the hypothesis testing. The mediation tests yielded a significant positive index of mediation, supporting H1. Specifically, AR (vs. mobile phone) had a positive indirect effect on work performance mediated through work attentiveness, both in terms of visual performance (Index = 0.332, SE = 0.143, 95% CI [0.073, 0.637]) and action performance (Index = 0.263, SE = 0.069, 95% CI [0.124, 0.396]).

Table 7 summarizes the results of hypothesis testing for H2 and H3. For the moderating effect of information complexity, AR (vs. mobile phone) had a negative moderated indirect effect on visual performance (Index = -0.087, SE = 0.025, 95% CI [-0.141, -0.043]), indicating that the mediation effect of work attentiveness on the relationship between AR and visual performance varied depending on the level of information complexity. Similarly, this moderated mediation effect was significant for action performance (Index = -0.208, SE = 0.064, 95% CI [-0.345, -0.098]). Thus, H2 was supported.

Regarding the moderating effects of information dependency, AR (vs. mobile phone) showed a positive moderated indirect effect on visual performance through the interaction of work attentiveness and information dependency (Index = 0.132, SE = 0.033, 95% CI [0.070, 0.201]). The moderated mediating effect on action performance (Index = 0.496, SE = 0.095, 95% CI [0.325, 0.698]) was consistent with that on visual performance. H3 was thus supported. Overall, the bootstrapping analyses demonstrated highly consistent evidence with the main regression analysis.

## Additional Analysis on Perceived Cognitive Load

We hypothesized that the benefit of AR would be weakened when the information delivered was more complex due to the limited cognitive capacity of peripheral vision. Hence, we also measured how workers perceived the information processing load across different channels (e.g., “I felt it was difficult to process the instructional information”). Assuming our arguments held, subjects would have perceived a higher cognitive load as the complexity of information increased, and this would have been more evident when the information was presented via AR. This also means that in cases of high complexity, subjects might have perceived a higher cognitive load when the information was presented via AR versus the mobile phone. Consequently, there would have been an increased need for attention switching in the AR conditions as the information became more complex, diluting the effect of AR on attentiveness and thus performance.

The results show that when information complexity was low (i.e., Type I and Type II), there was no significant difference between the AR and mobile phone conditions in perceived cognitive load in processing information $( M _ { \mathrm { m o b i l e } } { = } 4 . 3 8 , M _ { \mathrm { A R } } { = }$ $4 . 4 8 , F [ 1 , 1 2 0 ] = 0 . 1 6 , p = 0 . 6 9 , \eta ^ { 2 } = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . However, when information complexity was high (i.e., Type III), subjects reported a significantly higher cognitive load in the AR condition compared to the mobile phone condition $( M _ { \mathrm { m o b i l e } } =$ $4 . 7 , M _ { \mathrm { A R } } = 5 . { \dot { 6 } } 1 , F [ 1 . 5 9 ] = 7 . 8 4 , { \dot { p } } < 0 . 0 1 , \eta ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 1 7 )$ . The results thus support our argument that as information complexity increases, the advantages of AR will be diminished due to a heightened cognitive burden, which leads to increased attention switching.

## Additional Analysis on Spillover Effects and Workload

Our main analysis revealed that AR use increased attentiveness and work performance compared to mobile phone use during the nine steps where instructional information was provided. We were interested in whether this improvement might spill over to subsequent unmanipulated steps (i.e., steps without instructional information, see Appendix F for details). The results suggest that the effects of AR in enhancing worker attentiveness during instructional steps persisted through the first and second post-instruction steps. This lasting effect implies that AR can enhance workers’ overall focus even during tasks with more widely spaced information requirements. Nonetheless, we found no spillover effect in terms of work performance, plausibly because performance heavily relied on subjects’ processing of step-specific information, which was absent in the post-instruction steps.

We also explored how the effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) might vary based on workers’ pre-task workload levels (see Appendix G for details). We expected that AR’s benefits in integrating information with the physical context would be more pronounced for users with a higher workload who had more limited cognitive resources and thus faced greater attention management challenges. The results revealed that for subjects with high workload (those who had worked over 4 hours before the experimental task), AR use significantly enhanced their attentiveness and performance compared to mobile phone use. However, for workers with lighter workloads, the advantages of AR use over mobile phone use were less evident.

## Discussion

This study examined the effect of information provision channels (AR glasses vs. mobile phone) on users’ work performance. Results from our field experiment showed that the effect on work performance of providing information via AR vs. a mobile phone is mediated by work attentiveness. We found that AR enabled users to leverage peripheral attention to more seamlessly process virtual information in the context of physical tasks, thereby reducing distractions and facilitating better integration of information into work execution.

This study also identified two key moderators of the aforementioned effect: information dependency and information complexity. Specifically, providing information with a high degree of dependency further amplified the effect of AR (vs. mobile phone) on work performance because the strong relevance of the information to the specific physical environment made parallel processing of the task and the virtual information more critical to work performance. In addition, we found that when the information delivered was highly complex, the benefits of AR diminished. Users might struggle to process intricate details using their peripheral vision and hence might tend to switch their focus between the physical task and the virtual information delivered via AR. In such circumstances, mobile phones might prove even more effective in facilitating information processing and enhancing performance, particularly in terms of performing standard inspection actions.

## Theoretical Implications

This study has several theoretical implications. First, while prior studies have demonstrated the value of AR in providing real-time and multimodal information (e.g., Windhausen et al., 2024; Wuttke et al., 2022), theoretical understanding of the benefits and limits of AR relative to existing digital information channels remains limited. This study addresses this gap by focusing on a unique and important feature of AR: information display location. Through the lens of divided attention theory, this study demonstrates how presenting information in a user’s line of sight reduces attention switching costs and facilitates information integration. This perspective raises important questions about how spatial display configurations might affect attention management and task coordination. As the widespread adoption of metaverserelated technologies continues, the understanding of sensory processing and attention management must also evolve (Lowry et al., 2023). Divided attention theory provides a promising foundation for future investigations of the business value and information design of AR-related technologies.

Second, through examining contingency factors, this study reveals a key insight: AR’s impact diminishes when information dependency is low and complexity is high. By demonstrating how these factors moderate the effect of AR on work performance through attentiveness, this study provides a more nuanced framework for understanding AR’s role in cognitive processing. These boundary conditions also suggest intriguing possibilities for future research in terms of how AR’s effectiveness varies across task types and user expertise levels. The findings point to critical areas for IS and HCI research, particularly in optimizing spatial information design (Park & Lee, 2024) and preventing attention overload in complex tasks that blend virtual and physical elements (Chiossi et al., 2024).

Third, this study extends the divided attention literature by examining how AR information design shapes cognitive load and attentional control. While prior research has applied this theory to guide the design of user interfaces and instructional materials (e.g., Colley et al., 2019; Kunze et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2015), our findings uncover a complex relationship between AR information, attentional resources, and work performance. Specifically, we identify limitations in users’ ability to process complex information in their peripheral vision, which raises further questions about the plasticity of attention boundaries and potential interventions to enhance processing capacity. These insights also point to new avenues for exploring how emerging mixed reality technologies can both facilitate and challenge users information processing and how users’ cognitive abilities adapt to technology advancement (Xi et al., 2023). Overall, our work provides a foundation for developing more nuanced and comprehensive theories of attention management in mixed-reality environments.

Finally, our study extends the extant work by demonstrating the value of AR in improving worker performance in industrial settings. While previous research has mainly demonstrated AR’s value in enhancing production efficiency metrics, such as task completion time and error rates (e.g., Ariansyah et al., 2022; Windhausen et al., 2024), our study focuses on work processes and multidimensional work performance. Through a field experiment, we evaluated performance using multisource data, including first-person perspective video captured by AR, inspection behaviors, and questionnaires. By analyzing user performance at each task step, our study provides a more nuanced understanding of AR’s impact, offering deeper insights into user behaviors than previous research focused solely on overall performance.

## Practical Implications

This study offers some practical implications. First, our findings offer valuable guidance for industrial practitioners seeking to enhance worker productivity through emerging technologies. In manufacturing, maintenance, and healthcare settings, workers are often required to integrate information from multiple sources while interacting with physical environments. Our study demonstrates that AR’s spatial delivery of information enables workers to more seamlessly integrate and interpret virtual information within the physical context, leading to improved work performance. These findings suggest that companies should consider implementing AR solutions to optimize workflows, particularly for tasks that require high precision and attention to detail.

Second, practitioners implementing AR systems should carefully curate information delivery rather than displaying all available data. Our findings suggest that displaying too much detailed information via AR can overload users. During AR implementation, teams should prioritize identifying essential information and presenting it concisely to ensure effective processing. In addition, our study reveals that AR’s effect on attentiveness extends to subsequent steps, but its benefits in improving task performance are limited to the current step. Consequently, practitioners might consider reducing attentionrelated prompts (such as reminders and warnings) to optimize AR’s limited display space for critical operational instructions. Companies should also invest in comprehensive training programs before adopting AR to help workers effectively manage both virtual information and physical tasks.

Finally, given the relative benefits of AR and mobile phones revealed in this study, as well as the current development of ARrelated technologies, a hybrid approach combining AR and mobile phones may be worth considering for companies’ digital transformation processes. AR’s near-eye display may help reduce workers’ attention shifts and enhance work performance, while mobile phones can support more complex information provision and richer user interactions when needed. Companies could implement a complementary system where AR handles real-time, task-critical information display, while mobile devices serve as platforms for in-depth information access and detailed user interactions. This integrated approach would allow organizations to maximize the benefits of both technologies while managing their respective limitations, potentially leading to more effective digital workplace solutions.

## Limitations and Future Research

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, the subjects in our study generally had limited experience with AR technology. Although we provided a one-month training period, further research targeting users that are more familiar with AR and processing virtual information in physical contexts is needed. Second, our study focused on a specific context—aircraft maintenance—with a relatively small sample size. Moreover, due to the nature of the inspection tasks and the limitations of the AR glasses, we employed only one method to manipulate information dependency and complexity. Future studies should investigate whether our findings are generalizable to different settings with varying operationalizations of these concepts, especially with the development of more advanced AR applications. Researchers could also explore additional moderators, such as the difficulty level of physical tasks, to further enhance our understanding of AR use. Third, more empirical evidence is needed to elucidate the cognitive mechanisms underlying AR effects. This could be achieved through finer-grained data collection methods such as eye-tracking and neuroimaging. For instance, while our study highlighted the variations in attention shift patterns between AR and mobile phones, future research using eye-tracking data could provide more direct empirical evidence. Furthermore, we discussed potential limitations in the capacity of peripheral vision to perceive complex information. Future studies employing neuroimaging and other advanced techniques could gather richer data to uncover these cognitive processes in greater detail.

## Appendix D

## Image Recognition in Video Analysis

To analyze the first-person perspective videos to derive visual performance measures, we employed a multi-step process using advanced computer vision techniques. First, we captured approximately 3,000 images that contained 12 key aircraft components recorded by AR glasses to train the object recognition model. These images were collected during both the instructional steps (including seven components) and the post-instructional steps (including five components). We then recruited three domain experts (i.e., senior mechanics) to annotate the dataset. These experts identified and labeled the key components within the images. The inter-annotator agreement was above 85%, and any disagreements were resolved through a consensus process. The dataset was then split into training, validation, and testing sets in an 8:1:1 ratio.

Next, we implemented the YOLOv5 algorithm, a leading model in the “you only look once” (YOLO) family of computer vision models, which has been widely applied for object detection (Wu et al., 2021). The model was trained and fine-tuned by the technical team of our partner company based on industry standards and data quality. A series of data augmentation techniques were used for improving model robustness, including translations, rotations, brightness adjustments, darkening, random rotations, and mirror symmetries. The key hyperparameters of the model are presented in Table D. The learning rate was set to 0.01 to balance convergence speed and stability. A batch size of 8 was used, suitable for the available computational resources. The model was trained for 120-150 epochs, allowing sufficient learning time, and input images were resized to 640×640 to balance efficiency and detail preservation. These settings are commonly used in YOLOv5 for optimal performance (Zhang et al., 2022).

Finally, the output from YOLOv5 included a list of detected objects, each represented by a bounding box. In the main analysis, we used a 2-second time window (i.e., 10 frames) for video analysis. To check the robustness of our results, we varied this window up to 3 seconds. Our findings showed that within the 2- to 2.8-second range (i.e., 10 to 14 frames), AR consistently enhanced workers’ visual performance, aligning with our main findings. Due to space limitations, we only present the results using the 14-frame window in Table E1 (columns 1 to 4 in Panel B).

We also tried an alternative method for constructing the visual metrics. Instead of using binary coding (i.e., 1 for the presence of a key component for a specified time range and 0 otherwise), we counted the number of consecutive frames featuring each key component. We performed negative binomial regression analysis on this measure. The results presented in columns 1 to 4 in Table E1 (Panel C) were consistent with our main findings.

An alternative measure for action performance: For action performance, we used another indicator, pointing inspection, where mechanics pointed to key components during visual inspections. Pointing inspection was a way to guide the eyesight so that the mechanics could focus better on the target components, especially when the components were far from where they were standing (e.g., the upper areas of the engines) or were difficult to distinguish among other adjacent components (e.g., one of several screws). Based on the maintenance manual, 11 pointing actions were required during the nine instructional steps, with each step involving at least one pointing action. The regression results, shown in Columns 1 to 4 in Table E1 (Panel D). revealed a significant main effect of AR on the number of pointing actions and a significant mediating effect of attentiveness. We found significant moderating effects of information dependency and complexity. Thus, the results were consistent with our main findings.

Subsample analysis for each inspection step: To further examine the robustness of our findings across different steps, we conducted a regression analysis for each step (see Table E2). The results were largely aligned with the main analysis for most steps, except for Steps 5 and 6. A plausible explanation was that these two steps involved inspecting multiple components deeply embedded within a complex physical workstation (i.e., wheel wells), which often required users to focus intensely on identifying the physical components. Consequently, user might have needed to allocate separate time for processing physical and virtual information, potentially diminishing the differences between information delivery channels.

## Analysis on Workload

To explore the heterogeneity of AR’s effects among different worker groups, we examined how varying levels of workload prior to the experimental task influenced the outcomes. We conducted a median split on workload (median = 4 hours), dividing workers into two groups accordingly. The subgroup regression results are presented in Table G. For workers with higher workload, AR significantly enhanced attentiveness, visual performance, and action performance compared to a mobile phone. The moderating effects of information dependency and complexity were also significant under these conditions. However, for workers with lower workload, the advantages of AR over mobile phones were only significant for work attentiveness and action performance, but not for visual performance.
