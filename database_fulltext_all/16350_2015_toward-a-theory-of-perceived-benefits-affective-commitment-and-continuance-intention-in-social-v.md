# Toward a theory of perceived benefits, affective commitment, and continuance intention in social virtual worlds: cultural values (indulgence and individualism) matter

Zhongyun Zhou<sup>1</sup>, Xiao-Ling Jin<sup>2</sup>, Yulin Fang<sup>3</sup> and Doug Vogel<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Economics and Management, Tongji University, Shanghai, China; <sup>2</sup>Information Management Department, Shanghai University, Shanghai, China; <sup>3</sup>Department of Information Systems, City University of Hong Kong, Kowloon, Hong Kong, China; <sup>4</sup>School of Management, Harbin Institute of Technology, China

Correspondence: Xiao-Ling Jin, Information Management Department, Shanghai University, 99 Shangda Road, Yangpu District, Shanghai 200092, China. Tel: +86 21 66133790; Fax: +86 21 66134284; E-mail: kathyjin2011@shu.edu.cn

## Abstract

In this study we examine the contingent effects of two cultural values, indulgence and individualism, on the relationships between perceived benefits (utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital), affective commitment, and continuance intention in social virtual worlds. We test our theoretical model in multi-group analyses using SmartPLS 2.0, based on a sample of 414 Second Life users from the U.S.A., Great Britain, and numerous other countries. By comparing the high- vs the low-indulgence subsamples, we find that indulgence weakens the effect of utilitarian value, but strengthens the effect of hedonic value, on affective commitment. Furthermore, compared with low-individualism users, the users with high-individualism showed a weaker effect of relational capital on affective commitment, as well as a weaker effect of affective commitment on continuance intention. The theoretical, empirical, and practical implications of these findings are also discussed. Keywords: social virtual worlds; continuance intention; affective commitment; perceived benefits; indulgence; individualism

## Introduction

Understanding users’ continuance intention (i.e., subjective willingness of continued usage) in social virtual worlds (SVWs) has drawn a considerable amount of research effort from information systems (IS) scholars in recent years (Merikivi & Mantymaki, 2009; Mantymaki & Merikivi, 2010; Goel et al, 2011; Jung, 2011; Mantymaki & Salo, 2011). Affective commitment has been repeatedly found to be the most important dimension of commitment predicting continuance intention in SVWs, among other online services (Li et al, 2006; Kim & Son, 2009; Zhou et al, 2012). Affective commitment to SVWs, in turn, is the result of users perceiving three types of bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from using SVWs: utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital (Zhou et al, 2012).

Despite the advancement in the understanding of the aforementioned mechanisms leading to continuance intention, we do not as yet have a suf<sup>fi</sup>cient understanding of the contextual conditions under which certain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts may be the most effective in increasing users’ affective commitment and their subsequent continuance intention with respect to SVWs.

An answer to this issue would be useful in advising developers of SVWs to focus on delivering the most desired bene<sup>fi</sup>ts to users according to different contextual conditions so as to attract and retain users in a more costeffective manner.

This paper approaches this issue from a cross-cultural perspective, in a special consideration of the global distribution of the user base for many SVWs. Our choice of the cross-cultural angle is also inspired by prior empirical observations that users in some cultures intend to continue using SVWs for both their hedonic and utilitarian values, but not in others (Mantymaki, 2011; Mantymaki & Salo, 2011), implying that culture might be a notable contingency factor. Hence, the objective of the current study is to investigate the contingent effects of indulgence and individualism, which are two cultural values that affect the relationships between users’ perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital) and affective commitment, and that between affective commitment and continuance intention in SVWs.

A careful scrutiny of the literature, however, suggests that theory-driven, systematic investigations of the effect of national culture in the context of SVWs are few. Thus, we draw on the cross-cultural research on commitment as our theoretical base to pursue our research objective. This literature suggests that the antecedents and effects of affective commitment are contingent on culture (Oishi et al, 1999; Meyer et al, 2002; Gelade et al, 2008). Speci-<sup>fi</sup>cally, we anchor on the values-as-moderators framework (Oishi et al, 1999; Gelade et al, 2008), which suggests that attaining goals or satisfying needs deemed important in a particular culture has a greater effect on affective commitment to organizations than do attaining goals or satisfying needs deemed less important in that culture.

Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we extend this values-as-moderators framework to address our research objective in two ways. First, because empirical evidence of this cross-cultural perspective of commitment is rather sparse and has been limited to organizational settings (Gelade et al, 2008), we extend its domain of application to an online setting, such as that of SVWs. Second and more importantly, as we will more fully elaborate upon later in the paper, we focus on two cultural values, namely, indulgence and individualism, in view of the speci<sup>fi</sup>c context of our work. Indulgence (i.e., whether users emphasize pleasurable as opposed to utilitarian needs) is a new dimension in Hofstede’s national culture model (Hofstede et al, 2010) with little empirical research. Individualism has been studied, but not in the context of SVWs. Overall, we focus on addressing the following research question.

How do indulgence and individualism moderate the relationships between perceived benefits (i.e., utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital) and affective commitment, as well as the relationship between affective commitment and continuance intention in SVWs?

To address this question, a research model on the moderating role of cultural values is developed and empirically tested with a sample of 414 users of different country origins from a popular SVW (i.e., Second Life). By so doing, this study makes several important contributions. First, it contributes to the literature on user continuance intention in SVWs by drawing on the cross-cultural perspective to gain an understanding of the contingent effects of culture on the development of affective commitment and the effect of affective commitment on continuance intention in an online setting (i.e., SVWs, in particular). Second, it contributes to the cross-cultural literature on commitment by extending its applicability to the IS literature in general and the research on SVWs in particular. In doing so, the study also enriches the cross-cultural literature by extending our understanding of indulgence, a new cultural dimension with limited prior empirical investigation. Finally, our study makes practical contributions by offering insights into the design and usage of global SVWs under different cultural circumstances.

In the remainder of the paper, we present the theoretical background and develop our hypotheses. We then describe our methodology and the data analysis results. These are followed by a discussion of the research implications, the study’s limitations, suggestions for future research, and conclusions.

## Theoretical background

## Social virtual worlds (SVWs)

SVWs are a subset of virtual worlds. Virtual worlds are computer-simulated, three-dimensional environments visually mimicking complex physical spaces, where people interact with each other and virtual objects via avatars (i.e., animated characters) (Bainbridge, 2007; Saunders et al, 2011). SVWs are virtual worlds that have no prede<sup>fi</sup>- ned objective or closed culture; they support multipurpose ‘in-world’ applications, and empower users to create their own virtual experiences in a completely open fashion. One example of SVWs is Second Life (www.secondlife.com), wherein every virtual object is created by its users.

In several ways, SVWs differ from gaming virtual worlds (also known as massively multiplayer online role playing games (MMORPGs), another category of virtual worlds). First, SVWs are not structured with any prede<sup>fi</sup>ned goals or storylines (i.e., a closed culture), which are typi<sup>fi</sup>ed in gaming virtual worlds (Bray & Konsynski, 2007). Instead, SVWs embrace a build-it-yourself ideology by providing tools for users to create their own cultural artefacts with an emphasis on creativity and self-expression (Chesney et al, 2009; Franceschi et al, 2009). In this regard, SVWs provide users with more open-ended experiences (Book, 2004), allow them to participate with various motivations (Messinger et al, 2009), and grant them full autonomy in determining their own experiences in the world (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2010). These include socialization, learning, virtual business, entertainment, and many others (Jung, 2011).

Second, in contrast to the popular medieval, literary, fantasy genre, or science <sup>fi</sup>ction settings of gaming virtual worlds, SVW maintain deep ties to the of<sup>fl</sup>ine world by providing reality-based thematic settings that are typically derived from modern-day real-world environments (Book, 2004). For example, real nations and cultures are commonly referenced by recognizable landmarks, which sometimes serve as expressions of patriotism that pointedly reference users’ of<sup>fl</sup>ine national citizenship, as was the case in Second Life’s Americana project in which users painstakingly recreated American landmarks such as the Washington Monument and Route 66 (Book, 2004). Thus, unlike gaming virtual worlds in which players follow a pre-de<sup>fi</sup>ned ‘closed culture’, the cultures emerging in SVWs tend to be more directly in<sup>fl</sup>uenced and inspired by cultures of everyday of<sup>fl</sup>ine life (Book, 2004).

Third, in contrast to gaming virtual worlds, SVWs are more similar to the real world in that the boundary between work and play is blurred in SVWs (Blodgett & Tapia, 2011). As such, SVWs accommodate users with different motivations. While players in MMPORGs mainly participate for fun, that is, the hedonic reasons (Zhou et al, 2011), users participate in SVWs not only for hedonic reasons but also for more utilitarian purposes (Chesney et al, 2009; Franceschi et al, 2009; Verhagen et al, 2011). Indeed, a common theme in SVWs is augmenting the members’ real social or business lives (Messinger et al, 2009).

In summary, users of open-culture SVWs are empowered with complete autonomy in determining their virtual lives, through references to their of<sup>fl</sup>ine or real lives. Also, unlike playing online games purely for fun, users become affectively committed to SVWs and thus their continuance intention is strengthened as a means of pursuing the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of various utilitarian, hedonic, and social domains. With these distinctive features, participation in SVWs is more likely to incorporate the values and preferences of users that are consistent with their real-world cultural distinctions.

## Affective commitment, perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, and continuance intention in SVWs

Affective commitment is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a desire-based attachment that attracts a user into a lasting relationship with SVWs (Zhou et al, 2012). It is developed on the basis of personal dedication, that is, the desire of an individual to maintain a relationship that centres on the prospect of long-term mutual bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (Bendapudi & Berry, 1997; Kim & Son, 2009). Prior research suggests that affective commitment is an important predictor of continuance intention in online services (Malhotra & Galletta, 2005; Li et al. 2006). including SVWs (Zhou et al. 2012). The underlying rationale is that affective commitment re<sup>fl</sup>ects one’s involvement and sense of belonging in a relationship due to a related rewarding experience (Iverson & Buttigieg, 1999). Consequently, it encourages the individual to continue in the relationship because of the favourable attitudes, affects, emotions, and perceptions of his or her experience in the relationship (Jaros et al, 1993).

Affective commitment to SVWs is enhanced by users perceived benefits, that is, their perceptions of the value of using it (Zhou et al, 2012). Given their open-ended and self-determined features, SVWs offer the possibility for users to attain bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from a wide variety of sources, including utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital, as reported in prior research (Zhou et al, 2012).

Utilitarian value refers to users’ cognitive evaluation of the utility of using an SVW in terms of its purpose ful<sup>fi</sup>lment and problem solving potential (Babin et al, 1994). SVWs provide utilitarian value to their users across a wide variety of domains, including learning, education, research and business (Mennecke et al, 2008; Chesney et al, 2009; Franceschi et al, 2009; Messinger et al, 2009; Zhou et al, 2011).

Hedonic value refers to users’ positive emotions or feelings (e.g., fun and playfulness) derived from the appreciation of an experience for its own sake, rather than from task completion (Babin et al, 1994). Hedonic usage is common in SVWs (Verhagen et al, 2011), wherein the boundary between work and play is often blurred (Chesney et al, 2009), and the entertainment component is embedded in the form of exploration, game playing, escapism, and fantasy (Zhou et al, 2011).

Relational capital refers to the level of trust, respect, and friendliness characterizing the interpersonal relationships between SVW users and their peers. SVWs embrace a high degree and salience of social interaction (Bray & Konsynski, 2007), pointing to the importance of relational capital in determining users’ affective commitment to SVWs (Zhou et al, 2012).

The rationale underlying the effect of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on affective commitment is the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of users’ personal needs, ranging from their work (i.e., utilitarian) to their social lives (i.e., relational capital), to their entertainmentoriented (i.e., hedonic) applications. In anticipation of high levels of future value, a user is likely to favour a longterm relationship with a particular SVW (Kim & Son, 2009). Through this process, the user develops an affective commitment towards the SVW.

As mentioned earlier, our study aims to extend this line of research by incorporating the cross-cultural perspective in examining the relative importance of various perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital) in predicting affective commitment to SVWs. Next, we review the cross-cultural research to form the theoretical base for our model development.

## Cross-cultural research on commitment: values as moderators

Culture is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as ‘the collective programming of the mind which distinguishes the members of one group or category of people from another’ (Hofstede, 1991). Prior research suggests that the relationships between affective commitment and its antecedents and the consequences can be contingent on cultures (Meyer et al, 2002; Gelade et al, 2008). The values-as-moderators framework, proposed by Oishi et al (1999) and extended by Gelade et al (2008), underlies this line of research. The framework suggests that the positive effect of satisfying personal needs on affective commitment is moderated by individuals’ value orientations, which, in turn, are re<sup>fl</sup>ected in their culture. As such, individuals who stress work achievement would feel more affectively committed when their work-oriented needs are ful<sup>fi</sup>lled. That is, people gain a stronger sense of affective commitment out of needsful<sup>fi</sup>lling activities that are congruent with their values (Oishi et al, 1999; Gelade et al, 2008).

In organizational settings, scholars demonstrate that the sources of affective commitment are culturally conditioned (Palich et al, 1995; Buchko et al, 1998; Meyer et al, 2002; Andolsek & Stebe, 2004), and that their effects are predictable from Hofstede’s value dimensions (Gelade et al, 2008). Gelade et al (2008) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the dependence of affective commitment on its antecedents (more speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, various job characteristics) varies by culture in such a way that job characteristics that are most highly valued in a speci<sup>fi</sup>c culture have a stronger effect on affective commitment in that culture than in others.

Similar evidence has been found in other studies (Palich et al, 1995; Buchko et al, 1998; Meyer et al, 2002; Andolsek & Stebe, 2004), suggesting that satisfying needs or goals congruent with a particular aspect of culture may have a stronger impact on affective commitment than those that do not. For example, personal needs are considered to be more important in individualistic societies than in collectivist ones, and in such a context, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling personal needs or goals is found to have a stronger in<sup>fl</sup>uence on affective commitment when the level of individualism is higher (Palich et al, 1995). Thus, we extend the crosscultural perspective to the context of SVWs by examining how different cultural values can affect the formation and impact of affective commitment to SVWs.

## Model and hypotheses development

In this section, we develop a research model by incorporating cultural values into the existing research on affective commitment, its antecedents, and its impacts in SVWs. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we propose that the respective effects of the three types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (i.e., utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital) on affective commitment and continuance intention, which we reviewed earlier, are moderated by cultural values.

Given that cultural values are multi-dimensional, we need to select the cultural values that <sup>fi</sup>t the context of the current study. Based on our literature review on culture, we identify two cultural values, namely, indulgence and individualism, which we consider to be related to the importance of the three types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts. Indulgence refers to the degree to which people emphasize pleasure as opposed to duty (Hofstede et al, 2010), which might in<sup>fl</sup>uence the relative importance of utilitarian and hedonic values on affective commitment. Individualism refers to the degree to which a group member emphasizes his/her own needs (as opposed to the group needs) and prefers to act as an individual rather than as a member of a collective (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). This cultural value might in<sup>fl</sup>uence the effect of relational capital (representing a relationship- or collective-focused need) on affective commitment.

While prior cross-cultural research on affective commitment provides reasons why the sources of affective commitment and their relative effects vary by culture, we extend this rationale to study how cultural values may affect the importance of affective commitment to continuance intention. By de<sup>fi</sup>nition, affective commitment is relational, representing individuals’ need to feel comfortable and be involved in a certain relationship (Allen & Meyer, 1990). This relationship-based need may be more highly valued in one particular culture (e.g., a collectivist culture) than in another. As mentioned earlier, the needs or goals deemed as being the most important may have the strongest impact. Consequently, affective commitment might have a stronger effect on continuance intention in a particular culture. In the current study, we focus on the moderating effect of individualism on the relationship between affective commitment and continuance intention in SVWs.

In what follows, we develop hypotheses by arguing how and why indulgence and individualism moderate the relationships between affective commitment and its consequences and antecedents. The full research model is shown in Figure 1.

## Effects of indulgence

Indulgence (vs restraint) is the most recently proposed dimension in Hofstede’s model of national culture (Hofstede et al, 2010), and has rarely been studied in empirical research. As a cultural value espoused by individuals, the notion of indulgence vs restraint is similar to the notion of leisure vs work ethics proposed in the literature (Tang & Baumeister, 1984). In a low-indulgence (i.e., restrained) culture, people tend to endorse the work ethic, emphasizing the importance of responsibility in a social or working environment. Prior research on attitudes towards the issue of work vsplay indicates that subjects endorsing the work ethic tend to spend more free-choice time performing activities labelled as ‘work’ than do subjects endorsing the leisure ethic (Tang & Baumeister, 1984). In contrast, people espousing an indulgent (i.e., high-indulgence) culture focus more on pleasure (or leisure) than on duty (or work).

Indulgence is relevant to the context of the current study, as it relates to the relative importance of both utilitarian value and hedonic value. As mentioned earlier, SVWs provide users with utilitarian and hedonic values (Zhou et al, 2011), both leading positively to affective commitment (Zhou et al, 2012). However, utilitarian and hedonic values represent different categories of needs in the context of SVWs. Utilitarian value taps into the work-related or productivity-oriented bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using SVWs to ful<sup>fi</sup>l users’ instrumental needs. Hedonic value accounts for user evaluation of the leisure-related or entertainment-oriented value of using SVWs to ful<sup>fi</sup>l recreational needs such as enjoyment (Wetzels et al, 2009), <sup>fl</sup>ow experiences (Csikszentmihalyi, 1990), and cognitive absorption (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000). It is notable that utilitarian value and hedonic value can be enacted at the same time in the usage of SVWs for various (e.g., educational) applications. Although individuals might use SVWs for both utilitarian and hedonic values, the relative importance of these two types of values can differ according to the culture espoused by them.

As mentioned earlier, ful<sup>fi</sup>lling needs deemed more important in a particular culture would have a stronger effect in the culture than in other cultures (Oishi et al, 1999; Gelade et al, 2008). For example, utilitarian value and the work ethic are valued more in a restrained culture than in an indulgent culture. Hence, the effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment is likely to be stronger for low-indulgence (i.e., restrained) users than for their high-indulgence counterparts. Conversely, hedonic value is deemed to be more important by people espousing an indulgent culture or leisure ethic, consequently having a stronger effect on affective commitment for these people than for restrained users. This leads to the following hypotheses:

The positive impact of utilitarian value on affective commitment is weaker for high-indulgence users than for low-indulgence users.

The positive impact of hedonic value on affective commitment is stronger for high-indulgence users than for low-indulgence users.

## Effects of individualism

Individualism (vs collectivism) is one of the most widely studied cultural values in cross-cultural research (Srite & Karahanna, 2006). It is also an important cultural value in Hofstede’s theory of national culture (Hofstede, 1980; Hofstede, 1991; Hofstede et al, 2010). As well, individualism negatively relates to in-group collectivism and institutional collectivism, two of the nine cultural dimensions in the GLOBE study (House et al, 2004). The ties between individuals are loose in high-individualism (i.e., individualist) societies (Hofstede et al, 2010). Members in an individualist society tend to be independent of one another and separated from society, guide their social behaviours primarily by personal goals, and pay little attention to group memberships and relationships (Srite & Karahanna, 2006; Gelade et al, 2008). By contrast, group identity and cohesion are valued in collectivist societies (Gelade et al, 2008).

Individualism is relevant to the context of the current study in that it stresses the importance of relational capital to affective commitment. As mentioned earlier, relational capital is an important source of affective commitment (Zhou et al, 2012). It characterizes the quality of interpersonal relationships among users of SVWs, which are often collaborative systems supporting communities of users. In essence, receiving relational capital through the continuance intention of SVWs represents the ful<sup>fi</sup>lment of relationship-oriented needs. These needs are congruent with a low-individualism (i.e., collectivist) culture. In a collectivist society, people value the degree of cohesion and their relationships with other members of the society, as well as the quality of these relationships (as represented by relational capital in the context of this study). Inversely, satisfying these needs is deemed to be less important by users espousing an individualism culture. This assertion leads us to formulate the following hypothesis:

## The positive impact of relational capital on affective commitment is weaker for high-individualism users than for low-individualism users.

Individualism might also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the degree of impact of affective commitment on its consequences. In the context of this study, we have discussed earlier that affective commitment has a positive effect on users’ continuance intention in SVWs (Zhou et al, 2012). De<sup>fi</sup>ned as an affective or emotional attachment to a relationship, affective commitment is often developed on the basis of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation and internalization of the relationship, consequently re<sup>fl</sup>ecting a social in<sup>fl</sup>uence mechanism (Malhotra & Galletta, 2005). The notion of affective commitment represents a user’s need to be involved in a certain relationship (Allen & Meyer, 1990).

In essence, affective commitment represents a relationship-focused psychological state, which should be more highly valued by people espousing a collectivist culture. Owing to its congruence with a collectivist culture, affective commitment will likely have a stronger effect on resulting behaviour when individualism is lower. On the contrary, being affectively committed to a relationship may be deemed to be less important by high-individualism users, consequently having a weaker impact on a user’s continuance intention. Thus, we hypothesize:

The positive impact of affective commitment on continuance intention is weaker for high-individualism users than for low-individualism users.

## Methodology

## Primary data collection

Primary data were collected from real users of Second Life, using an online survey. Second Life is one of the most popular and typical SVWs, supporting a broad range of applications (Messinger et al, 2009; Zhou et al, 2012), thus making it a suitable choice for the current study. Furthermore, Second Life has millions of registered accounts, and at any time, approximately 30,000 users are in Second Life, amounting to 20 million user hours every month (Chesney et al, 2009). The large number of active users in Second Life constitutes a valuable source of data for academic research.

The survey method is used here because prior research suggests that surveys are the best means of obtaining data regarding social norms as well as personal beliefs, and attitudes, while also enjoying the merit of enhancing the generalizability of research <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Qureshi et al, 2009b), which is the case in our study. We employed an online survey because of its advantages as compared with a traditional paper-based survey, including a minimal amount of missing data, accurate and ef<sup>fi</sup>cient data entry, and largely comparable reliability, validity, and quality (Boyer et al, 2002). The survey included questions about all the constructs in the model except for the two cultural values (i.e., indulgence and individualism).

We measured all the main constructs using multiple re<sup>fl</sup>ective indicators adapted from previous studies to <sup>fi</sup>t the research context (Appendix A), based on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from ‘extremely disagree’ (1) to ‘extremely agree’ (7). To further ensure the validity of the constructs, we used card sorting (Moore & Benbasat, 1991) and conducted a pilot study among staff and students in a large university before <sup>fi</sup>nalizing the questionnaire. Our survey also asked the respondents to report their real-life demographics (i.e., age, gender, education, and work experience) and usage experiences with Second Life (length, frequency, and duration).

We took two measures in the data collection procedure to ensure the representativeness of the data sample. First, in order to access a wide variety of Second Life users, we distributed the invitation message (containing a link to the online survey) through multiple channels, including (1) ‘in-world’ invitations in Second Life, (2) group mailings to Second Life-related mailing lists, (3) postings to the of<sup>fi</sup>cial Second Life forum, and (4) postings to various Facebook and Twitter sites (pro<sup>fi</sup>le postings and postings to special interest groups related to Second Life). Second, we controlled the timing and ‘places’ of the ‘in-world’ invitation distribution in order to cover users from various ‘geographical’ sectors in Second Life located in diverse time zones.

To increase the response rate, we sent numerous rounds of reminders and used nominal economic incentives. We received a total of 414 valid responses from various countries and used them for our analysis. We assessed nonresponse bias by using Chi-square tests to verify that the early and late respondents were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different in terms of their demographic features and usage experiences with Second Life (Armstrong & Overton, 1977). More speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the early and late respondents did not differ signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly in terms of their distribution across nations. As late respondents are more like non-respondents than are early respondents (King & He, 2005), the results of our testing suggested that the respondents in this study were probably similar to the non-respondents and re<sup>fl</sup>ected the actual user distribution across nations. Furthermore, our data sample was comparable with those reported in previous studies, especially in terms of user distribution across nations (Fetscherin & Lattemann, 2008).

## Group classi<sup>fi</sup>cation based on secondary data

We sourced published data to measure indulgence and individualism due to the availability of objective data and the merit of alleviating common method bias between cultural values and the other variables that must be measured in the survey. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we adopted Hofstede’s dimension data matrix (Hofstede et al, 2010) for three reasons. First, as indulgence is a new cultural dimension recently de<sup>fi</sup>ned by Hofstede et al (2010), no other national culture studies provide detailed data regarding this cultural dimension. Second, Hofstede’s dimension data matrix also provides detailed data regarding various countries’ scores on individualism. Although individualism has been discussed in other national culture theories such as the GLOBE study (House et al, 2004), it is decomposed into two dimensions in the GLOBE study, making it impossible to obtain detailed data regarding individualism as a single factor. Third, various versions of the Hofstede's dimension data matrix have been widely used and are validated in prior cross-cultural research.

Appendix B details the scores of nations reported by the respondents on indulgence and individualism. Based on these scores, we categorized the overall sample (N = 414) into two pairs of comparable subsamples, namely, high- (N = 315) vs low-indulgence (N = 99) and high- (N = 328) vs low-individualism (N = 86) in order to test the moderating effects of indulgence and individualism. Given that most scores in Hofstede’s dimension data matrix range from 0 to 100, we used 50 as a threshold for categorizing the sample. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the high-indulgence (high-individualism) subsample contained respondents scoring 50 and above on indulgence (individualism), whereas those scoring lower than 50 on the same dimension were classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as low-indulgence (low-individualism).

## Data analysis and results

We used PLS, a second-generation causal modelling technique, to test the research model because of its advantage in an exploratory study and its appropriateness in comparing between-group differences (Qureshi & Compeau, 2009a; Sia et al, 2009). The statistical package used was SmartPLS 2.0 (Ringle et al, 2005).

## Measurement model assessment

The results of the con<sup>fi</sup>rmatory factor analysis based on the overall sample revealed that the <sup>fi</sup>rst item of utilitarian value (UV1) has a poor loading (0.33), indicating that it may not be a good indicator of the construct. After removing this item (Thatcher & George, 2004), all of the constructs in the model are of good reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity for the overall sample. As shown in Table 1, composite reliability, Cronbach’s alpha, and average variance extracted values for all of the constructs exceeded 0.70, 0.70, and 0.50, respectively. All of the item loadings (Table 2) are over 0.70 with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant t-values (t > 1.96, P<0.05). The square root of the average variance extracted for each construct is higher than 0.70 and is larger than all of the correlations (Table 1). Such results indicate good construct reliability, high convergent validity, and adequate discriminant validity (Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994; Gefen & Straub, 2005; Sia et al, 2009).

Two complementary analyses indicated that common method variance is not a major concern for this research. ## Discussion and conclusion

This study examines the moderating effects of two cultural values (i.e., indulgence and individualism) on the relationship between affective commitment, continuance intention, and three types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts in SVWs. With all hypotheses supported, the results reveal a set of interesting <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

First, indulgence moderates the effects of utilitarian value and hedonic value on affective commitment, so that it decreases (increases) the effect of utilitarian (hedonic) value. When indulgence is high, hedonic value has the strongest impact on affective commitment among the three types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts, whereas the effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment is the weakest. However, when indulgence is low, the effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment is much stronger, with the path coef<sup>fi</sup>cient being signi<sup>fi</sup>cant and close to that of hedonic value. Probably because of this additional signi-<sup>fi</sup>cant effect of utilitarian value, the low-indulgence model explains more variation in affective commitment than does the high-indulgence model.

Interestingly, the path from utilitarian value to affective commitment is signi<sup>fi</sup>cant only for the low-indulgence model. One possible explanation is that a psychological process of self-justi<sup>fi</sup>cation underlies this effect. Because SVWs are most often treated by some users as being more entertaining and less ‘serious’, hedonic value tends to be the most important motivator for such users to be affectively committed to these environments. This is especially true for indulgent users. However, for low-indulgence (i.e., restrained) users, even though they recognize an SVW as being primarily a place to play, they tend to convince themselves that they are affectively committed because they <sup>fi</sup>nd the SVW to be useful. Hence, in the low-indulgence model, utilitarian value provides additional explanation power to the variation in affective commitment.

Second, individualism moderates the effect of relational capital on affective commitment as well as the effect of affective commitment on continuance intention in SVWs. In particular, these effects are stronger and explain more variations in affective commitment and continuance intention for the low-individualism subjects, as compared with the high-individualism ones. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings support our theoretical predictions, indicating that compared with their individualist counterparts, SVW users espousing a low-individualism (i.e., collectivist) culture tend to think more highly of social relationships and collective memberships.

## Theoretical contributions

This study makes key theoretical contributions to the literature on SVWs and cross-cultural commitment research. First, it contributes to the literature on SVWs by extending our current understanding of continuance intention in SVWs from the cross-cultural perspective, a research endeavour subject to very little attention in prior research on SVWs. While prior research has examined the impact of affective commitment on continuance intention in SVWs and the impact of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of using SVWs on affective commitment (Zhou et al, 2012), the current study extends this knowledge base by incorporating cultural values as the boundary condition of these known relationships. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, it adds to our understanding by establishing the contingent role of the two key cultural values, indulgence and individualism, speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the context of SVWs.

Our study also extends the cross-cultural research on commitment in two aspects. First, while prior crosscultural research on commitment has been done primarily in organizational settings, our study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to extend this line of research to an online setting (i.e., the context of SVWs), thereby extending the applicable domain of cross-cultural research. In particular, we extend the general framework of values-as-moderators (Oishi et al, 1999; Gelade et al, 2008) to the context of SVWs by contextually specifying two relevant culture values. By doing so, the results of this study offer rich insights into how indulgence and individualism play a contingent role in the development and the effect of affective commitment in SVWs. Second, we contribute to the cross-cultural research on commitment by investigating a new moderating relationship, that is, the moderating effect of individualism on the relationship between affective commitment and continuance intention, thus extending this line of research to account for the cultural impact on the consequence of commitment.

## Empirical contributions

As mentioned earlier, the empirical evidence for cultural differences in the antecedents of commitment is rather sparse (Gelade et al, 2008). As one of the early steps to extend the cross-cultural research on commitment to the IS literature, the current study provides empirical evidence on cultural differences concerning the impact and sources of affective commitment in the context of SVWs. For instance, although individualism has been widely studied in cross-cultural research, our study enriches the empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings in the literature by investigating the impact of this cultural value on not only the sources, but also the effect of affective commitment in the context of SVWs.

Furthermore, as mentioned earlier, little empirical research has been conducted regarding indulgence, the most recently proposed cultural dimension in Hofstede’s work. And to the best of our knowledge, this study is among the <sup>fi</sup>rst to empirically investigate the roles of this cultural value. In particular, we provide empirical evidence for the moderating effects of indulgence in the relationship between utilitarian/hedonic value and affective commitment in the context of SVWs.

## Practical contributions

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that developers should not use a onedesign-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all strategy in designing SVWs. Instead, they should ‘localize’ their products to <sup>fi</sup>t local markets, especially by considering cultural differences when designing their products for users who come from different cultural backgrounds. For instance, developers of SVWs often think highly of increasing the hedonic value of their products by incorporating numerous features such as anthropomorphic interfaces supporting humanoid embodiment and multimodal presentations, voice output and realworld simulation, all of which can enhance the enjoyment of users (Qiu & Benbasat, 2009). However, while this strategy might be effective for developing affective commitment among some users, especially those espousing an indulgent culture, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that this strategy is insuf<sup>fi</sup>cient (if not ineffective) for retaining the more restrained (i.e., low-indulgence) users who put more emphasis on utilitarian value. For these users, developers of SVWs must provide functions deemed to be effective in satisfying certain work-related needs or in particular applications. Our study also advises developers of SVWs that the strategy of using affective commitment to improve continuance intention is more effective for users with low individualism. For highly individualist users, developers of SVWs should be cautious in applying this strategy because it would be less effective.

These cultural differences might explain the geographically skewed distribution of Second Life users towards North America (http://secondter.wikispaces.com/Second +Life+User+Statistics). The current study also sheds lights on the failure of SVWs, such as Second Life, to attract and retain users from a particular foreign country because of the cultural differences with respect to usage of SVWs.

## Limitations and future research

As with any other research, the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of our study should be interpreted with certain limitations in mind. First, we used only two items to measure utilitarian value (UV) in the analysis (i.e., UV1 was removed because of its poor loading). Although removing a poorly loaded item(s) from the analysis is common practice in the literature, this might have introduced several unexpected impacts on our results. To explore this issue, we added this item back to the model and re-conducted the SmartPLS analyses. This modi<sup>fi</sup>cation changed the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of the path from utilitarian value to affective commitment for two subsamples, namely, high-indulgence (path coeff. = 0.114, P<0.05) and high-individualism (path coeff. = 0.142, P<0.05). By contrast, the signi<sup>fi</sup>cance of this path remained unchanged for the low-indulgence (path coeff. = 0.199, P<0.05) and low-individualism (path coeff. = 0.119, P > 0.05) subsamples. However, adding UV1 back to the model only brought very minor (if any) changes to the coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of the other paths, and did not change the results of the hypotheses testing (i.e., moderation test), as presented in Table 4. In all cases, the effect of utilitarian value on affective commitment is weaker than that of hedonic value and relational capital, which is consistent with the results of prior research, suggesting that utilitarian value is probably the least important in predicting affective commitment among the three types of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., Zhou et al 2012). Future research can further explore the relative strength of the impact of the various bene<sup>fi</sup>ts on affective commitment.

Second, the survey data were collected from a single SVW, which has potentially introduced a threat to the external validity of the research <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Cook & Campbell, 1979). Although we have mitigated this concern by carefully choosing our research site, Second Life, which is one of the most popular SVWs and has numerous similarities with other SVWs (Messinger et al, 2009), it was not possible for us to directly estimate the extent to which the theory derived from the current study is generalizable to other SVWs. Given the limitations of time and resources, we were not able to con<sup>fi</sup>rm the <sup>fi</sup>ndings of the current study in other settings. However, we urge future researchers to perform the cross-population generalization (Tsang & Williams, 2012) by replicating this study in other contexts.

Third, the current study uses Hofstede’s dimension data matrix to operationalize the two cultural values. Despite being widely cited and used in previous studies, Hofstede’s model and data matrix have been questioned by some scholars (Myers & Tan, 2002). Future researchers should consider drawing upon other models and data sources (House et al, 2004) or directly measuring cultural dimensions at the individual level (Srite & Karahanna, 2006).

Finally, there are a few other directions that future research may dig into. While we investigate only two cultural values (i.e., indulgence and individualism) in the current study, future researchers could consider other cultural dimensions. In addition, although the current study focuses on affective commitment because of its known importance to continuance intention in SVWs (Zhou et al, 2012), future research could investigate the other types of commitment. Further, future cross-cultural research on commitment could extend the current one by investigating other antecedents and consequences of commitment.

## Conclusion

As a function of perceived bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (including utilitarian value, hedonic value, and relational capital), affective commitment promotes continuance intention in SVWs. This study reveals that the impact and sources of affective commitment are contingent on cultural values such as indulgence and individualism. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, indulgence weakens the effect of utilitarian value, but strengthens the effect of hedonic value on affective commitment.
