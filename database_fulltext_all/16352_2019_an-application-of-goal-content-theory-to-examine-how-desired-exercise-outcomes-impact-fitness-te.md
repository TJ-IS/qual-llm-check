# An application of goal content theory to examine how desired exercise outcomes impact fitness technology feature set selection

Tabitha L. James<sup>1</sup> | Jason K. Deane<sup>1</sup> | Linda Wallace<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup> Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, Virginia

<sup>2</sup> Department of Accounting and Information Systems, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, Virginia

Correspondence Tabitha L. James, Associate Professor, Department of Business Information Technology, Pamplin College of Business, Virginia Tech, 1007 Pamplin Hall, Blacksburg, VA 24061. Email: tajames@vt.edu

## Abstract

Fitness technologies are a prominent example of the societal trend towards personal informatics. These technologies pair devices that have various embedded sensors with one or more apps to allow for the collection, analysis, socialization, and management of individuals' health and wellness data. We have limited insight into what drives the use of fitness technologies or how they may contribute to wellness outcomes. To address this gap, our study examines how exercise goals are related to fitness technology feature set use and how the use is associated with a measure of the exercisers' psychological well‐being (ie, subjective vitality). We find that intrinsic exercise goals (eg, enjoyment or competence) are associated with the use of features that allow users to collect, analyse, and inform their exercise progress. Body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals (eg, appearance) are also associated with the use of such features but are negatively associated with features that socialize exercise. The social extrinsic exercise goal is associated with the use of more controlling features that can provide external pressure from the technology or other people. The social features of fitness technologies are associated with higher levels of subjective vitality. Our findings indicate that exercisers who have goals where measuring progress may be desired (eg, losing weight and training for a marathon) are more likely to be drawn to the data management aspects of fitness technologies, whereas using fitness technologies to socialize exercise may add an element of enjoyment that is attractive to some users and may lead to increased well‐being.

fitness technology, goal content theory, self‐determination theory, theory of affordances, wearables

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

With the explosion of small form factor computing devices that have various embedded sensors (eg, accelerometers and barometric altimeters), such as Fitbits and Apple Watches, society has entered an era where it is possible that individuals are carrying around at least one data capture device, in addition to their smartphone, for most of their day. Data capture devices paired with apps collect, store, and format for display the users' sensor (and logged) activity that can then be used for their own purposes or shared with others through apps, messaging, social media, etc. Such bundled personal technologies have enabled the “quantified self” and “lifelogging” trends, more recently referred to as personal informatics (Epstein, Kang, Pina, Fogarty, & Munson, 2016; Patel, Asch, & Volpp, 2015; Rapp & Cena, 2016; Ware, 2013), where people collect, store, and analyse the data (eg, financial, video or social media diaries, exercise, health, and nutrition) that they generate throughout their day. One of the more prominent early example of quantified self technologies are fitness technologies (eg, Fitbit and its app). We define fitness technology as any combination of hardware<sup>1</sup> and software that enables the collection, storage, analysis, socialization, and management of individuals' health and wellness data, including but not limited to exercise activity, nutrition, and sleep data

Ouantified self technologies have experienced tremendous growth and rapid advancement: for example. the wearables market saw a 17% increase in units shipped worldwide in 2016 (Lamkin, 2017). While features are continuously being added (eg, heart rate sensors), most fitness technologies provide exercisers the ability to perform tasks such as automatically tracking physical activity, generating reports or visualizations (eg, dashboards), obtaining reminders, setting exercise goals, sharing data with others, receiving encouragement from others, competing with others, comparing their exercise to others, searching for information to support exercise regimes, obtaining reward for achieving exercise goals, and getting help from live coaches or trainers. However, while increasingly popular and rapidly advancing, research has only begun to investigate how and why people use them (eg, Hamari, Hassan, & Dias, 2018; Wu, Kankanhalli, & Huang, 2015; Zhou, Kankanhalli, & Huang, 2017). Furthermore, the ultimate hope is that fitness technologies will help people improve their health and well‐being (Patel et al., 2015; Sjöklint, Constantiou, & Trier, 2015). In this regard too, we have only initial insights, with some early studies providing discouraging results (Etkin, 2016; Jakicic et al., 2016). In the current study, we take an initial step towards addressing this gap by examining how exercise goals are associated with the use of fitness technology features and how the use is related to a measure of exercisers' psychological well‐being (ie, subjective vitality).

Grounding our study in goal content theory (GCT) (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2012: Kasser & Ryan, 1996) which is a subtheory of self‐determination theory (SDT) (Deci & Ryan, 2008, 2012), we start with the premise that individuals could be exercising in order to realize different goals or outcomes (eg, to look more attractive and to become better at their exercise activity) (Ryan, Frederick, Lepes, Rubio, & Sheldon, 1997). We extend this thinking by proposing that different exercise goals will drive the use of different feature sets. To illustrate, consider an avid runner who is training for a marathon and uses fitness technology to gauge the increase in his or her competence as a runner over time (ie, to keep track of how much progress he or she is making in his or her marathon training). Such a user may find dashboards displaying the time, distance, and map information of his or her runs over days, weeks, and months, to b helpful because it keeps track of his or her marathon preparation and allows the user to easily see the progress being made. However, because the avid runner enjoys exercising regularly, he or she may not need, or even like, being reminded to exercise (a common feature of fitness technologies). As another example, consider an individual wh is drawn to exercise because he or she enjoys sport (ie, is competitive and likes to exercise with others). Such a user may employ the challenge and messaging features of fitness technologies and share his or her fitness data in order to enable competition and socialization. Therefore, we posit that users' reasons for exercising (ie, their exercise goals will drive how they use fitness technologies (eg, whether they use the social features).

Technologies that enable personal informatics are becoming increasingly popular (Rapp & Cena, 2016; Shin & Biocca, 2017). The ultimate goal of such technologies is to help people change their behaviours (eg, exercising, eating, spending, and studying) in positive ways that will improve desired outcomes (eg, well‐being, health, and financia stability) (Hamari et al., 2018; Patel et al., 2015). Fitness technologies are attempting to use features such as analytics, automated prompts and gamification, and socialization to keep users motivated and inform them of their progress (Hamari et al., 2018; Patel et al., 2015; Shin & Biocca, 2017; Sjöklint et al., 2015). However, because they are emerging technologies, research is only beginning to examine the impact of early generations of fitness technologies (Etkin, 2016; Jakicic et al., 2016) and what may drive user preferences for the different features they offer (Hamari et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2017). Early research is also offering initial suggestions on how to manipulate fitness technologies to improve use and affect better outcomes (Epstein, Caraway, et al., 2016; Epstein, Kang, et al., 2016; Rapp & Cena, 2016; Shin & Biocca, 2017). Our study contributes to this conversation by examining how activity (ie, exercise) goals drive use at a feature set level and how exercise goals and technology use are associated with one outcome—subjective vitality, a measure of psychological well‐being.

In the current study, we are examining an emerging technology that is intended to assist people's motivatio towards an activity. Specifically, we are studying a novel personal technology that is quantifying, managing, and socializing activities that have not been traditionally technologically assisted in such ways. This results in the need to combine traditional theories of motivation with theories of technology use in unique ways. Our study does this by grounding our model in GCT and using subjective vitality as an outcome. In an offline context, the relationship between intrinsic and extrinsic goals and subjective vitality have been studied in prior research (Deci & Ryan, 2012; Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996). We extend the context in which these relationships are tested by examining the addition of technological assistance to the exercisers' environments. To do so, we draw on the theory of affordances, which has been adapted to the information systems (IS) context to explore use at a feature set level (Bygstad, Munkvold, & Volkoff, 2015; Leonardi, 2011, 2013; Markus & Silver, 2008; Volkoff & Strong, 2013). Hence, our study contributes to theory by combining motivation theory with the theory of affordances to study an emerging personal technology.

We argue that technologies for persongl informatics are an important emerging area of personal computing that needs to be extensively examined because they hold the promise of assisting users' self‐improvement initiatives Lowry, Gaskin, and Moody (2015) state that to encourage positive interactions with technology, it is important to dis: tinguish between the intrinsic and extrinsic motives of individuals and to determine how different stimuli help fulfi them. Our study provides an initial step at doing so for a novel class of technologies by demonstrating how traditiona theories of human motivation (ie, GCT) can be extended to explore technologically‐assisted environments. Our results are useful to practitioners because they provide insight into how exercise goals drive feature use and what, if any, association use has with well‐being. Thus, we provide details that can help explain how people are using fitness technologies, which can be used to inform the design and development of usage suggestions for subsequent generations

## 2 | THEORETICAL DEVELOPMENT

Our study is grounded in GCT, which is a subtheory of SDT. SDT is an umbrella theory of human motivation that distinguishes between intrinsic (ie, driven by and important to oneself) and extrinsic (ie, motivated through pressure from others) motivations (Deci & Ryan, 2008, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2000). GCT proposes that different people are motivated towards an action (eg, exercising) by desires to reach different goals and that the goals may be either intrinsically or extrinsically motivated (Ryan et al., 1997). For example, a person who is motivated to exercise in order to improve his or her physical appearance may be performing that activity because of perceived outside pressure to obtain a particular physique. In comparison, a person who is exercising with a goal to have fun derives his or her motivation internally.

We also draw from the theory of affordances, which stems from work by the ecological psychologist Gibson (1979) on visual perception (ie, how humans see and react to objects in their world). Gibson “argued that we perceiv in order to operate on the environment, [that] perception is designed for action, [and] called the perceivable possibil ities for action affordances” (Ware, 2013, p. 18). This concept has been adapted to the IS context, where researchers stated that “one technology can support multiple affordances, and, consequently, that each member of the sam social group can enact a different affordance or set of affordances when using the same technology” (Leonardi 2013, p. 751). Following this logic, we propose that individuals with different exercise goals will enact different features of fitness technologies.

The current study examines fitness technology feature selection through the lens of GCT and the theory of affordances. Specifically, we examine how intrinsic and extrinsic exercise goals drive fitness technology feature set use. In addition, we examine the associations between exercise goals, fitness technology feature set use, and a well‐being indicator—subjective vitality. A meta‐framework of our model is provided in Figure 1. In the following sections, we provide further details of our guiding theories, which frame our overarching model, and then develop our hypotheses.

## 2.1 | Goal content theory

GCT arose from work examining intrinsic and extrinsic life goals (Deci & Ryan, 2012). Starting from the premise that money is an external incentive, SDT researchers examined the hypothesis that individuals who valued financia success over affiliation, community feeling, and self‐acceptance would be less psychologically well‐adjusted (Kasse & Ryan, 1993). Financial success, affiliation, community feeling, and self‐acceptance were described as “goal contents” by Kasser and Ryan (1993, p. 411). Following the logic of SDT, financial success (ie, material success and wealth) is an extrinsic incentive, the pursuit of which will focus individuals on the external reward and thwart autonomy, thereby diminishing intrinsic motivation and well‐being. Prior studies had empirically demonstrated that rewards (ie, externa incentives) in general can be controlling and undermine intrinsic motivation (Ryan, Mims, & Koestner, 1983; Schwartz 1990). Indeed, Kasser and Ryan found that individuals who valued affiliation, community feeling, and self‐acceptance reported better well‐being and less psychological distress than those who valued financial success. These findings provided support for the proposition that “when goals regarding extrinsic rewards or the contingent approval of others are more central to an individual than are goals concerning intrinsic actualizing tendencies, lower well‐being and greater distress may occur” (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, p. 420).

Kasser and Ryan (1996) extended their initial exploration of goal contents to explore intrinsic versus extrinsic goals in more depth. This work found that extrinsic goals (aspirations) for financial success, social recognition, an appealing appearance were negatively associated with vitality and self‐actualization and positively associated with depression, anxiety, and narcissism, while the opposite was true regarding intrinsic goals for affiliation, community feeling, self‐acceptance, and physical health (Kasser & Ryan, 1996). These studies provided the foundation for GCT and clarified the distinction between intrinsic goals that “tend toward congruence with growth and actualization tendencies and thus those that provide satisfaction of inherent psychological needs” and extrinsic goals that “ar typically means to some other end and are focused on obtaining external rewards or praise from some other person” (Kasser & Rvan. 1996, p. 285), Furthermore, focusing on extrinsic goals has been shown to result in decreased well being and increased psychological distress, while the opposite is true for intrinsic goals (Kasser & Ryan, 1996; Schmuck, Kasser, & Ryan, 2000). This result has been taken a step further, with research finding that not only valuing and pursuing extrinsic goals but also attaining them can lead to ill‐being (Niemiec, Ryan, & Deci, 2009).

In the exercise context, researchers have long been interested in what motivates people to participate in sport (Ryan, Vallerand, & Deci, 1984). Early work on why young people participate in sport uncovered primary motives such as fun, skill development, challenge, and fitness (D. L. Gill, Gross, & Huddleston, 1983; Robinson & Carron, 1982; Wankel & Kreisel, 1985). In a study of youth sports, intrinsic factors (eg, improving skills or testing one's skills against others, excitement from the sport, performing the activity, and personal accomplishment) were rated mos important (Wankel & Kreisel, 1985), whereas extrinsic factors (eg, winning rewards or the game and pleasing others) were rated least important. The study by Wankel and Kreisel (1985) also found that social items (eg, being on a team or with friend) were of intermediate importance to the participants. Erederick and Ryan (1993) examined three motives (ie, goals), interest/enjoyment, competence, and body‐related, in their study of adults participating in eithe an individual sport or in a fitness group. They found that participants in fitness groups were more likely to report body‐related goals, whereas those in an individual sport were more likely to have interest/enjoyment or competence motives. They also found that all the motives they examined were correlated with participation but that the body‐related goal was correlated with depression and anxiety while the interest/enjoyment and competence goals were correlated with positive psychological indicators.

In a later study, Ryan et al. (1997) examined how exercise goals impacted sustained participation in severa physical activities (Tae Kwon Do, aerobics, and nautilus). In their first study of the paper, they used the three motives for exercise that had been investigated in Frederick and Ryan (1993) and similarly classified interest/enjoyment (“desire to have fun, pursue interests, be stimulated”) and competence (“desire to engage challenges and exercise and expand skills”) (Ryan et al., 1997, p. 336) as intrinsic goals and body‐related motives as extrinsic. In their second study of the paper, they expanded the goals identified by Frederick and Ryan by splitting the body‐related motives into two separate goals (ie, fitness and appearance) and including a social motive. Prior studies had considered fitness and appearance to be a single factor (Frederick & Ryan, 1993; D. L. Gill et al., 1983; Morris, Clayton, Power, & Han, 1995) because both are body‐focused goals, but Ryan et al. (1997) considered them separately, basing this decision on the study of Kasser and Ryan (1996) that found appearance goals to be more related to extrinsic life goals such as fame and money and health goals to be more closely related to intrinsic goals of affiliation and self‐acceptance. While Ryan et al. split the body‐related motives into appearance and fitness, they still considered both of them to have an extrinsic focus and found neither to predict adherence. In their paper, appearance is described as revolving around physical body image (eg, muscles and physique) and fitness as encompassing physical well‐being or health (eg, stamina and energy). Ryan et al. (1997) also proposed that increasing social contact could be an extrinsic motive for exercise. They based the addition of the social motive on prior research that suggested that social interaction through exercise activities may increase enioyment and thus improve adherence (Spink & Carron, 1992: Wankel 1993). While Ryan et al. argue social motives are extrinsic to exercise, in their study, they expected and found similar outcomes from the social goal and the intrinsic goals.

The study of Rvan et al (1997) introduced the revised Motivation for Physical Activities Measure (MPAM-R) which includes items for the five exercise goals (ie, interest/enjoyment, competence, fitness, appearance, and social) and is popular in the exercise motivation literature (Teixeira, Carraça, Markland, Silva, & Ryan, 2012). We use th MPAM‐R in the current study to explore the associations between the intrinsic and extrinsic exercise goals and the use of fitness technology feature sets. We group the five exercise goals given in the motivation literature based upon their intrinsic or extrinsic focus: intrinsic exercise goals (interest/enjoyment and competence), body‐focuse extrinsic exercise goals (appearance and fitness), and the social extrinsic exercise goal.

## 2.2 | Theory of affordances

Drawing on the theory of affordances, Leonardi (2013, p. 751) states that the idea of affordances is “that objects have properties (or features, in the context of IT use) and animals that make use of objects have their own physica characteristics and a host of needs.” Furthermore, in explaining Gibson (1979) and the use of affordances in IS, Leonardi (2013, p. 751) argues:

Any animal can perceive an object's features (e.g., its flatness or roundness), but the utility of those features—what they afford (e.g., walking on or rolling on)—is “relative to the posture and behavior of the animal being considered” (Gibson, 1979, pp. 127–128). In this way, affordances are relational (Hutchby, 2001; Zammuto, Griffith, Majchrzak, Dougherty, & Faraj, 2007). They are given neither by the technology nor by the perceiver alone. Information systems researchers who adopt a relational view o affordances stress that people's goals shape what they come to view the features of the technology as affording them the ability to do (Leonardi, 2011; Markus & Silver, 2008)

Adopting this view, we posit that different exercise goals will lead to the enactment of different fitness technol ogy feature sets. That is, we propose that exercisers will view the utility of the fitness technologies through the lens of their exercise goals, which will lead to different exercise goals being associated with the use of different fitness technology feature sets. It is important to examine this phenomenon because fitness technologies promise to provid environmental support to individuals hoping to improve their health and well‐being, which is a valued outcome in societies struggling with increasingly sedentary lifestyles and obesity epidemics (World Health Organization, 2017). However, we illustrate in this paper that fitness technologies offer different types of environmental support through different feature sets and the choice of which of the features to use is left to the exerciser. Thus, in order to maximiz benefits from fitness technologies, we must first obtain a better understanding of how and why people use them.

Appendix S1 provides details about how the fitness technology features were identified. To summarize, we identified the most common features of the current generation of fitness technologies at the time our data were collected. These features were then grouped into second‐order factors. Specifically, we grouped together features that had a social interaction focus: fitness data sharing, encouragement, competition, comparison, and coaching. We call this feature set social interaction features (SIFs). Features that attempt to control the users' exercise were grouped together: rewards, reminders, and goal management. We refer to this feature set as exercise control features (ECFs). Finally, we group together features that help users manage fitness data: data analysis, data collection, progress updates, and information searching. We call this feature set data management features (DMFs). Our conceptual model, in which the features are shown and defined, is provided in Figure 2

## 2.3 | Exercise goals and feature set use

Motivation and system use have been studied in the IS literature (eg, Cheng, 2011: Ke. Tan, Sia, & Wei, 2013: Lowry et al., 2015; Lowry, Gaskin, Twyman, Hammer, & Roberts, 2013). However, one unique aspect of our study is that we are considering use at the feature set level rather than use of the technology as a whole. That is, we are examining whether people with intrinsic exercise goals will use the DMFs of fitness technologies rather than whether they wil use a particular fitness technology (eg, a Fitbit/app). In doing so, we are acknowledging that fitness technologies have the possibility of assisting the attainment of both intrinsic and extrinsic goals depending on how they are used. Prior research has similarly proposed that technologies can satisfy both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations depending o how they are designed and used (Lowry et al., 2015). In the current study, we are examining how people with intrinsic and extrinsic exercise goals use the fitness technologies without guidance, which may provide insight into how to develop guidance on more customized use

Also differentiating our study from past efforts is that we are examining how intrinsic and extrinsic exercise goals drive feature set use rather than exploring intrinsic or extrinsic motivations to use the system itself. That is, we are proposing that intrinsic goals (ie, exercising for enjoyment or to become a better exerciser) may be associated with different use of the fitness technologies than extrinsic goals (ie, wanting to improve appearance, better health, or socialize the activity). Hence, the goal content is what individuals hope to obtain through their exercise (Ryan et al., 1997), not what they hope to obtain through using the fitness technologies. Nevertheless, we can draw insight from past studies of motivation and system use to help us explain how different exercise goals may influence wha features of the fitness technologies are employed.

IS research has examined intrinsic and extrinsic motives for system use, arguing that different goals may lead to different use outcomes (Cheng, 2011; Chiu & Wang, 2008; Lee, Cheung, & Chen, 2005; Roca & Gagné, 2008; Sørebø Halvari, Gulli, & Kristiansen, 2009). Notably, much of this research has been conducted in the online learning context which is another activity where understanding motivation is critical. For example, one study explored intrinsic and extrinsic motivations towards online learning and found that one particular extrinsic (usefulness) and one intrinsic motive (enjoyment) predicted intention to use (Lee et al., 2005). Cheng (2011), using GCT, also explored the impacts of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivations on the acceptance and use of organizational e‐learning systems within a technology acceptance model framework. Specifically, he found that users' learning goal orientation is a predictor o perceived enjoyment, which leads to intention to use and usage of the e‐learning system. Such studies lend suppor to the proposition that intrinsic and extrinsic motives may have differing impacts on technology use.

Early work by Deci (1971) solidified one of the core tenets of SDT, which is that environmental factors can thwart (or support) basic psychological needs and thus diminish (or enhance) intrinsic motivation. The study of the impact of environmental factors on motivation was first focused on rewards, where experimental results illustrated that giving money as a reward for performing an activity decreased intrinsic motivation for that activity (Deci, 1971). However, in the same study, positive feedback and verbal reinforcement showed the opposite pattern, leading to increased intrinsic motivation for the activity (Deci. 1971). This early work provided the foundation for another subtheory of SDT. called the cognitive evaluation theory (CET) that explains how environmental factors shape intrin sic motivation (Deci & Ryan, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2002). CET “describes the impact of psychological needs and socia conditions on the propensity to regulate behavior for intrinsic reasons” (Wilson, Mack, & Grattan, 2008, p. 251). The features of fitness technologies correspond to many environmental factors described in the motivation literature (eg, rewards and competence‐supporting feedback) (Ryan & Deci, 2000). Hence, we can draw from motivation theory to characterize the feature sets we examine in terms of whether they include environmental factors that are known to support or thwart the basic psychological needs. Ryan and Deci (2000, p. 70) state that environmental factors such as rewards, deadlines, imposed goals, negative feedback, and directives can diminish intrinsic motivation, whereas “chal lenges, effectance‐promoting feedback, and freedom from demeaning evaluations” can facilitate intrinsic motivation

Underlying both CET and GCT is the importance of the basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness (Deci & Ryan, 2000, 2012). The principle is that “social context and individual differences that suppor satisfaction of the basic needs facilitate natural growth processes including intrinsically motivated behavior and integration of extrinsic motivations, whereas those that forestall autonomy, competence, or relatedness are associated with poorer motivation, performance, and well‐being” (Deci & Ryan, 2000, p. 227). The need for autonomy is defined as “being the perceived origin or source of one's own behavior" (Rvan & Deci, 2002, p. 8). The need for competence revolves around “feeling effective in one's ongoing interactions with the social environment and experiencing opportunities to exercise and express one's capacities” (Ryan & Deci, 2002, p. 7). Lastly, the need for relatedness is defined as “feeling connected to others, to caring for and being cared for by those others, to having a sense of belongingness both with other individuals and with one's community” (Ryan & Deci, 2002, p. 7). The idea that systems or system features that are psychological need supporting may be more attractive has appeared in the IS literature where it has been proposed that motivation to adopt may be eased in systems that increase users' sense of control (T. G. Gill, 1996). We will use this background to assist us in developing our hypotheses below.

## 2.3.1 | Exercise goals and DMFs

SDT research concludes that environmental factors “that conduce toward feelings of competence during action can enhance intrinsic motivation for that action” and that “optimal challenges, effectance‐promoting feedback, and freedom from demeaning evaluations were all found to facilitate intrinsic motivation” (Ryan & Deci, 2000, p. 70). It has been shown that competence and autonomy are somewhat interrelated (Fisher, 1978; Ryan, 1982). That is, competenc needs to be accompanied by autonomy to enable intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000, 2002). An informational environment (ie, competence‐supporting environment) is described as one that provides individuals with relevant feedback regarding their capability to perform the activity in such a way as to not make them feel pressure to achieve a specific outcome (Ryan, 1982). Therefore, feedback that makes an individual feel like he or she is good at the activity without pressuring him or her to achieve a particular level of performance is ideal (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999; Ryan & Deci, 2002), Such an environment would be supportive of both the autonomy and competence needs of the individual

We propose that features of fitness technologies that revolve around providing exercisers with feedback about their performance (DMFs: data collection, data analysis, progress updates, and information searching) can be consid ered effectance‐promoting feedback because these features provide exercisers mechanisms through which to gauge their exercise competency while providing them with the tools to do this on their own, which allows the activity to remain self‐determined (ie, autonomous). These tools offer statistics and visualizations that let the exerciser know for example, how many steps they took each day of the week. Zhang (2008) proposed that timely and positive feedback is a system design element that could afford competence and achievement, and Zhang and Lowry (2016) took a similar approach by classifying timely feedback and exercise reports under perceived affordances for task accomplishment.

A recent study on different gamified elements grounded in SDT found performance graphs to be positivel associated with satisfaction of the competence need (Sailer, Hense, Mayr, & Mandl, 2017). In their study, Sailer et al. (2017) tested different gamified elements (eg, badges, performance graphs, and leaderboards) in an orderpicking simulation and the findings revealed that different gamified elements relate to different basic psychologica needs. In a qualitative study of a gamified learning environment, the need for competence was found to be the most important need, followed by the need for autonomy (van Roy & Zaman, 2018). The authors argue that th educational context in Western societies is perceived by students to be a controlling context where they are pressured to achieve superior outcomes, which may lead students to value competency even if they have to sacrifice autonomy or relatedness (van Roy & Zaman, 2018).

A strong relationship between cognitive absorption (a second‐order construct that includes intrinsic motives such as control, curiosity, and joy as first‐order factors) and intention to use gaming software was uncovered in Lowry et al. (2013). In this study, the authors classify gaming software as a hedonic‐motivation system, which means that the system's primary use is enjoyment (ie, hedonistic). One interpretation of their results could be that intrinsic motives (ie, joy and control) predict use of systems that support intrinsic motivation (ie, enjoyment or pleasure). In another study, data analytic features were found to be important to users focused on goal outcomes, having a mastery goal orientation, and having specific goals they wanted to accomplish (Hamari et al., 2018), characteristics that could be descriptive of intrinsic exercisers. Following the logic of the theory of affordances, it is necessary to “consider interactions between actors and artifacts in light of the actors' goals and capabilities” (Markus & Silver, 2008, p. 620). We posit that exercisers who have intrinsic exercise goals (ie, interest, enjoyment, and competence) are likely to use the DMFs that provide them with the ability to gauge their competency while maintaining autonomy. That is, features that collect and provide analysis of users' fitness data should provide an informational environment that is appealing to our example exerciser training for a marathon because the DMFs can be used to determine how much progress he or she is making each day (eg, the length and path of each run).

We also propose that users who are exercising for body‐related extrinsic goals are likely to use the DMFs because the information and data analysis provided by this feature set should similarly help these users see how much progress they are making towards losing weight or being healthier, for example, Prior research has found that the fitness body-related goals result in similar exercise adherence as intrinsic exercise goals (Ingledew & Markland, 2008; Ryan et al., 1997), which provides support for this argument. Exercisers with body‐related motives value the outcomes (eg, being more physically attractive), which is why they are classified as extrinsic. Exercisers who have extrinsic goals (eg, appearance or fitness) may feel a need to maintain achievements (eg, losing weight or taking more steps) in order to avoid feeling like they have wasted their time (Sebire, Standage, Gillison, & Vansteenkiste, 2013), and the DMFs would help in this pursuit by informing exercisers of their activity over time. Sebire, Standage, and Vansteenkiste (2009) found evidence in their qualitative study that exercisers with intrinsic and extrinsic goals both focused on progress indicators, although the progress indicator used may differ (eg, exercisers with intrinsic goals may use physical exertion, whereas those with extrinsic goals may use a mirror). Adding fitness technologies to their environment provides a means for exercisers with both intrinsic and body‐focused extrinsic $\mathtt { g o a l s } ^ { 2 }$ to quantify their competency or progress. This leads us to the following two hypotheses.

H1a. Users with intrinsic exercise goals will be more likely to use the DMFs.

H1b. Users with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals will be more likely to use the DMFs.

## 2.3.2 | Exercise goals and ECFs

Work that formed the foundation of SDT initially focused on the impact of rewards on motivation (Deci et al., 1999; Deci & Ryan, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2002). Much research has confirmed that external rewards undermine intrinsic motivation because it shifts the perceived locus of control away from the individual (Deci et al.. 1999: Rvan & Deci 2000). For example, if a child is given a money for doing his or her homework, then the child may not be doing the homework because he or she enjoys it or is interested in it, but rather may feel pressured by his or her parents to comply. In other words, the promise of a reward shifts the motivation from performing the activity for the sheer enjoyment of it or interest in it towards performing it because it is an activity that others reward. The impact of rewards can be seen in a study of a gamified learning environment where the authors noted that “the drive for collecting badges … came with the drawback that some students were fixated on receiving as many badges as possible resulting in repeatedly performing the behavior for which they were already rewarded” (van Roy & Zaman, 2018, p. 7).

Despite research on rewards strongly indicating that they diminish intrinsic motivation. this finding remains controversial (Ryan & Deci, 2000, 2002), and even today, we find rewards used as environmental factors to try to motivate activity. In fact, rewards (eg, badges) are a common feature employed by fitness technologies. Some gamification studies have argued that badges can be perceived as competence‐supporting (Sailer et al., 2017; van Roy & Zaman, 2018)—although van Roy and Zaman (2018) note the issue of fixating on collecting badges described above. Fitness technology studies have categorized them as perceived affordances for task accomplishment (Rockmann & Gewald, 2017; Zhang & Lowry, 2016). The argument for badges in particular is that they can be seen as a form of feedback, and some studies have found that they are linked to the satisfaction of the competence need (Sailer et al.. 2017) and increased system use when implemented as the primary component a gamified system (over the nongamified version) (Hamari, 2017). However, research has also qualified the impact of badges by finding that use increased only in individuals that regularly monitored their own and others' badges (Hamari, 2013). Other studies have also examined the timing of badges as an expression of goal attainment and found that user behaviour differs as an effect of when and how frequently badges are given, observing that desired behaviours decrease after goal attainment and may not fully recover to obtain subsequent badges, suggesting that continuous external incentives may be necessary to prompt behaviours and even that may not be sustainable (Goes, Guo, & Lin, 2016; Yang, Yang, & Teo, 2017). Furthermore, the addition of gamified elements to a learning environment, in particular badges and leaderboards, has been shown to decrease intrinsic motivation and lead to poorer performance (Hanus & Fox, 2015). Another gamification study also found that points, levels, and leaderboards did not impact participants competence or intrinsic motivation but did increase performance, which the authors interpreted as the gamification elements “functioning as extrinsic incentives” (Mekler, Brühlmann. Tuch. & Opwis, 2017 p. 525)

In the SDT literature, a meta‐analysis of 128 studies indicates that engagement‐, completion‐, and performancecontingent rewards thwart autonomy, diminishing intrinsic motivation by shifting the individual towards an externa perceived locus of control (Deci et al., 1999). These include both tangible (eg, money) and symbolic (eg, “good player awards”) rewards (Deci, 1971: Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett, 1973: Ryan & Deci, 2002), Research also indicates that “threats, deadlines, directives, pressured evaluations, and imposed goals diminish intrinsic motivation because, like tangible rewards, they conduce toward an external perceived locus of control” (Ryan & Deci, 2000, p. 70). Thus, we propose that the ECFs that mimic these environmental factors (ie, reward, reminder, and goal management features) are autonomy‐thwarting environmental factors that may diminish intrinsic motivation.

Lowry et al. (2015) use the example of “Mr. Clippy,” the animated assistant in Microsoft Office that was ver poorly received, to describe a situation where motivation and system features may be in conflict. They argued, for example, that a “fun” or hedonistic system feature like “Mr. Clippy” may be in conflict with more work‐related intrinsic or extrinsic goals for using a system, resulting in distraction and leading to annoyed users. Some earl findings on fitness technology use may lend themselves to a similar explanation. Using the argument that rewards diminish intrinsic motivation, Etkin (2016) argued that by calling attention to outputs, fitness technologies may make exercise feel like work for individuals who would otherwise enjoy exercise. We would extend that argument and posit that users who have intrinsic exercise goals (ie, exercise for enjoyment, interest, or competence) will be turned off by features that attempt to control their exercise. The ECFs beep and buzz at the user (eg, to stand up and take more steps), or they provide rewards for particular amounts of activity (eg, badges). If a user exercises for enjoyment, they are unlikely to need such rewards and reminders. These types of features may cause a similar reaction to “Mr. Clippy” or take some of the enjoyment out of an otherwise enjoyable activity (diminish intrinsic motivation). Based on this logic, we propose that exercisers who have intrinsic goals are less likely to use the ECFs

Exercisers with extrinsic motivations have the most trouble sustaining long‐term exercise because they are not purely intrinsically motivated (ie, exercising for the enjoyment of it) (Brunet & Sabiston, 2011). While fitness and social motives have been shown to be better, in terms of exercise adherence, than appearance motives (Ingledew & Markland, 2008; Ryan et al., 1997), research still advises that these three goals have extrinsic foci (Ryan et al., 1997). Individuals with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals are exercising due to perceived pressure from others (eg, a doctor telling them they need to exercise for their health or exercising to look more attractive to a potentia mate). Thus, they are not exercising for enjoyment and may think that the digital “push” provided by the fitness technology is a control that will help force them to exercise. Prior research has submitted that exercisers with extrinsic goals follow “investment‐reward behaviour” and may feel they need external controls to sustain the activity (Sebire et al., 2013). Furthermore, it has been argued that gamification elements like points and levels can function as extrinsic incentives to control behaviour (Mekler et al., 2017). Users that reported features such as medals and levels to be important gamification elements were found to be those focused on a particular outcome and proving their abilities to others (Hamari et al., 2018), both of which suggest they are focused outward.

Exercisers with a social extrinsic goal are exercising to increase their social interaction. While the ECFs are not technically “another person,” they function similarly to a digital assistant by providing the user messages like “time to move!” or “feed me more steps.” Thus, similar to those with body‐focused goals feeling the need to use controls to sustain their activity (Sebire et al., 2013), those with a social extrinsic goal are also likely to be more reliant on external controls because their goals are also extrinsic. In addition, the ECFs and the SIFs both rely heavily on notifications (ie, beeping and buzzing messages on the wearable and smartphone/app). Users that are drawn to, accepting of, or feel reliant upon this sort of control of their exercise are more likely to use the ECFs. Hence, exercisers with both body‐focused and social extrinsic exercise goals are already relying on external pressure, and thus, we posit that they would be most attracted to the ECFs that provide digital extrinsic pressure to exercise. In summary, we propose the following set of hypotheses:

H2a. Users with intrinsic exercise goals will be less likely to use the ECFs.

H2b. Users with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals will be more likely to use the ECFs.

H2c. Users with a social extrinsic exercise goal will be more likely to use the ECFs.

## 2.3.3 | Exercise goals and SIFs

The need for relatedness is based on work that culminated in what Baumeister and Leary (1995) called the need to belong, which submits that all humans have an innate need for a number of satisfying and pleasant social relationships. The need for relatedness accounts for the social nature of people, and in the sports, psychology literature has been associated with increased enjoyment of exercise (Ryan et al., 1997; Spink & Carron, 1992; Wankel 1993). In the gamification literature, having “teammates,” which means being part of a team that cooperates towards an objective, has been associated with satisfaction of the relatedness need (Sailer et al., 2017). It is possible, however, that integrating others into one's exercise could also thwart autonomy by serving as external pressure to exercise or thwart competence by providing a means to make harmful social comparisons. A study that implemented a gamified learning environment found “group competition that was predominantly designed to foster feelings of relatednes also diminished feelings of competence (when someone's team was low in the rankings) and autonomy (as th success of the Google+ Community not only depended on one student, but also on teammates)” (van Roy & Zaman 2018, p. 8).

Ryan et al. (1997) argue that one potential goal of exercise can be to increase social contact because people can include others in their exercise activity. In a study of youth sports, Wankel and Kreisel (1985) found that social factors (such as being with friends or on a team) were consistently ranked in‐between intrinsic and extrinsic factors underlying enjoyment. Reasons that individuals may be attracted to group physical activities include “group identification and commitment, social reinforcement, competitive stimulation and the opportunity for team activities” (Wankel, 1993, p. 155). One study reported that camaraderie with other exercisers influenced continued participation (Wankel, Yardley, & Graham, 1985). Spink and Carron (1992) also uncovered some evidence that group cohesion may improve exercise adherence. The argument made regarding the social goal of exercise is that although it may be extrinsic to the primary activity (exercise), it may increase enjoyment and thus have a positive impact on outcomes such as adherence (Ryan et al., 1997)

In the IS literature, system socialization (eg, social communication and playing with others) has been classified as an intrinsic motive (Lowry et al., 2015). Zhang (2008) state that technologies can afford relatedness through design elements that allow users to play games with each other and communicate. Fitness technology studie have considered social features such as leaderboards, competitions, and data sharing to provide affordances for self‐presentation, social comparison, and leadership (Rockmann & Gewald, 2017; Zhang & Lowry, 2016). A numbe of fitness technology features involve incorporating other people into the user's exercise regime. Competing with others, comparing to others, sharing exercise data with others, obtaining live coaching, and receiving encouragement from others all integrate other people into the exerciser's fitness activity. We propose that all of these features could support the need for relatedness through the integration of other people into the user's fitness regime.

We will use two examples to contrast how the SIFs may function for different individuals. First, consider an elderly parent who has a health issue that is mitigated by exercise. His or her child uses a Fitbit and purchases one for the parent, using the SIFs to make the two “friends” through the Fitbit app, which means that they can see each other's step counts. The child uses the Fitbit/app to monitor the parent's exercise to make sure he or she is following the physician's recommendation, and the parent views this as being monitored. Such monitoring reinforces the external pressure from the child to exercise and could thus be autonomy-thwarting, Socializing exercise could be viewed by those with body‐focused extrinsic goals as autonomy‐thwarting (Sebire et al., 2013). For example, Sebire et al. (2013) stated one of their participants made “fitness-based upward comparisons but she assumed that fitter others would negatively evaluate her fitness and felt self‐conscious around them” (p. 314). In a study of the fitness app Runkeeper, results indicated that a large percentage of higher‐performing friends in the app had a negativ impact on users' physical activities (Zhou, Kankanhalli, & Huang, 2016)

As a second example, consider a married couple who try to go on walks or runs with each other as much as possible. They add each other on the Fitbit app so they can run “challenges” to compete based upon step count when they are not together. This could be viewed as adding gamification to the exercise regime, and such socializatior could increase enjoyment of the exercise (Wankel, 1993). In the first example, the user has body‐focused goals and the SIFs are adding undesired social pressure to their exercise. In the second example, the users have social goals in exercise and are using the SIFs to add a fun competition element to their exercise programme. Prior research submits that exercisers with intrinsic exercise goals may have differing views of socializing exercise from those with other types of goals (Sebire et al., 2013). A qualitative analysis uncovered a consensus of participants “wh reported relatively strong, intrinsic goals in that they accepted their level of fitness compared with others' and focused on self‐referenced improvements” (Sebire et al., 2013, p. 313). The researchers interpreted their findings as portending that individuals with intrinsic exercise goals may be better able to use others as inspiration (Sebire et al., 2013).

In their study of the Runkeeper app, Zhou et al. (2017) classified exercisers as inactive, moderately active, active, and highly active and reported that active exercisers had the most social interaction on the app. They further contended that highly active users may be intrinsically motivated and thus rely less on social support and comparison. However, the same study also found the leaderboard to have an impact on all four types of exercisers, but that unlik the inactive, moderately active, and active, the highly active users did not respond to their friends recent activity. A different study on the Runkeeper app found that a higher number of “likes” and a higher number of lower‐performing friends led to more physical activity (Zhou et al.. 2016). Users intent on proving their capabilities to others (ie, are more outwardly focused) view socialization features to be important according to a study by Hamari et al. (2018), and another study by this research group showed that people will exercise for “likes” (Hamari & Koivisto, 2015). Results of using competition to stimulate activity were mixed in a study gamifying a workplace training task, and the authors concluded that not all types of competition will work for all types of individuals (Santhanam, Liu, & Shen, 2016). Thus, the literature has provided some support that while different types of exercisers may respond to social factors in their environment differently, they may still be appealing to many users. However, exercisers who are prone to unfavorable social comparisons, which would most aptly describe those with body‐focused motives for exercise, may not find the social features appealing.

It is straightforward to propose that exercisers with social exercise motives would be more likely to use the SIFs These users want to socialize their exercise, and the SIFs would assist them in doing so. While extrinsic to the exercise, social goals may increase enjoyment and the fitness technologies are providing environmental suppor towards this end. Exercisers that are motivated by intrinsic goals (ie, interest/enjoyment or competence) may also see the SIFs as fun and be able to use others as inspiration. Therefore, we propose that users with interest/enjoyment or competence exercise goals will be more likely to enact the SIFs. These goals are intrinsic, and the SIFs support the need for relatedness, which is an environmental factor beneficial to intrinsic motivation. However, we posit that exercisers with extrinsic body‐related exercise goals (appearance or fitness) will be less likely to see the utility in the SIFs. We argue that users exercising to improve their appearance or health will be less likely to use features that enable social interaction that may insert autonomy‐thwarting surveillance‐like controls on their activity or encourage self‐consciousness or unflattering social comparisons. Thus, we posit that exercisers with appearance or fitness goals will be less likely to use the SIFs. In summary, we propose the following set of hypotheses:

H3a. Users with intrinsic exercise goals will be more likely to use the SIFs.

H3b. Users with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals will be less likely to use the SIFs.

H3c. Users with a social extrinsic exercise goal will be more likely to use the ECFs.

## 2.4 | Exercise goals, fitness technology feature set use, and subjective vitality

Subjective vitality is defined as “a positive feeling of aliveness and energy” (Ryan & Frederick, 1997, p. 529) and “results from satisfaction of the basic psychological needs, is an important indicator of health, and provides the necessary energy for effective self‐regulation and coping with challenges” (Deci & Ryan, 2012, p. 427). It is a measure of eudaimonic well‐being that is commonly employed in motivation studies because it has been associated with psychological need satisfaction (Ryan & Deci, 2002). Research has indicated that intrinsic goals are positively associated, and extrinsic goals negatively associated, with well‐being (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996; Miquelon & Vallerand, 2006). Extrinsic goals such as physical appearance and financial success (ie, wealth) have been found t be associated with less vitality (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996). Research has also indicated that autonomous (intrinsic)

goals are positively associated with hedonic well‐being (ie, happiness), where controlled (extrinsic) goals are not (Miquelon & Vallerand, 2006). Furthermore, the same study used a different eudiamonic well‐being measure and found autonomous goals to be positively associated, and controlled goals to be negatively associated, with it. Thus a positive relationship between intrinsic exercise goals (ie, interest/enjoyment and competence) and subjectiv vitality would mimic previous motivational findings, as would a negative relationship between body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals and subjective vitality. We hypothesize a positive relationship between the social extrinsic exercis goal and subjective vitality because social interaction during exercise may increase enjoyment (Spink & Carron, 1992; Wankel, 1993) and hence lead to better well‐being outcomes. In summary, we propose the following set of hypotheses:

H4a. Intrinsic exercise goals will be positively associated with subjective vitality.

H4b. Body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals will be negatively associated with subjective vitality.

H4c. The social extrinsic exercise goal will be positively associated with subjective vitality

Prior research on gaming has advocated that technology that is supportive of the basic psychological needs may show more positive well‐being effects (Ryan, Rigby, & Przybylski, 2006), concluding that for some, gaming may lea to restoration. Ryan et al. (2006) found that psychological need satisfactions could account for individual's attractio to games and that multiplayer games incorporated the relatedness need. The SIFs, in some ways, are similar to a multiplayer gaming environment. Need satisfactions have been shown to be important to well‐being outcomes i past research (Gunnell, Crocker, Mack, Wilson, & Zumbo, 2014; Gunnell, Crocker, Wilson, Mack, & Zumbo, 2013) lending support to the argument that feature sets that are supportive of the basic psychological needs are more likely to be positively associated with subjective vitality. Following this logic, we propose that the use of the DMFs that are supportive of the basic psychological needs of autonomy and competence, as well as the use of the SIFs that could support the relatedness need, will be positively associated with subjective vitality. The ECFs mimic autonomy thwarting environmental supports such as rewards, and thus, we posit that they will be negatively associated wit subjective vitality. In summary, we propose the following set of hypotheses:

H5a. Use of the DMFs will be positively associated with subjective vitality

H5b. Use of the ECFs will be negatively associated with subjective vitality.

H5c. Use of the SIFs will be positively associated with subjective vitality.

## 3.1 | Scale development, data collection, and respondent profile

We followed accepted practice to develop our survey and test our model (Churchill, 1979: MacKenzie, Podsakoff, 8 Podsakoff, 2011), including using existing scales whenever possible, employing an expert panel to evaluate the instrument, piloting the survey, confirming the validity and reliability of the scales, conducting checks for multicollinearit and common method bias, and testing the structural model. We will detail each step of the instrument developmen: and statistical testing below and supplement it with statistics provided in the Supporting Information.

We used several existing scales. The MPAM‐R items were used to measure the respondents exercise goals (Frederick & Ryan, 1993; Ryan et al., 1997). We also included items to measure subjective vitality (ie, energy and aliveness) in a physical activity context, using the subjective vitality scale developed in previous research (Bostic, Rubio, & Hood, 2000; Ryan & Frederick, 1997). Items to measure the use of fitness technology features were not available and thus were developed by the authors. To do so, we began as described in Appendix S1 by collecting lists of fitness devices and applications available for retail sale in the winter/spring of 2015. The authors independentl went to the website of all the devices listed in Table B2 in Appendix S2 and compiled the advertised features. Al of the lists were combined and discussed until an agreed upon set of features had been extracted and summarized These features are listed and defined in Figure 2 above. Items were then developed for each feature and are presented in Appendix S2.

Once constructed, the entire survey was given to an expert panel for review. The expert panel included two faculty members with expertise in survey methods and two employees of a fitness technology company. All four members of the expert panel were also users of fitness technologies for personal means. Along with asking the expert panel to review the language of all the items, we asked them to consider the identified features and indicate if the would add any others. Feedback from all the expert panellists was compiled and each suggestion considered by the authorship team.<sup>3</sup> The final survey, including all the items, the anchors and references for each scale, and the mean and standard deviations from the full data collection are reported in Appendix S2.

We developed an online version of the survey using the Qualtrics platform (http://www.qualtrics.com), which is an online survey tool. In developing the online survey, we ensured the respondents of their anonymity and did not collect any identifying information. We also randomized the survey items within blocks. These measures have bee suggested to decrease common method bias (Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Lee, & Podsakoff, 2003). We employed “attention trap” questions within blocks that instructed the respondents to select a particular choice from the Likert‐type scale anchors. Attention trap questions are included to determine whether respondents are cognitively engaged in the process and allow for the exclusion of any data points from which the respondent was disengage (Oppenheimer, Meyvis, & Davidenko, 2009).

Because we are exploring enacted feature sets, we needed respondents that were current or past users of fitness technologies. Therefore, we posed filter questions at the beginning of the survey that asked the respondents if they currently (or had in the past) used fitness technologies. Furthermore, respondents were asked to list all fitnes devices and apps they were currently using or had used in the past. The use statistics are provided in Table B2 in Appendix S2, which shows that the most popular devices at the time of our data collection were the Fitbit devices/apps, Nike devices/apps, MapMyFitness, Runkeeper, and MyFitnessPal

We collected pilot data on Amazon's Mechanical Turk (mTurk) crowdsourcing platform and paid each respondent a small amount of money (U.S.\$0.50) for taking the survey. Research has advocated that mTurk is an acceptable means to collect data for IS studies (L owry, D'Arcy, Hammer, & Moody, 2016). Research has also indicated that data collected on mTurk are as reliable as those obtained by traditional survey data collection mechanisms and may provide a sample with more diverse demographic characteristics (Buhrmester, Kwang, & Gosling, 2011; Mason & Suri 2012). For the pilot test, we collected 100 usable responses (ie, responses where the filter questions were answered indicating current or past use of fitness technologies, the attention traps were answered correctly, and the survey was fully completed). A factor analysis was run on the 100 responses to determine if the newly developed items and the scales borrowed from previous literature were functioning as expected. The factor analysis on the pilot data revealed the expected factor structure

We obtained 1034 responses on mTurk for our full data collection. Of the 1034, a total of 112 respondents di not complete the survey, 157 started the survey but did not pass the filter questions (ie, did not currently, nor in th past, use a fitness technology), and 146 did not pass at least one of the attention trap questions. Thus, the fina sample contained useable responses from 619 respondents. Demographics are presented in Table 1.

## 4 | DISCUSSION

The intent of our study was to gain insight into how exercise goals drive the use of specific fitness technology feature sets. Our results, shown in Table 2, reveal that different exercise goals are indeed associated with the use of different fitness technology feature sets. By itself, this is a useful finding. At the moment, fitness technologies tend to be marketed and explained in similar ways. They are not tailored towards different exercise goals, and our result indicate that there may be some advantage in doing so. Niche markets may exist for fitness technologies with features geared towards those exercising for body‐focused extrinsic (ie, to look better and to be healthier), intrinsic (ie, to improve competence and for interest/enjoyment), or social extrinsic (ie, for social interaction) motives.

As hypothesized, users with intrinsic exercise goals are more likely to use the DMF $( 1 1 1 a ; 0 . 2 4 0 , P \le 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). While we hypothesized that exercisers with intrinsic goals would be less likely to use the ECFs and more likely to use the SIFs, we found no significant relationship between them instead. These results provide support for the argument that environmental factors providing informational support (ie, that are competence-supporting) will be most attractive to individuals exercising for enjoyment or competence reasons. The DMFs allow exercisers who have intrinsic goals to measure their progress and gauge their competence when they want to do so. For example, an exerciser can monito his or her progress by examining the dashboards provided by the Fitbit or Apple Activity apps that visualize the data collected by the fitness tracker, and this competence‐supporting information can be accessed on demand. While this information is always available for the user to access, it is not forced on the user (eg, like a notification of a goal achieve ment) or intended to prompt their activity (eg, like a notification to keep up the good work), which is the case with th ECFs. While we expected the SIFs to appeal to exercisers with intrinsic goals because they could increase enjoyment, our finding supports the contention in a recent study that intrinsically motivated exercisers may be less reliant on socia support and take less advantage of the social features of fitness technologies (Zhou et al., 2017).

Exercisers with body‐focused and social extrinsic exercise goals have different patterns of fitness technology feature set use. The differing results are not terribly surprising because there is some uncertainty in prior exercise and motivation literature over how to treat these goals (Ingledew & Markland, 2008; Ryan et al., 1997). Furthermore, research in IS has found differences in the relationships between different types of extrinsic motivations and task effort in open‐source software projects (Ke & Zhang, 2010), following the organismic integration subtheory of SDT (Deci & Ryan, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2000). Ke and Zhang (2010) advise that extrinsic motivations are often considered similarly in prior research, and the results of their study indicate they should not be. Our results suppor this recommendation for extrinsic goals.

The results of our analysis indicate that body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals have a positive relationship with the DMFs $( \mathsf { H 1 b } ; 0 . 1 9 1 , P \le 0 . 0 1 )$ and a negative relationship with the SIFs $( 1 1 3 \mathrm { b } \colon - 0 . 0 9 8 , P \le 0 . 0 5 )$ , as hypothesized. Body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals have no significant relationship with the ECFs. The DMFs provide support for the competence and autonomy needs, which may be appealing to users exercising for health or appearance reasons because it allows them to self‐monitor progress towards body‐related goals (eg, weight loss). The DMFs allow th user to recognize progress towards health, fitness, and appearance goals with autonomy as long as the data ar not being shared with others through the SIFs. A notable result is that exercisers with body‐focused extrinsic goals did not report using the ECFs. We expected exercisers with extrinsic goals to be drawn to features that provided extrinsic controls on their behaviour (eg, notified them to take a walk or stand up). However, the finding corresponds with the stance in the SDT literature that rewards and reminders should not be used to motivate activities because such environmental factors shift the perceived locus of control away from the individual, diminishing intrinsic motivation, which is undesirable in terms of adherence to the activities (Ryan & Deci, 2000).

One possible explanation for the negative relationship between body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals and the SIFs is the possible sensitivity of the information being shared in the use of such features. Individuals who are exercising, for example, to lose weight or because they have a health condition for which a doctor recommended exercise, may not want others to know how well they are doing (or not doing) in terms of exercise progression to avoid feelings of guilt or embarrassment. This reflects the argument made by Sebire et al. (2013) that exercisers with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals may be more self‐conscious. Prior research has also advised that social physique anxiety may itself be a controlling factor that thwarts the basic psychological needs (Brunet & Sabiston, 2009). Future research may want to examine this finding further and explore options to avoid use of the SIFs resulting in embarrassment or negative socia comparison because it is possible that socializing exercise in a way that avoids such negative outcomes may add socia support or gamification that could be effective in progressing exercise goals. For example, one article quoted a user as stating “when I got a Fitbit 18 months ago, it was amazing. My daily dashboard, focusing mostly on a 10,000‐step targe was incredibly motivating. Was I hitting the target? Answer: sometimes. Connecting with my friends added great gamification. I began making different choices in my daily routine to take more steps” (Cotgreave, 2015).

The social extrinsic exercise goal has positive relationships with both the ECFs (H2c: 0.475, P ≤ 0.001) and the SIEs (H3c: 0 633 P. ≤ 0 001). as hypothesized The social extrinsic exercise goal was added to the MPAM because research indicated that while this goal is extrinsic to exercise itself, it may increase overall enjoyment with the activity and could increase adherence. This means that even individuals who do not like to exercise might be enticed to do so more often if they find the social interaction enjoyable. We contend that our findings could be a result of exercisers who have a social extrinsic exercise goal perceiving the SIEs to afford entertainment possibilities. Previous technol ogy acceptance research studying intrinsic motivation, albeit with regard to the use of technology itself rather than exercise, has argued that computer playfulness will lead to individuals using new technology just for the fun of it (Venkatesh. 2oo0), Using the fitness technologies for fun could provide a partial explanation for our findings with regard to the social extrinsic exercise goal. Social exercisers may be looking for extrinsic entertainment to motivate an otherwise unenjoyable activity. It should be noted that in the current generations of fitness technologies, the SIFs rely heavily on notifications. That is, the wearable/app notifies, usually through beeps and buzzes similar to the ECFs, that another user has, for example, passed that individual in a challenge or sent the person a message. Users relying on social interaction through notifications may find the buzzing and beeping from the ECFs more tolerable. This ma also provide a partial explanation for the social extrinsic exercise goal result regarding the ECFs.

The results for the controls indicate that as the length of fitness technology use increases, so does the reported use of the DMFs and SIFs. This is unsurprising because the ECFs are typically on by default in most fitness technol ogies and are in many ways the easiest feature set to use. For example, when a user gets a Fitbit/app, it automatically beeps and buzzes at him or her to take more steps and awards badges when he or she reaches certain step counts However, the user has to add “friends” to the app and start a challenge with them, and finding the historical analytics (eg, the past week of activity) requires some familiarity with the app. Hence, our results indicate that there may be a learning curve to using the DMFs and SIFs. These findings reinforce the need for more guidance on the use of fitness technologies for exercisers with different motivational, as well as demographic, characteristics. As the age of the respondents increased, they were less likely to use the DMFs and they reported higher levels of subjective vitality. One interpretation of this result is that older users have more trouble using the app, which is typically necessary to take full advantage of the DMFs.

Motivation theory tells us that intrinsic goals lead to better well‐being (Kasser & Ryan, 1993, 1996; Miquelon & Vallerand, 2006). This is confirmed in our model by the positive, significant path between intrinsic exercise goals and subjective vitality (H4a: 0.560, $P \leq 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). Both the body‐focused and social extrinsic exercise goals had no significant relationship with subjective vitality. Motivation theory also tells us that some of the most prominent features of current fitness technologies may diminish intrinsic motivation (eg, ECFs: rewards, reminders, and goal management) which should not lead to better reported well-being (ie, subiective vitality). We found no significant relationshir between the ECFs and subjective vitality. However, we also found no significant relationship between the DMFs and subjective vitality. Because the DMFs are competence‐supporting, we argued that there would be a positive relationship between them and subjective vitality. Future research should investigate this finding further to explore how the DMFs may contribute to wellness outcomes. The relationship between the SIFs and subjective vitality was positive and significant (H5c: 0.097, P ≤ 0.05). This finding indicates that there may be promise in using the SIFs to promote well‐being, and we would encourage future research to pursue this avenue.

## 5 | IMPLICATIONS FOR SOCIETY AND PRACTICE

Fitness technologies may be attractive to individuals looking for a way to increase their exercise. This may be especially true for exercisers with extrinsic goals who do not enjoy exercise but want to improve their appearance or health (ie, exercisers who have body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals) or like to socialize exercise. However, fitness technologies may be providing features that are not fully supportive of the basic psychological needs that promote intrinsic motivation, which may reduce their effectiveness. The reminders, rewards, and goal management features are often defaulted on and thus are the easiest to use. However, motivation theory advises that such features are autonomy‐thwarting, which means that they may not facilitate intrinsic motivation. For example, one user was quoted in an article as saying that the fitness technology was like “having a tiny, overbearing mother always on you person” (Garber, 2015, p. 1), which is a reference to the reminder feature of the ECFs. In fact, our results, summarized in Table 3, indicate that only one exercise goal (social extrinsic) has a positive relationship with the ECFs. The relationships between both intrinsic and body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals and the ECFs are insignificant.

Thus, the most promoted fitness technology features are likely to be the least attractive feature set for many exercisers. This indicates that as quantified self technologies advance, the focus should be on enhancing features that are supportive of the basic psychological needs of autonomy, competence, and relatedness. A similar argument has been made for other technologies such as virtual communities, online learning, and video games (Roca & Gagné, 2008; Ryan et al., 2006; Sailer et al., 2017; Sørebø et al., 2009; van Roy & Zaman, 2018; Yoon & Rolland, 2012)

The DMFs are used by exercisers with both intrinsic and body‐focused extrinsic goals towards exercise. One explanation of this result is found in our example marathoner who uses the fitness technologies to keep track of his or her progress training for a race. Motivation theory tells us that competence‐supporting feedback facilitates intrinsic motivation (Ryan & Deci, 2000), and training users on the most effective uses of the DMFs may be a worthwhile approach to help support more types of exercisers. Designers of future generations of fitness technologies may also want to consider expanding the DMFs to include features that provide insightful analytics on progress towards body‐focused goals, as well as advancing the presentations of the common fitness and health analytics (Epstein, Caraway, et al., 2016). Motivation research in the exercise context recommends emphasizing intrinsic goals to exercisers with extrinsic goals in an attempt to switch their focus (Sebire et al., 2009; Seghers, Vissers, Rutten Decroos, & Boen, 2014), an intervention that fitness technologies could assist.

The SIFs are used by exercisers with social goals. Notably, the SIFs are the only feature set to have a positive relationship with subjective vitality. Taken together, these results indicate there is promise in using the fitness technologies to socialize exercise for some individuals and that doing so could potentially have positive ramifications on well‐being. However, exercisers with body‐focused goals are less likely to use the SIFs. One explanation may be found in the suggestion that such exercisers may be more self‐conscious (Sebire et al., 2013). Hence, guidance on how to best use these features should be provided for the users who may need to employ them differently to benefit The design of future generations of fitness technologies may also want to consider the addition of SIFs that make social comparisons more difficult. For example, providing users the ability to run challenges or be “friends” with other exercisers without displaying their overall step count to the other users (eg, an option to opt out of displaying step counts) may be better for users who are sensitive to unfavourable social comparisons.

## 6 | CONTRIBUTIONS TO RESEARCH AND THEORY

Our study contributes to the nascent research on quantified self technologies. The proliferation of quantified self and Internet of Things technologies is creating an increasing need to explore and better understand how these omnipresent personal data collection and analysis technologies are impacting the lives of individuals and reshaping society. The import is well summarized in the statement “the Internet of Things weaves together humans, things, and data in ways envisaged to overcome our current understanding of what constitutes a living space” (Prasopoulou, 2017, p. 287). Our study contributes to this conversation by showing how individuals find utility in and derive benefit from quantified self technologies intended to stimulate motivation for a particular activity. Specifically, we develop and test a model that explains how exercisers with different goals use fitness technologies and derive (or not) benefit from that use (see Table 3 for a summary of the results). We provide a brief summary of the contribution of our study in Table 4.

While a prominent stream of literature exists on motivation (see Deci & Ryan, 2012; Ryan & Deci, 2002) and motivation for exercise (see Teixeira et al., 2012), the insertion of technologies into individuals' motivational environments has not been extensively examined in this literature and our study provides an initial step in doing so. We leverage the existing body of knowledge on human motivation (ie, SDT and its subtheories) to distinguish users of fitness technologies by the exercise goals they hope to achieve, and integrate this motivation theory with the theory of affordances, which provides a basis for examination of the technology at a feature set level. This theoretical integration allows us to develop a model where the use of specific fitness technology feature sets can be integrated into what is already known about how, goal contents are associated with a well-being indicator (ie, subjective vitality). Thus, we can see which features of these novel technologies different users are drawn to and the routes to better well‐being that may be enabled through use.

Investigations drawing from motivation theory by IS researchers have concluded that it is important to determine how different technological stimuli help fulfil various intrinsic and extrinsic motives of individual users in order to facilitate positive interactions with technology (Lowry et al., 2015). Our study lends further credence to this position because our results suggest that the most promising route to well‐being through fitness technology use ma currently be through the use of the SIFs. However, only exercisers with social goals were drawn to the SIFs, which suggests that more work needs to be done to make these features more attractive to exercisers with intrinsic and body‐focused extrinsic goals. It will also be necessary to determine how to create new features that are positively associated with well‐being outcomes and perhaps especially to focus on new DMFs, which our results showed were used by exercisers with both intrinsic and body‐focused motives. Correctly matching users to features that will best facilitate positive outcomes will be crucial for both maintaining continued use of the technologies and achieving well‐being outcomes from use.

There is also a flourishing stream of IS literature that examines how gamification might best be leveraged to motivate tasks at work or school (Liu, Li, & Santhanam, 2013; Liu, Santhanam, & Webster, 2017; Mekler et al., 2017; Sailer et al., 2017; Santhanam et al., 2016; Suh, Cheung, Ahuja, & Wagner, 2017; van Roy & Zaman, 2018). Fitness technologies provide gamification elements in a volitional activity, which makes their study all the more critical to understanding what benefits can be achieved by gamifying an activity for which people may lack intrinsic motivation, Our study complements investigations into hedonic systems through our determination of which features from the menu provided by current generations of fitness technologies, some of which gamify the activity, appeal to users with different goal contents. Only exercisers with social extrinsic goals reported using the ECEs and SIEs that contain the most common game elements (rewards and challenges). Additionally, while the use of ECFs, which include rewards, were not positively associated with well‐being, SIFs, which include features such as challenges, were positively associated with well‐being. This suggests that not all gamified elements may lead to similar outcomes which is an area future research should explore in more detail. Studies of the impact of quantified self technologies are going to be increasingly necessary as their use continues to increase, and we would encourage IS researchers to build from our initial examination. Our approach could be adapted to study other personal technologies that have motivational elements. Technologies intended to help people better manage aspects of their lives are becoming increasingly prevalent. Some examples include financial apps that are intended to help people manage spending, time management or academic apps that are meant to help students achieve better educational outcomes, or the use of personal assistants (eg, Alexa) that help people manage their households. Using GCT to examine how individual's goals shape how they use such technologies is a promising area for further work that could build from our model.

Finally, our study contributes by providing an initial examination of an emerging technological context that has large societal implications. Successful use of technology to help individuals lead healthier and more eniovable lives is an outcome that many would like to see (Patel et al., 2015). Fitness technologies are one of the first quantified self technologies intended to help individuals better manage an aspect of their lives that if accomplished could lead to better health and well‐being. Our results suggest that fitness technologies show some potential to help facilitate motivational environments that may lead to positive well‐being outcomes. However, our results also indicate that future generations of fitness technologies may need to add or modify features that better support individuals with different motivational characteristics.

## 7 | LIMITATIONS AND DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE RESEARCH

Our study is not without limitations. Fitness technologies are relatively new, and thus, their development and use are still in the early stages. As such, fitness technology makers and consumers are learning about what works and what does not in order to inform future generations of products (Benbunan‐Fich, 2017). This research provides one snapshot of early use that indicates that there are complex use issues at play when employing such novel technologies. Longitudinal studies may add much value to our understanding of fitness technology use. Our results indicate that different exercise goals lead to differing patterns of use of fitness technologies. Future longitudina research could provide a structured use pattern for exercisers and see how such guidance impacts exercise and well‐being outcomes. A longitudinal study could also be employed to examine changes in exercise patterns (eg, whether the amount of exercise increased with various uses of the fitness technologies), which we were unable to do with cross‐sectional data. Future research may also want to consider other health and wellness outcomes (eg, happiness/affect and weight loss), as well as technological outcomes (eg, continuance)

Future research may also want to delve into the cognitive and psychological processes that guide users' decisions to employ fitness technologies in particular ways. Our study provides insight into how intrinsic and extrinsic goal lead to the use of different feature sets, but future work could extend our examination by further integrating concepts from other motivation (Lowry et al., 2013; Lowry et al., 2015; Ryan, Patrick, Deci, & Williams, 2008) or technology acceptance models (Cheng, 2011; Chiu & Wang, 2008; Lee et al., 2005; Roca & Gagné, 2008; Sørebø et al., 2009; Suh et al., 2017) to tease out the allure of different features for different types of exercisers.

Exercise goals are just one way of categorizing users. Other physical, motivational, and psychological characteristics of users or exercisers may be explored that may add to the conversation (eg, Mettler & Wulf, 2018; Wankel, 1993). For example, exploring the impact of social physique anxiety (Brunet & Sabiston, 2009) could be an interesting direction for future research. The motivation literature is rich, and there are many possibilities of drawing on it to further explore the use of technologies meant to motivate behaviour. Finally, characteristics of the technology itself other than the feature sets may be integrated into future models (eg, ease of use, usefulness, privacy, security, and invasivenes perceptions and concerns) (eg, Zhang, Dibia, Sodnomov, & Lowry, 2015)

## 8 | CONCLUSION

Our study draws on GCT and the theory of affordances to examine how intrinsic and extrinsic exercise goals drive enacted fitness technology feature sets. We find that intrinsic exercise goals (ie, interest, enjoyment, and competence) are associated with the use of the DMFs that allow the user to collect, analyse, and inform their progress in exercise. Body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals are also positively associated with the use of the DMFs but negatively associated with the use of the SIFs. This could indicate that users with body‐focused extrinsic exercise goals (eg, to improve their health or their appearance) could have privacy or anxiety concerns that make socializing exercise through the fitness technologies less attractive. The social extrinsic exercise goal is the only one positively associated with both the ECFs and SIFs. This indicates that some exercisers may enjoy socializing exercise and that the fitness technologies provide the capability of adding a social component or gamification without the necessity o physical presence. Furthermore, the SIFs are the only feature set that is positively associated with subjective vitality.

This could indicate that the most promising way for fitness technologies to have a positive impact on well‐being may be through socializing exercise.

Our results also point to design considerations (eg, providing more flexibility in how the users may share their information with “friends”) for future generations of fitness technologies to make them more universally appealing We also advocate for providing guidance on how users with different motivational and demographic characteristics should use the fitness technologies to achieve maximum benefits. Our study provides an initial examination of how one motivational characteristic (ie, exercise goals) is associated with the use of fitness technology feature sets that future studies can extend in order to provide increasingly more nuanced and extensive guidance for use and desig of future generations of quantified self technologies.
