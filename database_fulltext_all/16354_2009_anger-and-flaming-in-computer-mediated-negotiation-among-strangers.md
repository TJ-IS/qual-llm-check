# Anger and <sup>fl</sup>aming in computer-mediated negotiation among strangers

Norman A. Johnson ⁎, Randolph B. Cooper <sup>1</sup>, Wynne W. Chin <sup>2</sup>

C.T. Bauer College of Business, University of Houston, Houston, TX 77204-6282, United States

## a r t i c l e i n f o

Article history: Received 1 December 2007 Received in revised form 17 July 2008 Accepted 27 October 2008 Available online 5 November 2008

Keywords: Flaming Anger Negotiation Computer-mediated negotiation

## a b s t r a c t

The present study explores the antecedents of the antinormative hostile communication of emotion, termed <sup>fl</sup>aming, in the context of computer-mediated negotiation. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fl</sup>aming is associated with anger directed toward the negotiating context; this can occur when a negotiator becomes frustrated due to problems with communication channel characteristics or due to uncertainty surrounding the negotiation task. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that <sup>fl</sup>aming is associated with anger directed toward a negotiator's partner, which can occur when a negotiator perceives that the partner is being unfair. We <sup>fi</sup>nd further that anger totally mediates the relationships between <sup>fl</sup>aming and its antecedents. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide the basis to argue that anger reduction can effectively decrease <sup>fl</sup>aming, independent of the existence of <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents, such as unfairness. We therefore provide suggestions that can help with the reduction of anger in such circumstances.

## 1. Introduction

Negotiation is a process by which a joint decision is made by two or more parties, who perceive that their interests con<sup>fl</sup>ict [69]. Driven by the increase in online transactions enabled by the Internet [44], a recent trend is for negotiations to occur over computer media, such as email and instant messaging [32,44,88]. For example, in 2001 there were over 2 million transactions per week on eBay. Many of these transactions take place between buyers and sellers with con<sup>fl</sup>icting interests, and as result there is a need for negotiations [88]. Computer mediated dispute resolution services, such as Square Trade, handle a large number of these negotiations in over 120 countries [80]. By allowing buyers and sellers to engage in “direct negotiation” via computers, this type of service can lower the cost of negotiation, increase its speed, make it more informal, and reduce the need for third parties [44].

Unfortunately, using computer media for negotiation is not without its challenges. For a variety of reasons, this media can make it more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for negotiators to reach agreement [84,89], which is the primary goal of negotiation [36,50]. We are interested here in <sup>fl</sup>aming (e.g., the use of profanity to in<sup>fl</sup>ict harm) during computer-mxediated negotiations. This is important because (1) <sup>fl</sup>aming can signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduce the likelihood of negotiated agreements [37,71]; (2) the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming is signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly higher when using computer mediated channels [41,71]; and (3) computer-mediated channels are increasingly being used for negotiations [44].<sup>3</sup>

Given the potential bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of computer mediated negotiation and the potential for such negotiations to be severely disrupted by <sup>fl</sup>aming, it is of interest to gain a better understanding of what causes <sup>fl</sup>aming so that we can <sup>fi</sup>nd ways to reduce its in<sup>fl</sup>uence on computer mediated negotiations [68]. We therefore explore <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents within the context of computer-mediated negotiation. Our focal construct is the emotional state of anger, which we propose mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of many <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents, such as perceptions of unfairness. This mediation role is reasonable since anger can result in aggression [17], and <sup>fl</sup>aming is a form of aggression [26,83].

Our objective is not to identify new antecedents, but rather to explain theoretically and demonstrate empirically that anger signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on <sup>fl</sup>aming of important antecedents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by prior research. The advantage of this approach is to offer researchers and practitioners another avenue that can be pursued in order to decrease <sup>fl</sup>aming. The <sup>fi</sup>rst avenue would be that offered by others: to reduce the levels of the <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by earlier researchers. The alternative avenue offered here would be to reduce the level of anger, when the reduction of the antecedents' levels may be too costly or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult

## 2. Theory and research model

In this section we <sup>fi</sup>rst de<sup>fi</sup>ne negotiation, <sup>fl</sup>aming, computer mediation, and anger, that serve as the foundation of our research model (Fig. 1). We then develop our hypotheses that are based on these concepts and informed by prior theories.

## 2.1. Negotiation

Negotiation is a special case of group decision-making, where negotiating parties perceive each other's goals to be incompatible with their own [69]. However, the parties also believe that they can reach an agreement which makes each better off, and that they must cooperate to reach that agreement [61]. This results in a mixed-motive relationship “in which the parties cooperate by competing for divergent ends” [70]. However, this competition is tempered by the realization that as one party claims more of the resource, the likelihood of the other party cooperating (i.e., agreeing to the resource distribution) decreases; and lack of agreement (or impasse) decreases the potential utility for both parties. Our speci<sup>fi</sup>c concern is with <sup>fl</sup>aming that occurs during a distributive negotiation, because anger is frequently linked to such negotiation [14,65].

## 2.2. Flaming and computer mediation

Flaming is generally de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the antinormative hostile communication of emotions that includes the use of profanity, insults, and other offensive or hurtful statements [51,71]. Flaming may be directed at an individual's partner<sup>4</sup> (e.g., calling the partner a vulgar name) or at the negotiation context (e.g., cursing bad luck) [71]. The literature suggests that, compared to face-to-face, the increased incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming when using computer-mediated communication is due to reductions in the transfer of social cues [41,78], which decrease individuals' concern for social evaluation and fear of social sanctions or reprisals [66,79]. When social identity and ingroup status are salient, computer mediation can decrease <sup>fl</sup>aming because individuals focus their attention on the social context (and associated norms) rather than themselves [51,68]. However, we are interested in negotiations between parties who are strangers (i.e., unfamiliar with each other), so that social identity and ingroup status become less salient, and therefore do not serve to mitigate the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming.

Computer-mediated negotiation systems can vary regarding the degree to which they convey communication beyond the simple exchange of offers and counter-offers. One might argue that systems restricting communication to offers and counter-offers may be better suited for negotiations concerning more simple, commodity-like items, while systems allowing communication in addition to offers and counter-offers may be better suited for negotiations regarding more complex, non-commodity items. However, independent of whether the item is simple or complex, more effective negotiations can occur by promoting positive feelings between negotiators, and such feelings can be developed through positive affective communication [69,59]. And such affect can only be conveyed when communication beyond offers and counter-offers is allowed. Unfortunately, when such communication is permitted, <sup>fl</sup>aming can occur.

## 2.3. Anger

Our focus on anger does not include strategic motivation as an antecedent to <sup>fl</sup>aming. An attempt to intimidate one's partner through demonstrated anger can be strategically motivated, independent from feelings of anger [15], and can be successful in situations where fear is justi<sup>fi</sup>ed, such as where physical violence or other signi<sup>fi</sup>cant retribution is possible [30]. It is therefore less important in computer-mediated negotiations among strangers and concerning typical goods and services. In addition, the likelihood of intimidation being successful increases when anger is demonstrated via body, facial, and vocal cues [30,76]. Because these cues are absent in computermediated communication, intimidation is less likely to be successful, and negotiators will be less likely to attempt such a strategy [7,57]. We therefore are less concerned here with strategically motivated <sup>fl</sup>aming. This issue is addressed further in the discussion section.

The next section addresses the antecedents of <sup>fl</sup>aming when <sup>fl</sup>aming is directed at an individual's negotiation partner. We propose that such <sup>fl</sup>ames are more likely to occur when an individual is angry with a partner, and that this anger can arise from perceptions that the partner is not being fair. The following section addresses antecedents of <sup>fl</sup>aming when <sup>fl</sup>aming is directed at the negotiation context. We propose that such <sup>fl</sup>ames are more likely to occur when an individual is angry with the context, and that this anger can arise from frustration caused by a lack of media richness and by uncertainty surrounding the negotiations. These antecedents to anger and therefore <sup>fl</sup>aming are not meant to be exhaustive, but are those that have been identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by prior research and that are likely to be important during distributive negotiations conducted via computer-mediated channels.

## 2.4. Partner-directed flaming hypotheses

Partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming is aggressive behavior in the form of negative socio-emotional communication. This characterization of <sup>fl</sup>aming has been described in earlier studies as verbal aggression, hostile verbal behavior, and a form of social aggression [26,83]. Anger is associated with aggression according to Catharsis theory, which is one of the more popular and authoritative theories on this relationship [17]. The theory holds that acting aggressively<sup>5</sup> against a provoking individual (e.g., venting) will improve one's psychological state [17]. The theory suggests that it is better to let anger out by directing it against a provoking entity and thereby reduce arousal, rather than keep it inside and have it build up to the point at which a more dangerous uncontrollable reaction may result [17]. Furthermore, studies have shown that people often engage in such aggressive behavior because they believe that it improves their mood (e.g., [9,19]). In other words, aggression is undertaken in the service of affectregulation goals [3,19]. Although there may be social norms restricting the venting of anger in the form of <sup>fl</sup>aming [90], angry individuals tend to have less restraint, and express their anger as a form of disrespect and reproach [35,72]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H _ { 1 } } .$ The anger of an individual toward a partner will increase <sup>fl</sup>aming that is directed at that partner.

Anger that is associated with partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming can result from the violation of social norms, which are beliefs as to what constitutes approved and disapproved conduct [2]. The degree to which a negotiator believes a partner is following social norms associated with fairness determines his or her perceptions of whether that partner is negotiating in good faith [56]. Appraisal theory suggests that emotions, such as anger, are elicited and differentiated based on individuals' evaluations of such norms [74,77]. This theory has been empirically supported with, for example, violations of social norms resulting in feelings of moral outrage and anger [65].

One of the most important social norms for negotiation has to do with fairness. Though negotiators expect fairness, their fairness judgments are subjective, and they tend to interpret fairness in ways that favor themselves [11]. Therefore, negotiations can result in situations where both negotiators see the other as being unfair, with each feeling that he or she is being taken advantage of by the other. Such perceptions of unfairness can result in one or both negotiators feeling anger toward the other [62,65]. We next describe unfairness associated with vested interests and reciprocity.

## 2.5. Vested interests

Equity is an aspect of fairness, and dictates that people should receive equitable compensation [1]. For instance, in an organizational work group, the ratio of rewards received to resources provided should be equal for each member [1]. Vested interests are resources that an individual believes to be of value and to which he or she is entitled. In negotiation, individuals taking advantage of their partners by not giving adequate consideration to, or compensation for, the value of these resources are perceived as violating the equity norm, which can lead to anger [71]. For example, if an individual values time and expects serious negotiation, perceptions that a partner is wasting negotiation time (e.g., making offers that are frivolous rather than serious or not communicating back within a reasonable time frame) can lead to anger [71]. This leads to the following hypothesis.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 2 } .$ Inadequate consideration of an individual's vested interests by his or her partner will increase anger that the individual feels toward the partner.

## 2.6. Reciprocity

Reciprocity is another aspect of fairness, and dictates that favorable treatment provided by one party is to be met by the other party returning the favor [40]. This sense of obligation has been credited with the notion of being human [52], with enabling the division of labor thereby facilitating the human adaptive ability [85], and with facilitating “sophisticated and coordinated systems of aid, gift giving, defense, and trade…” [24]. Within negotiations, this norm prescribes that a concession by an individual (e.g., a seller lowering his or her price) should be met with a concession by a partner (e.g., the buyer raising his or her price). Violations of reciprocity can lead to anger. For example, Torestad [87] found that subjects got angry when they were kind to a friend and the friend did not reciprocate. The subjects felt this way because they wanted to be treated in the same manner in which they treated their friends.

A lack of reciprocity can occur in negotiation, for example, when one party employs a positional commitment tactic by holding <sup>fi</sup>rm to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c position [69]. Such behavior often results in a partner feeling angry [65]. In general, reduced reciprocity leads to dislike and distrust [43,54], and these feelings can lead to anger because they promote beliefs that the other's intentions are harmful to one's own interest [86]. This leads to the following hypothesis.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 3 } .$ Violations of reciprocity by an individual's partner will increase anger that the individual feels toward that partner.

## 2.7. Context-directed flaming hypotheses

The negotiation context includes all aspects of negotiation except for the negotiators themselves. Consistent with Catharsis theory [17], an individual can become angry and behave aggressively toward aspects of the negotiation context [13]. For example, a survey of 1250 workers found that 80% had seen colleagues shouting abuses at their personal computers [60]. In general, individuals become angry at the context when elements of the context, such as the computer or communication media, frustrate their ability to resolve problems or ful<sup>fi</sup>ll their needs [13]. Such anger can lead to aggressive behavior, such as <sup>fl</sup>aming [13,37]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 4 } .$ Anger of an individual born of frustration with the context will increase <sup>fl</sup>aming by the individual directed at the context

## 2.8. Communication media richness

Reduced communication media richness can lead to frustration during negotiation. For example, the lack of feedback immediacy can be frustrating for two reasons. First, decreased immediacy reduces negotiators' abilities to understand what is being communicated because more rapid exchanges enable people to identify communication patterns [29]. In addition, understanding is facilitated with the ability to interject brief clarifying questions, which are more effective when rapid exchanges are possible [29,84]. Second, negotiators tend to behave as if they are in synchronous (face-to-face) negotiations, independent of the actual communication media [84]. When computer mediated communication is employed and the expected (face-to-face) conversational turn taking does not occur, negotiators can become frustrated with the process [84]. Thus, reduced feedback immediacy can lead to frustration and anger due to increased misunderstandings as well as the violation of behavioral (turn taking) expectations [84].

In addition, the reduction in a communication media's ability to transfer verbal (linguistic), pictorial (image), and nonverbal (facial expressions, vocal intonations, etc.) cues can lead to frustration due to the attendant reduction in understanding that can be conveyed [47,92]. For example, nonverbal cues such as vocal intonations can be employed to emphasize the in<sup>fl</sup>exibility of an individual's bargaining position and the inability of computer-mediated communication to convey such cues will reduce an individual's understanding of a partner during negotiation.<sup>6</sup> Taken together, reduced feedback immediacy along with the reduction in multiple cues can increase misunderstandings and result in frustration and anger during negotiation when using a leaner media [84]. This leads to the following hypothesis.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 5 } .$ Perceived lack of richness will increase anger directed toward the communication media

## 2.9. Task Uncertainty

Like communication media, negotiation tasks can be characterized in a number of ways [84]. Frequently, these tasks are characterized in terms of uncertainty relating to the value individuals place on the object of negotiation (e.g., [16,89]). For example, when negotiating the ownership of a company's stock,the degree of uncertainty surrounding the likelihood that the stock price will increase in the future affects the perceived stock's value during negotiation [16]. Increased task uncertainty combines with individual's biases and result in an increased likelihood that their expectations will not be met, which in turn, leads to anger.

Individuals' biases in regard to uncertainty include the misconception of chance [10] and the illusion of control [49]. Misconception of chance relates to individuals' beliefs that the probabilities of winning will “even out” over time even though the uncertain outcomes are independent of each other. For example, after being dealt a number of losing poker hands, individuals tend to believe that the likelihood of being dealt a winning poker hand increases. In such situations, the likelihood of frustration increases because, while the likelihood of receiving a winning poker hand stays constant, individuals' expectations of winning increase. Higher expectations coupled with individuals' failures to achieve their goals can result in frustration and thus in anger [9,14]. Illusion of control is associated with individuals' perceived abilities to control the outcomes of uncertain events [49]. Such beliefs can increase motivation and persistence [81]; however, they can also lead to ineffective decision making [10] since individuals do not actually have the level of control upon which they base their decisions. Therefore, when negotiation has an element of uncertainty, the illusion of control may prevent negotiators from realizing their expectations, which can lead to frustration and anger [14]. This leads to the following hypothesis:

$\mathbf { H _ { 6 } } .$ Task uncertainty will increase anger directed toward the negotiation task.

## 2.10. Displaced aggression hypotheses

Displaced aggression occurs when an individual directs aggression to an entity other than the one that caused the anger associated with the aggression [18,20]. This type of aggression occurs when an individual is constrained in aggressing against the cause of anger [20]. For example, if the cause of an individual's anger is likely to retaliate against the individual, then the individual is likely to displace aggression toward another entity that is less likely to retaliate [20]. Choosing to displace aggression is a quick and impulsive decision that is made by an angry individual [82].

Displaced aggression is similar to direct aggression in all respects, except for the relationship between the source of anger and the target of the aggression. Thus, anger toward the negotiation context can lead to partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming, particularly when the individual cannot aggress against the context (e.g. because there are salient sanctions against physical abuse of the computer). Similarly, anger toward a partner can lead to context-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming, particularly when the negotiation partner can retaliate by, for example, not agreeing to a reasonable price. We therefore hypothesize the following:

$\mathbf { H } _ { 7 \mathbf { a } } .$ The anger of an individual toward the context will increase <sup>fl</sup>aming directed at the partner.

$\mathbf { H } _ { 7 \mathbf { b } } .$ The anger of an individual toward a partner will increase <sup>fl</sup>aming directed at the context.

## 3. Method

These hypotheses were tested in a laboratory study that involved 148 undergraduate students (40% female) in a large university. Each student was randomly assigned to a negotiating dyad<sup>7</sup> (resulting in seventy-four dyads) and the role of either seller or buyer; the identity of each dyad member was unknown to the other. Students participated in nine rounds of negotiating with the same partner using computermediated communication. We next describe the study task and procedure, followed by a discussion of how the constructs were operationalized.

## 3.1. Task

The negotiation object for each round was a different computerdisplayed lottery ticket. Negotiations involved determining a mutually agreeable price for the lottery ticket to be exchanged from one negotiator to the other. Lottery ticket prices were measured in terms of points. Individuals were given \$10 for participating and were told that after all rounds were over, one of the rounds would be chosen at random, and the individual in each dyad with the most points for that round would receive an extra \$10. This reward scheme helped motivate individuals to negotiate in each round independent of past or future round outcomes. The points associated with each ticket were determined by two chance events. (1) Each ticket was displayed with two potential values (say 15 and 30 points), each of which had a 50% chance of being the actual value. (2) Along side the ticket, a prize wheel was displayed (see Fig. 2) that had the following differently colored portions: one (say 75%) representing the likelihood of winning if the actual value were 15, and one (say 95%) representing the likelihood of winning if the actual value were 30. With this example, the ticket holder had a 37.5% chance of winning 15 points $( 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 7 5 ) ,$ a 47.5% chance of winning 30 points $( 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 9 5 )$ , and a 15% chance of winning nothing $( 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 2 5 + 0 . 5 ^ { * } 0 . 0 5 )$ . If the seller decides to keep the ticket and won the lottery (the prize wheel landed on the win area), the seller receives points equal to the actual ticket value; otherwise the seller receives zero points. In either case, the buyer receives zero points. If the ticket is sold, then the seller receives points equal to the selling price, and the buyer receives points equal to the lottery winnings (determined as described above for the seller) less the amount that is paid for the ticket.

Sellers and buyers were situated in separate locations. Each seller and buyer used a personal computer to see the lottery tickets and prize wheels as well as to converse with each other, using their PCs for text-based instant messaging. Personal computers were connected such that each simultaneously displayed the same information to individuals in the same dyad.

## 3.2. Procedure

As subjects arrived at the experimental site, they were randomly assigned a role (buyer or seller) and escorted to one of two locations, depending on that role. There they received instructions about the game via a power point presentation, and completed three practice rounds of negotiation. During these exercises, individuals were encouraged to ask questions if they were uncertain about the game procedures and objectives. In the practice rounds, they were told that the experiment began when a lottery ticket, a prize wheel, a timer, and instructions appeared on their computer screen. The buyers (sellers) were also told that sellers (buyers) were in a remote location and would see the same information at the same time. Further, they were told that either individual could initiate negotiation by contacting their dyad partner via the PC. Finally, they were told that they had three and a half minutes with which to complete their negotiations. Before the time elapsed, the seller had to con<sup>fi</sup>rm, via the PC that the ticket was sold (if an agreement with the buyer was reached) or that it was not sold. If the seller did not make such a con<sup>fi</sup>rmation and the time elapsed, both the seller and buyer would receive no points.<sup>8</sup>

## 3.3. Identification of flaming communication

Earlier, we de<sup>fi</sup>ned <sup>fl</sup>aming as the anti-normative hostile communication of emotions. Therefore, a three-step process was employed to identify <sup>fl</sup>aming. First, transcripts of the negotiations were examined in order to identify hostile communication. Two undergraduate student raters blind to the research hypotheses worked independently and coded each negotiation message in terms of the degree to which it conformed to Bales [6] negative socio-emotional communication category 12: an individual shows antagonism, de<sup>fl</sup>ates other's status, and/or defends or asserts self [6]. The resulting inter-rater reliability was 0.88.

Second, these student raters took all transcripts that either one or both identi<sup>fi</sup>ed as being in Bales' category 12, and independently evaluated the messages regarding the degree to which they appeared to be anti-normative. The resulting inter-rater reliability was 0.95. These students then discussed and reconciled their different evaluations, and <sup>fi</sup>nally agreed on messages they believed were <sup>fl</sup>ames. This resulted in 25 dyads that had at least one round in which at least one <sup>fl</sup>ame occurred. For dyads having more than one <sup>fl</sup>aming round, the round in which the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fl</sup>ame occurred was chosen.

Third, as described next, a different set of undergraduate student raters blind to the research hypotheses employed multiple scales to evaluate the degree to which context and partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming occurred in these 25 rounds. This approach is reasonable since individuals are generally able to discern others' communicated emotional states [33].

## 3.4. Preparation of transcripts

Data to test the hypotheses were gathered by having student raters not involved in the task examine the communication between dyad partners and determine the levels of model constructs. Transcripts of dyads' communications were prepared as follows to facilitate these determinations.

1. As described above, 25 dyads had at least one <sup>fl</sup>aming message. These dyads represented 1/3 of the total dyads. When <sup>fl</sup>aming occurred in more than one round for a dyad, only the round where the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fl</sup>ame occurred was selected. This resulted in 25 transcripts in which <sup>fl</sup>aming occurred.

2. The transcripts were edited such that messages after the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fl</sup>ame in the round were deleted. For example, if in round 4 there were 20 messages, and the <sup>fi</sup>rst <sup>fl</sup>ame occurred on the 10th message, then messages 11 through 20 were deleted.

3. Transcripts of all rounds after the <sup>fl</sup>aming round were deleted.

4. Transcripts of all rounds up to but not including the round prior to the <sup>fl</sup>aming round were deleted. In this example, transcripts for rounds 1, 2, and 3 were deleted.

5. The lottery outcome (how many points were won by whom) for the round prior to the <sup>fl</sup>aming round was included with the transcript. In this example, the outcome might indicate that the buyer purchased the ticket for 8 points though he or she received zero points from the round-3 lottery.

6. One non-<sup>fl</sup>aming dyad was randomly paired with each <sup>fl</sup>aming dyad. Transcripts for each non-<sup>fl</sup>aming dyad were edited so that they matched their dyad pair. In the example above, the non-<sup>fl</sup>aming dyad transcript for round 4 would be chosen, and would have messages after 10 deleted. Transcripts of all rounds after round 4 were deleted. Transcripts for rounds 1, 2 and 3 were deleted. The lottery outcome (how many points were won by whom) for round 3 was included with the transcript.

This resulted in 34 transcripts in which the last message was from the buyer (17 <sup>fl</sup>ame and 17 non-<sup>fl</sup>ame messages) and 16 transcripts in which the last message was from the seller (8 <sup>fl</sup>ame and 8 non-<sup>fl</sup>ame messages).

## 3.7. Construct variances

There was no experimental manipulation of the independent constructs' operationalizations. Variances found in these operationalizations were therefore due to naturally occurring individual differences within and across dyads. One might reasonably expect naturally occurring variances in anger and fairness (vested interests and reciprocity). However, <sup>fi</sup>nding variance among context-associated variables may not appear as straightforward. For example, since all dyads were using the same computer communication media, there is less reason to believe that variance would exist among perceptions of media richness. In fact, prior research has found considerable variance among such perceptions with individuals employing the same media because these perceptions are in part socially de<sup>fi</sup>ned [38,42]. In addition, perceptions of uncertainty (misconceptions of chance and illusion of control) have been found to vary signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly among individuals [10].

## 3.8. Control variable

One control variable was employed with paths leading to context and partner anger as well as to both <sup>fl</sup>aming constructs. This control re<sup>fl</sup>ects whether the buyer or the seller sent the <sup>fl</sup>aming message, and accounts for the fact that twice as many <sup>fl</sup>ames were sent by buyers than sellers.

These data were analyzed with partial least squares (PLS). Given the relatively small sample size and the fact that all of the constructs have multiple indicators, use of PLS was preferred to covariance structure analysis approaches such as LISREL (which typically require larger samples) and to more traditional techniques such as 2SLS (which cannot accommodate multiple measures) [22,23]. The path coef<sup>fi</sup>cients obtained from a PLS analysis are standardized regression coef<sup>fi</sup>cients, while the loadings of items on individual constructs are factor loadings. Factor scores created using these loadings are equivalent to weighted composite indices. Thus, PLS results can be easily interpreted by considering them in the terms of regression and factor analysis. As such, PLS allows us to assess how well the set of items used to measure one construct (e.g., anger toward partner) served to predict a dependent construct (e.g., partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming). Before examining the relationships among our theoretical constructs, the measures employed to represent the constructs (the measurement model) must be determined to be valid and reliable.

## 4.1. Measurement model results

The <sup>fi</sup>rst step in examining the measurement model is to determine measure adequacy. To ensure that items reliably measure constructs they are designed to measure, each item should have a loading of at least 0.6 on its own construct [5]. As illustrated in Tables 2a and 2b, all items exceed this requirement. In addition, items should load more on their own construct than the other constructs. As illustrated in Tables 2a and 2b, this is the case for all items. As a result of these analyses, it is reasonable to include all construct measures in the following analyses.

Composite reliabilities were calculated for the constructs based on item loadings, and as illustrated in Tables 3a and 3b, were all above the 0.70 threshold [64].<sup>9</sup> Construct convergent and discriminant validities were determined by comparing the square root of the average variance extracted for each construct with the correlations among constructs. When the square root of the average variance extracted for a construct is larger than the construct's correlations with other constructs, this indicates that each construct is more highly related to its own measures than to other constructs, and that convergent and discriminant validities are supported. As presented in Tables 3a and 3b, this is the case for all constructs. Moreover, all average variances extracted were above the recommended level of 0.50 [22,23].

## 5. Discussion

A basic assumption of this paper is that <sup>fl</sup>aming can have negative consequences for negotiation. However, it may be the case that, rather than jeopardizing potentially successful negotiation processes, <sup>fl</sup>aming occurs when individuals realize that no agreement is possible. If this were the case, then we would expect that dyads in which <sup>fl</sup>ames occurred would not have reached agreement. Of the 25 dyads in which <sup>fl</sup>aming occurred, 11 reached agreement. In contrast, 17 of the 25 non-<sup>fl</sup>aming dyads reached agreement. This suggests that, though agreement can be reached when <sup>fl</sup>aming occurs, <sup>fl</sup>aming jeopardizes potentially successful negotiations by decreasing the likelihood of agreement.

Our focus thus far has been on how anger can lead to <sup>fl</sup>aming. However, one might also question whether <sup>fl</sup>aming can cause anger. We examined the transcripts to see if there was evidence that the recipient of a <sup>fl</sup>aming message became angry. Though there were 5 dyads in which the <sup>fl</sup>ame sender continued sending <sup>fl</sup>aming messages, we found no dyads in which the <sup>fl</sup>ame recipient reciprocated with a <sup>fl</sup>aming message. This does not necessarily mean that the <sup>fl</sup>ame did not anger the recipient. For example, Table 4 illustrates two negotiations in which a seller's offer increased. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst instance, the buyer did not respond with a <sup>fl</sup>ame, and agreement was reached. In the second instance, the buyer responded with a <sup>fl</sup>ame and no agreement was reached. Though the seller did not come back with a <sup>fl</sup>ame in the second instance, it is reasonable to interpret the seller's further increase in selling price as an angry response to the buyer's <sup>fl</sup>ame. In addition, there were two dyads in which the <sup>fl</sup>ame recipient stopped responding; this may also be the result of anger.

Thus, there is evidence that <sup>fl</sup>aming can be detrimental to the negotiation process. As indicated earlier, our objective is not to identify new <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents, but rather to explain theoretically and demonstrate empirically that anger signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence on <sup>fl</sup>aming of important antecedents identi<sup>fi</sup>ed by prior research. We suggested that the advantage of this approach would be to offer researchers and practitioners another avenue that can be pursued in order to mitigate <sup>fl</sup>aming. That is, rather than focus efforts on reducing the levels of the <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents, such as frustration, individuals could focus on the reduction of anger when the reduction of the antecedents' levels may be too costly or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult.

To that end, we identi<sup>fi</sup>ed four <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents commonly found in the literature. Two antecedents involved violations of fairness norms, and led to <sup>fl</sup>aming directed toward the negotiating partner, while two other antecedents focused on the media and task, and led to <sup>fl</sup>aming directed toward the negotiating context. We hypothesized that the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of these antecedents on <sup>fl</sup>aming were mediated by anger, and these hypotheses were supported by our data analyses. These results have some practical implications for how computer systems that are used in negotiations can help reduce anger, and thereby the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming. We describe these implications below. But before we do so, we address our study's limitations and associated future research opportunities.

## 5.1. Limitations and future research opportunities

Contributions of this study should be viewed with recognition of its limitations, and these limitations suggest areas for future research. The study context may limit its external validity because our subjects were strangers to each other. This is less limiting than it might appear since there is an increasing incidence of such negotiations, at least for initial encounters and associated agreements. This is especially true with online dispute resolution, which often occurs between buyers and sellers who are strangers to each other [88].

However, <sup>fl</sup>aming decreases when individuals who are not strangers negotiate via computer media [84]. Thus, in such circumstances, our model may require modi<sup>fi</sup>cation. For example, media richness problems may be less important because over time individuals can overcome many communication richness restrictions [21,91]. In addition, when negotiators are friends they may be more altruistic and less likely to engage in negotiating behavior that angers each other [31]. However, when negotiators are friends, perceptions that one or the other is being unfair may lead to even greater anger than if they were strangers [55]. It is therefore of interest to expand the examination of <sup>fl</sup>aming during negotiation to the population of individuals who are familiar with each other.

Our investigation was within the context of distributive negotiation concerning a single issue. It would be helpful if the associations we have identi<sup>fi</sup>ed among the antecedents, anger, and <sup>fl</sup>aming were also tested within integrative negotiations over multiple issues. Since integrative negotiation focuses on win–win agreement [84], principles of fairness are less likely to be violated and thus partner-associated anger and <sup>fl</sup>aming may also be less likely. However, if more issues are under negotiation, the potential for task uncertainty increases along with the associated potential for increased context-associated anger.

Our experiment had a time limit. Time limits are common in negotiation because negotiators desire an end to the uncertainty and emotional demands of the process and because negotiators' constituents often press for quick resolution [27]. However, negotiations with little or no time limitations would be interesting to examine. For example, more negotiation time could lead negotiators to better understand each other, and thereby help them more accurately predict and interpret each others' actions [31]. This can reduce the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming since negotiators may try to eliminate behaviors that can anger each other.

Our experiment was also limited in terms of the number of rounds that subjects were able to negotiate with each other. As such, they might not have had a chance to adapt their bidding strategies and their tendency to <sup>fl</sup>ame. One could argue that if more rounds of negotiation were permitted, then subjects could have learned to adapt in ways that would have helped them to overcome <sup>fl</sup>aming. It would be interesting for future research to examine whether or not there is empirical support for this idea.

Another limitation of our study has to do with the way we obtained data on constructs, such as anger and unfairness, by using student raters instead of those who were involved in the game. However, assessing these constructs from actively engaged participants would raise other concerns. For example, if protocol analyses were employed, there would be concerns about potential reactive effects, as participants would be required to interrupt negotiation in order to describe their psychological states. In addition, if post hoc questionnaires were administered to participants, questions regarding the accuracy of participant perceptions would arise. However, it would be interesting to triangulate with these other data gathering strategies to see if they produce results that are in accord with ours.

Because our experimental design did not involve the manipulation of independent variables, our results provide correlational support for our hypotheses. In addition, support of anger as a total mediator between antecedents and <sup>fl</sup>aming was limited in two respects. First, it would have been nice to test for total mediation with a power greater than 0.75. Second, there are other antecedents to <sup>fl</sup>aming that have been described in the literature but not included here. As such, our hypotheses and the correlational support provided by our data offer a basis for further investigation of cause-and-effect relationships in future studies that focus on anger and <sup>fl</sup>aming.

We did not include <sup>fl</sup>aming as a strategy in our model. We earlier indicated that such strategies in negotiation contexts represented by our laboratory task would be less likely to be successful and therefore less likely to be employed. In fact, we found that anger explained 53% of partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming variance. This large degree of explained variance supports our contention that <sup>fl</sup>aming would come largely from anger, and therefore supports our decision not to include <sup>fl</sup>aming as a strategy in our model.

Finally, we found that anger toward a partner resulted in displaced aggression in the form of context-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming. However, anger toward the context was not found to be associated with displaced aggression in the form of partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming. This lack of association may be an artifact of our study design, in that we did not record vocal aggression toward the computer system. For example, a subject may well have cursed at the computer without communicating this aggression to a partner. This suggests that the form in which displaced aggression occurs is quite important to its identi<sup>fi</sup>cation.

## 5.2. Practical implications

The results of this study point to a number of practical implications for negotiators, that will increase the likelihood of reaching agreement. The results also have implications for businesses that offer computer mediated dispute resolution services. Such services depend on satis<sup>fi</sup>ed negotiators; and negotiators are more satis<sup>fi</sup>ed when agreements are reached [50]. We therefore provide below suggestions for computer mediated dispute resolution services that can help them reduce the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming, the effects of <sup>fl</sup>aming, or both, and thereby increase the likelihood that negotiating parties will reach agreement.

## 5.2.1. Anger and flaming reduction

Flaming can be reduced by decreasing negotiator anger through (1) reinterpretation, (2) managing provocation, and (3) relaxation. Prior to beginning negotiations, a computer system could indicate to the negotiating parties that they should work as partners and then provide them with these anger-reduction insights.

5.2.1.1. Reinterpretation. When a person is distressed (bothered, worried, and/or anxious), he or she tends to make extreme and more personal interpretations of events; and these interpretations are likely to lead to anger [12]. For example, when the results of negotiations are important to an individual, distress can take the form of anxiety. In such circumstances, the individual will tend to perceive an event (e.g., the negotiation partner taking a long time to respond to an offer) to be a personal affront that is speci<sup>fi</sup>cally aimed at him or her by the partner [12]. In this way, the more personal and subjective interpretations aggravate anxiety, which can increase the likelihood of anger [12,63]. Since these interpretations are less focused on the objective content of the event and more on things that are subjective [12], techniques offered by researchers to reduce the likelihood of anger have individuals reinterpret events by attending to more objective aspects [58].

5.2.1.2. Managing provocation. Anger can be reduced by increasing a person's competence in managing their own emotional responses to perceived provocations. Such competence typically involves basing behavior more on cognition than on emotions [34]. Continuing with the example above, consider the situation in which a person is anxious during negotiations because the outcome is important to him or her. This person therefore tends to make extreme and more personal interpretations of events, and these interpretations are likely to lead to anger. When faced with a provocative situation (e.g., the partner taking a long time to respond to an offer), the negotiator may become angry because he or she feels less in control; this anger occurs because it is a natural response that helps individuals take charge of situations [63]. Researchers suggest that this anger can be decreased if individuals increase their perceptions that they are in control by, for example, repeating to themselves statements such as: “As long as I keep my cool, I'm in control here” or “Don't get bent out of shape; just think of what to do here” [39,63].

5.2.1.3. Relaxation. Anger can also be reduced through relaxation. An individual can learn to become aware of his or her agitated state, such as tension, stress, or frustration, which can lead to anger [63]. Such agitation can be recognized due to the attendant physiological changes. For example, stress reduces blood <sup>fl</sup>ow to internal organs [53] and increases the cardiovascular and neuronendocrine functions aimed at the muscles and the heart [4]. As a result, a person can become aware stress when he or she experiences rapid heartbeats. Once aware of stress the individual can reduce stress through the employment of relaxation techniques, such as deep breathing through the mouth and nose, counting to ten, taking timeouts from an immediate situation, and thinking of images of being in control or thinking of something humorous [28,63]. Though relaxation is more dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to apply than reinterpretation or managing provocation [63], it can be combined with the other methods in order to achieve a more effective reduction in anger [28].

## 5.2.2. Reduction in the partner's need to flame

We earlier provided examples of how an individual's perceptions of vested interest or reciprocity violations can lead to partner-directed anger, and thus <sup>fl</sup>aming. To reduce the potential for the individual to become angry, it can help if the individual's partner becomes aware of how his or her actions might be perceived by the individual in terms of such violations. For example, the partner may be taking more time responding to an individual's latest concession because the partner is having trouble determining or communicating an appropriate response. In our bargaining context, this might occur if a partner is unsure about how to calculate probabilities associated with lottery outcomes or if he or she is having trouble typing. With computer-mediated negotiations, nonverbal cues that could provide an indication of these dif<sup>fi</sup>culties are not available, and the individual might interpret the extra time taken to respond as part of a hold <sup>fi</sup>rm strategy; this would lead to perceptions that the partner is being unfair by not reciprocating.

In order to mitigate this perception, the computer system could keep track of the time taken by negotiators before they respond. When a reasonable time is exceeded, the system could prompt the negotiator to send a message that acknowledges any delay and that explains why it happened. In addition, the system could assist negotiators by, for example, providing decision support for issues that might be the reasons for slow responses. In the case of our lottery, the system could provide help in determining probabilities and expected values.

As with partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming, a computer system can help reduce potential anger associated with context-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming. For example, context-directed anger may be reduced by providing negotiators with decision support that helps reduce task uncertainty. Uncertainty that is associated with anger often results from the illusion of control, which prevents negotiators from realizing their goals. This illusion can be avoided by encouraging negotiators to think about uncertainty in terms of long-run relative frequencies [48]. For example, a negotiator facing uncertainty might be told that in hundreds of similar situations, the fractions of outcomes of one kind or another will be very close to a speci<sup>fi</sup>c fraction, say forty percent. With the negotiation task employed in our experiment, the computer system could provide the expected values of lottery tickets.

In addition, anger associated with reduced communication richness can occur because negotiators expect conversational turn taking to occur as rapidly over a computer-mediated medium as it would in face to face [84]. Less rapid conversational turn taking can also reduce communicators' ability to understand each other by limiting their abilities to interject brief clarifying questions [29,84]. The impact of these factors can be mitigated by, for example, enabling computer systems to provide automatic receipt noti<sup>fi</sup>cation for messages. And, users of these systems might also be advised about adapting their expectations concerning conversational turn taking throughout negotiation. These measures could reduce anger directed toward the context, and thereby context-directed <sup>fl</sup>aming.

## 5.2.3. Altering the response to a flame

When a partner-directed <sup>fl</sup>ame is received, the system could direct the recipient to examine the negotiation transcript in order to determine what caused the anger that precipitated the <sup>fl</sup>ame, recognizing that this anger may be associated with the recipient's action or with the negotiation context. The recipient could then appropriately respond (e.g., apologizing or pointing out why he or she believes his or her negotiating behavior is fair) in order to reduce the <sup>fl</sup>ame sender's anger and thereby increase the likelihood of reaching agreement. Note that even when negotiators have an understanding of fairness norms in negotiation, their fairness judgments are subjective, and they tend to interpret fairness in ways that favor themselves [11]. The recipient of a <sup>fl</sup>ame may be aided in his or her response by this knowledge, and start a dialog regarding what each negotiator believes to be fair.

## 6. Conclusion

Negotiations are increasingly being conducted over computer media, with some web-based sites devoted to online dispute resolution. Employing computer media in this way has the potential to increase negotiation ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency by both lowering its cost and increasing its speed [44]. However, this media has also been found to increase the incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming, especially when used for tasks (such as negotiation) in which con<sup>fl</sup>ict and tensions exist. And, <sup>fl</sup>aming signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly reduces the likelihood of reaching agreement, which is the primary objective of negotiation.

We therefore explored the antecedents to <sup>fl</sup>aming during computermediated negotiations with the aim of determining whether the emotional state of anger mediated the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of <sup>fl</sup>aming antecedents that had been previously identi<sup>fi</sup>ed. The purpose of this focus was to provide an alternative approach to the reduction of <sup>fl</sup>aming. That is, if anger mediates the in<sup>fl</sup>uence of other antecedents on <sup>fl</sup>aming, then researchers and practitioners could focus on reducing anger when the reduction of other antecedents (such as unfairness) is too costly or dif<sup>fi</sup>cult. Our laboratory study supported our view of anger as a mediator.

As a result, we provided suggestions for negotiators as well as suggestions for changes to computer mediation systems, such as online dispute resolution web sites, that could help reduce anger and the associated incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming among negotiators. Our suggestions included techniques that could be offered by an online dispute resolution site in a tutorial format to help negotiators manage their own anger and thereby reduce the potential that they would <sup>fl</sup>ame. We also suggested ways in which these sites could help individuals reduce the anger felt by their negotiating partners so that partners' incidence of <sup>fl</sup>aming could be reduced. Finally, we suggested ways in which the sites could help individuals who receive <sup>fl</sup>ames respond in ways that could increase the likelihood of a negotiated agreement. In these ways, the likelihood would increase that computer mediation would lower the cost and increase the speed of negotiation without sacri<sup>fi</sup>cing negotiation success.

He earned his masters and doctorate degrees from Baruch College, City University of New York. His current research interests include computer-mediated negotiation, communication and decision-making under uncertainty, affective communication, and trust.

Randolph B. Cooper is a Professor in the Decision and Information Sciences department in the Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. Wynne W. Chin is a Professor in the Decision and Information Sciences department in the Bauer College of Business at the University of Houston. He received his doctorate from the University of Michigan in Computers and Information Systems, M.S. in chemical engineering (biomedical option) from Northwestern University MBA from the University of Michigan and a bachelors in biophysics from UC. Berkeley. He has published in journals such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, and Decision Sciences. Dr. Chin's substantive interests include modeling the individual IT adoption process, end-user satisfaction, and developing group process measures such as cohesion, satisfaction, and consensus to understand the impact of electronic meeting systems. More recently, he has begun work on cross-cultural analysis. His research is largely empirical and quantitative relying on lab and Monte Carlo experiments as well as surveys. Methodologically Dr. Chin focuses on construct development through the use of structural equation modeling (both covariance-based and partial least squares) as well as developing new causal modeling techniques for topics such as assessing interaction effects.
