# Conceptualizing Organizational Innovation: The Case of the Brazilian Software Industry

## Abstract

This paper presents and tests a conceptual model that explains innovation practices and innovation outcomes in Brazilian software firms. By considering internal and external innovation capabilities, the ability to manage the relationship between people and their organizational contexts plays an important role for organizational innovation. Based on these results, we propose that the disparities between software firms in building organizational innovation can be attributed, in part, to differences in their internal capabilities. The paper aims at encouraging and supporting managers to develop internal capabilities as a way of effectively leveraging the internally and externally distributed knowledge essential to innovation development.

Keywords: innovation management; open innovation practices; people capabilities; development processes; survey research; partial least square (PLS).

# Conceptualizing Organizational Innovation: The Case of the Brazilian Software Industry

## 1 Introduction

The software industry plays a considerable and increasing role in the world economy. In the so-called BRIC countries (Brazil, Russia, India, and China), software production exhibits the highest growth rates in the world [1], indicating the growing role of technological development in these emerging economies. In essence, it is an industry strongly influenced by knowledge and innovation [2-4], where learning and the capability to develop new solutions are seen as important factors in firm survival and growth [2, 5-7].

The dynamic and competitive nature of the software industry has placed its innovation in a position that is key to firm survival [8] in most sectors of the economy [9]. The industry has been characterized by low entry and exit barriers, minimal marginal costs of production, and rapid and disruptive innovation. Given this, firms’ competencies and strategies are critical for their survival and competitive positioning [10]. It is an extremely “porous” industry, as illustrated by the fact that from 1995 to 2007, firms entered the software industry at 1.2 times the rate of pharmaceuticals and 2.2 times the rate of computer hardware manufacturers [2].

The dynamic nature of the software industry—the rapid pace of technological change, the subsequent emergence of new markets, the easy entry of new competitors, and the continuous need for innovation to mitigate the threat of disruptive new technologies—represents a considerable challenge to software firms [2]. Under these circumstances, a firm’s innovative capabilities play a central role [4, 11].

Developing and introducing new software solutions are essentially intellectual efforts and ones highly dependent on human capital, with a range of knowledge and skills being required [2, 4, 12]. In essence, organizational innovation in software firms demands the development of (1) internal capabilities and (2) a variety of ties between the firm and external agents. The former is related to the firm’s capability to promote an internal environment conductive to cross-functional collaboration [13], openness to experimentation and idea generation [14], and routines that promote retention of knowledge and emphasize the non-linearity of innovation development [15]. The latter relates to the support that software provides in a wide range of industries—the financial, automotive, and mining sectors are but three that are of key importance to Brazil [16, 17].

Software companies are required to develop knowledge in a diverse set of domains [4]. These transformations require that software companies have the ability to identify and understand latent needs in areas that transcend the borders of knowledge of software and developmental technologies [4, 12, 18]. Also, software firms are facing rapid technological development in the industry. Often, new base technologies (e.g., programming languages, architectures, platforms, services, and algorithms) [19] are developed by enterprises or open source communities, thus becoming potential “raw materials” for the development of new software solutions to diverse segments [11, 20].

Based on the context described herein, together, these two demands may require that software firms develop internal capabilities and a variety of ties with external agents to identify, assimilate, and exploit knowledge for creating new solutions. Through internal capabilities, firms can direct their accumulated experience and knowledge to develop innovative solutions [21]. By relying on relationships with external agents, firms can absorb complementary external knowledge to generate innovations [22].

The treatment of internal capabilities has long been recognized as playing an important role in the successful outcome of innovation in software firms [21, 23]. Additionally, software firms’ capabilities to promote relationships with the external environment have also been addressed [24]. However, two

#

limitations stand out in the current literature. First, there is a lack of models that simultaneously address (1) the effects of internal capabilities to convert the firm’s internally accumulated experience in innovative solutions and (2) the firm’s capability to promote ties with external agents to leverage external capabilities to improve the firm’s innovative capacity. Romijn and Albaladejo [24] addressed software firms’ capability to promote relationships with the external environment. Other studies addressing innovation and external relationships in the software industry focus mainly on the open source context [25-27]. Specifically, these studies are concerned with the business models that software firms develop to gain private value by engaging with open source communities. Studies focusing on internal capabilities [21, 23] do not address firms’ capability to promote relationships with external parties to develop innovations.

With the Brazilian context specifically in mind, the question that arises is, therefore: What are the main drivers for innovation capability in [Brazilian] software firms, and how important are these drivers, internally and externally? To answer the question, we propose and test a model that blends innovation drivers associated with (1) the software firm’s internal capability to convert its accumulated knowledge and experience into innovative software solutions and (2) the software firm’s capability to maintain external relationships and to rely on external knowledge to improve the firm’s innovative capacity. The proposed model was tested via a survey of respondents from Brazilian software companies. The aim was to contribute to a better understanding of the effects and importance of the main drivers of innovation in software firms.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In the next section, the research model is presented and hypotheses are developed. Then, section 3 deals with methodological considerations, and section 4 presents the results of the study. Next, in section 5, a discussion highlights the theoretical and managerial implications of the study. Finally, section 6 provides a summary of our major conclusions.

## 2 Research model and hypotheses

Software innovation is one of the key elements of competitive success in mature software markets [28]. It can be seen as a process leading to the achievement of outcomes in software innovation formed by (1) new organizational structures and (2) new products and services [12, 29]. New organizational structures can be explained as new or significantly improved processes [30, 31], revamped organizational structures [32, 33], and changed marketing strategies [34]. The development of new products and services includes new or significantly improved products or services [35], increased profits [36], and/or the discontinuation of existing products and services [37].

Within a technology-based industry such as software, innovation is extremely important because new versions of products, processes, and services proliferate rapidly, making older solutions obsolete, while providing competitive advantages to those organizations that are capable of innovating effectively [2, 10]. Thus, the skills to absorb, master, and improve existing technologies and to create new ones—innovation capability [38], in other words—are of central concern to software firms. The stronger a firm’s innovation capability, the more effective is their innovation performance [39]. Indeed, innovation capability is ‘at the heart’ of software firms and is positively correlated with firm performance [39-41].

However, the question remains as to how innovation capability in software firms can be explained and gained. In this paper, we consider whether innovation capability may be improved by the presence of internal capabilities and open innovation concepts and practices [42-44]. It has been argued that the process of improving the performance/output of software firms (e.g., new profitable software products and services) has been largely influenced by the openness of the innovation process [42, 43, 45] to the external environment. In addition to external relations within the environment [42, 44, 46, 47], the literature on software innovations suggests that innovation capability can also be influenced by other aspects, such as intellectual capital transactions [42, 48-50].

Internal capabilities have also been considered relevant in explaining innovation capabilities, and given the intellectual nature of the software development process, they can be described as “people capabilities” [51-53], development processes [54, 55], and knowledge retention and use [44, 56, 57]. Each of these concepts is represented in our research model as a latent variable and is explained in the following subsections. ## 2.1 Intellectual capital transactions

Intellectual property (IP) transaction is defined as a way for software firms to effectively take advantage of IP by using it to generate innovations. Internal development and protection and applications of IP are seen to be of major concern to firms seeking competitive advantages through innovations, especially firms using closed and vertically integrated approaches to innovation [48-50]. Given this, strong IP protection is important for the software industry because it is essential for the promotion and development of new applications and services [58]. According to Chesbrough [59], however, companies utilize less than half of their IP technologies. This being so, firms have begun to actively commercialize their technological knowledge by means of out-licensing, strategic alliances, and spin-offs, where firms allow external partners to use some of their own technology [56, 60, 61]. In a similar vein, companies are also seeking to acquire IP from external sources—through the acquisition of companies and technologies, for instance—to obtain and absorb knowledge critical to innovation [56, 59]. In essence, these mechanisms are implemented to provide the conditions for firms to effectively convert IP assets into innovative solutions, either through licensing and creating external paths to innovations or through the absorption of externally generated IP.

Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 1: High rates of intellectual capital transactions will increase innovation capability in software firms.

## 2.2 People capability

People capability is defined as the ability of an organization to manage the relationship between people and their organizational contexts [51]. Regarding innovation, it goes without saying that people form the central component of any effort to develop and introduce innovations, especially in knowledge-intensive industries such as software [3, 4]. It is people who provide the skills for creating, absorbing, and applying the knowledge needed to generate new solutions that provide superior value to customers [62, 63]. The development of these skills is based on the use of multifunctional or interdisciplinary teams [52, 53, 64], as well as the use of training and recruitment techniques for improving the innovative profile of teams—such as focusing on teamwork, collaboration and communication skills, and creativity [52, 53]. People capability is particularly important in the software industry, given the frequent discontinuities of both software technologies and business models [2, 65], particularly in relation to rapid technological development [66, 67].

In the innovation process, the role of people and the knowledge they possess is twofold [46, 68, 69]. First, it is necessary that firms maintain capabilities for internally generating IP and the other types of knowledge necessary to develop innovations [62, 70]. Second, people capability is essential in identifying, evaluating, and absorbing external innovations and knowledge, with a view to their incorporation into internal processes of innovation development [46, 68]. This is consistent with the importance of organizations maintaining internal levels of knowledge and competencies so that they can identify, absorb, and apply external knowledge [69, 71].

#

Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 2: The higher the software firm’s people capability, the greater its innovation capability.

## 2.3 External relationships

An external relationship is defined as the building of networks and communities of mutual interest by companies to facilitate the development of new practices [72]. Openness in innovation is in part defined by various relationships with external actors [61]. Although some researchers have pointed out that there is no support for the contention that the overall intensity of external interaction affects innovative performance [47], others have argued that external relationships can be an important means to achieve innovation [42, 46, 73]. Through their external relationships, firms can capture what is known as relational rents (i.e., obtaining rents from ties with external actors, not necessarily from their own resources) [42, 73]. Through these rents, companies can also use external relationships to market internal ideas and knowledge [46].

Relationship networks can also be developed, allowing enterprises to rapidly fill specific knowledge needs without spending enormous amounts of time and money internally in developing needed knowledge or acquiring it through vertical integration [56]. In the software industry, external relationships can include a large array of actors—for example, customers [74]; partners, such as open source communities [43]; lead users, which are the market leaders that have needs that go far beyond those of the average user [75]; partner companies [76], and universities and R&D centers [76]. Focusing essentially on relationships that aim to provide inbound flows of knowledge, this research highlights the importance of software firms maintaining a set of relationships to absorb innovation inputs. Through such external relationships as these, organizations have shared problems and challenges, with new ideas emerging as a result [77]. Producing new ideas through these external relationships can be more difficult than achieving them internally; however, since organizations may have less control over their external environment, organizations have to stimulate idea creation and adopt schemes that reward employees for their creative ideas.

Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 3: The intensity of external relationships will increase innovation capability in software firms.

## 2.4 Development processes

A development process is based on the specification of requirements to design, build, and test software and produce useful software quickly [78]. Essentially, innovation development involves the creation and introduction of something new; it is a quest into the unknown [79]. This being so, models and processes that treat the development of innovation as a smooth, well-behaved, linear process inadequately describe the nature of efforts aimed at creating new solutions [54]. The innovation developmental process has therefore been treated as a journey, with setbacks, complexities, and detours [80]. Innovation development is thus seen as a dynamic and emergent process in which learning, experimentation, creativity, trial and error, and real-time problem identification and solving are emphasized over deterministic and rigid approaches [47, 52, 54, 81]. Attendant processes are a complex and iterative series of decisions involving the blending and implementation of new ideas [52]. In consequence, success depends on developmental processes that can draw upon widely distributed knowledge, dealing with (1) the social character of innovation, where people with different backgrounds interact [52, 82] and (2) the material properties of technologies that constrain and/or enhance the involvement and action of people within the innovation process [52, 83-85].

Technology and its material properties are also seen as important components in terms of providing platforms that encourage creativity, learning, and engagement of people in organizational practice [83, 84]. In software innovations, technology (e.g., design patterns, developmental tools, software components, and libraries) and other physical artifacts can play critical roles, both in communication and as boundary objects [86] around which ideas, conversations, negotiations, decisions, and sense-

#

making can converge [52, 55, 84]. Additionally, in the context of software development, the successful use of these technologies and techniques based on emergent and dynamic processes are frequently associated with the use of prototypes. Software prototypes can act as boundary objects upon which people can effectively exercise their creativity and apply their knowledge [52, 55, 82].

Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 4: Developmental processes will increase innovation capability in software firms.

## 2.5 Knowledge retention and use

Knowledge retention and use is defined as the capacity of firms to create new knowledge (explorationbased activities) or to replicate existing knowledge (exploitation-based activities), being essential to innovation development processes [56, 60, 73, 87]. Organizations with this ability have been called ambidextrous [88]. Indeed, the notion of exploration together with exploitation (ambidexterity) is at the heart of many approaches aimed at understanding the innovation capabilities of firms (e.g., [52, 56, 73, 89, 90] and of recent approaches to information systems strategizing [91, 92]. However, the capacity of firms to retain knowledge over time is also a central component [73]. Organizations must engage in routines for retaining knowledge previously developed and/or absorbed for future use [44, 73]. Over time, activities that retain knowledge contribute to organizations’ inter-temporal integration of knowledge and capabilities, enabling them to apply previously developed knowledge, past experience, and lessons learned to current and future innovation initiatives [73, 93, 94]. As a result, it is argued that effective documentation, the use of lessons learned, and mechanisms to retain knowledge over time can perform important functions in sustaining the innovation capabilities in organizations, thus contributing to innovation outcomes [53, 73, 94].

In open innovation environments, where knowledge is highly distributed inside and outside the firm, knowledge retention is consequently performed both within the company and externally. “Internal knowledge retention” refers to the firm’s capacity to retain internal knowledge. External knowledge retention refers to the firm’s capacity to retain knowledge through its interorganizational relationships, such as alliances and partnerships [73]. Internal and external knowledge retention can be realized within the classic evolutionary process of variation-selection-retention [73, 95]. In this process, new knowledge and organizational routines are evaluated and selected for retention and subsequently incorporated into organizational practice. In this evolutionary approach, knowledge retention performs an important role in bridging knowledge creation and knowledge use, serving as a mechanism to (1) improve inter-temporal integration of knowledge and (2) effectively incorporate new knowledge and capabilities critical to innovation development [73, 93, 95]. Thus, we propose:

Hypothesis 5: Knowledge retention and use will increase innovation capability in software firms.

## 2.6 Innovation capability

Previous sections have described the antecedent factors of innovation capability: IP transactions, people capability, external relations, developmental processes, and knowledge retention and use. But how can we describe innovation capability? According to Lawson and Samson [39], innovation capability is defined as the ability to transform knowledge into new processes, organizational structures, and products and to make improvements and modifications to create new technologies. It generally requires certain conditions: (1) new or improved processes, (2) new organizational structures, and (3) the development of new products or services [96-99], as described below.

First, the firm’s capacity to implement a new design, analysis, or development method that changes the way products are created is called “process innovation” [100-104]. It enables that innovation to be part of a firm’s operation through changes in how products and services are created, sold, and delivered to customers [105]. Process innovation can affect the development of products or services in positive ways: (1) introducing new products or improving the quality of existent ones and (2) new ways to deliver these products and services to customers, affecting market positions [105-107]. Next, “innovation of products and services” is related to the production and release of products or services that are technologically new or significantly improved [101-104, 108-111]. Third, there is “innovation in organizational structures,” which means the organization has been using either new or significantly improved organizational structures [12].

Once innovation in software can be described as an array of new solutions providing a series of positive outcomes, including (1) development processes (process innovation), (2) new products or services delivered to customers (products or service innovation), and (3) new organizational arrangements [12], it is expected that innovation capability will contribute to build these outcomes as well [39]. Therefore, organizations with innovation capability also have the ability to integrate key capabilities and sources through measuring and reinforcing innovation, keeping the focus on innovation outputs, and achieving higher business performance [39].

## 3 Research Methodology

## 3.1 Measurement

Our target population was software firms located in Brazil. We chose to analyze Brazilian firms because of the recent growth in its software and services sector, which was almost 24% in 2010 [112], as compared to the worldwide rate of a mere 0.5%. Brazil is therefore demonstrating exceptional growth, and its \$19.04 billion software sector had risen to 11th in the worldwide rankings at that time (ibid.). Our sample is therefore taken from a highly innovative context.

A qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews was conducted between November 2011 and April 2012, before the quantitative study. The study was undertaken with a view to understanding how the constructs that comprise the model were demonstrated in practice. Initially, a literature review was performed to develop the constructs used for measurement purposes (see Table 1) in the quantitative study, and as a means to identify the categories to be considered in the qualitative study, leading to the creation of a semi-structured script. The script was pretested, and the scales were subsequently validated in the quantitative study by discriminant and divergent validity. The survey instrument was developed by building on the extant theoretical base (as described above) to ensure content validity. Pretesting involved a focus group of three academics with expertise in the field and seven semistructured interviews with chief information officers (CIOs). To ensure face validity these CIOs were not interviewed as part of the actual survey. An important outcome of the pretesting was a review of questions for clarity and precision undertaken to avoid any misinterpretation in the actual survey. In addition to this pretesting, a pilot study was conducted on a small sample of Brazilian firms, on the basis of which the set of measurement items was expanded. We sent letters to participants explaining the study’s aims and procedures, assuring them that individual responses would remain confidential. All constructs in the proposed model were based on reflective multivariate scales. All items were measured using a 6-point Likert scale, with the anchors being “strongly disagree” and “strongly agree.” We used a rating scale of 6 points because it has higher convergent validity and reliability coefficients relative to scales with fewer points [113].

As the literature reviewed was in English, our initial questionnaire was also written in English and was reviewed for content validity by a group of five international Information Systems academics. As the questionnaire was to be administered in Portuguese, we translated the English version into Portuguese and then translated it back into English to ensure translation equivalence [114]. Two professional translators independently translated the original items from English to Portuguese. They analyzed the independently translated Portuguese versions of the questionnaire and approved the final version. The questionnaire was then translated back into English by another professional translator to confirm translation equivalence. The questionnaire was pilot tested in three Brazilian firms: Devex, SW Factory, and Polaris. These firms are located in the same region as four of the researchers, which facilitated applications and observations in person. The feedback received was used to adjust the survey questions. As a result of this pilot, questions regarding the use of specific software applications for open innovation were simplified and synthesized. These questions were reviewed due to the low standard deviation achieved in this pilot [115]. Table 1 summarizes the operationalization of the constructs using items adapted from prior research.

## 3.2 Data

Data were collected between March and April 2012 via a survey of 2,879 Brazilian firms listed in a database published by the Ministry of Science, Technology and Innovation (Ministério da Ciência, Tecnologia e Inovação – MCTI). This database was formed by Brazilian software companies involved with the federal government’s promotion of innovation and development in science. Phone calls to companies listed in this database were made to collect the e-mail addresses of senior managers so that the questionnaire could be sent directly to them. A total of 829 personalized e-mails were sent to respondents, including an introduction explaining the research objectives, the importance of each respondent’s answers, and the confidentiality of responses, together with a link to the questionnaire itself. The questionnaire was addressed to senior managers on the assumption that they would have adequate knowledge about software innovation in their company. Of the 320 useable returns, 281 were obtained because of the initial invitation and a further 39 after the second. To test for nonresponse bias, we compared the first and second respondent groups and found that the early and late surveys shared similar characteristics [116]. We used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test to compare the sample distributions of the two groups (ibid.). The K-S test results, shown in Table 2, suggest that the sample distributions of the two independent groups do not differ statistically [116], and therefore, we can claim that there is no bias in terms of nonresponse. We checked for common method bias by using Harman’s one-factor test [117]. An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the data for all items, and considering that (1) the analysis produced more than one factor and (2) the first factor explained only 34.58% of the sample, no significant common method bias was detected in the data (op. cit.).

## 4 Results

To test the hypotheses arising from the conceptual model, a structural equation model (SEM) was applied. As items in our data were not distributed normally (p<0.01 based on Kolmogorov-Smirnov’s test), and as the research model was in an early stage of development and had not been previously tested, the PLS method was considered appropriate [118, 119]. PLS also requires a sample size equal to the largest of the following: (1) 10 times the number of indicators of the scale with the largest number of formative indicators or (2) 10 times the largest number of structural paths directed at a

#

particular construct in the model [118, 120]. In our conceptual model, the largest number of structural paths directed at a particular construct was five, which means that the minimum size required was 50. Given that the sample population was larger (n = 320), the PLS method was deemed to be appropriate. Smart PLS 2.0 M3 [121] was the software used to test the measurement and conceptual models. Before testing the conceptual model, we examined the measurement model to assess internal consistency, indicator reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity.

## 5 Discussion and Implications

Our results provide empirical support for the importance of the internal capabilities developed by software firms: development processes (H4), people capability (H2), and knowledge retention (H5). Thus, the innovation capability of the software firms studied is constructed by the firms’ internal experience and intellectual capital, accumulated and retained over time, together with development processes that are iterative, oriented to creativity, and prototype based. However, we do not find support for the contention that the intensity of external relationships (H3) and intellectual capital transactions (H1) are conducive to intellectual capital (IC) in software firms.

The more knowledge a firm retains, the easier it is to maintain and to reactivate additional knowledge. From the results obtained in this work (Figure 2), these capabilities, supported by mechanisms to store and retrieve knowledge, to record knowledge from external relationships, and to use lessons learned, appear to be the most important for software firms trying to build IC.

Other internal mechanisms contribute to explain IC. Specifically, the structural model emphasizes the importance of emergent and dynamic processes and people management to build IC in software firms. This is aligned with the complexities and uncertainties pervading the development of innovations [54, 79]. To account for such characteristics, less rigid, dynamic and emergent processes can contribute more effectively to the treatment of contingencies and the demands of learning inherent in the development of innovations. People capability highlights the role of knowledge and people in software innovation development. Software development is an intellectual activity requiring creative problem solving [2, 3]. On this premise, the structural model indicates that cross-functional teams and people with innovative profiles, training, and tools for supporting creativity are all central to the development of IC.

In the structural model, external relationships and intellectual property transactions appear not to be relevant to explain IC. Our results are therefore in alignment with previous findings in the literature on software innovation, especially those of Romijin and Albaladejo [41] and Bianchi et al. [133]. The relationships with external entities are not significant in explaining innovation capability in the software firms studied. A possible explanation of these results can be found in the work conducted by Allen and Marquis [134]: the process of a researching group in solving a new innovation-related problem depends on whether the solutions it used in the past fit the new problem. Through this basis, we can point to the concept of problem-solving economy of scale [135], which refers to an increasing specialization of workers and tools to provide efficiency in problem solving. In this context, Brazilian companies may be closed unto themselves in terms of searching expertise and efficiency in software solutions for specific segments (e.g., mining and automotive). This “closedness” can limit the external search to support innovative initiatives since the firm relies on internal capabilities that are accumulated over time within the firm. However, since our data do not support claims about the concentration of the studied firms on specific or diverse sectors, future research can investigate this topic in more detail.

## 5.1. Managerial implications

The study’s findings suggest a set of practices that can be explored by software firms seeking to improve their innovation capabilities. Our results suggest that managers in software firms striving to increase IC should consider a combination of mechanisms to promote knowledge retention, dynamic and emergent processes, and adequate levels of people capability. Specifically, our results indicate that development processes that are suitable for innovation should be emergent and dynamic and provide internal and external dissemination of preliminary samples of new products. Through these characteristics, development processes can better handle the demands for learning, experimentation and real-time problem identification and solving [81].

Over software development processes, knowledge-retention mechanisms may provide the necessary conditions for firms to incorporate previous knowledge and experience into innovation development. Specifically, such mechanisms comprise recording knowledge from individuals who are likely to leave the organization and from external relationships that may prove to be temporary. In addition, software firms may consider assisting their employees in using lessons learned from previous experiences. In line with Lichtenthaler and Lichtenthaler [73], this perspective can contribute to a better inter-tempora integration of knowledge and, according to our results, improve IC in software firms.

Further, for the execution of development processes and the retention of knowledge to be effective, the involvement of skilled people in the operation of these processes is recommended. In order to develop such skills and competence and to contribute to a better IC, our results suggest the use of the following: recruitment techniques focusing on professionals with innovative profiles, internal training to develop communication skills, teamwork and technology to support team learning and creativity in software development, and cross-functional and interdisciplinary teams. Thus, based on our findings, we encourage managers in software firms to develop internal capabilities—emergent and dynamic processes, people capability, and knowledge retention and use—to effectively take advantage of the internally and externally distributed knowledge that is essential to innovation development.

## 5.2 Theoretical implications

There is a long tradition [e.g., 36, 52, 54] of seeing innovation as a process involving high levels of uncertainty and learning. Linear models are increasingly rejected in favor of approaches that are more dynamic, iterative, and innovative. Representative of this trend is the chain-linked model proposed by Kline and Rosenberg [54]. Our results indicate that emergent and dynamic development processes should be viewed as important mechanisms for building external and internal relationships. In such processes, interactions can be facilitated using internal and external agents that are distributed over different organizations and geographical regions. The use of an iterative development approach and a constant dissemination of preliminary samples can contribute to better visualization of partial solutions by various agents and then lead to more effective development of new solutions. Thus, we suggest that, although not considered in previous studies [56, 57, 136, 137], process development might be an important construct for extending our understanding of empirical results and mechanisms for open innovation.

In the same vein, our results suggest that people capability is also a construct that enriches the understanding of the mechanisms and results of innovation. The disposition of software companies toward their innovation capabilities requires firms to facilitate greater participation of people in this process and thus contributes to the development of their innovation capability. Naturally, people are central in creating innovative solutions. Thus, by proposing and testing the people capability construct in relation to its contribution to the innovation capability in software firms, we extend the literature by empirically confirming the previous contentions developed by Newell et al. [52] and Aaen [53]. This work highlights the role of people capability through increasing the innovative profile of staff and

#

ensuring an adequate level of interdisciplinary work in the teams involved in software innovation development—the drivers of IC in software firms.

## 5.3 Limitations

While our findings advance knowledge about using different mechanisms to enhance IC, the study has several limitations. First, we have focused on Brazilian firms developing software products. This limits the generalizability of the research results. Future studies could usefully test our hypotheses in other contexts. Second, it is expected that the relationship between open innovation mechanisms and IC can be moderated by variables associated with firms’ strategies and external environments. Thus, further research could include such variables to test and enhance the proposed model. Third, it is probable that innovation mechanisms affect radical and incremental innovations differently. Thus, futures studies could explore the influences of the four independent constructs (external relations, people capability, development processes, and IP transactions) on radical and incremental innovation results in software firms.

## 6 Conclusion

In this paper, we sought to answer the following question: What are the main drivers for innovation capability in [Brazilian] software firms, and how important are these drivers, internally and externally? Based on data collected from Brazilian software firms, our results indicate that internal factors drive innovation capabilities. Specifically, the results indicate that firm’s people capability and development processes are the main drivers of innovation capability, followed by the capability to retain and apply knowledge. The proposed model extends previous contentions that people and knowledge retention are central elements in enhancing innovation capability. In addition, it includes the nature of development processes as a factor affecting innovation capability. Overall, this paper contributes to the understanding of the role of firms’ internal capabilities to promote innovation capabilities.
