Abstract. As artificial intelligence and digitalization technologies are flourishing real-time, online interaction–based commercial modes, exploiting customers’ purchase intention implied in online interaction processes may foster huge business opportunities. In thi study, we target the task of voice chat–based customer response prediction in an emerging online interaction–based commercial mode, the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. Prior research shows that satisfaction, which can be revealed by the discrepancy between prior expectation and actual experience, is a key factor to disentangle customers purchase intention, whereas black-box deep learning methods empirically promise us advantageous capabilities in dealing with complex voice data, for example, text and audio information incorporated in voice chat. To this end, we propose a theory-driven deep learning method that enables us to (1) learn customers’ personalized product preferences and dynamic satisfaction in the absence of their profile information, (2) model customers’ actual experiences based on multiview voice chat information in an interlaced way, and (3) enhance the customer response prediction performance of a black-box deep learning mode with theory-driven dynamic satisfaction. Empirical evaluation results demonstrate the advantageous prediction performance of our proposed method over state-of-the-art deep learning alternatives. Investigation of cumulative satisfaction reveals the collaborative predictive roles of theory-driven dynamic satisfaction and deep representation features for cus tomer response prediction. Explanatory analysis further renders insights into customers personalized preferences and dynamic satisfaction for key product attributes.

## 1. Introduction

The sustained development of digitalization, big data, and artificial intelligence has spawned various realtime interaction–based online commercial modes, exemplified by e-commerce live streaming (Fei et al. 2021), social network–based marketing (Lien et al. 2017), and robocaller-based telemarketing (Azad et al. 2020). A striking advantage of these emerging commercial modes is that they enable closer customer–retailer interaction. Besides, multiform communication channels, such as voice and video, allow retailers to interact with their customers directly as in a traditional off-line face-to-face sales scenario. This offers enormous commercial opportunities to retailers because closer customer–retailer interaction implies deeper customer engagement, which is positively related to sales (Morgan et al. 2018). As cus tomers interact intensively with retailers, improving customers’ satisfaction during the interaction to enhance their purchase intention is attracting widespread interest (Ba and Johansson 2008).

Some off-line retailers, such as retailers of fixed assets (e.g., houses and cars) and experiential services (e.g., dining and entertainment), have to sell their products in brick-and-mortar stores but may also benefit from real-time interactions with customers. In particular, inviting potential customers online for off-line product experience and sales (i.e., invite online and experience in store)—the focus of this study—has emerged as a cutting-edge marketing tool for off-line retailers. In a typical invite-online-and-experience-in store mode, potential customers browse products online and may further conduct a simple registration on the platform—either a third-party platform or the retailer’s platform—to learn more product information. The offline retailer entrusts a caller agent (hereafter referred to as agent) service provider to invite potential customers to the store and pays the service provider for the number of actual customer arrivals. An agent, acting on behalf of the retailer, calls potential customers several times in succession to invite them to brick-and-mortar stores. Unlike those online commercial modes that wait for customers passively, the invite-online-and-experience-instore mode identifies and invites customers actively. Besides, the use of an agent service provider centralizes customer–retailer interactions, easing the operational management of customer satisfaction (Radziwill and Benton 2017, Chung et al. 2020).

Effectively predicting customers’ off-line responses to online invitations, that is, customer response prediction, is key to the success of the invite-online-and-experiencein-store mode. On the one hand, there is a significant discrepancy between the huge potential customer market and the limited marketing capability of the agent service provider. Success in attracting customers to the store depends on widespread outbound calls to and long-term follow-ups with potential customers. However, because of limited time and capacity, the agent service provider may not be able to implement a point-to-point precision marketing strategy for all potential customers. On the other hand, if customers’ willingness to respond can be predicted accurately in time (e.g., once the first call has ended), the agent can subsequently focus on customers with high response willingness, thereby not only improving the success rate of online invitation, but also enhancing the marketing efficiency.

Customer response prediction for online invitation, albeit with great importance, is fraught with challenges. One major challenge lies in the lack of personal information. Different from conventional telemarketing modes, which target existing customers whose personal details are well-recorded (Moro et al. 2014, Apampa 2016), and standard online/off-line commercial modes, in which detailed customer information is conveniently available once customers have placed orders (van Dolen et al. 2007, Stein and Ramaseshan 2016), little customer information is accessible to the agent before the first call in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. During the interaction with customers through voice chat, the agent is tasked to proactively promote candidate products to customers, learn customers’ preferences and demands, and respond to customers’ concerns. Thus, the voice chat well-accommodates the product, customer, and interaction factors that are verified as strong indicators of customer satisfaction (Stein and Ramaseshan 2016, McLean and Osei-Frimpong 2017). In this sense, the voice chat should be a valuable data source that can be leveraged for customer response prediction. Besides, the limited capacity and high running cost of the agent urge customer response to be accurately unraveled as soon as possible. Therefore, customer response prediction based on the voice chat is of great importance in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode.

Prior research studies prediction tasks similar to online invitation–based customer response prediction, such as marketing response prediction (Apampa 2016), telemarketing success prediction (Moro et al. 2014), and response prediction for advertising (Chapelle et al. 2015). However, the methods developed for these tasks cannot deal with voice chat–based customer response prediction. First, these methods target regular customers and, thus, are established upon rich customer information, whereas the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode targets potential new customers, whose personal information is extremely difficult to obtain in advance. Second, these methods are developed largely for quantitative features, such as customer attributes, product attributes, and trans action data (Chun 2012, Olson and Chae 2012), whereas voice chat data are unstructured and more complex.

Extant research provides theoretical guidance for customer response prediction. There is a consensus that satisfaction is a decisive factor for customers’ purchase intention (Kuo et al. 2009, Fang et al. 2014). Specifically in the context of online customer–retailer interaction, researchers further find that customer satisfaction de pends predominantly on online experience (Novak et al. 2000, Rose et al. 2011), which is a holistic process made up of a customer’s online interaction with an organization and can be derived from a set of touch points (McLean and Wilson 2016). Studies employing the expectation-disconfirmation theory show that, in addition to online experience, customer satisfaction is also affected by expectation (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). The discrepancy between expectation and actual experience constitutes disconfirmation with positive disconfirmation associated with satisfaction and negative disconfirmation associated with dissatisfaction (McKinney et al. 2002).

In light of such a theoretical foundation, it is sensible to conduct customer response prediction for online invitation by modeling customers’ experiences and expectations with the voice chat data. The voice chat is well suited for deriving customers’ experiences as it covers those crucial touch points, including factors related to customers’ behavior, the retailer’s performance, and customer–retailer interaction, which are influential to the formation of actual experience (Rose et al. 2011, Klaus and Maklan 2013). However, it is an extraordinary challenge to capture customers’ expectations without their personal information, not to mention that customers adjust their expectations ceaselessly as their actual experiences evolve (Fan and Suh 2014, Lankton et al. 2016). Furthermore, customers’ expectations are inherently rooted in their preferences for certain product attributes, and such preferences may differ across customers (Lemon and Verhoef 2016). To overcome these difficulties, we pose our first research question (RQ1): how to learn customers’ differential preferences and dynamic expectations in the absence of their personal information. In response to RQ1, we propose an embedding strategy named attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding to construct a personalized preference template for each customer. Then, we split the voice chat into multiple dialogue fragments according to the customer’s and agent’s pauses and further transform each preference template into an attribute–fragment matrix. In doing so, we accommodate the dynamics of the expectation as attribute–fragment expectations allow distinct expectations toward various attributes over time steps (i.e., fragments).

As for customer experience, we model it from two aspects: information satisfaction, which is affected by the quality of information used for purchase decision making, and attribute satisfaction, which indicates the level of contentment of the customer with certain product attributes (Spreng et al. 1996). In this sense, both textual and audio (multiview)<sup>1</sup> information from the voice chat should be subsumed when measuring customers’ experiences. Textual information describes the key product attributes, whereas audio information reflects the way the informational content is delivered, and the two types of information synergistically determine customers’ perceived information quality (Lee et al. 2002). With a voice chat, it is natural to expect that the customer experience evolves as the conversation goes on. In line with the dynamic expectation, we also capture the evolution of customer experience. Additionally, we notice that the voice chat is essentially a flow of question–answer pairs between the customer and agent. During different fragments of the dialogue, the customer and agent discuss different topics, resulting in differential experiences. Specifically, the agent’s replies to the customer’s questions tend to affect the customer’s satisfaction, whereas the customer’s responses to the agent’s questions tend to reflect the customer’s product preference and purchase intention. This indicates that customers’ experiences for the voice chat–based online invitation are formed and affected in an interlaced way. Given these requirements for modeling customers’ experiences, we pose another research question (RQ2): how to learn customers’ dynamic experiences from the multiview voice chat information. To this end, we design a multiview deep learning method named cross-view attention–based deep learning, which learns customers’ dynamic experiences in an interlaced way from the multiview voice chat information.

Given the constructed customer experience and expectation, we have two options to achieve customer response prediction. One is to characterize the disconfirmation by the discrepancy between experience and expectation as inspired by the expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) such that customers’ satisfaction and willingness to visit a store can be causally revealed. However, it is difficult to optimize the parameters associated with customers’ expectations and experiences directly because of the lack of groundtruth labels. Alternatively, we can liberate the predictive power of dynamic expectation and experience features by reconstructing them in hidden spaces with deep learning methods in response to the complexity of the multiview voice data. As deep learning models are well-known as “black boxes” (Adadi and Berrada 2018), we propose to learn theory-driven satisfaction to enhance deep learning–based customer response prediction. Hence, we investigate our third research question (RQ3): how to leverage theory-driven dynamic customer satisfaction to enhance the perform ance of voice chat–based customer response prediction. To tackle RQ3, we synthesize the attribute-frequencyand-order-based preference embedding strategy and the cross-view attention–based deep learning model into a bimodal sequential neural network structure and optimize them coordinately with a learning objective named bidirectional sparsity-based contrastive learning, which accommodates the dynamic expectation and experience for theory-driven satisfaction generation and response prediction.

Overall, we propose a theory-driven deep learning method named dynamic satisfaction–empowered deep learning (DSDL) for voice chat–based customer response prediction in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. By synergizing its three core components (i.e., attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding, cross-view attention–based deep learning, and bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning), DSDL leverages theory-driven satisfaction to enhance the performance of deep learning–based customer response prediction. Experiment results in the context of agent-based car sales show that DSDL outperforms relevant, state-ofthe-art deep learning methods in prediction and representation learning performance. Explanatory analysis results render insights into DSDL’s predictive advantages and its learned customer satisfaction.

## 2. Literature Review 2.1. Factors Influencing Online Customer Experience

Advances in social media and artificial intelligence technologies promote the emergence of omnichannel retailing, which entails various online channels to realize seamless customer-to-business interaction (Song et al. 2020b). Typical channels for customer-to-business interaction include social media, email, short message, web portal, and telephony, which can be categorized into two types according to the data form they involve, that is, text and voice based. A common goal shared by the two interaction types is to provide high-quality service so as to boost customer satisfaction (Chen et al. 2018). With an online chatbot, customers can interact with a retailer through text chat without temporal and geographical boundaries such that their requirements, such as information searching and complaint, can be satisfied automatically (Radziwill and Benton 2017, Chung et al. 2020). Call centers, another prevalent channel, resolve customer queries through voice chat (Kumar and Telang 2012). More recently, the use of an intelligent conversational bot, which provides online live voice chat service by imitating human service representatives, has achieved great success in many domains, including digital banking (Kaur et al. 2020), intelligent education (Edwards et al. 2019), interactive marketing (Pradana et al. 2018), and healthcare (Denecke et al. 2019). Given the ubiquity of online chat–based service, research is underway to leverage rich customer interaction information to provide customized goods, services, and experiences so as to create more customer value (Cai and Lo 2020). Because high-quality experience during the purchase decision-making process can boost customer loyalty, business revenue, and business agility (Klaus and Maklan 2013, Shi et al. 2020), much research is devoted to customer experience evaluation.

Prior studies investigate factors influencing online customer experience. Novak et al. (2000) measure customer experience in online environments and reveal a set of factors (e.g., customer type and interaction speed) that influence the forming of online experience. van Dolen et al. (2007) provide evidence for the impact of customers’ characteristics on their perceived satisfactions from online group chat and highlight the importance of the communication style of the advisor, who facilitates interaction to help customers attain their objectives, for customer satisfaction. Rose et al. (2011) review the online experience literature to unravel the antecedents and consequences of customer experience in the purchase context and summarize the differences between online and off-line contexts, such as the degree of personal contact and the manner in which information is provided. Stein and Ramaseshan (2016) uncover that customer experience is mainly determined by touch points and episodes encountered during the service delivery process. This rationalizes the voice chat–based customer response prediction in our study because almost all of the customer experience touch-point elements identified by Stein and Ramaseshan (2016), including atmospheric, communicative, process, employee–customer interaction, and product interaction elements, are wellcovered by the voice chat. From an omnichannel customer experience view, Shi et al. (2020) conclude that perceived compatibility is positively related to customers’ omnichannel shopping intention. Overall, these studies regarding online experience lay a sound foundation for using customers’ voice chat with an agent to predict their response behavior.

## 2.2. Features for Customer Response Prediction

Predicting customer response to marketing is of great importance for retailers to exploit more customer resources and boost sales (Cui et al. 2006). Customer response prediction is conventionally conducted in direct marketing using largely quantitative features. Olson and Chae (2012) show the usefulness of quantitative transactional features, such as recency, frequency, and monetary values, for modeling customer response. Based on customer, product, and social-economic attributes, Moro et al. (2014) predict the success of telemarketing calls for selling bank long-term deposits using machine learning methods and conclude that inviting customers through telemarketing phone calls constitutes an effective strategy for building longer and tighter customer relationships. Li (2018) predicts customers’ willingness to accept online invitations by inferring a network structure among customers with a competing risk model and highlights the necessity of constructing response prediction models without personal information because individual attributes are often unavailable because of privacy concerns. To predict customer response to display advertising, Chapelle et al. (2015) extract quantitative features related to customers and the advertiser and find that the response rate of an ad depends on the way the published information is rendered and the way customers receive it. All of these studies manifest the value of taking customer response prediction as a marketing strategy for increasing product sales and demonstrate that customer response is predominantly affected by factors related to customers, retailers, and their interactions.

## 2.3. Methods for Customer Response Prediction

Traditional machine learning methods are commonly used to deal with quantitative features for customer response prediction. Chun (2012) predicts customer response to elapsed time in direct marketing using machine learning methods. Apampa (2016) ascertains the effectiveness of ensemble learning methods (e.g., random forest) for the success of a bank customer marketing campaign for deposit subscription. Machine learning methods are also used to explore customers’ satisfaction based on online review texts (Lucini et al. 2020).

With the rise of omnichannel interactions between customers and retailers, multimodal data (e.g., textual and audio interaction data) and deep learning methods become prevalent for customer response prediction. In the context of a live voice chat service, McLean and Osei-Frimpong (2017) demonstrate the importance of theoretical constructs, for example, information providing and delivery, in influencing one’s satisfaction with the experience throughout the online interaction. Ramaswamy and DeClerck (2018) use different deep learning methods to model customers’ perceptions based on their multimodal data, that is, social media texts and audio recordings. Ling et al. (2019) predict customers’ purchase intents by their interactions with the retailer on multiple promotion channels. Kim et al. (2021) develop a hybrid deep learning method with various customer-oriented types of data. However, existing deep learning–based customer response prediction methods are opaque and commonly rely on semantic tagging, for example, tagging customers’ sentiment tendencies and experienced satisfactions on certain topics to understand their response intents. Semantic tagging lacks efficiency as it requires manual annotation to ensure prediction accuracy.

In this study, we leverage contrastive learning to learn customers’ satisfactions so as to enhance customer response prediction. Contrastive learning is a self-supervised learning pattern that enhances the deep representation learning effect by pulling together positive instances and pushing apart negative instances in the embedding space (Khosla et al. 2020). Because customers’ satisfactions change dynamically as the dialogue proceeds, our bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning method uses a direction-variable contrastive learning objective that adjusts the contrastive learning direction conforming to the customer’s response (i.e., visiting store or not) to regulate the learning of the constantly changing expectations, experiences, and their disconfirmation.

## 2.4. Research Gaps

Our review of existing related studies reveals their incompetence for voice chat–based customer response prediction. First, existing response prediction methods have been developed predominantly by relying on quantitative features and textual data, failing to model the complex data patterns embedded in richer multimodal information, such as audio information. Second, synthesizing theory-driven constructs and data-driven deep learning methods may lead to improved prediction performance but has not been explored.

Our proposed method (i.e., DSDL) aims to fill these gaps in the literature. First, unlike prior studies that evaluate online customer experience by virtue of rich personal information (van Dolen et al. 2007, Stein and Ramaseshan 2016), DSDL allows personalized customer preferences to be estimated in the absence of explicit personal information, better satisfying today’s imperative requirements of privacy protection and data security. Besides, instead of investigating customer satisfaction statically (Novak et al. 2000, van Dolen et al. 2007), DSDL measures dynamic customer satisfaction in an interlaced way. Second, unlike existing methods that use telemarketing data in quantitative ways (Moro et al. 2014,

Apampa 2016) and predict customers’ responses with traditional machine learning methods (Chun 2012, Chapelle et al. 2015), DSDL extracts multiview features and constructs a deep learning model. Third, the theory-driven satisfaction generated by DSDL not only enables more rational model training, but also enhances predictions.

## 3. Theoretical Foundation and Methodological Challenges

Extant research shows that customers’ satisfactory experiences during their purchase decision making positively influence their purchase intentions, so the overall satisfaction can be regarded as an important antecedent for actual purchase behavior (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010, Lankton et al. 2016). Such evidence inspires us to capitalize on satisfaction to predict whether a customer will make an off-line response after being invited by an agent via online voice chat. When modeling customer satisfaction, Spreng et al. (1996) identify two kinds of satisfaction, information and attribute satisfaction. Informa tion satisfaction is contingent on the informational content and the way it is delivered (McKinney et al. 2002). The more the agent-provided information conforms to customers’ requirements, the more satisfied the customers are. Furthermore, more acceptable and intelligible delivery (e.g., appropriate tone, intonation, and speech rate of the voice and exquisite speechcraft) by the agent tends to elicit higher customer satisfaction. Attribute satisfaction is fundamentally determined by some core attributes associated with the product as these attributes are indispensable in forming customers’ preferences (Szymanski and Henard 2001, Ofek and Srinivasan 2002). Product attributes used to model attribute satisfaction should be explicitly tailored to customers preferences.

Given the determinants of customer satisfaction, another important issue is how customer satisfaction is achieved. The expectation-disconfirmation theory (Oliver 1977, Venkatesh and Goyal 2010) elucidates the mechanism of customer satisfaction commendably. The theory asserts that the rationale behind satisfaction is rooted in disconfirmation, that is, the discrepancy between prior expectation and actual experience (Oliver 1977). Expectation is a set of customers’ preexposure beliefs about a product, whereas actual experience is the actual performance of the product as perceived by customers (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010). Experience exceeding expectation yields positive disconfirmation (i.e., satisfaction), whereas expectation exceeding experience yields negative disconfirmation (i.e., dissatisfaction) (McKinney et al. 2002, Lankton et al. 2016). Satisfaction increases as the degree of positive disconfirmation increases (Fan and Suh 2014). These conclusions motivate us to capture the causal relationship between customers off-line responses and the agent’s online invitations by modeling two antecedents—expectation and experience— and their disconfirmation.

The expectation-disconfirmation theory entails complex dynamics, whereas final satisfaction is a cumulative function of prior disconfirmation (Lankton and McKnight 2012). Prior research demonstrates that the belief and attitude toward a product vary over time as customers gain new experiences continually (Bhattacherjee and Premkumar 2004). Given the sequence nature of voice chat, it is reasonable to differentiate disconfirmations resulted from different fragments of a dialogue. After the agent starts a dialogue with a customer, both interact dynamically when discussing multiple topics, and different topics with their related contexts (i.e., questions and answers) tend to impact disconfirmation differently. The multiple fragments of the dialogue inevitably give rise to fluctuation in disconfirmation, thus precipitating the variance of the overall satisfaction.

In summary, the aforementioned prior studies jointly reveal that satisfaction is a focal point in forming customers’ response intention and, thus, can be used as a strong predictor of actual response behavior. Additionally, the response intention reflected by disconfirmation and satisfaction holds considerable continuance (Venkatesh and Goyal 2010), so the impact of a moderate time lag between the predicted off-line response behavior and the online invitation on prediction is negligible. Furthermore, the expectation-disconfirmation literature highlights the predictive roles of expectation, experience, and disconfirmation for satisfaction (Oliver 1977, Brown et al. 2008). In this sense, the task of predicting customer satisfaction can boil down to (1) modeling the dynamic expectation, experience, and disconfirmation implied in the voice chat context and (2) aggregating them into cumulative satisfaction.

However, solving this problem is methodologically challenging. First, as indicated by the aforementioned satisfaction determinants, modeling customer satisfaction should take account of informational content and its delivery mechanism simultaneously. To achieve this, we extract multiview features from voice chat data with (1) context-based textual features to capture semantic information and (2) narrative patterns and voice signal–based audio features to depict the delivery of informational content. Besides, identifying key product attributes conforming to customers’ preferences is also challenging because the agent knows little about the customers before inviting them to the store. To this end, we propose a key-attribute extraction scheme, which can cover important attributes tailored to customers preferences. Furthermore, considering that customers inherently hold heterogeneous willingness to pay for different attributes (Ofek and Srinivasan 2002), we model customers’ potential preferences via text analysis based on the voice chat content and embed them into keyattribute spaces (i.e., preference templates).

Second, modeling the expectation, experience, and disconfirmation is a particularly challenging problem in the absence of customers’ personal information. To deal with this difficulty, we learn fragment-wise expectations based on preference templates as customers form their expectations by projecting future product performance into their preferred product attributes (Lankton et al. 2014). Besides, we propose a cross-view attention mechanism for capturing attribute-wise experiences of each dialogue fragment. The rationale behind this is that each voice chat is indeed a question–answer session, where the agent’s answers to customers’ questions tend to determine the degree of customer satisfaction whereas the customers’ answers to the agent’s questions tend to reflect the intensity of their willingness to participate. Thus, the actual experience can be measured by attending to customer–agent dialogue fragments alternately. To distinguish between positive and negative disconfirmation, we construct a class-wise contrastive learning objective. That is, we regard customer response prediction as a binary classification task, that is, whether a customer responds to an online invitation, with responsive customers as ones of satisfaction and unresponsive customers as ones of dissatisfaction.

Third, given that the formation of customer satisfaction is a dynamic process in which the disconfirmation between expectation and experience evolves over dialogue fragments, we model the expectation, experience, and disconfirmation at the granularity level of dialogue fragment and aggregate the fragment-wise satisfactions into the cumulative satisfaction. When estimating the cumulative satisfaction, we impose bidirectional (i.e., attribute and fragment) sparsity constraints on the contrastive learning objective, considering that the role of a certain attribute-wise or fragment-wise satisfaction for forming the cumulative satisfaction varies across customers because of their personalized preferences (Ofek and Srinivasan 2002). For example, if a satisfied customer experiences negative disconfirmation at some time step or attribute, this time step or attribute is inappropriate for modeling the customer’s positive contrastive (i.e., disconfirmation) pattern and, thus, should be eliminated. With the bidirectional sparsity constraints, the finally derived cumulative satisfaction is more robust to attribute-wise satisfaction variations and fragmentwise satisfaction fluctuations. ## 4. Proposed Method

Our theoretical analysis lays a foundation for under standing the indispensable role of dynamic customer satisfaction for voice chat–based customer response prediction, and the revealed methodological challenges (Table 1) motivate us to design a theory-driven deep learning method to enhance black-box predictions. Specifically, the method is expected to (1) learn customers personalized preferences and dynamic expectations in the absence of their personal information, (2) learn customers’ dynamic experiences from multiview voice chat information in an interlaced way, and (3) generate theorydriven dynamic customer satisfaction to enhance prediction performance. Accordingly, we propose DSDL, which consolidates three innovative mechanisms, that is, (1) attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding for personalized preference construction, (2) crossview attention–based deep learning for experience modeling, and (3) bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning for dynamic satisfaction-empowered prediction, within a multiview deep learning framework.

To better present DSDL, we illustrate its components and learning phases using a case study, which we also use for empirical evaluation. In this case study, we collaborated with a Chinese agent service provider, which is committed to providing customer invitation services specialized for car sales. We are tasked to predict each customer’s off-line response to an online invitation (i.e., the probability of visiting the store after being invited online) based on the voice chat between the customer and the human agent.

## 4.1. Overview of DSDL

Figure 1 outlines the framework of DSDL. Given the voice chat data, DSDL first conducts dialogue segmentation through speech activity detection and obtains a sequence of dialogue fragments, each of which corresponds to a section of dialogue. Then, considering the multiview nature of the voice chat data, DSDL extracts audio features and, meanwhile, converts audio dialogue fragments to texts to extract text embeddings using a pretrained neural network. In addition, to cap ture customers’ personalized preferences, DSDL derives a preference template for each customer based on the textual fragments using an embedding strategy. Next, DSDL constructs a multiview deep learning model with two parallel long short-term memory networks (LSTMs) for producing the text and audio embedding sequences, respectively. Subsequently, DSDL separates the dialogue sequences into two groups according to their speaker roles (i.e., customer or agent). Then, DSDL introduces an attention mechanism, cross-view attention, to learn the interlaced dialogue patterns between the customer and agent. Finally, DSDL learns dynamic disconfirmation via a learning strategy, bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning, based on the preference template and cross-view embeddings and outputs the final prediction by synergizing theory-driven satisfaction with deep learning–based prediction.

## 4.2. Data Collection and Preprocessing

All sample customers provided by our collaborator showed some initial purchase intention elsewhere before being invited. For example, every customer provided registration information<sup>2</sup> (e.g., phone number) on the platform. The existence of customers who did not visit the store after being invited because of the lack of any purchase intention can be largely ruled out in our prediction context. Given a list of customers with their voice chat data (i.e., the first calls with one-to-one correspondence to the customers) and the corresponding class labels (i.e., whether a customer subsequently visited the store), we discard the instances in which the dialogue was unexpectedly interrupted or the duration was shorter than 30 seconds. The voice chat data were recorded by a dual-track system, which stored the voices of the customer and agent on two tracks separately. Even if the dialogue between the two speakers overlapped to some extent, their voices can be neatly separated by the system, allowing us to segment each voice chat by the speaker role.

To better capture the interaction pattern between the customer and agent, we further conduct dialogue segmentation. Specifically, given a dialogue, we split it into multiple fragments by speech activity detection (Gelly and Gauvain 2018). We adopt a well-established speech activity detection deep learning method<sup>3</sup> that is released by Lavechin et al. (2019) and Bredin et al. (2020) and is pretrained on the well-known AMI Corpus (McCowan et al. 2005). This pretrained speech activity detection model, by detecting the pauses and silence, allows the natural dialogue fragments during the voice chat to be identified. After dialogue segmentation, the voice chat distributes alternately on two tracks (i.e., speaker roles) in chronological order. For each voice chat, we remove its dialogue fragments that are shorter than two seconds in case they may result in empty texts. Furthermore, we filter out the instances in which only one speaker role remains (i.e., no interaction).

To adapt the dialogue sequences to the subsequent model training process, we preprocess them into a matrix via padding mask, an operation commonly used in deep learning models to shape the input sequences into the same length and dimension (Vaswani et al. 2017). All dialogue sequences are padded to a fixed length by truncating the sequence elements beyond the fixed length and padding the sequence positions shorter than the fixed length with zeroes (Dwarampudi and Reddy 2019). With the mask strategy, the manually padded values are excluded from subsequent training and testing (Song et al. 2020a).

## 4.3. Multiview Embedding Extraction

After the preprocessing, we obtain a set of n instances $\pmb { \Omega } = \{ \Omega _ { 1 } , \hat { \Omega } _ { 2 } , \hat { \Omega } \cdot \cdot \cdot , \Omega _ { n } \}$ with their speaker-wise dialogue sequences. Because both the verbal information and vocal signals contained in the voice chat data are valuable for reflecting customer–agent interaction patterns and customers’ experienced satisfactions, we extract multiview features, that is, textual features from the conversational content view and audio features from the delivery view. To extract textual features, we first convert the dialogue sequences to text sequences by calling a widely used commercial application program interface,<sup>4</sup> which is shown to be competent for various Chinese voice-to-text tasks. Then, we employ bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT), a pretrained text representation model that has achieved enormous success in natural language processing (Devlin et al. 2018), to get embeddings of the text sequences. Specifically, we map each text fragment into BERT and derive its embedding from the CLS-token space (i.e., a 768-dimensional hidden space for document representation) of BERT, thereby obtaining the embedding matrices of all text sequences,<sup>5</sup> that is, $\bar { C ^ { D } } = [ C _ { 1 } ^ { D } , C _ { 2 } ^ { D } , \ \cdots , { C _ { n } ^ { D } } ]$ and $C _ { k } ^ { D } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 7 6 8 \times L }$ , where L stands for the sequence length after the padding.

To extract audio features, we adopt the mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCC), a common technique for deriving a parametric representation of acoustic signals based on the human auditory system (i.e., with the frequency varying within the human ear’s critical bandwidth) (Zheng et al. 2001). Following a standard procedure of MFCC feature extraction (Muda et al. 2010), we process original audio signals through seven phases successively: (1) preemphasis to increase the energy of the signal at higher frequency, (2) framing to divide the audio signal into small frames, (3) hamming windowing to integrate all the closest frequency lines and eliminate possible signal discontinuity at the ends of each frame, (4) fast Fourier transform to convert each frame from the time domain into the frequency domain, (5) mel-scale filter to approximate frequencies in the fast Fourier transform spectrum to a mel scale to conform to the human auditory characteristic, (6) discrete cosine transform to convert the log mel spectrum into the time domain, and (7) energy and spectrum differences to derive features related to the dynamic variation in cepstral features over time. In doing so, we obtain an MFCC feature matrix for each audio dialogue fragment. To further convert multiple fragment-wise MFCC matrices of a dialogue sequence into an instance-wise matrix, we aggregate each of them into an MFCC feature vector along the frame dimension, retaining the dimensionality<sup>6</sup> of MFCC features $( \mathrm { i } . \mathrm { e } . , d _ { M } ) .$ , thereby getting MFCC feature matrices for all dialogue sequences, that is, ${ \boldsymbol { C } } ^ { M } =$ $[ C _ { 1 } ^ { M } , C _ { 2 } ^ { M } , \cdots , C _ { n } ^ { M } ]$ and $C _ { k } ^ { M } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { M } \times L }$

## 4.4. Personalized Preference Template Construction

It has been extensively demonstrated that users’ textual descriptions carry important clues for inferring their personalized preferences because users tend to highlight their attended attributes when they express their experiences, opinions, and concerns toward a product (Jiang et al. 2015, Li et al. 2015). In this vein, we propose an attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding method to model customers’ personalized preferences over key product attributes. For each of our derived textual dialogue sequences, there are two subsequences, belonging to the customer and agent, respectively. We take the customers’ text sequences for modeling their preferences. First, we aggregate all text fragments of a customer into a customer-wise document. Then, given all customer-wise documents, we use latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) to extract their embed ded topics. We further select a key attributes from the topics through manual screening to ensure that the attributes are closely related to the product or customer service.

The proposed preference embedding method follows the intuition that an attribute that is more frequently and preferentially mentioned in a conversation signifies the customer’s higher priority assigned to it and, thus, is more likely to reflect the customer’s preference. Given the a key attributes, the preference embedding method establishes a square attribute matrix $A \in \mathbb { R } ^ { a \times \bar { a } } -$ as key attribute spaces—for each customer to accommodate the customer’s personalized preferences, that is, $A \subseteq$ $\{ A ^ { ( 1 ) } , A ^ { ( 2 ) } , \ldots , A ^ { ( n ) } \}$ }. Both the row and column elements in A are subject to the a-dimensional attribute space. Then, we construct a preference template for each customer. Specifically, for the kth customer, we segment its text sequence into a word list with a length of $r _ { k }$ . For each diagonal element $A _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) }$ , where $i = j$ and $k = 1 , 2 , \cdots ,$ $n ,$ we fill it with the frequency of its corresponding attribute word in the word list, that $\mathrm { i s } , a _ { i } ^ { ( k ) } / r _ { k }$ , where $a _ { i } ^ { ( k ) }$ is the number of occurrences of the ith attribute in the kth word list $( { \mathrm { i . e . } }$ , the words used by the kth customer in the conversation). For each nondiagonal element $A _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) }$ where $i \neq j ,$ we fill it with $O _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) }$ , the average order difference between the ith and jth attributes, that is,

$$
O _ {i j} ^ {(k)} = \overline {{O}} _ {i} ^ {(k)} - \overline {{O}} _ {j} ^ {(k)},\tag{1}
$$

$$
\overline {{O}} _ {i} ^ {(k)} = \frac {1}{r _ {k} u ^ {(k)}} \sum_ {u = 1} ^ {u ^ {(k)}} p _ {i u} ^ {(k)},\tag{2}
$$

where $\overline { { O } } _ { i } ^ { ( k ) }$ is the average order of the ith attribute in the kth word list and $p _ { i u } ^ { ( k ) }$ is the order of the ith attribute that appears for the uth time—totally, $u ^ { ( k ) }$ times—in the kth word list. For the kth customer, $\overline { { O } } _ { i } ^ { ( k ) }$ can be calculated as follows. First, for the ith attribute from the key attribute list, we count its occurrences and record the order(s) in which the attribute appears in the kth word list. Second, we average the derived orders of the ith attribute for the kth customer. Third, we divide the average order by the length $\left( r _ { k } \right)$ of the kth word list. By filling in the matrix $A _ { \iota }$ , we derive the kth customer’s preference template $A ^ { ( k ) }$

The preference template matrix reflects the customer’s preference in two ways. First, the diagonal element $a _ { i } ^ { ( k ) } / r _ { k }$ (i.e., the narrative intensity) reflects the extent to which the ith attribute is valued by the kth customer in the conversation, and it is natural to expect that an attribute with a higher frequency should be more preferred. Second, a larger $\overline { { O } } _ { i } ^ { ( k ) }$ (i.e., the chronological priority) signifies that the ith attribute is preferentially mentioned in the conversation so that the nondiagonal element $O _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) }$ indicates the extent to which the kth customer has assigned higher priority to the ith attribute over the jth attribute.

## 4.5. Model Construction

To model customers’ experiences, we propose a multiview deep learning model, that is, the cross-view attention–based deep learning model, which synergizes multiview features with a bimodal neural network framework and coordinates the two speaker-wise dialogue subsequences with a cross-view attention mechanism. The bimodal framework consists of two parallel LSTMs: a text and an audio LSTM. Both neural networks are trained by recursive time-step iterations with one dialogue fragment corresponding to a time step. Each dialogue sequence covers L fragments after the padding, and there are L hidden cells in each LSTM. By taking the states of the last hidden cell of the text LSTM, we obtain the text embedding $\begin{array} { r } { \pmb { H } ^ { D } = [ \pmb { h } _ { 1 } ^ { D } , \pmb { h } _ { 2 } ^ { D } , \cdots , \pmb { h } _ { L } ^ { D } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { h } \times L } , } \end{array}$ where $d _ { h }$ is the dimensionality of the hidden embedding. Similarly, we obtain the audio embedding $H ^ { M } = [ h _ { 1 } ^ { M }$ $h _ { 2 } ^ { M } , \cdots , h _ { L } ^ { M } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { h } \times L }$ based on the audio LSTM.

Given the question–answer nature of the dialogue sequence, there are two possibilities for the fragment at each time step: a response made by the customer to the agent or vice versa. The interaction patterns associated with both cases can affect the customer’s experienced satisfaction, depending on whether the product is promoted appropriately and whether the customer’s requirements about the product are addressed satisfactorily. The expe rienced satisfaction in the first case is reflected in the response made by the customer to the agent $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , the query vector belongs to the agent), whereas the experienced satisfaction in the second case is reflected in the agent’s responses to the question raised by the customer (i.e., the query vector belongs to the customer). Our designed cross-view attention mechanism captures these two possibilities for the role of the query vector in the customer–agent interaction patterns, conforming to the requirement of modeling customers’ experiences. Specifically, at each time step, our cross-view attention–based deep learning model con catenates the hidden embeddings from the two neural networks into a multiview embedding, that is, $h _ { i } =$ $[ ( \boldsymbol { h } _ { i } ^ { D } ) ^ { T } , ( \boldsymbol { h } _ { i } ^ { M } ) ^ { T } ] ^ { T }$ , and arranges the multiview embedding sequence $\tilde { H = } [ h _ { 1 } , h _ { 2 } , \cdots , \tilde { h } _ { L } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { 2 d _ { h } \times L }$ into two subsequences alternately according to the speaker roles. That ${ \mathrm { i } } \mathbf { s } ,$ the ith multiview embedding belongs to either the customer (if $\pmb { h } _ { i } ^ { c } \in { \pmb { H } } ^ { c } , i \in [ 1 , L ] )$ or the agent (i $: h _ { i } ^ { g } \in H ^ { g } )$ ).

Then, we synergize the two speaker-wise multiview embedding sequences $H ^ { c }$ and $H ^ { \bar { g } }$ by training them coordinately using a cross-view attention mechanism. Attention is a recently prevalent weight learning scheme aimed to assign and optimize a set of weights corresponding to the input sequence so as to endow a neural network with the ability to attend more to those salient elements (Chaudhari et al. 2019). For each element of the input sequence, the attention mechanism assigns it three weight vectors: the key vector $( k \in K )$ for encoding the input element uniquely, the value vector $( v \in V )$ for deriving hidden representations of the input element, and the query $( q \in Q )$ for capturing the relevance between the current element and other contextual elements. During the training iterations, each query acts as a target query once, and the target query is, respectively, matched with the vectors in K by a compatibility function $f ( q , K )$ (Ma et al. 2017, Vaswani et al. 2017) to produce a weighting scheme (subject to the target query) corresponding to V. For each target query, the vectors in V are mapped into an embedding space $\boldsymbol { Z }$ with the derived weighting scheme. Formally, given H as the input sequence, denote its corresponding keys, values, and queries as $K = [ k _ { 1 } , k _ { 2 } , \cdots , k _ { L } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { k } \times L } , \ V = [ v _ { 1 } , v _ { 2 } ,$ $\boldsymbol { \cdots } , \boldsymbol { v } _ { L } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times L }$ and $Q = [ q _ { 1 } , q _ { 2 } , \cdots , q _ { L } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { k } \times L }$ with equal feature space dimensionality for k and $q .$ . Then, unlike standard attention mechanisms, for example, self-attention and multihead attention (Vaswani et al. 2017, Chaudhari et al. 2019), which match the query with keys within the same sequence, the cross-view attention mechanism performs the matching across the two subsequences $\bar { \boldsymbol { H } } ^ { c }$ and H<sup>g</sup> interactively, that is, matching the customer’s query with the agent’s keys and the agent’s query with the customers’ keys. In doing so, our cross-view attention–based deep learning model yields a cross-view embedding $( z _ { j } \in \mathbf { Z } =$ $[ z _ { 1 } , z _ { 2 } , \cdots , z _ { L } ] \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times L } )$ for each time step, in which the customer’s experience is captured in a customer–agent interaction way rather than based on what the customer or agent said unilaterally.

## 4.6. Learning Dynamic Satisfaction

Given the preference templates and cross-view embeddings, DSDL leverages the bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning method to learn dynamic customer expectation and experience to yield dynamic satisfaction $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ disconfirmation) for customer response prediction. Figure 2 illustrates the learning process of DSDL. Given the kth customer with preference template $A ^ { ( k ) }$ and crossview embedding matrix $Z ^ { ( k ) }$ , our bidirectional sparsity– based contrastive learning method projects $A ^ { ( k ) }$ and $\check { Z } ^ { ( k ) }$ along the dialogue sequence (time steps), respectively, to model their variation patterns. For the preference template $A ^ { ( k ) }$ , we project it into a dynamic expectation matrix $\dot { \boldsymbol { E } } ^ { ( k ) } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { a \times i }$ with a learnable weighting scheme $W ^ { E } \in$ $\mathbb { R } ^ { a \times L }$ , that is, $E ^ { k } = A ^ { ( k ) } W ^ { E }$ . An element $\mathbf { \widetilde { \cal E } } _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) } \in E ^ { k }$ can be understood as the kth customer’s expectation on the ith product attribute at the jth time step, and such an expectation evolves over time steps as the dialogue proceeds. An element $\boldsymbol { w } _ { j } ^ { E } \in \boldsymbol { W } ^ { E }$ in the weighting scheme can be deemed to reflect the extent to which the key product attributes are expected by the customer at the jth time step.

For the cross-view embedding matrix $Z ^ { ( k ) }$ , we convert it into a dynamic experience matrix $\boldsymbol { F } ^ { ( k ) } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { a \times L }$ with another learnable weighting scheme $\boldsymbol { W } ^ { F } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { d _ { v } \times a }$ , that ${ \mathrm { i } } s ,$ $\boldsymbol { F } ^ { ( k ) } = [ ( \boldsymbol { Z } ^ { ( k ) } ) ^ { T } \boldsymbol { W } ^ { F } ] ^ { T }$ . An element $F _ { i j } ^ { ( k ) } \in F ^ { ( k ) }$ herein indicates the kth customer’s experienced satisfaction about the ith product attribute at the jth time step, and the experienced satisfaction evolves over time steps. Because the cross-view embeddings derived by the cross-view attention mechanism essentially accommodate the customer’s experience, the role of $\mathbf { \boldsymbol { w } } _ { i } ^ { F } \in \mathbf { \boldsymbol { W } } ^ { F }$ can be regarded as to project the d<sub>v</sub>-dimensional experience features ${ \pmb Z } ^ { ( k ) }$ to a-dimensional attribute-wise experienced satisfactions at the jth time step.

Based on these two pipelines (i.e., expectation and experience), our contrastive learning method derives fragment-wise satisfactions according to the dynamic disconfirmation. For all key attributes of the kth customer at the jth time step, denote the total expected satisfaction over them as $\bar { U } _ { k j } ^ { E }$ and the total experienced satisfaction over them as $U _ { k j } ^ { F }$ such that

$$
U _ {k j} ^ {E} = [ 1 + \exp (- (\mathbf {E} _ {j} ^ {(k)}) ^ {T} \pmb {w} _ {j} ^ {U _ {E}}) ] ^ {- 1}, j = 1, 2, \dots , L,\tag{3}
$$

$$
U _ {k j} ^ {F} = [ 1 + \exp (- (\mathbf {F} _ {j} ^ {(k)}) ^ {T} \pmb {w} _ {j} ^ {U _ {F}}) ] ^ {- 1}, j = 1, 2, \dots , L,\tag{4}
$$

where $w _ { j } ^ { U _ { E } }$ and $w _ { j } ^ { U _ { F } }$ are used to weight customers attribute-wise expected and experienced satisfactions, respectively, at the jth time step, conforming to their personalized preferences for different product attributes. Then, the kth customer’s disconfirmation at the jth time step $S _ { j } ^ { ( k ) }$ can be calculated as

## 4.7. Synthesizing Theory-Driven Satisfaction Generation and Deep Learning–Based Prediction

To maintain powerful predictive capability along with the theory-driven satisfaction returned by bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning, DSDL further embeds $E ^ { ( k ) }$ and $F ^ { ( k ) }$ into another two LSTMs, respectively, and makes an initial prediction with the hidden representation features $x _ { k } ^ { E }$ and $x _ { k } ^ { F }$ output by the last LSTM cells. Specifically,

$$
\tilde {y} _ {x} ^ {(k)} = [ 1 + e x p (- [ (\pmb {x} _ {k} ^ {E}) ^ {T}, (\pmb {x} _ {k} ^ {F}) ^ {T} ] \pmb {w}) ] ^ {- 1},\tag{8}
$$

where $[ ( x _ { k } ^ { E } ) ^ { T } , ( x _ { k } ^ { F } ) ^ { T } ]$ is the concatenated representation feature vector. $x _ { k } ^ { E }$ and $x _ { k } ^ { F }$ can be deemed as cumulative expected and experienced satisfaction, respectively, because the last LSTM inherently captures the sequential dependencies among dynamic satisfactions $e _ { j } ^ { \bar { ( k ) } } \in E ^ { ( k ) }$ and $f _ { j } ^ { ( k ) } \in F ^ { ( k ) }$ and refines the chronological cross-view satisfactions into the hidden space at its last cell. In line with the learning strategy for the satisfaction in Equation (7), DSDL also adds a contrastive constraint when making predictions, conforming to the discrepancy between expectation and experience. Hence, the learning objective for deep learning-based prediction is

$$
\Gamma_ {2} = \frac {1}{b} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {b} \left[ - \lambda_ {5} s i g n \left(y _ {k} - \frac {1}{2}\right) (\| \pmb {x} _ {k} ^ {F} \| _ {2} ^ {2} - \| \pmb {x} _ {k} ^ {E} \| _ {2} ^ {2}) + \lambda_ {6} \| \pmb {w} \| _ {2} ^ {2} \right],\tag{9}
$$

where a weight decay regularization term $\lambda _ { 6 } \| w \| _ { 2 } ^ { 2 }$ regulates the overfitting risk.

Having obtained both the cumulative satisfaction and the initial prediction, DSDL synthesizes them to achieve a more reliable prediction. That is,

$$
\tilde {y} ^ {(k)} = \underbrace {\alpha \tilde {y} _ {x} ^ {(k)}} _ {P r e d i c t i o n} + \underbrace {(1 - \alpha) \tilde {S} ^ {(k)}} _ {S a t i s f a c t i o n G e n e r a t i o n},\tag{10}
$$

where α synergizes the black-box prediction with the satisfaction generation. α is a learnable parameter automatically optimized through the learning process of DSDL.

Overall, the optimization objective of DSDL is

$$
\begin{array}{l} \operatorname{argmin} _ {W _ {k} ^ {U _ {E}}, W _ {k} ^ {U _ {F}}, w _ {k} ^ {S}, w} \frac {1}{b} \sum_ {k = 1} ^ {b} \left[ y _ {k} \log \tilde {y} _ {k} + (1 - y _ {k}) \log (1 - \tilde {y} _ {k}) \right] \\ + \Gamma_ {1} + \Gamma_ {2}. \end{array}\tag{11}
$$

By minimizing this objective, DSDL can achieve enhanced predictive capability of customer response prediction and, meanwhile, obtain assessments of customers’ dynamic satisfactions, which are of great avail to ameliorate customers’ online experiences and boost the agent’s service efficiency. ## 5. Empirical Evaluation 5.1. Data

We have evaluated DSDL in the case study. We obtained 10,625 valid instances (i.e., customers), each of which corresponds to a voice chat. All of these customers had registered on the official platforms of their intended automobile brands, and the agent service provider was informed of the customers’ phone numbers.<sup>7</sup> The customers had never been invited by the call center before. Two hundred agents were randomly tasked to invite the customers through voice chat. The 3,226 customers who did not visit the store within 30 days after being invited are regarded as unresponsive (i.e., negative) instances. No second invitation was extended during the 30 days. We set a 30-day duration here to accommodate customers who were willing to visit the store but were unable to do so promptly. The time span covered by the sample is from January to December 2019. Descriptive statistics about the collected full data set are available in Online Appendix B.

We conducted experiments based on four data sets, that is, the full data set and three subsets generated from the full data set with different data characteristics. Because the class distribution of the full data set is imbalanced, we randomly sampled 3,226 positive instances along with the 3,226 negative instances to form a balanced data set. In addition, we sorted all instances by the conversation text length in descending order and took the top 5,312 instances as a long-conversation data set and the remaining 5,313 instances as a short-conversation data set.

## 5.2. Experiments

Based on each of the four data sets, we conducted several experiments to test DSDL’s prediction performance and the quality of its generated theory-driven customer satisfaction. We compared DSDL against 12 state-of-theart deep learning methods. Specifically, we first included three baseline deep learning methods: (1) LSTM, (2) LSTM with self-attention (LSTM-self), and (3) LSTM with multihead attention (LSTM-mh). In view of the multiview features incorporated by DSDL, we identified three benchmarks from multimodal deep learning methods, which try to synergize multiple data modalities within a unified deep learning framework (Ngiam et al. 2011). The multimodal benchmarks include (4) Concatenat (Ngiam et al. 2011), which concatenates the representation features from all data views together for prediction; (5) Latent Space (Baltrusaitis et al. 2019), which projects multiview representation features into a shared latent space to model their connections for prediction; and (6) Distance Loss (Ding et al. 2015), which jointly trains multi ple neural networks via relative distance (L2-norm in the experiment) maximization to produce discriminative fea ture representations. We identified two attention-based benchmarks developed for conversation analysis, including (7) attentive network with hierarchical encoderdecoder (AHED) (Gu et al. 2018), which learns word- and conversation-level features with an attention mechanism, and (8) guided attention–based sequential method (GAS) (Colombo et al. 2020), which models speakers’ sentencewise dependencies in a conversation with an attention based persona hierarchical encoder. Considering the contrastive learning objective incorporated by DSDL, we also identified four benchmarks from contrastive learning–based deep learning methods, including (9) contrastive predictive coding (CPC) (van den Oord et al. 2018), which uses a probabilistic contrastive learning objective to learn useful representations from se quence data; (10) mutual information maximization– based contrastive learning (MIMCL) (Hassani and Khasahmadi 2020), which learns multiview representations with a mutual information maximization–based con trastive learning objective; (11) mutual information reduction–based contrastive learning (MIRCL) (Tian et al. 2020), which learns multiview representations by minimizing the mutual information across data views; and (12) mutual information and information entropy– based contrastive learning (MIECL) (Lin et al. 2021), which maximizes the mutual information between two data views and increases the information entropy of each data view.

For each setting in the experiments, we estimated the prediction performance through five independent runs of tenfold cross-validation, resulting in 50 estimates (the results reported later are based on the 50 test folds not used during model training). We used the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as an aggregate performance metric, and for robustness, two additional performance metrics, that is, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic and H measure (Hand 2009) (the results are available in Online Appendix G). We used ANOVA with Tukey–Kramer post hoc pairwise comparison for the statistical significance test of the experiment results (Online Appendix H). Information on the method implementation, execution, and parameter setting is available in Online Appendix C. Prior to the main experiments, we tested the predictive effectiveness of some conventional machine learning methods, including lasso, support vector machine (SVM), multilayer perceptron, random forest (RF), and XGBoost (detailed in Online Appendix D). Besides the main experiments (Section 5.3), we also conducted sensitivity analysis (Online Appendix E), which shows how the key attribute size influences the performance of DSDL; explanatory analysis (Section 5.4), which provides insights into customers personalized preferences and dynamic satisfactions for key product attributes; and a detailed analysis of a pair of selected instances (Online Appendix I).

5.3.1. Experiment 1: Prediction Performance of DSDL. In Experiment 1, we compared DSDL against the 12 benchmarks in terms of prediction performance. We tested the 13 methods with textual chat information (i.e., BERT embeddings), audio chat information (i.e., MFCC features), and their combination, respectively. Tables 2 and 3 summarize the prediction performance (AUC) based on the short- versus long-conversation data set and the balanced versus full data set, respectively.

DSDL consistently outperformed all benchmarks under every feature set on every data set. This elucidates the potential offered by the theory-driven satisfaction of DSDL for breaking through the prediction bottleneck of deep learning methods. Notably, by incorporating the cross-view attention mechanism into its learning process, DSDL realized AUC improvements over LSTM, LSTM-self, and LSTM-mh, respectively with the audio features, indicating that the predictive power implied in the audio chat data may be more thoroughly released in an interlaced (i.e., between customer and agent) way. Among the benchmark deep learning methods, GAS, which uses different speaker roles and an attention mechanism for conversation analysis, outperformed others, manifesting the advantage of attention mechanisms for dealing with the interaction patterns implied in the voice chat data. On all four data sets, DSDL yielded AUC improvements over LSTM-self and LSTM-mh. This result demonstrates the facilitating role of the cross-view attention mechanism and the theory-driven dynamic satisfaction, which constitute major differences between DSDL and the common attention mechanism–based LSTMs, for the prediction performance of deep learning methods. DSDL also outperformed the contrastive learning–based benchmarks, indicating that the theory-driven contrastive pattern with robust learning strategies (e.g., the bidirectional sparsity mechanism) is more powerful than the single-direction (e.g., maximization- or minimizationbased) contrastive patterns.

It is worth noting that the agents in our case do make binary response predictions (i.e., yes/no) after their calls with customers. Based on the full data set, the agents achieved an overall binary prediction accuracy of about 44.45%, which is lower than that (about 80.43%) achieved by DSDL.

## 5.4. Explanatory Analysis

5.4.1. Key Attributes. We divided 200 key attributes regarding the case of car sales into five groups according to the different subjects they convey, namely, product-, service-, customer-, price-, and promotion-related subjects. Then, we calculated the relative importance scores of these attributes based on their normalized LDA probabilities and selected up to 20 top representative examples for each group (the results are available in Online Appendix F.1).

5.4.2. Dynamic Satisfaction. We generated the cumulative expectation $\pmb { U } ^ { E } ,$ , cumulative experience $u ^ { F } ,$ , and dynamic satisfaction S along the time steps using the trained DSDL model. Then, we averaged each of the three kinds of satisfaction (i.e., expected, experienced, and cumulative satisfaction) across all customers of the same class $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ either zero or one). For the averaged $s ,$ we further weighted it with its weight vector $w ^ { \bar { S } }$ after being trained. Then, for each time step, we derived a cumulative satisfaction by weighted sum. Figure 3 con trasts the two classes of customers in terms of expected, experienced, and cumulative satisfaction, respectively.

As Figure 3(a) shows, there exists dramatic fluctuation among customers’ expectations. This does not mean that the expectations were not stable, but rather just verifies the existence of personalized preferences of customers. That is, as different audio fragments (i.e., time steps) involve distinct topics, DSDL used the cross-view attention mechanism to capture the interlaced dialogue patterns between customer and agent, so the fluctuation herein can be attributed to the varying of the dialogue topic. Overall, there is no universal comparative pattern in the magnitude of expectation between the two classes of customers. This implies that, although the expectation, as an inherent facet of customers, exerts significant impact on their satisfaction, such impact may be out of proportion with satisfaction. In other words, customers with low initial expectation may still be difficult to satisfy. As Figure 3(b) shows, the volatility of the experienced satisfaction of the class 0 customers (i.e., who did not visit a store after being invited) was more intense than that of the other class of customers, reflecting that, for customers with higher final cumulative satisfaction, their online interaction experiences over different product attributes tended to be more stable. As Figure 3(c) indicates, the two classes gradually diverge from each other in terms of cumulative satisfaction as the dialogue goes on. This demonstrates the powerful capability of DSDL for learning dynamic satisfaction in the absence of customer profile information.

## 6. Contributions and Implications

The main contribution of this study is methodological. It proposes a theory-driven deep learning method DSDL, which draws strength from three learning strategies, that is, attribute-frequency-and-order-based preference embedding, cross-view attention–based deep learning, and bidirectional sparsity–based contrastive learning, to leverage dynamic customer satisfaction for customer response prediction in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. To the research stream of customer response prediction, DSDL offers a methodological tool for assessing customer satisfaction and purchase intention based on voice chat data. Besides, the empirical findings in a real invite-online-and-experience-in-store case broaden our outlook about (1) which product attributes customers prefer the most, (2) how customers actual experiences for key product attributes come about, (3) to what extent customers’ expectations are satisfied during online interaction and how such satisfaction evolves over time, and (4) to what extent a deep learning method’s performance can be enhanced by incorporating the dynamic satisfaction.

Our experiments and explanatory analysis provide managerial insights.

1. Implications from the response prediction capability of DSDL: The advantageous predictive capability of DSDL for customer response prediction has practical implications. As artificial intelligence, internet, and digitization technologies are flourishing online interaction–based business modes, such as realtime voice- and video-based online retailing, DSDL serves as a tool for leveraging the audio and text interaction data resources to identify and retain more high-value customers so as to enhance the profit and competitive advantage of retailers. Besides, DSDL helps retailers cut their marketing cost and improve their marketing efficiency. By predicting customer response based on voice chat accurately and timely, retailers or agents can focus on those high-value customers to improve their invitation success rate.

2. Implications from leveraging theory-driven satisfaction for customer response prediction: Our experiments provide evidence for the prediction enhancement effect of incorporating theory-driven satisfaction into deep learning models for customer response prediction. This offers managerial implications for the design science paradigm of information systems, that is, synergizing theory-driven constructs and context-specific knowledge with data-driven opaque predictive capability can lead to more effective prediction models and, meanwhile, reveal more practical business insights. For example, by incorporating dynamic satisfaction into the deep learning process, we not only obtained enhanced customer response prediction performance, but also gained business insights regarding customer interest and demand that cannot be derived directly from either raw data or general deep learning models.

3. Implications from dynamic satisfaction learned by DSDL: The theory-driven satisfaction generated by DSDL offers insights for understanding customers personalized preference, expectation, experience, and satisfaction with implications for managing customers online experience and purchase intention. For example, for marketing managers, the dynamic satisfaction generated by DSDL offers them opportunities to supervise the marketers’ service qualities during online interaction. A lot of voice or video chats are produced every day, and it is prohibitively time-consuming for a manager to supervise their service qualities by reviewing all of them.

4. Implications from attribute-wise satisfaction learned by DSDL: The personalized customer preferences for key product attributes learned by DSDL provide insights, helping retailers promote product quality and service level to meet customer demand. For example, when developing new products, the expected satisfactions learned by DSDL inspire retailers to focus more on the product attributes that are mostly expected by customers. Besides, the attribute-wise satisfactions allow agents to train and improve their speechcraft according to customers’ demand and satisfaction.

## 7. Conclusion

We propose a theory-driven deep learning method (i.e., DSDL) for voice chat–based customer response prediction in the invite-online-and-experience-in-store mode. Empirical evaluation results in a real case demonstrate DSDL’s superior prediction capabilities over state-ofthe-art alternatives.

Although we apply the proposed method to a case study involving car sales, the method is general and can be applied in other contexts, such as restaurant invitation calls, fitness program–selling calls, and real estate–selling calls. In such contexts, customer satisfaction is also critical for customer response. When applying the method to a new context, some parts are necessarily context-specific and need to be tailored.

Our work has several limitations, which may be add ressed in future research. First, although we evaluate DSDL in an invite-online-and-experience-in-store case, its cross-domain generalizability needs to be validated in a wider range of business contexts. For example, the pref erence template attributes we extract specifically in the car sales context may be limited in their generalizability. Some service- and price-related features are somewhat general and may be, to some extent, generalizable to other online customer invitation contexts, for example, real estate–selling calls. However, the key product attributes for modeling dynamic attribute-wise expectation are inherently domain-specific and need to be adapted to the focal domain as the sales context varies. Second, the deployment of DSDL has certain requirements on the voice chat data. For example, generating effective theory driven satisfaction relies on proper modeling of a customer’s experience and expectation, which entails sufficient coverage of the preference template attributes in the customer–agent conversation. On this account, when deploying DSDL, there is a need to balance the trade-off between expanding the preference template to cover a wider range of customer interest and containing the computational complexity of the model. Third, as our proposed method incorporates multiple key parameters, their collaborative effect on the prediction performance of customer response prediction deserves further investigation. Meanwhile, overfitting may arise and need to be controlled if the sample size for tuning DSDL is limited. Fourth, examining how agents can benefit from the response prediction by DSDL deserves further behavioral research in the field.
