Abstract. Extant research has revealed that direct communication between buyers and sellers on digital platforms facilitates transaction completion. However, the understanding of how direct communication influences the matching quality of these two-sided transactions is still limited. This study uses a multimethod approach to provide a perspective on how direct communication contributes to matching quality. In an observational study in the context of a peer-to-peer platform for long-term real estate rental properties, the study shows that longer direct phone communication between the renter (i.e., buyers) and the host (i.e., sellers) enables the renter to choose a relatively more unique alternative within her consideration set (i.e., an alternative that is distant from the average attribute levels of the consideration set) for the transaction. Also, we find that the relationship between having direct phone communication and ex post transaction satisfaction is stronger when a relatively more unique alternative is chosen. The implication is that direct phone communication allows the renter to collect supplemental information that is not observable online. Consequently, the renter can better leverage the breadth of her search results (i.e., the consideration set) in making her choice decision. We complement the observational study with a field survey to verify this informational mechanism. Furthermore, we conduct two randomized experiments to verify the motivation for engaging in direct phone communication and for choosing unique alternatives within the consideration set. Taken together, these insights extend the existing understanding of the benefits of direct commu nication on digital platforms.

## 1. Introduction

On digital peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms, the matching between buyers and sellers is often not under the complete control of the platform operator (Azevedo and Weyl 2016), and therefore, it requires extensive efforts from the participants in information generation, search, and communication (Einav et al. 2016). Consider as an example P2P platforms in the real estate rental property markets (our study context), which are characterized by a large number of fragmented buyers (renters) and sellers (hosts of properties) and substantial heterogeneity in demand and supply (Ramirez et al. 2016). On these platforms, renters typically engage in platform-mediated direct communication (e.g., phone calls or live chats) with hosts to solicit information before making choice decisions. Direct communication often occurs when the renter has formed a short list of candidate options (referred to as a consideration set (Howard and Sheth 1969, Shocker et al. 1991)) from the broad online search and would like to collect and verify additional information (that is not observable online) to ensure an appropriate choice decision. Our research question is as follows. How does direct communication contribute to the quality of two-sided matching by influencing the choice decision of the buyer (renter) within the consideration set?

In this study, we build upon the theoretical lens of the consideration set-based choice of consumers to provide an explanation of how direct communication contributes to matching quality by influencing consumers’ choice decisions. Our perspective is that direct communication with sellers enables consumers to collect additional information that supplements online observable product features and supports choice decisions. In an online search, consumers rely on online observable product features to form their consideration sets. Their final choices tend to be close to the centers of their consideration sets (i.e., intermediate options), as suggested by the theory of consideration set-based choice (e.g., Simonson and Tversky 1992) and evidenced in studies of online search (e.g., Bronnenberg et al. 2016). By bringing in supplemental information, direct communication allows consumers to anchor less on the centers of their consideration sets and select those unique alternatives within their consideration sets (that are distinct from the average attribute levels of their consideration sets) that they would otherwise not select. This influence, in turn, has implications for the quality of matching. Supported by the supplemental information from direct communication, consumers can reasonably deviate from their original anchoring points in their consideration sets (which are based mainly on online information) and choose relatively unique alternatives that better fit their needs. This should help consumers better leverage the breadth of their consideration sets and overcome the potential limits of online information, and it can eventually result in more desirable matching outcomes.

We adopt a multimethod approach to corroborate our theoretical propositions. Our analysis includes an observational study using operational-level proprietary data from a major Chinese P2P platform of real estate rental properties, a field survey of renters on this platform, and two randomized experiments built upon the same business context. The observational study data include the detailed time and duration of any direct phone communications between the renter and the hosts of the renter’s sampled alternatives (i.e., properties) in the pretransaction search process. A renter’s sampled alternatives with direct phone communication constitute the renter’s consideration set (i.e., the set of alternatives that she seriously considers out of all the alternatives she has browsed online). The rationale is that in this real estate rental property market, renters make choice decisions very carefully, and no renter in our context would sign a long-term lease without calling the host beforehand for inquiry and verification. Our results suggest that the relative uniqueness of a renter’s chosen alternative within her consideration set is positively associated with the duration of the direct phone call about this chosen alternative. Furthermore, the likelihood of ex post dissatisfaction (i.e., filing an appeal after the transaction) decreases with the duration of the direct phone call about the chosen alternative, and this relationship is more prominent when the uniqueness of the renter’s chosen alternative is higher within her consideration set. This empirical evidence supports our theoretical view that, by facilitating information collection from the host, direct communication supports the renter to reasonably choose a relatively unique alternative within her consideration set to enhance matching quality.

To address the confounding issues and alternative explanations that are challenging to address in the observational study alone, we conducted a field survey of renters who recently completed transactions on this platform. The survey responses confirmed that the phone calls served primarily to provide information about the specific listed properties and that other confounding effects $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ sales persuasion and advertising) and potential selection biases were very limited in our context. Also, to provide more evidence of our causal mechanisms regarding initiating phone calls and making choice decisions, we conducted two randomized experiments using the same business context of the platform. The first experiment concerns the renters’ ex ante decision of incurring a long phone call. It examined if a longer phone call was incurred mainly because of the uniqueness of the alternative (that necessitates the col lection of more information). The second experiment investigated their ex post decisions—whether a longer phone call about a unique alternative in the consideration set fosters the choice of this alternative. By zooming in on the specific steps in the renter decision, these experiments provide evidence on direct communication facilitating information collection and choice decision and support our proposed theoretical perspective.

The contributions of our study are twofold. First, our study extends the research on direct communication on platforms. Although the existing research has primarily focused on how direct communication facilitates transaction completion through information exchange (Hong et al. 2018) and trust building (Tan et al. 2019), we complement this research by illustrating the informational role of direct communication and showing how it influences the consumer’s choice within the consideration set and eventually contributes to matching quality. Second, our study adds to the research on two-sided matching by addressing matching quality. We contribute by showing that a possible way in which direct communication contributes to matching quality is by supporting consumers’ choice of unique alternatives within their consideration sets that may better fit their idiosyncratic needs, rather than choosing intermediate alternatives close to the average attribute levels of their consideration sets.

## 2. Theoretical Background and Hypothesis Development

## 2.1. Direct Communication and Two-Sided Matching on P2P Platforms

The extant platform research has primarily focused on the factors that influence the realization of two-sided matching, such as the design of matchmaking mechanisms examined in the economics literature (e.g., Hitsch et al. 2010, Azevedo and Weyl 2016) and various features of participants and transaction contexts examined in the Information Systems (IS) literature (e.g., Burtch et al. 2013, Bapna et al. 2016, Hong and Pavlou 2017). Although this work has generated good insights, the quality of twosided matching deserves additional research attention. For example, in real estate markets, the complexity and heterogeneity of demand and supply often hinder satisfaction with realized transactions (Cahill 1995, Levitt and Syverson 2008). Dissatisfaction and the realization that the chosen alternative is not a good fit (i.e., low matching quality) often arise posttransactions because of disconfirmed expectations (Oliver 1980). We, therefore, examine “matching quality” by focusing on the ex post renter appeals to the platform, which occur when renters find that the completed lease transactions are not consistent with their expectations.

The IS research has noticed the role of platformmediated direct communication (e.g., live chat) between buyers and sellers in facilitating the completion of online matching (Hong et al. 2018, Lv et al. 2018, Tan et al. 2019). The effects of direct communication are theorized primarily from two perspectives. First, from an informational perspective, direct communication assists the two sides to mitigate information asymmetry and uncertainty, and therefore, it facilitates transaction completion (Hong et al. 2018). Second, direct communication supports trust building between the two sides, which in turn, increases the chance of transaction completion (Lv et al. 2018, Tan et al. 2019). The focus of our study is on how direct communication, in the form of a direct phone call, contributes to the quality of completed transactions. Our perspective is that a mechanism through which direct communication improves matching quality is through enabling renters to choose more appropriate unique alternatives within their consideration sets. The additional information provided in direct communication can facilitate and justify such a choice for renters. We build upon theories on consideration set and online search to advance this understanding.

## 2.2. Information Search, Consideration Set, and Choice Decision

In the consideration set theory, consumers’ purchasing decisions are characterized by two stages: a consideration stage and a choice stage (Gensch 1987). The first stage (i.e., the consideration stage) is featured by the process of information search, in which consumers sample alternatives, evaluate their attributes, and form a consideration set (Shocker et al. 1991, Hauser et al. 1993, Mehta et al. 2003, De Los Santos et al. 2012). A consideration set is the reduced set of heterogeneous alternatives that are considered for purchasing (Shocker et al. 1991, Roberts and Lattin 1997). In online search, observable online information is the basis on which consumers form their consideration sets (Hoffman and Novak 1996, Parra and Ruiz 2009). The second stage (i.e., the choice stage) is characterized by a utility maximization-based decision process to select a single alternative from within the reduced consideration set. The consumers’ purchasing decisions are better characterized as choosing from the consideration set than as choosing from all available alternatives (Andrews and Srinivasan 1995, Roberts and Lattin 1997).

In observational studies when consumers’ consideration sets were latent and not directly observable, it was necessary to consider both stages jointly to infer consumers’ consideration sets and their choice decisions. However, when the consideration sets can be observed (e.g., with online clickstream data and filtering data), researchers need only use the observable consideration sets to examine choice decisions in the choice stage $( \mathrm { e . g . , }$ Moe 2006, Gu et al. 2012, Bronnenberg et al. 2016). This is because consumers’ decision criteria in the two stages are different (Moe 2006), and the choice stage has more explanatory power in purchasing decisions (Hauser 1978). Given that, in our context, we observe the consideration sets of renters using their phone call data; as renters seriously choose among the alternatives that they made phone calls about, we can just focus on how renters choose within their consideration sets.

Research on online search has found that with consideration sets of searched alternatives, consumers’ choices tend to be close to the average attribute levels of their consideration sets (Bronnenberg et al. 2016). In other words, consumers typically favor intermediate alternatives and avoid extreme alternatives within their consideration sets. This behavioral pattern can be explained by the consideration set theory from two angles: extremeness aversion and local trade-off contrast (Huber et al. 1982, Simonson and Tversky 1992). Extremeness aversion suggests that consumers tend to favor intermediate alternatives and avoid extreme, or unique, alternatives within their consideration sets (Neumann et al. 2016). This is mainly because when unique alternatives deviate from intermediate alternatives in both strengths and weaknesses, weaknesses loom larger (and thus, are weighted more heavily) than the respective strengths, and riskaverse consumers typically disfavor this uncertainty (Tversky and Kahneman 1991). Local trade-off contrast suggests that the advantageous position of an alternative, in contrast to its locally similar alternatives, helps increase its attractiveness within the consideration set. Intermediate options with more locally similar alternatives to contrast with are more likely to be favored than unique alternatives. Therefore, after an online search, consumers will be more likely to choose an intermediate alternative from the consideration sets they constructed based on online search (Bronnenberg et al. 2016).

## 2.3. Direct Communication, Consideration

Direct communication is a process for consumers to collect supplemental product information that is not observable online, and a longer-duration phone call allows the renters to collect more supplemental information from the host. For example, in our research context, the friendly neighbors of a listed property, the history of ownership, and the records of past rental transactions are all relevant information for a rental decision but are often not displayed online. Therefore, online information may be incomplete, and the renter’s initial average preference based mainly on online search may not necessarily be her true preference. When collecting supplemental information through a long phone call, the renter may find that a unique alternative is closer to her true preference, as such information collection can mitigate the uncertainty about the unique alternative (Simonson and Tversky 1992, Sheng et al. 2005). This is also consistent with the view that a consumer can update her preference through a learning process before a decision (De Los Santos et al. 2017). For example, a renter may initially prefer a large property size and sample alternatives based on this observable online feature. A direct phone communication can better inform the renter about the advantage of location convenience. Consequently, it becomes more likely for the renter to favor and finally choose a unique alternative, even though this unique alternative deviates from the renter’s initial consideration-set average attribute level formed based on online information.

Additionally, direct communication is also considered a trustworthy source of information collection (McKnight et al. 2002, Tan et al. 2019) through enhancing social relationships (Bhattacherjee 2002) and reducing perceived risks (Hong et al. 2018). The collection of trustworthy information should help further alleviate the renter’s perceived uncertainty that prevents the choice of a unique alternative, which makes it more likely for a unique alternative within the consideration set to become the final chosen alternative. We, therefore, hypothesize the following positive relationship.

Hypothesis 1. On average, the relative uniqueness of a renter’s chosen alternative within her consideration set is positively associated with the duration of direct phone communication about this chosen alternative.

We expect that the influence on the choice from within the consideration set is also how direct communication improves matching quality. From an informational perspective, a longer direct phone call can directly enhance matching quality because given the complexity of real estate markets, online observable property features are often insufficient for making optimal decisions (Capon and Kuhn 1982). By providing additional information, a longer direct phone call helps the renter reduce uncertainty and ensure that the chosen alternative fits her true preferences. More importantly, we expect that this informational benefit is more significant when a direct phone call influences the renter to choose a relatively more unique alternative from within her consideration set.

When the renter avoids choosing unique alternatives from within her consideration set, she tries to improve matching quality by minimizing the risk of the choice decision based on online information (used to construct the consideration set). If she follows this decision strategy, the contribution of direct communication in improving matching quality is incremental to that of online information. When a long phone call convinces the renter to deviate and choose a unique alternative, she essentially uses the phone call information to over come the limitations of her initial online search (Johnson et al. 2004). In this case, the quality of matching is dependent less on online information, and the phone call information plays a more critical role in ensuring the fit between the chosen alternative and the renter’s true needs. By supporting the choice of a unique alternative, the direct phone call essentially enables the renter to leverage the breadth of her consideration set and avoid missing a better fitting alternative. In this regard, a longer direct phone call will contribute more to matching quality when the alternative under consideration is a unique alternative, leading to higher posttransaction sat isfaction and fewer posttransaction appeals. We therefore hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. The likelihood of posttransaction appeals decreases with the duration of direct phone communication about the chosen alternative, and this relationship is stronger if the renter’s chosen alternative is relatively more unique within the consideration set.

## 3. Research Context and Observational Study

## 3.1. Research Context

Our research context is one of the top 5 P2P real estate rental property platforms in China. Although the business model of this platform resembles that of Airbnb, it focuses on the market for relatively long-term rentals that usually span at least several months. The platform is operated primarily through a mobile channel, mainly because of the specific environment of internet diffusion in China. Using the corresponding mobile app, prospective renters can search for listed properties. A typical search process starts with the input by the renter about a preferred location. The app returns the listed properties found around the preferred location. The renter can then click to browse property pages (Online Appendix A provides sample screenshots). Because of the constraints of the mobile interface, the search functions on the platform are limited. For example, there are no options to sort, filter, or search by different criteria (e.g., by hosts).

Renters can also contact hosts through direct phone calls. For the purposes of control (e.g., preventing side contracting) and privacy protection (e.g., protecting both sides from harassment), phone calls are all mediated by the platform. Phone numbers of hosts are not directly posted on property pages. Rather, renters use a dedicated button on the property page to dial hosts (illustrated in Online Appendix A). Typically, both hosts and renters talk to multiple candidates before transactions, which reflects two-sided matching.

Hosts pay the platform listing fees based on the duration of listing and the rental rates. Therefore, hosts have no incentive to continue listing their rented properties. The platform also prevents the listing of rented properties by conducting verifications and encouraging reports by renters. Because listing fees are partially tied to rates, hosts also have limited incentive to lower rental rates in deal making. If needed, the platform assists in managing the appointments and scheduling of on-site tours before contracting.

The hosts on the platform include both individual hosts and professional hosts (typically small real estate firms or rental agency companies). The platform classifies individual hosts and professional hosts based on hosts’ selfreporting in registration and platform verification. The platform does not price discriminate between individual and professional hosts. Therefore, mimicking the other type of hosts is neither practical nor motivated. The platform also does not disclose the host type to renters (Online Appendix A illustrates the similarity between property pages of individual hosts and professional hosts). Therefore, the host type is not likely to confound the renter’s choice decision, which limits self-selection bias.

## 3.2. Data

From the company’s operational database, we randomly selected 55,000 search processes made by unique renters in the five top cities in China from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2017. We chose to consider unique renters mainly because in this long-term rental property market, repeated purchases are rare over our observation window. For each search process, we retrieved the renter’s history of calling different hosts. The consideration set includes all the alternatives (properties) that a renter called about before the transaction. The rationale is that phone calls indicate the renter’s serious consideration of the corresponding alternatives, and we do not observe any renter renting a property without first communicating with the host through direct phone calls. We also verified this reasoning in a subsequent field survey (in Section 4). Because of operational reasons, we did not observe renters’ online browsing behavior, and therefore, we also address this issue in the field survey.

We excluded 15,075 search processes with fewer than three sampled alternatives (in which uniqueness cannot be measured), 430 rare cases (<1%) with more than 28 sampled alternatives, and $^ { 8 , 4 7 2 }$ processes without transactions. The main analyses are based on the remaining 31,023 search processes with transactions. We also removed from the renters’ consideration sets the sampled alternatives that were already unavailable (i.e., already leased out)

at the time of transactions as they were not viable alterna tives. An average search process spans 2.101 days from the first call to the time of transaction.<sup>1</sup>

In the 31,023 search processes with transactions, a renter’s consideration set includes an average of 6.32 alternatives. Our sample contains 60,370 unique hosts and 84,065 unique alternatives. These alternatives (properties) reside in neighborhood communities constructed by real estate builders, which were grouped by the platform into so-called “neighborhood blocks” using related zoning standards in the industry. Our sample covers 765 neighborhood blocks. On average, each individual host offered 1.21 properties, and each professional host offered 2.69 properties. Although theoretically, a renter may call a host to talk about multiple listed alternatives from this host, we find that this case is rare in practice (as also evidenced in the field survey reported). It occurs in only 4.2% of cases with transactions, and in only 1.9% of transactions, the chosen alternatives share a common host with other sampled alternatives. In our sample, the average phone call duration about a chosen alternative is 5.24 minutes.

## 3.4. Analysis on Matching Quality

To assess matching quality in this study, we adopt an operational measure—renter appeals to the platform. In our context, hosts and renters are provided an option to appeal directly to the platform in the lease period if they are not satisfied with each other. Appeals are only observable to the platform, and the objective of appeal (from either side) is mainly to let the platform step in and resolve the issue. For all the completed transactions in our sample, we check whether appeals occurred within two weeks after the transactions. This is because the problems arising early in the lease period are likely to be attributed to deficient communication in the precontract stage of matching. The use of other alternative observation windows (e.g., one week or three weeks) generates consistent results. We also ensure validity by checking the types of appeals. The platform classifies appeals from renters into seven broad categories: (1) property already leased out, (2) incorrect rental rate, (3) inaccurate photos, (4) communication difficulty, (5) hidden fees, (6) inaccurate property type, and (7) others. In our study, we focus on types (2)–(6) as they are more relevant to precontract communications. The platform does not classify appeals from hosts as these types of appeals are extremely rare. We, therefore, consider appeals from renters.

## 4. Field Survey

We employed a field survey to address some key issues because of data limitations in the observational study. Table 5 summarizes these issues and the related findings from the field survey. We coordinated with the platform to send out questionnaires to 1,300 renters who had completed a rental transaction within the past six months. We asked respondents to recall their online search processes, direct phone communication with hosts, and choice decisions in their last transactions. Online Appendix E provides the complete list of survey questions and responses. As summarized in Table 5, based on 350 valid responses (a response rate of 26.92%), we confirm three key aspects of our study. First, the renters’ unobservable online search processes were not likely to confound our findings, as renters have generally suggested that their choice decisions were primarily based on their observable consider ation sets rather than the search processes prior to consideration set formation. Second, renters used phone calls to mainly collect additional information about alternatives that they considered. They were subject to very limited other influences (e.g., advertising, persuasion, and negotiation). This provides support for the informational role of direct phone calls. Third, selection bias was not likely to be a confounding factor. Renters generally felt that they did not make their final decisions before making phone calls. Overall, these findings support our view of direct phone communication as an information collection mechanism to facilitate choice decision.

## 5. Randomized Experiments

To further explore the underlying mechanisms driving phone call duration and the renter’s choice decision, we conducted two randomized experiments using the same platform business context as in the observational study. In the first experiment, we examined the renter’s ex ante decision of incurring a long phone call (i.e., would the relative uniqueness of an alternative motivate the subject to collect more information using a longer phone call?). We recruited 276 participants and randomly assigned them into two groups (Online Appendix F provides a detailed description of the experiment design). In the control group, four properties were displayed to participants as their consideration sets. To simplify the scenario, properties are characterized using only four attributes: price, size, number of bedrooms, and orientation.<sup>3</sup> The four displayed properties were similar and based on participants’ indicated preferred values of these four attributes (so as to qualify as the consideration set). In the treatment group, participants were also displayed four properties; three were similar to each other, whereas one was manipulated to be distinct from the others. The participants were asked to imagine that they would choose between these alternatives, and they could call the host of each property for more discussion. We asked the participants to specify how long they would talk to the host of each property over the phone. We also used a pretreatment survey to verify that participants did not have a predetermined bias toward any alternative within their consideration sets, and there was no ex ante difference between the treatment and control groups (details in Online Appendix F.1).

In the second experiment, we recruited 645 participants and randomly assigned them into six groups (the detailed experiment description is in Online Appendix G). In all groups, three similar properties and one unique property (based on the same set of four attributes as in the first experiment) were presented to participants. The values of property attributes were set based on the participants’ indicated preferences (in a pretreatment survey), and we ensured that participants had no ex ante biases toward any alternative. We asked participants to imagine that they had called the hosts of these properties, and the duration of each phone call was presented to them. In the control group, all four phone calls had similar duration. In treatment groups 1, 3, and 4, the phone call for one of three nonunique properties was significantly longer than calls for other alternatives. In treatment group 2, the phone call for the unique property was significantly longer than calls for other alternatives. In treatment group 5, the phone calls for all four properties were similar and significantly longer than calls in the control group. We asked participants which alternatives they would choose.

In group 2 (longer call about the unique alternative), 51.4% of participants indicated willingness to choose the unique alternative. In the control group, only 11.9% of participants did so. The ANCOVA results in Table 6 indicated a significant treatment effect (F � 41.732, p < 0.01) on choosing the unique alternative, confirming the long phone duration effect. The treatment effect is not significantly influenced when the participants preferred attribute values and demographic features were included as covariates. Moreover, the ex ante preferences about the unique alternative did not differ between the treatment groups and the control group (F � 0.527, n.s.). In a postsession survey question, 91.7% of participants indicated that they felt that longer phone calls would provide them with more supplemental information not displayed on the property pages, which would facilitate the choices that fit their needs. These findings support the view that information collection through a longer phone call allows the renter to select a unique alternative from within the consideration set.

In a pool of groups 1, 3, and 4 (with a longer call about a nonunique alternative), 10.5% of participants were willing to choose the unique alternative. Table 6 also shows that compared with the control group, the treatment effects in this pool are overall nonsignificant (F � 0.374, n.s.). In group 5 (with longer calls about all properties), 24.8% of participants were willing to choose the unique alternative, and the treatment effect is significant $( F = 1 2 . 6 8 2 , p < 0 . 0 1 )$ but is weaker than that in group 1. Although a longer phone call may also increase the renters’ willingness to choose a nonunique alternative, this does not change the significant impact of long phone calls to increase the attractiveness of unique alternatives. The implication is that renters have more information needs for the unique alternative than for other nonunique alternatives, and a longer phone call is required to better fulfill these information needs and enable the choice of the unique alternative.

## 6. Discussion and Conclusion

The theoretical implications of our findings are twofold. First, our study provides a unique perspective to complement the research on direct communication on digital platforms. Although the existing research has focused on the influences of direct communication on matching completion (e.g., Hong et al. 2018, Lv et al. 2018, Tan et al. 2019), our study advances this research by illustrating how direct communication influences consumer choice from within their consideration set in two-sided matching. Theoretically, our perspective is aligned with the existing research (e.g., Hong et al. 2018) that suggests the informational benefit of direct communication. However, the contribution of our study is to better illustrate how the informational benefit, through a specific mechanism of supporting the choice of unique alternatives within the consideration set, can eventually contribute to the quality of two-sided matching.

Second, our consideration of matching quality contributes to the overall research on two-sided matching on platforms, as this issue has been relatively underexplored (McIntyre and Srinivasan 2017). We find that direct phone communication helps mitigate posttransaction dissatisfaction, and this effect is more prominent when the renter chooses a relatively more unique alternative in her consideration set. The implication is that a possible way to enhance matching quality is to facilitate rational choices of unique alternatives within consumers’ consideration sets. When information collection through direct communication appropriately supports such choices, consumers essentially can leverage the breadth of their search results (i.e., consideration sets) to achieve better matching outcomes. This insight advances the understanding of how to improve matching quality on twosided platforms.

As for practical implications, our study informs platform operators about the importance of aligning two critical practices: supplying renters with useful online information and supporting their direct communication with hosts. Platform operators can provide more online information for renters to better distinguish between different alternatives. They can also assist renters and hosts to effectively communicate with each other. The joint use of the two mechanisms can motivate renters to more actively search in a broad range of differentiated alternatives and better choose best-fit options. For renters and hosts, our study suggests the importance of utilizing direct communication on the platform to enhance matching quality. Direct communication enables renters to better revisit their initial preferences and leverage the breadth of their consideration sets to make appropriate choice decisions. Hosts can also assist renters through direct communication to realize desirable matching outcomes.

Our research has several limitations, which lay out avenues for future work. First, we do not observe the detailed content of direct communication. Although our field survey helped confirm the main objective of such communication (i.e., information collection), future research can use more specific content analysis to gain insights on the specific mechanisms embedded in direct communication and how they influence the choice decision and the resulting matching quality. Second, in the field survey, renters acknowledged their time cost in making phone calls. Given such cost, it remains unclear how each phone call may influence the renter’s subsequent decisions of continuing/stopping to contact other hosts. Therefore, future research on direct communication may incorporate such considerations. Third, we have reasonably abstracted away the online search/browsing process and primarily focused on the choice stage. Future research with granular data on online search footprints can also integrate the examination of search processes with the consideration of choice decisions. Fourth, future research with appropriate data access may also examine the possible offline direct communication between buyers and sellers. Such investigation can potentially add to the research stream on online/offline interactions (e.g., Luo et al. 2020) by bringing in the perspective of direct communication.
