## A R T I C L E I N F O

## A B S T R A C T

This paper reviews the emerging literature on blockchain, structuring key insights in a conceptual framework and setting out a research agenda from a socio-technical and information systems (IS) perspective. The review covers 234 blockchain-focused papers across IS, management, computer science and related fields. It categorises blockchain use into three main types and reframes blockchain use within the socio-technical phenomena of which it is a part. This broader frame work enables us to aggregate, integrate and synthesise knowledge about blockchain, its values, drivers and barriers, social and technical subsystem(s), as well as potential consequences, both intended and unintended. Using this framework as a basis for analysis, we identify gaps in the existing blockchain literature and outline a research agenda for advancing blockchain studies within the IS field.

## Introduction

This paper, which forms part of The Journal of Strategic Information Systems (JSIS) series of review special issues (Pan et al., 2022), examines what we know about blockchain and identifies areas for future research. The review adopts the socio-technical theoretica perspective of information systems (IS) research (Sarker et al., 2019).

The original blockchain concept was introduced in 2008 as the underlying technology behind Bitcoin (Nakamoto, 2008). Today, the uses of blockchain are significantly more diverse (e.g., Lacity, 2022). From a technical perspective, a blockchain is an encrypted, linked and timestamped distributed database (ledger) in which distributed nodes reach an agreement over ledger status via a consensus mechanism (Nakamoto, 2008). From a practical perspective, blockchain has been described as supporting transactability, autonomy, immutability, pseudonymity, and a decentralised and distributed network (Iansiti and Lakhani, 2017).

The use of blockchain is an important and relevant phenomenon in the IS field (Hendershott et al., 2021; Lacity, 2022). Published blockchain research has increased exponentially since 2017 (Scopus. 2024). While the novelty of blockchain technology is noteworthy. its true importance as a research topic lies in its potential for long-term, transformative impact. Beyond the initial hype surrounding blockchain as the underlying technology of Bitcoin (Nakamoto, 2008), the existing literature has found that blockchain’s underlying principles of decentralisation, transparency and immutability have far-reaching implications across various sectors – such as the financial industry (Hendershott et al., 2021), cyber security (Luo and Choi, 2022), data governance (Drummer and Neumann, 2020), supply chain management (Centobelli et al., 2022) and the metaverse/Web 3.0 (Smethurst, 2023). Blockchain is reshaping how we approach and conceptualise trust (Shao et al., 2022), security (Pagnotta, 2020) and decentralisation (Mueller-Bloch et al., 2022) in complex systems. This blockchain research provides ‘slices of [the] complex sociotechnical fabric’ (Williams and Pollock, 2012, p. 19) of blockchain use phenomena.

However, what is currently absent is a comprehensive overview that can theoretically synthesise knowledge claims across different types of blockchain use. As these transformations gradually unfold, a review becomes crucial for accumulating knowledge claims on ‘what-we-know’ and to guide future research on ‘what-we–need-to-know’. Hence, in line with the purpose of this review special issue and the common purposes of literature reviews of this type (e.g., Seymour et al. (2023); Vial (2019); Zuchowski et al. (2016)), we contribute to blockchain research via a literature review (Pan et al., 2023; Webster and Watson, 2002) of blockchain use phenomena. Specifically, this study asks the following questions:

RQ1: What has been researched and published on blockchain use? (Review).

RQ2: How can we frame, integrate and synthesise these knowledge claims from a socio-technical perspective? (Framework)

RQ3: Where do we need, but currently lack, research knowledge on socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use? (Agenda)

To answer these questions, we performed a structured, concept-focused literature review (Webster and Watson, 2002). We iden tified and reviewed 234 relevant papers from the IS field and neighbouring fields of management/business and computer science/ human–computer interaction (HCI). We selected this wide review scope as studies in neighbouring disciplines may have a comparable socio–technical interest to that in our, the IS field (Sarker et al., 2019). For our analysis, we combined a structured literature review with systematic coding inspired by grounded theory (Wolfswinkel et al., 2013). That is, we performed a careful and detailed coding analysis of the reviewed corpus (i.e., the texts of the reviewed papers).

The review uses a socio-technical theoretical lens as a conceptual foundation for the integration and interrelation of the diverse and dispersed findings on blockchain and blockchain use in the reviewed literature. This approach emphasises equal consideration of the social and technical aspects of IS, focusing on how these elements interact and form a unified whole (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977; Orlikowski, 1992; Sarker et al., 2019). This perspective is particularly relevant for blockchain use where social and technical sub systems are deeply intertwined (Rossi et al., 2019). For example, the governance of blockchain use has both social aspects (e.g., community rules) and technological aspects (e.g., a consensus mechanism), which are interrelated. For instance, an open-source community is more likely to adopt a different consensus mechanism than a proprietary, commercial developer.

To coherently aggregate and integrate these knowledge claims for an IS research audience – i.e., one interested in technical, socia and business dimensions – we developed a framework focused on socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. The frame work aggregates and synthesises knowledge claims on blockchain regarding values, drivers and barriers, social and technical sub systems, along with intended or unintended consequences. Research to date has made substantial contributions to our knowledge about blockchain. However, building on this framework as both an analytical foundation and using it as a platform for future research, we examine the theoretical and practical implications of blockchain use, identify existing knowledge gaps in the blockchain literature and outline a research agenda for future IS studies.

The theoretical contribution of this review lies in three aspects. It offers a conceptual framework that identifies the key themes in the current blockchain research, synthesises novel and informative knowledge on the phenomena, identifies gaps, and provides a guide for future research opportunities, thus advancing the overall understanding of blockchain use in this way (Leidner, 2018; Webster and Watson, 2002).

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 discusses how this review differs from prior blockchain reviews and argues for a broader focus on socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. Section 3 describes the structured literature review method. Section 4 summarises key knowledge claims from the reviewed literature and proposes the conceptual framework of sociotechnical phenomena involving blockchain use. Section 5 proposes the future research agenda. Section 6 provides a brief conclusion.

## Prior work and socio-technical perspective

## Prior literature reviews on blockchain

Given the novelty of blockchain use, only a limited set of prior literature reviews is available; these are discussed in this section. As this is a literature review, the blockchain research literature itself is reviewed in the main part of this paper, in Section 4.

Prior literature reviews summarise blockchain knowledge on very specific topics and perspectives, such as smart contract languages (Dwivedi et al., 2021) and ethical marketing (Tan and Salo, 2023).

Table 1 presents an overview of prior key blockchain literature reviews, including research topics and analytical perspectives. Prior literature reviews make considerable contributions to the articulation of blockchain knowledge, with most focused on either the socia or technical perspectives of blockchain use.

Some reviews prioritise the technical elements of blockchain use and have limited concern about the social elements. These reviews focus on the technical issues of blockchain technology, aiming to direct its future development or improvement. For example, one review compares the governance and decision rights of public and private blockchain use at the protocol level (Smit et al., 2020). Another review focuses on different types of attacks on blockchain use, distinguishing between those targeting peer-to-peer networks, consensus mechanisms, applications or wallets (Guggenberger et al., 2021a).

Another set of reviews almost exclusively focuses on the social components in blockchain use. For example, Agerskov et al. (2023) review ethical issues related to blockchain that should be addressed in blockchain use and design guidelines. Another review in vestigates how seven blockchain-based artefacts can benefit different stakeholders in the sports sector (Schellinger et al., 2022a)

Lei and Ngai (2023) offer an analysis of blockchain research before 2022 within the IS discipline, combining meta-analysis with a comprehensive literature review. While their review offers valuable insights, it differs from the current study’s contributions. Instead of synthesising existing literature’s knowledge claims into a coherent conceptual framework, as done in the current study, Lei and Ngai’s work focuses on a categorisation of the existing blockchain research itself, such as its methodologies, research paradigms and theoretical frameworks.

This highlights the need in current blockchain literature reviews for a conceptual framework that effectively synthesises knowledge claims from existing research (what we actually know).

## From blockchain to socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use

Blockchain(s) is researched and conceptualised on multiple levels (Lacity, 2022; Ostern, 2020). We define blockchain as a tech nology based on an encrypted distributed ledger with append-only logic that relies on a consensus mechanism to reach agreement on ledger status among a (peer-to-peer) network of nodes (Nakamoto, 2008). Focusing reviews solely on blockchain technology narrows the scope, as it implies the technology is the relevant unit of study, or it leaves unresolved the specific area or unit of analysis.

We define blockchain use as the use of and engagement with systems where blockchain technology serves as a core component, driven by specific antecedents and resulting in various outcomes (Lacity, 2022). This includes both the use of information generated by the system and interaction with the system itself at individual, organisational or societal levels (Burton-Jones and Straub Jr, 2006).

Blockchain use occurs within complex systems that incorporate a range of social, organisational, technological and cultural ele ments. Hence, we consider blockchain and blockchain use in the context of the larger socio–technical phenomena of which they are a key part. This perspective is particularly suitable to investigating the use of information technology (IT) within social contexts (DeSanctis and Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1992).

The socio-technical perspective. originated from the Tavistock Institute. has been further developed and expanded widely across disciplines. According to foundational socio-technical work (Leavitt, 1965), organisations can be understood to consist of four main interrelated components: tasks, organisational structure, technology and people with qualifications. In IS research, the socio-technica perspective has been further developed. It posits that neither the social nor the technical aspects of IT use can be understood in isolation but must be viewed as part of a holistic system(s) (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977: Orlikowski, 1992: Robey and Boudreau, 1999) This perspective enables technology use to be understood in a social context from both instrumental and humanistic perspectives (Sarker et al., 2019). Studies leveraging a socio-technical perspective typically explore four key, well-defined concepts: people (the human element); structure (the social and organisational arrangements); technology (the technical components); and tasks (the processes and activities performed) (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977; Lee, 2004; Orlikowski, 1992; Robey and Boudreau, 1999).

The interplay between these social and technical subsystems have been theorised differently (Sarker et al., 2019), including as ‘reciprocal interactions’ (social-technical view) (DeSanctis and Poole, 1994; Orlikowski, 1992) and ‘constitutive entanglements (sociomaterial view) (Orlikowski, 2007). In this study, we adopt the former, the reciprocal interactions perspective to analyse blockchain use. This perspective is one of the main socio-technical approaches (Sarker et al., 2019) and is aligned with Mumford’s classical socio-technical perspective (e.g., Mumford. 1983). It provides a suitable worldview for examining the social elements. technical components and the dynamically reciprocal interplay between technology and social structures, Our choice of this perspective is driven by the flexibility inherent in this approach, which allows us to examine the dynamic influences between social and technical elements over time and accommodating studies with varying degrees of socio-technical integration (i.e., it aligns with the reviewed literature body). This approach aligns with our research objective of synthesising and integrating the diverse body of knowledge in the existing blockchain use research, offering a balanced and pragmatic means to understand ‘socio-technical phe nomena involving blockchain use’.

The socio-technical perspective is often applied to (IT) ‘systems’, but it can also be applied to (IT, digital) ‘phenomena’, the wider context of these systems. In the context of blockchain use, a socio-technical blockchain system refers to a system consisting of blockchain technology and the social community of users surrounding this blockchain system (Ziolkowski et al., 2020). For example, to fully comprehend Bitcoin, it is insufficient to examine only its technological elements (e.g., blockchain technology). Social elements, including users, miners and regulators, are integral to understanding Bitcoin use, too (Beck et al., 2016). Bitcoin is a specific socio technical system with the wider ‘cryptocurrency’ socio-technical phenomenon.

The current literature review has several benefits. Firstly, our socio-technical perspective provides a distinctive analytical lens for this review. Secondly, the existing literature reviews focused on literature published before 2022. Given the expanding literature and rapidly evolving technology, this space naturally needs updated reviews. Our review extends this timeline by including studies in 2024, thereby offering insights into the most recent developments in the field. Thirdly, our review diverges by examining various blockchain uses rather than viewing all types of blockchain uses in the same way. This broader perspective allows us to capture a more diverse range of knowledge claims associated with different blockchain uses. In short, the current literature review distinguishes itself by employing a socio-technical theoretical lens, extended temporal scope and comprehensive coverage of blockchain use types. As outlined in detail below, our approach synthesises disparate knowledge claims across various blockchain uses into a coherent con ceptual framework, offering a fresh IS perspective on this rapidly evolving phenomenon.

## Review method

We conducted a structured literature review. that is. the structured narrative review identified in the Paré et al. (2015) literature review typology. Following guidelines from Levy and Ellis (2006), Pan et al. (2023), Webster and Watson (2002) and Wolfswinkel et al. (2013), our structured literature review method employed the pre-defined inclusion/exclusion criteria from systematic literature review methods (Morrell, 2008). We combined structured and narrative approaches ‘to develop a comprehensive understanding and critical assessment of knowledge relevant for the topic’ via ‘a dialogical interaction among the research and the literature’ (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015, p. 164).

This method helps us to answer this study’s proposed research questions. It offers several benefits: it provides a narrative summary of the knowledge claims and findings in the existing blockchain research (to address RQ1); it analyses the content of these knowledge claims and findings: and synthesises them into a framework (to address RO2). This combination helps to identify future research opportunities and ask valuable research questions (Rai, 2017) (to address RQ3). Through this combination, a structured literature review method provides defined, structured search strategies with the richness, comprehensiveness and sensitivity of a qualitative analysis of the contents of the reviewed studies (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic. 2015: Kitchenham et al., 2009: Webster and Watson 2002). The benefit of this best of both worlds method is that it provides not only a clear link to sources but also an analytical approach that only human reviewers can provide (Pan et al. 2023) – namely, comprehensively understanding, reframing and making theoretica and conceptual contributions (Webster and Watson, 2002, p. xiii).

## Review scope

Scholars from various disciplines have brought different perspectives to their interest in blockchain use. An interdisciplinary literature review is required for two reasons: (1) to gain a comprehensive understanding of blockchain use phenomena; and (2) to ensure the inclusion of sources of interest to the IS discipline but published in neighbouring disciplines, such as business/management and computer science/human–computer interaction (HCI).

Based on our research questions, the inclusion criteria for the reviewed literature are as follows: firstly, papers that primarily investigate blockchain–related phenomena; and, secondly, papers published in reputable journals within the relevant disciplines. The following leading journals and conferences were included: the FT50 journal list in management; the Senior Scholars’ List of Premier Journals identified by the Association for Information Systems (AIS) and AIS conferences in IS; the Association for Computing Ma chinery (ACM) Web Conference (WWW); and the ACM Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI) in computer sci ence. In total, 58 journals and six conferences were selected within our review scope (see Appendix A). The third criterion was papers written in English, with the fourth criterion being journal articles published between 2009 and 2024, and conference papers published between 2021 and 2024 (as earlier conference papers may have evolved into journal papers). These criteria ensure that the selected literature is relevant. of high quality and comprehensive in relation to the current state of blockchain research

We systematically incorporated relevant sources within this scope and considered significant external sources, as described in the following.

## Search

Three searches were used in sequence: keyword-based searches; forward-and-backward searches (Webster and Watson, 2002); and non-systematic (search) strategies. The final literature search and update took place on 5 May 2024.

Keywords based searches: A keyword-based search effectively identifies academic sources (in this case focusing on blockchain technology) (Webster and Watson 2002; Wolfswinkel et al. 2013) but naturally produces a large amount of low-quality or irrelevant results (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic 2014: Rousseau et al. 2008: Rowe 2014). Considering the active role of reviewers and human-inthe-loop nature of the literature review process we used reputable academic databases as our primary search resources (Pan et al. 2023). Three databases were used: Web of Science, Science Direct and Scopus.

‘Blockchain’ was the only term used as our keyword for database searching. In the exploratory stage, we found that the terms ‘blockchain’ or ‘blockchains’ signify the key concept of ‘blockchain use’ in our review – rather than terms like ‘blockchain use,’ ‘blockchain-based artefacts,’ ‘blockchain use cases,’ or ‘blockchain applications’ which only resulted in a significantly limited search result. Hence, to comprehensively collect sources focusing on blockchain use, we used ‘blockchain’ as our search term.

The search term ‘blockchain’ yielded a substantial search result of 61,902 hits, with 19,693 from Web of Science, 4,058 from Science Direct and 38,151 from Scopus. To manage the overwhelming volume of papers, we employed EndNote’s automated duplicate detection function, which compares DOIs, author names, paper titles and journal metadata. Naturally, many papers appeared multiple times in the searches, vastly inflating the initial apparent volume of possible papers. This automated function was supplemented by manual verification in Microsoft Excel to ensure comprehensive duplicate removal. We then applied our predetermined inclusion criteria (the second, third and fourth criteria described above) to eliminate out-of-scope papers by using relevant functions in Endnote. Finally, we manually reviewed the content of the remaining papers for their relevance to blockchain use phenomena (Criterion 1). To validate our search comprehensiveness, we double-checked by manually examining the e-libraries of the selected journals and con ferences. We excluded non-primary research (e.g., position papers) and incomplete work (e.g., work-in-progress short papers). This rigorous search and selection process resulted in an initial corpus of 159 relevant in-scope papers.

Forward-and-backward searches: Forward-and-backward searches comprise a complementary approach to identify relevant literature by starting with key papers and examining their citations (forward search) and references (backward search) (Webster and Watson, 2002). In our case, we used keyword-based search results as key papers and examined their citations (citing) and references (cited) to identify papers focusing on blockchain use without using blockchain as a keyword. For both types of searches, we relied primarily on the corresponding feature of Web of Science. Using the forward search strategy, we examined 3,728 (citing) sources, while the backward search strategy led to the examination of 9,954 (cited) sources. We used the same examination and selection process as above on these 13,682 hits, leading to an additional 32 relevant in-scope sources that were not already in the corpus bringing the total to 191 sources.

Non-systematic search: While a systematic approach can provide consistency and thoroughness within the search scope, other insightful resources may not be captured due to the overly rigid bounds of this type of approach (Boell and Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014; Zuchowski et al., 2016). In recognition of this point, we added, based on scholarly judgement, additional relevant references based on recommendations by colleagues and garnered through workshops, as well as informed understanding derived from our practica experience and research in the blockchain space. In total, 43 additional sources were included, based on recommendations not already in the corpus. bringing the total to 234 sources as the final corpus to be analysed. ## Coding

We adopted a systematic, grounded theory-inspired coding approach (Strauss, 1990; Wolfswinkel et al., 2013) to help analyse, aggregate and synthesise knowledge claims on socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use.1

Following the guidance of Wolfswinkel et al. (2013), our coding process includes three phases, namely open coding, axial coding, and selective coding. Throughout the coding process, we continuously compared new knowledge claims with those previously identified, looking for similarities, differences and evolving perspectives. This ongoing comparison allowed for the refinement of codes and themes, ensuring that the final analysis accurately represented the current state of knowledge claims in blockchain research (Wolfswinkel et al., 2013).

Specifically, the open coding phase of analysis involves a detailed examination of each selected paper (Wolfswinkel et al., 2013), with particular attention paid to explicit knowledge claims on blockchain uses. These claims are systematically identified and high lighted within the text. For example, a study found that ‘individuals’ political values influence their attitudes toward Bitcoin affor dances and their Bitcoin use behaviour' (Lichti and Tumasian. 2023. p. 645). We highlighted this knowledge claim and coded it as ‘political values’.

Following the open coding phase. we engaged in axial coding, a process of identifving relationships and connections between the initial codes (Wolfswinkel et al., 2013). This stage involves grouping related codes into broader, more abstract sub-themes. To continue the example, codes such as political values' or Bitcoin ideology' could be consolidated under a higher-level category of drivers'.

In the selective coding phase, we integrated and refined the themes and codes identified during axial coding (Wolfswinkel et al. 2013). This process, grounded in the knowledge claims gathered from the reviewed papers, aimed to uncover the key mechanisms that can help our understanding of the complex socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. Through this process, we identified the relationships between five key themes (values, drivers and barriers, social subsystem(s), technical subsystem(s), and consequences) in the existing blockchain literature, contributing a new conceptual framework that can advance knowledge in the field of blockchain use research, as analysed in Section 4 (see Appendix A for further details). These higher-level themes and their relationships emerged from the interpretation of the reviewed corpus, with these aligned with well-established concepts in previous work (Wolfswinkel et al.. 2013), in our case, work on socio-technical systems and phenomena. Therefore, we utilised existing theoretical terminology to maintain consistency. In our case, higher–level elements included drivers and barriers, social and technical subsystem(s), and con sequences of blockchain use. Importantly, an additional higher-level theme emerged, that of values. Values have not been typically or explicitly discussed in previous socio-technical frameworks. ## Framework and findings

This section analyses and synthesises the diverse and dispersed research knowledge claims in the reviewed corpus of 234 sources into a conceptual framework of socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. In reviewing the corpus, certain knowledge claims on blockchain use are universally applicable across all types of use, while others are relevant only to specific types or even a single type of blockchain use. Hence, we categorised these different types of blockchain use into three Types (I–III), as discussed below.

## Blockchain use

Based on 186 sources from the reviewed corpus of 234 publications, three types of blockchain use were identified, as shown in Table 3. The remaining sources either discuss blockchain in general or view blockchain as a general concept capable of achieving transactability, autonomy, immutability, pseudonymity, and a decentralised and distributed network (Lansiti and Lakhani, 2017), without defining the specific use type.

The first type (Type I) of blockchain use reported in existing blockchain research can be characterised as blockchain use as digital assets, in which blockchain functions primarily as a store of value, a medium of exchange or an investment vehicle. Cryptocurrencies (e.g., Dierksmeier and Seele, 2018) and non-fungible tokens (NFTs) (White et al., 2022) are two subtypes of digital assets studied in the existing literature.

Cryptocurrency (43 sources) refers to digital or virtual currency that uses cryptography for security, operating independently of a central bank. Bitcoin, widely recognised as the world’s first cryptocurrency, exemplifies this subtype. This type of blockchain-enabled digital asset enables users to earn economic benefits through mining, trading or competing to collect transaction fees from decen tralised exchanges (Lacity, 2022).

Non-fungible tokens (NFTs) refer to unique digital assets representing ownership of a specific item or piece of content, typically used for digital art, collectibles or other unique digital items. Unlike cryptocurrencies, which are fungible and interchangeable, each NFT has distinct characteristics that set it apart. For example, in the reviewed literature, Carvalho et al. (2023) provide design principles for the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) to support student athletes to profit from their name, image and likeness.

The second type (Type II) of blockchain use reported in existing blockchain research can be characterised as blockchain use as an institutional mechanism, in which blockchain functions primarily as a new form of organisational, governance or social coordination. This type of blockchain use fundamentally alters traditional institutional structures through pre-defined algorithmic rules, ensuring consistent and independent operation.

Smart contracts (14 sources) refer to self-executing contracts with terms directly encoded into computer programs, automatically enforcing and executing these terms when predefined conditions are met (e.g., Beck et al., 2018; Carvalho, 2021). Ethereum, widely recognised as one of the most popular smart contract platforms, exemplifies this subtype. This type of blockchain-enabled institutiona mechanism allows users to create and execute agreements without intermediaries, reducing costs and increasing efficiency in various sectors such as finance, real estate and supply chain management.

Decentralised autonomous organisations (DAOs) (20 sources) are digital organisations represented by rules encoded as computer programs that operate transparently, controlled by members without central government influence (Drummer and Neumann, 2020) Unlike traditional organisations with hierarchical structures, DAOs enable decentralised decision-making and governance.

The subtype of blockchain use as institutional mechanisms include blockchain-enabled business models and strategies (27 sources), which leverage blockchain use into their existing business process to create new value, such as Steemit. This use may not only include the use of blockchain systems per se but also the use of information from these systems. Cheng et al. (2019) find that investors react positively in the short term to blockchain-related disclosures in firms’ Form 8-K filings. This positive reaction occurs even when the disclosure merely mentions vague information involving blockchain use

The last subtype is utility tokens (16 sources) which are functional units allowing users to perform specific actions on, or to obtain benefits from, the issuing platform. In the reviewed corpus, the discussion of this subtype of use serves as a fundraising mechanism, where initial coin offerings (ICOs) are the main topic. For example, Fisch (2019) explores the influential factors of ICO effectiveness.

The third type (Type III) of blockchain use reported in existing blockchain research can be characterised as blockchain use as digital infrastructure, in which blockchain functions primarily as a foundational layer providing essential digital capabilities for data prov enance, privacy preservation and decentralised storage for various applications and services. This type of blockchain use represents an industrial-level infrastructure. Based on the key drivers, we characterise three subtypes: provenance tracing systems (20 sources), privacy-preserving data management (13 sources) and interorganisational information infrastructure (26 sources).

## Conceptual framework values

Fig. 1<sup>2</sup> presents the framework of the current themes in socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use research. The review of the specific knowledge claims in Sections 4.3–4.7, recognising different types as needed, allows us to combine these themes into a comprehensive socio-technical framework of blockchain use. This serves as a platform providing a synthesis of interesting and informative knowledge (Leidner, 2018) on blockchain use phenomena in a coherent manner. Fig. 1 and the following sections present our framework and summarise current knowledge claims on blockchain use within the five themes and the relationships between the themes identified.

This framework presents the drivers (or barriers) that trigger (or hinder) the creation, adoption, diffusion and implementation of blockchain socio-technical systems. The reciprocal interaction between social and technical subsystems generates intended and un intended consequences. Values occupy a central position in this process, serving multiple critical functions. They help to justify drivers and barriers, are embedded within social subsystem(s) and are inherent in technical systems. Moreover, values provide a lens through which to assess the intended and unintended consequences of blockchain use. Values create a feedback loop that interconnects all other components, hence forming a novel, but important part of the framework.

## Embedded and inherent values

The existing literature frequently addresses the value of blockchain, but it is crucial to distinguish between two ontologically distinct types of technological value: embedded and inherent values. The embedded values of IT’ refers to instrumental and/or human-centric values intentionally incorporated into IT design, which manifest during use (Flanagan et al., 2008; van Wynsberghe and Moura, 2013). Technology affords developers the ability to embody values in IS (Heitlinger et al., 2021; van Wynsberghe and Moura, 2013). These values are then present in, or in this case, designed into, the blockchain artefacts. For example, NFTs are considered valuable as scarcity is designed into systems, opening the possibility for NFTs to serve as an asset class (White et al., 2022).

However, this concept has faced criticism for its socially deterministic view of technology (Winner, 1980). By borrowing Winner (1980) philosophical injunction of artifacts’ politics, we conceptualise inherent values as those intrinsically linked to artifacts’ core design and capabilities and cannot be easily separated or modified without compromising the technology itself. This perspective is particularly relevant to blockchain technology, where the fundamental requirements of distributed record keeping and consensus mechanisms inherently shape how actors must interact with and through the system. The inherent values of the technology constrain its flexibility in design and use (Winner, 1980). For example, the mathematical properties of prime numbers, Merkle trees and, hence, the possibility of encryption seems to ‘want to’ afford privacy (Beck et al., 2018; Helm, 2021; Kappert et al., 2021; Schellinger et al., 2022b). These properties are inherent, they are not human made but mathematical in nature. This recognition points to technologica agency but does not mean technological determinism.

The values of blockchain use are often surrounded by its key instrumental properties: transactability, autonomy, immutability, pseudonymity and a decentralised and distributed network (Iansiti and Lakhani, 2017). These values are based on meeting an organisational or social need to resolve critical issues, such as double spending, and enabling secure, decentralised transactions in a peer-to-peer network (Nakamoto, 2008). Consequently, blockchain’s ability to effectively address these challenges through its inherent features is what underpins its instrumental values (Dierksmeier and Seele, 2018). Another key value of blockchain use is typically associated with libertarianism (Lacity, 2022: Lichti and Tumasian, 2023), an ideology that emphasises the values of individual liberty and seeks minimal governmental intervention (Wolff. 1998). The decentralised design removes the need for a central authority to set the rules and play the governing role, with decentralised design interpreted as supporting justice and empowerment (Hoess et al., 2021).

Some values inherent in blockchain can conflict. Blockchain technology is intentionally designed for both transparency and privacy (Nakamoto, 2008), even though these values seem at odds with one another. In a blockchain-based system, such as Bitcoin, a balance is often struck between these two values by using a ledger that is distributed for transparency, although the ledger is pseudonymous for privacy (Amiri et al., 2021; Anderson et al., 2023; Renwick and Gleasure, 2021; Straubert et al., 2021; Yin et al., 2019). A distributed ledger with append-only logic is maintained by each network participant and is, hence, fully transparent – essentially, public knowledge for the sake of transparency (Amiram et al., 2022: Nakamoto. 2008). Privacy is implemented via the pseudonymity mechanism of blockchain, where stored information belongs to an address rather than an identified person (Yin et al., 2019). This design (of Bitcoin and other blockchain systems), to a degree, supports the simultaneous actualisation of public accountability (transparency) and user protection (privacy) (Yin et al., 2019), but the tension between these values remains.

In addition to these general values, other values are embodied and implied in specific blockchain types of blockchain use. For example, the proof-of-stake (PoS) consensus mechanism was specifically developed in response to green and sustainability concerns (Cong et al., 2021; de Vries and Stoll, 2021), thus altering the level of proof-of-work (PoW) high electricity consumption given that environmental friendliness is considered valuable (Saleh, 2021). Following Bitcoin’s scarcity value, for blockchain-based digital asset use (Type I) (emphasising transactability), the recognition of the power of blockchain for unique digital assets has led to the intro duction of NFTs (i.e., each token is unique, for instance, a token representing a piece of art) (Wang et al., 2021), with uniqueness and scarcity considered valuable.

## Value judgements

In IS. value judgements about IT use have traditionally focused on the outcomes that result from using IT (adapted from Kahli and Grover, 2008). That is, the value of IT is judged based on its economic (Melville et al., 2004; Schryen, 2013) and operational efficiency (Barua et al., 1995) and its increased business performance (Bryniolfsson and Hitt. 1996). However. in the context of blockchain use. value judgements are diversified among different social communities (Lacity, 2022; Miranda et al., 2022); therefore, the broader notion of value judgements (beyond economic and efficiency gains for the immediate user) is to be considered. Besides economic values, other values, such as privacy or justice, are used to evaluate blockchain use. In the existing literature, the value judgements of blockchain are multifaceted. While most studies judge the value of blockchain technology based on economic and efficiency values (Fisch, 2019), social justice and privacy values are also highly valued in certain types of blockchain use (Heitlinger et al., 2021). Hence, we broadly classify the knowledge claims from the reviewed papers into economic and non-economic (e.g., political, cultural and ecological) value judgements.

Economic value assessment regarding blockchain commonly focuses on perspectives such as business value, strategic value and efficiency value. These evaluation perspectives are consequently related to the economic benefits for individual blockchain users and the organisational performance of organisational blockchain adopters.

The economic value of blockchain use is measured differently based on the different types of use. For blockchain-based digital assets (Type I), the value is primarily derived from the creation of new economies and assets, such as cryptocurrencies, non-fungible tokens (NFTs) and token issuances (e.g., Chen et al., 2024). Cryptocurrency markets, for instance, offer arbitrage opportunities where individuals or organisations can profit by buying low and selling high. However, as financial instruments, cryptocurrency prices are notoriously difficult to predict. Petryk et al. (2023) find that project popularity metrics (watches) and user feedback (issues) are positively associated with price, while code corrections (pull requests) are negatively associated with price. In predicting the cryp tocurrency’s price, the existing literature finds that even individual signals (price forecast, price momentum, price direction, code upgrade, future versus past, etc.) were particularly effective predictors (Subramanian et al., 2024). However, this type of blockchain use is controversial, with some critics arguing that it resembles a Ponzi scheme (Gao and Leung, 2022).

In contrast. the economic value of Types II and II blockchain uses is often assessed by how the use enhances system efficiency and. consequently, improves organisational profit or performance. For example, the existing literature finds that blockchain use can create value by solving information asymmetry and increasing data integration (Wang et al., 2022a).

Non-economic value judgements are related to ethical, moral, social, cultural or environmental considerations, which are not primarily related to financial or economic considerations. The value of blockchain use has been assessed and judged from the perspective of social recognition (Mattke et al., 2019; Schwiderowski and Beck, 2023): effects on people (Guggenberger et al., 2021b); effects on the environment (Cong et al., 2021; de Vries and Stoll, 2021; Guggenberger et al., 2021b); legality (Benedict and Gill, 2022; Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Schellinger et al., 2022b; Schlatt et al., 2021); politics (Dallyn, 2014); privacy (Luo and Choi, 2022); open-source stances (Greenberg, 2014); and even (cultural) fascination with new and exciting technologies (Roth et al., 2022).

These diverse value judgements are influenced by several social and technical factors. Firstly, different value judgements arise within various discursive fields (Miranda et al., 2022), which represent conflicting views and varying public sensemaking of block chain use (Amadoru et al., 2021; Miranda et al., 2022; Perdana et al., 2021). Secondly, different narratives, and different groups and their perceptions lead to different judgements. For example, with regard to privacy, blockchain use is thought to provide a solution that is often valued as positive (Amiri et al., 2021; Carvalho, 2020; Cui et al., 2021; Folha et al., 2022; Helm, 2021; Sadeghib et al., 2022; Schlatt et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang and Chen, 2022). However, privacy may also lead to negative value judgements (e.g., supporting crime) (Babich and Hilary, 2020: Drummer and Neumann. 2020: Renwick and Gleasure. 2021: Toufaily et al., 2021: Zachariadis et al., 2019). Different types of blockchain use also influence blockchain judgements. For example, while many see per missioned blockchain use as a violation of what blockchain represents, others see it as providing more privacy or security (Toufail et al., 2021).

In summary, the uncommon theme of values has been added to our socio-technical framework for blockchain use. This theme is crucial, as blockchain technology is inherently value-laden with libertarian principles, decentralisation, privacy and transparency in its very design (e.g., Yin et al., 2019). The economic value assessments of blockchain applications present a complex and often conflicting landscape. This divergence stems from the interpretive flexibility of blockchain use and varving presumptions about what constitutes economic value. While many see economic value in blockchain-based assets, traditional investors, operating under the assumption that economic value must be grounded in tangible, underlying assets, may ascribe zero value to cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, likening them to Ponzi schemes (Gao and Leung, 2022). The same applies to NFTs’ comparable economic scrutiny (Pawelzik and Thies, 2022) However, practical applications in non-financial sectors, such as supply chain management, demonstrate tangible economic benefits In these contexts, blockchain-based systems enhance transparency and reduce transaction costs, thereby improving organisationa performance (Hanna et al., 2022; Hastig and Sodhi, 2020). Social value judgements on blockchain uses are equally diverse. Blockchain applications are often viewed as symbols of various social and societal values, such as uniqueness value (e.g., Wang et al., 2021), scarcity value (e.g., White et al., 2022) and sustainability value (e.g., Saleh, 2021). The perceived success of these blockchain uses is contingent upon their ability to deliver these values in practice. For instance, the promise of decentralisation in financial systems may be judged based on its actual impact on reducing wealth inequality or enhancing financial inclusion

## Task–technology fit

The drivers of blockchain use are context dependent. Generally, drivers are primarily associated with the task-technology fit. That is, drivers are affected by the nature or primary goals of their use scenarios and are related to the ‘fit’ of blockchain technology to the issues and tasks that need to be addressed in the relevant context (Liang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022a; Wenke et al., 2022; Zielonka et al., 2022). For example, fit was found to be an important driver in the financial sector but less of a driver in the medical sector (Liang et al., 2021).

Drivers of blockchain use are related to their inherent values. The existing literature investigates how blockchain technology can be adopted to help the evolution of existing business practices (Chong et al., 2019). The existing literature also investigates why blockchain is an attractive option for sectors that demand high levels of security and data integrity, such as supply chains (Wang et al., 2021), voting (Zhao et al., 2022) and insurances (Zhang et al., 2021). This adoption builds on the inherent values of blockchain technology: privacy, transparency and autonomy (Liang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022a)

Beyond instrumental values, blockchain use is also associated with libertarianism (due to its emphasis on decentralisation, transparency and privacy) which impacts its adoption and use. For example, studies find that libertarian political values affect users perceptions and adoption of cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin (Lichti and Tumasjan, 2023). Some suggest that a ‘Bitcoin ideology exists around ‘decentralisation, the empowerment of the individual, and a weaker role of intermediaries’ (Mattke et al., 2019, p. 265) which, even if not stated in the source, aligns with libertarianism. Privacy values might be an immediate driver of (or a barrier to) the adoption of blockchain technology. The use of blockchain, in general, or the selection of a particular blockchain, is often linked to users’ desires for privacy (Erler et al., 2022; Raddatz et al., 2023; Sadeghib et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a).

## Support of social symbols

Blockchain users are motivated by their beliefs and social symbols and how these align with the values attributed to blockchain. These drivers are highly related to blockchain uses as digital assets and institutional mechanisms. The literature also shows that value alignment in other aspects drives blockchain adoption. One example is a power shift between consumers and users (Li and Liu, 2022; Liu et al., 2022). The reason is that, on a blockchain-based platform, the roles of consumers, producers and owners may fundamentally change: consumers become consumer-owners, producers become producer-governance influencers and owners become sponsors by giving up ownership (Burda et al., 2022). Moreover, users may be motivated by the desire to have social capital reflected in tokens (i.e., in PoS blockchain systems, social networks and token ownership linked to decision-making power) (Liu et al., 2022). Blockchain use is generally regarded as novel and risky in many aspects (Ostern et al., 2022); hence, users’ appetite for risk may also drive (or hinder) blockchain use (Huangfu et al., 2022). Values can be interwoven, such in the investment sector, where economic return is often considered the key driver, Bitcoin investment can be motivated by libertarian values (Mattke et al., 2019).

Social symbol alignment depends not only on the objective features of blockchain use but also on narratives and discourses. Multiple narratives are woven and constructed around blockchain use and its inherent values, influencing those contributing to blockchain use or non-use (Miranda et al., 2022; Roth et al., 2022). Organisations are engaged in reframing, re-emphasising or recasting the values embedded in, or attributed to, blockchain use (Roth et al., 2022). Often, ethical concerns are inherent to these narratives and discourses (per the papers we reviewed), with a variety of different ethical perspectives presented, based on, among others, teleology (utilitarianism). deontology or contractarianism (Dierksmeier and Seele. 2018: Leung et al., 2023: Sharif and Gho: doosi, 2022).

The existing literature finds that support of social symbols of blockchain use often combines with other drivers, such as economic expectations, which are discussed next.

## Economic expectations

Blockchain use is often motivated by expectations of economic benefits. This motivation is justified by economic value judgements (in Section 4.3).

Multiple economic drivers are found for blockchain use. One emerges from the creation of new asset types (e.g., Chen et al., 2024; Chong et al., 2019) and new economic plavers and structures (Beck et al., 2018). Most of the literature examines the economic drivers of blockchain use in relation to cryptocurrencies. Users are engaging in new economic activities in cryptocurrency spaces, such as (crypto) trading for speculation and investment (Luo and Choi, 2022) or raising funds via initial coin offerings (ICOs) (Fisch, 2019). The volatile cryptocurrency prices—in turn influenced by the fluctuating interest in cryptocurrency (Griffin and Shams, 2020; Guo et al., 2021) and a perceived lack of intrinsic value—fuels rather than hinders users’ interest in speculative investments and rapid gains (Kim and Daidj, 2021).

Instead of depending on centralised authorities, decentralised exchanges operate without a central authority, facilitating peer-to-peer trading via smart contracts.

## Clash of paradigms

Blockchain adoption is hindered by several barriers. One of the barriers comprises the adoption challenges faced by blockchain, as blockchain use is still viewed with mindsets shaped by centralised paradigms and potential users are faced with a lack of understanding and knowledge (Wenke et al., 2022). As a result, some studies in the reviewed papers propose that, instead of detailing the technical aspects of blockchain, it is more beneficial for most audiences to focus on practical guidance for its use (Lacity, 2022, p. 7). The ex pectations and evaluation criteria developed for centralised paradigms do not always translate well to decentralised paradigms, leading to perceived shortcomings in blockchain-based solutions and resulting in limited adoption. For instance, Chen and Llovd (2021) find, in China's developing carbon markets, that political concerns about loss of centralised control and organisational diffi. culties in coordinating decentralised systems are two current barriers. Similarly, this clash is further exemplified by the absence of lega and regulatory frameworks adapted to decentralised paradigms, creating uncertainties surrounding data ownership in a shared ledger system (Baytas¸ et al., 2022; Chen and Lloyd, 2021).

The clash between decentralised blockchain systems and centralised mindsets can lead to inappropriate standards for evaluating blockchain’s performance. For example, Hinzen et al. (2022) find that Bitcoin faces limited adoption due to negative network effects arising from its need for consensus and network delays. These lead to prohibitively long transaction settlement times as the network expands, driving users away from the system, and creating high entry barriers for users (Malik et al., 2021; Toufaily et al., 2021). Other technical challenges include scalability concerns (Drummer and Neumann. 2020: Hinzen et al., 2022: Malik et al., 2022: Toufaily et al.. 2021; Wenke et al., 2022; Zachariadis et al., 2019). However, these characteristics are inherent to blockchain’s decentralised, consensus-based nature. The misaligned criteria fail to account for the trade-offs between centralisation and decentralisation. potentially leading to limited adoptions of blockchain use.

As occurs with many new technologies, scepticism about the hype associated with blockchain use underlies many of the barriers to adoption. This scepticism is evident in the social challenges of insufficient understanding and distrust, economic concerns about high costs and uncertain returns, and technological doubts about blockchain’s capabilities (Chen and Lloyd, 2021). Due to the immutability of transactions, concerns arise that errors cannot be corrected (Toufaily et al., 2021). Additionally, ethical concerns are raised, and there is a lack of trust in autonomous machine decision-making (Schwiderowski and Beck, 2023). Potential adopters also have res ervations about using blockchain due to concerns over data privacy and security, technical maturity and trust in the consensus mechanism (Toufaily et al., 2021).

In summary, as alluded to above, the barriers to blockchain adoption are not simply technical or social in isolation but emerge from the ongoing reciprocal interaction between these domains. Technical features influence social perceptions and practices, while socia factors shape technical development and implementation strategies.

## Social Subsystem(s)

The existing literature provides several insights on the organisational and social aspects of socio–technical phenomena involving blockchain use. These social subsystem(s) are commonly (in socio-technical analysis) divided into structures and people.

## Structures

The knowledge claims on organisational structure around blockchain use can be summarised under three major sub-themes: governance of blockchain use, governance via blockchain use and supportive use context.

Table 6 presents an overview of the knowledge claims on blockchain use structure and the corresponding sources.

First, governance of blockchain technology refers to a set of mechanisms designed to ensure the delivery of blockchain capabilities to organisations (Zachariadis et al., 2019) or, to state this bluntly, dedicated to ‘taming algorithmic power’ (Mini et al., 2021, p. 2). In blockchain use, consensus is an important concept, with decisions made through two forms of consensus. First, consensus mechanisms (encoded protocols used to determine which node has the right to write the next block) ensure network consensus (all nodes agreeing on the blockchain’s current state). Second, there is community or developer consensus, which refers to social agree ment within the developer community (e.g., in open-source projects, decisions about the direction of the code are often made based on majority agreement, though governance structures can vary) (Beck et al., 2018; Nakamoto, 2008; Ziolkowski et al., 2020).

The governance of blockchain use in the use stage requires the cooperation of developers in the design stage (Beck et al., 2018; Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Zachariadis et al., 2019). It also needs to consider organisational objectives (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019). Three aspects of governance design are commonly regarded as being crucial to IT governance (Beck et al., 2018; Burda et al., 2022; Zachariadis et al., 2019): (1) decision rights (e.g., top-down, bottom-up); (2) control mechanisms (informal or formal); and (3) incentive structures (for producers and users). More specifically, in the case of blockchain use, three specific aspects need to be considered: (1) membership (e.g., users’ rights); (2) ownership (e.g., digital representation of legal rights); and (3) the issue of immutability versus reversals (e.g., restoring a wrongly transferred asset) (Ziolkowski et al., 2020).

Second, governance via blockchain use refers to using blockchain technology as a governance mechanism itself (Mini et al., 2021). It can enforce agreements and support cooperation and coordination (Lumineau et al., 2021); change the power hierarchy (Burda et al., 2022; Yermack, 2017); and reshape the regulation relationship (Gozman et al., 2020).

Although governance via blockchain use is always considered fully decentralised, the degree of decentralisation in blockchainbased systems is actually not a fixed property (Mueller-Bloch et al., 2022). Instead, it is a dynamic characteristic influenced by complex interactions between technological design, economic incentives and participant behaviours. Technological design elements include access to decision rights, process visibility, protocol automation, incentives for developers/miners and incentives for other stakeholders (Zhang and Ramesh, 2023). Jacob et al. (2024) find that even in the case of Bitcoin—long considered the archetype of decentralised systems—economic forces can lead to the concentration of mining power among a small group of well-resourced par: ticipants over time.

Blockchain enables the creation of decentralised autonomous organisations (DAOs) which are non-hierarchical and governed via blockchains (Hsieh and Vergne, 2023). These digital, algorithmic organisations govern contractual and business arrangements via selfenforcing and self-executing smart contracts (i.e., storing coded contracts on blockchain use) (Chong et al., 2019: Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Hsieh and Vergne, 2023; Mini et al., 2021; Schar ¨ et al., 2022). Although no central authority exists for blockchainbased platforms or projects, DAO developers can be viewed as a ‘benevolent dictatorship’, as they are digitising governance rules (Beck et al., 2018; Saadatmand et al., 2019). In many instances, DAOs make strategic and operational decisions via voting (Zhao et al., 2022). As governance mechanisms, DAOs are not perfect forms of organisation, as proven by various crises (community disagreement resulting in forking or resetting the DAO) (Zachariadis et al. 2019). Several critical issues around DAOs are still awaiting solutions including scalability, openness and security, as well as several tensions between interoperability and standards, liability and resilience, and transparency and privacy (Zachariadis et al., 2019).

Tokenisation, in the context of blockchain use, is also an important governance mechanism for organisations (or industries) (Sup et al., 2023). Tokenisation describes the process of converting rights to an asset into a digital token on a blockchain (Augustin et al., 2023a). It may inyolve smart contracts that can emit tokens to users, which in turn are empowered to cast their ballots' (Willrich et al. 2021, p. 13). Tokenisation introduces a new approach to raising money that has drawn attention across industries: initial coin offerings (ICOs) (Tapscott and Tapscott, 2017). Two types of ICOs, based on their backed assets, are utility tokens (buving their products and services) and equity tokens (becoming a shareholder or co-owner) (Gan et al., 2021; Shakhnov and Zaccaria, 2023). Issuing utility tokens allows for price discrimination in markets with network effects (Shakhnov and Zaccaria, 2023). As a fundraising avenue, combining output presale with incremental revenue sharing, issuing tokens can match the performance of traditional equity and debt financing in maximising profits, and can outperform equity in mitigating agency costs under conditions of moral hazard (Malinova and Park, 2023). The success of ICOs is influenced by ICO design (Gan et al., 2023); the technical characteristics of blockchain use; the business to which the ICO refers; and external factors. Relevant blockchain use aspects in this context are its mining mechanics (Holden and Malani, 2022), technical quality (Fisch, 2019), asset nature (Gan et al., 2021), sustainable orientation (Mansouri and Momtaz, 2022) and organisational performance (Zielonka et al., 2022). External factors include the overall environment (Bellavitis et al., 2022; Gan et al., 2021) and ratings from analysts (Barth et al., 2023).

Third, blockchain needs adaptations in and to the wider use context, with a supportive organisational context a necessity for successful blockchain use (Du et al., 2019). A supportive structure ensures that the adopted technology serves its strategic imperatives rather than becoming a stand-alone project with little integration or impact (Ostern et al., 2022). To be successful, the organisational context and capabilities need to align with blockchain use (Hastig and Sodhi, 2020). The relevant aspects of organisational context are the following: organisational culture (Roth et al., 2022); networks (Prockl et al., 2022); objectives (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019; L et al., 2023); strategies (Ostern et al., 2022); and configurations (Saadatmand et al., 2019).

To achieve a supportive organisational context for blockchain use, organisations use different strategies. To achieve a cultural fit between blockchain use and organisations, organisations adjust their strategies based on the narratives around blockchain use, including dropping, reframing, reimplementing, emphasising or recasting the values they see in the use of the technology (Roth et al., 2022). Organisational strategies towards the adoption of blockchain use may involve the transition from exploring use cases to becoming a technology leader (via blockchain), corresponding to changing resource allocation from (one-off) task forces to a (per manent) dedicated team (Ostern et al., 2022). While blockchain-based platforms are, in principle, based on horizontal (flat, peer-to peer) structures, blockchain developers and platforms become de facto leaders or owners, hence re-introducing vertical structures (Saadatmand et al., 2019).

The regulation of blockchain used to ensure compliance with the existing legal framework is another topic in the existing literature. For example, the lack of a regulatory framework regarding accounting practices for cryptocurrencies can lead to significant in consistencies and distortions in financial reporting across firms, potentially misleading users in assessing firms’ asset values, liquidity profitability and cash-generating abilities (Luo and Yu, 2024).

People’s perceptions are highly influenced by their values and impacting on how blockchain use is assessed (Lichti and Tumasjan, 2023). For example, persona political values have direct effects on how Bitcoin affordances (decentralised self-reliance, verification, value creation and identity protection) are evaluated and, thus, on user adoption (Lichti and Tumasjan, 2023).

In exploring user behaviours in the socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use, the existing literature found a herding effect that can be effectively reduced by technical assurance and blockchain management (Cai et al., 2024). These herding behaviours are more apparent among users with low identifiability (Cai et al., 2024). Bot involvement is positively associated with users’ engagement with blockchain-based online social media (Delkhosh et al., 2023). On a blockchain-based platform, a user’s trust in blockchain technology transfers to other users (Shao et al., 2022). Further, the use of blockchain may further influence users’ emotions. For example, in the charity sector, due to the use of blockchain-based smart contracts, donors experience a heightened sense of immediacy and ongoing engagement with causes as their donations are automatically triggered by real-world events rather than being forgotten after a one-time transaction (Bidwell et al., 2021).

Organisational adopters require blockchain-skilled experts and/or experienced leaders for successful blockchain use. The existing literature finds that the most valuable skills for successful blockchain use are related to blockchain development and cryptocurrency experience (Ge et al., 2022). Organisational adopters are more likely to succeed if their leaders have interdisciplinary experience in different contexts (Hojckova et al., 2020). Similarly, in start-ups, the professional experience of the founders is associated with a higher likelihood of developing new market applications for blockchain (Omezzine and Bodas Freitas, 2022). External consulting support is positively related to the survival of blockchain ventures (Grau et al., 2021).

The attitudes of blockchain users towards blockchain are generally very supportive (many even become blockchain evangelists). For example, in the case of Bitcoin, users normally have a high level of tolerance for transaction costs (Ilk et al., 2021). Similarly, users are willing to pay an entrance fee to ensure a baseline level of security in a blockchain payment system (He et al., 2023a). However, when transaction fees become very high during blockchain congestion (with the number of pending transactions exceeding blockchain capacity), some users may forgo blockchain transactions (Sokolov, 2021).

Second, non-users lack experience in using blockchain and may be more sceptical. In light of the reviewed literature, relevant nonusers include regulators, researchers, analysts and commentators in the media and on social media (e.g., Barth et al., 2023; Gozman et al., 2020; Miranda et al., 2022; Raddatz et al., 2023). Although they do not directly use blockchain, their judgements, actions and decisions will directly or indirectly influence others’ decisions regarding their use of blockchain. Non-users pay closer attention to the risks of blockchain use (Chen and Lloyd, 2021).

The existing literature suggests that the attitudes of non-users towards blockchain and the reasons for their scepticism or rejection of it are varied. Non-users’ perceptions of blockchain use are influenced by the complexity of the technology. Per the reviewed literature, when service providers wish to adopt blockchain-based systems in banking or accounting, non-users (customers or clients) often refuse or show reluctance to use blockchain for reasons of complexity; they are resistant to change or find existing systems to be more user-friendly (Raddatz et al., 2023; Seethamraju and Dyball, 2023). Other non-users are primarily concerned with privacy issues (not technical complexity) (Raddatz et al., 2023).

In a competitive market, blockchain non-users might lose competitive advantage by not being able to provide the information transparency expected by their customers (Bauer et al., 2022). Due to its unique technical nature, blockchain use is considered to benefit the market by providing more authentic and reliable product information (Wang et al., 2022d). For example, in the retail industry, blockchain use can increase the volume of purchases and the number of buyers (Ying et al., 2023). Consumer trust reviews and the traceability of international origins increase when blockchain is used (Ying et al., 2023).

Regulators are also often not experienced with blockchain, with this possibly explaining their laissez-faire attitude towards blockchain regulation and their neglect of regulatory changes (Renwick and Gleasure, 2021). Due to the lack of a central authority in blockchain use, regulators may need to engage with developers of the corresponding networks for regulation purposes (Renwick and Gleasure, 2021).

Some forms of influence and participation in blockchain use are shared by both users and non-users. Both groups, in general, even users, lack a deep understanding of blockchain (Raddatz et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022c). Both groups may impact the diffusion of blockchain use via comments in the media and on social media regarding their interpretation, (non–)legitimation, sensemaking and conduct in relation to blockchain (Amadoru et al., 2021). Miranda et al. (2022) emphasise that this contribution is not at an individual level, but rather at a discursive field level. Discursive fields, including those with low indirect engagement (non-users) in blockchain, contribute to blockchain diffusion through diverse framing, bridging perspectives, foreshadowing adoption, imitating ideas, expanding discourse, increasing coherence, explicating knowledge and promoting community learning about blockchain (Miranda et al., 2022).

## Technical Subsystem(s)

The existing literature also sheds light on the technical subsystem(s) of socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. These subsystem(s) are commonly divided into technology and tasks.

## Technology

Originally, the concept of blockchain was based on the description of how data and transactions were recorded in the Bitcoin blockchain system (Nakamoto 2008). To solve the double-spending problem, Nakamoto uses cryptography and a timestamp server, which “works by taking a hash of a block of items to be timestamped and widely publishing the hash … Each timestamp includes the previous timestamp in its hash, forming a chain, with each additional timestamp reinforcing the ones before it” (p. 2). Although the paper by Nakamoto (2008) does not directly use the term blockchain, it is widely accepted as the origin of the concept of a blockchain. Nakamoto describes five properties of the initial blockchain concept: transactability, autonomy, immutability, pseudonymity, and a decentralised and distributed network.

We organise key findings into three sub-themes: consensus mechanisms, forking and (other) technical concerns. Table 8 presents an overview of knowledge claims on the technical attributes of blockchain, along with corresponding sources.

First, consensus mechanisms are at the core of blockchain systems (Rossi et al., 2019; Zachariadis et al., 2019. They refer to the encoded rules of verification (especially which node has the right to write the next block) (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019; Nakamoto, 2008). It is the consensus mechanisms that remove the need for a central authority and intermediaries (Renwick and Gleasure, 2021). While they are used to establish consensus in a network of users (each node is typically both a computer and a human), they are primarily ‘technological’ consensus devices (code). As above, this is in contrast to the ‘social’ developer consensus, which refers to community and social agreements on the code and design direction among developers, typically in accordance with democratic principles, such as voting (e.g., Saleh, 2021).

The most studied consensus protocols in the existing literature are proof-of-work (PoW) and proof-of-stake (PoS). PoW requires participants (miners) to solve complex mathematical puzzles to validate transactions and create new blocks (Nakamoto, 2008). This process consumes significant computational power and electricity. Bitcoin is the most famous example of a PoW blockchain. In contrast, PoS selects validators in proportion to their stake (amount of cryptocurrency held) in the network (Saleh, 2021). Validators are incentivised to act honestly, as they have something at stake (Saleh, 2021). Ethereum is the most famous example of a PoS blockchain (as of 2024).

Different consensus mechanisms result in different blockchain properties. Compared to PoW, PoS blockchain use is less energy consuming (Saleh, 2021) and typically more efficient with regard to optimal block time, average settlement time, average settlement fees and total settlement lag (Li et al., 2023). Beyond PoW and PoS, other consensus mechanisms include the practical Byzantine fault tolerance mechanism (e.g., Wang et al., 2022a). Some researchers claim that PoW and PoS result in the Matthew effect of concen tration, wherein the rich get richer (Di Francesco Maesa et al., 2018). However, other studies suggest that PoS systems achieve longterm convergence and stability (Mueller-Bloch et al., 2022; Ros¸u and Saleh, 2021). Shifting back towards the social features of sociotechnical systems, committee-based consensus mechanisms with approval voting can boost efficiency and resilience when enough participants are actively involved (Benhaim et al., 2023).

Consensus mechanisms have been criticised for their inefficiencies and, in the case of PoW, for their massive energy consumption. With the increasing transactions and users, some blockchain networks may experience increased network delays and extended time required for consensus generation (Hinzen et al., 2022; Shang et al., 2023). This can result in extended transaction settlement times, leading to user attrition and limited system adoption (Hinzen et al., 2022). Mining pools and arms races by miners may significantly

The quality of blockchain systems, such as software systems, depends on their code quality (De Rossi et al., 2022a; Pagnotta, 2020). Many suggestions have been made on how to improve the code of, for example, the consensus mechanism. These suggestions focus on better fee-setting mechanisms (Basu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2024), fairer miner rewards in PoW blockchains (Qin et al., 2019) and improved token-weighted voting mechanisms (Tsoukalas and Falk, 2020)

Regarding accessibility, blockchains can be either permissionless (public, peer-to-peer) or permissioned (private, consortiumbased) (Toufaily et al., 2021). Permissionless blockchains are open to all participants and allow anyone to contribute to the code and network, whereas permissioned blockchains are restricted to a certain group of participants, with participants possibly having unequal rights (Toufaily et al., 2021).

Permissioned blockchains are found to be more attractive to incumbent organisations, as they can adjust the consensus protocols as they see fit, facilitate more cost-effective transactions, enhance confidentiality and reduce the threat of attacks (Lacity, 2022; Toufaily et al., 2021). Hence, permissioned blockchain are the preferred type of blockchain use in areas such as health care (Anderson et al., 2022; Erler et al., 2022) and supply chains (e.g., George, 2022; Sodhi et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2021).

Second, research has focused on forks, splits in the blockchain and/or code base. Forks result from situations in which blockchain communities fail to reach a consensus and two different streams are created, which typically means both the split of the blockchain and the community (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019; Faqir-Rhazoui et al., 2021; Overhage and Widjaja, 2022). In a fork, a blockchain bi furcates into two different blockchains, of which one may follow new rules or implement a new protocol while the other follows the old rules. The term forking means to ‘support and reproduce radically new objectives that are incompatible with existing capabilities’ (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019, p. 1249). The reasons contributing to the creation of a fork may be social or technical, such as a change in contextual conditions (Andersen and Bogusz, 2019), information delays (Biais et al., 2019), software upgrades (Biais et al., 2019) and increasing transaction rates (Hinzen et al., 2022). One example of forking is the split between Bitcoin and Bitcoin Cash due to philosophical and technical disagreements on network capacity (BitcoinCash, 2023); i.e., the ‘main’ line, Bitcoin, focused on maintaining the original design so as not to undermine credibility, while the ‘split’ line or fork, Bitcoin Cash, focused on increasing block size, hence transaction processing, in line with an ideal of financial inclusion.

Third, despite its ground-breaking achievements, blockchain technology faces various other technical concerns (Easley et al., 2019; Madnick, 2020). Similar to other IT, blockchain use is threatened by individual computer-based (phishing) and network-based (intrusion) vulnerabilities (Guo et al., 2021). However, fixed costs and specialised mining hardware in PoW blockchain use create downward rigidity in mining power and increase the cost of double-spending (51 %) attacks, thereby enhancing security (Garratt and van Oordt, 2023). Beyond this, the inherent limit to scalability is the most widely criticised flaw of blockchain technology (Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Malik et al., 2022; Toufaily et al., 2021; Wenke et al., 2022; Zachariadis et al., 2019). To solve the scalability problem, different approaches have been proposed. For example, Layer 2 technology has been developed to interface with the firstlayer infrastructure without modifying the core protocol (Cong et al., 2023; De Rossi et al., 2022b). In research on data markets based on blockchain use, two primary challenges are highlighted: ensuring verified data provenance and establishing incentives for genuine participation (Parra-Moyano et al., 2021). Finally, blockchain use may be threatened by the development of quantum computing (Kappert et al., 2021) (see Section 5).

The first task of blockchain use is to create a shared truth. The existing literature suggests that blockchain use promotes the creation of a shared truth based on blockchain’s key properties of decentralisation and immutability (Lacity and Van Hoek, 2021; Zachariadis et al., 2019). Shared truth replaces trust in a central authority, powerful intermediaries (Zachariadis et al., 2019) and third parties (Hsieh et al., 2018), with trust instead in algorithms. The shared truth is to address power imbalance (Avital, 2024).

This shared truth can be used to solve common business challenges in the digital era in new ways. Generally, it ensures the reli ability and auditability of data (e.g., Wang et al., 2021). Blockchain-based systems can address trust issues in different scenarios, including payment systems (Carvalho and Karimi, 2021), video games (Carvalho, 2021), energy trading (Islam et al., 2022) and survey systems (Zhang and Chen, 2022). Blockchain use also promotes new solutions for the self-sovereignty of digital identities (Smethurst, 2023).

The second task of blockchain use is to provide transparency. As Sarker et al. (2021) emphasise, a key capability of blockchain use is its ability to provide traceability by increasing data integrity through transparency. Transparency can facilitate the resolution of issues caused by information asymmetry, which exists in both inter-organisational (e.g., Bauer et al., 2022) and intra-organisational (e.g., Sarker et al., 2021) contexts. Hence, blockchain is used to prevent fraud in, for example, car sales (Chanson et al., 2019), taxes (Cho et al., 2021) and voting (Helm, 2021). Blockchain is also used to support scientific contribution measuring systems (Adams et al., 2023; Avital, 2024; Mohan, 2019), crowdworking systems (Amiri et al., 2021), food delivery systems (Folha et al., 2022) and pay-per-use businesses (Beverungen et al., 2021). However, as above, it may not equally benefit all participants (Dong et al., 2022).

Transparency can be used to track provenance, which refers to the history of an item, for example, the history of its ownership (George, 2022). Initially important for artworks and museums, provenance is now used for tracking the source and history of various products (George, 2022). Using blockchain to trace the provenance of virtual products (e.g., items in games) can benefit both the developer and the consumer (Carvalho, 2021; Tan, 2022). Provenance can also be used to meet the transparency expectations of ethical consumption (George, 2022).

The next key task of blockchain use is to keep privacy. According to the existing literature, blockchain, by keeping data and in formation confidential, is considered and used in industries with a high requirement for privacy. For example, blockchain use is considered as an approach to ensure voter privacy (Helm, 2021; Willrich et al., 2021), privacy in the context of supply chains (Wang et al., 2021), patient privacy (Anderson et al., 2023: Anderson et al., 2022) and the privacy of insurance clients (Zhang, W. et al., 2021) However, although challenging for privacy, blockchain can still offer some level of traceability (Kappert et al., 2021)

Blockchain can only provide a shared truth with transparency, as well as privacy, in relation to digital information. It cannot link digital information to offline scenarios per se (Tucker and Catalini, 2018). While offline scenarios and physical products can be represented in blockchain use, blockchain cannot directly control the link or the physical product (Tucker and Catalini, 2018). For example. NFT-to-asset links are very fragile, off-chain assets may not be accessible or there may be duplications (Wang et al., 2023b). The suitability of blockchain technology has been questioned in other use cases, such as token-weighted voting, where users may not fully understand the voting mechanism (Tsoukalas and Falk, 2020).

The existing literature finds that blockchain technology is mainly used as a complementary technology and is embedded into incumbent systems (Chanson et al., 2019; Iansiti and Lakhani, 2017; Lumineau et al., 2021; Malik et al., 2021; Sadeghib et al., 2022).

## Intended consequences

Most of the intended consequences of blockchain use are aligned with the drivers discussed above, that is, users generally achieving what they meant to achieve (hence this section is brief). Blockchain use generates intended consequences as expected through these drivers, such as the tech revolution and the creation of decentralised economies.

Blockchain use as digital assets (Type I) creates new economic markets, such as cryptocurrency markets, NFT markets and nove derivative markets (Allen et al., 2020). In addition to spot markets that facilitate the direct, immediate trading of blockchain-based digital assets, such as Bitcoin, derivatives markets have also been created. These markets encompass both spot trading of blockchain-based digital assets (e.g., Bitcoin) and derivatives trading, where financial contracts derive value from underlying blockchain assets. These new markets further contribute to the extension of the sharing economy (Tapscott and Tapscott, 2016). These markets are influenced by other economic markets: For example, Augustin et al. (2023b) find that the introduction of Bitcoin—US dollar (USD) futures on regular stock markets impact on Bitcoin spot markets.

Blockchain use as an institutional mechanism (Type II) can enhance business value for organisational adopters. They are considered to have the potential to improve the efficiency (e.g, Chiu et al., 2023) and performance of the (business) processes they support (Sun et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022). Empirical evidence suggests that financial markets respond positively to blockchain-related an nouncements (Cheng et al., 2019; Klockner ¨ et al., 2022).

Blockchain use as data infrastructure (Type III) creates a new paradigm of decentralised, transparent, and immutable data-keeping and information exchange. This has led to innovations in fields such as finance, supply chain management and identity verification. Existing research indicates that organisations leveraging blockchain as data infrastructure often outperform non-adopting competitors (Paul et al., 2022).

## Unintended consequences

The unintended consequences of blockchain use are related to unexpected, often long-term effects.

Considering blockchain use as digital assets (Type I), due to the high value of, for example, Bitcoin, arms races by miners have significantly increased the energy consumption of PoW blockchain use (Cong et al., 2021). Concerns also exist that cryptocurrencies are not having the effect of empowering non-bank users or disempowering banks but are, instead, benefiting a select few individuals while creating new inequalities (Di Francesco Maesa et al., 2018; Gao and Leung, 2022). Blockchain and smart contracts have also resulted in tensions between their technical rules and existing legal frameworks (Drummer and Neumann, 2020).

Blockchain technology has the potential to improve corporate information disclosure when using blockchain as an institutional mechanism (Type II). However, under certain conditions, it can lead to a mixed-adoption equilibrium where firms’ adoption decisions and blockchain’s information generation capabilities are impaired, potentially resulting in decreased overall information quality in the market, compared to that of traditional disclosure methods (Franke et al., 2024).

As a medium, blockchain can change people’s behaviours. In some cases, these changes are considered negative. For example, in the discussion of blockchain use in accounting, one concern is that the automation and real-time nature of blockchain could unin tentionally create a separation between users and their financial records. This could potentially reduce human engagement in the accounting process and limit the development of collective memory and shared understanding that were fostered by traditiona bookkeeping practices (Matringe and Power, 2024). In addition, for token rewards, users may produce more blog posts without concern for the quality of posts and impair long-term user creativity, such as on Steemit (Chen et al., 2023a). In other cases, unexpected benefits are found. For example, ICOs and tokenisation not only provide financing to start-ups (as intended) but also indirectly reduce moral hazard among founders and investors (Chod et al., 2022; Cui et al., 2023).

## Research agenda

In our analysis, we found that the existing literature on socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use, as we frame it here, provides relevant and interesting insights, as summarised above. However, considering the reviewed studies, research remains lacking in several aspects. With the above theoretical framework that organises current themes in socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use research, we are able to identify what is lacking in the prior literature and propose a corresponding research agenda for future IS blockchain research. This future research agenda is constructed to advance IS knowledge and is based on the recognition that socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use may challenge assumptions, theories, models, solutions, constructs or re lationships in the existing IS literature (Rai, 2017).

## Values

The discussion on embedded or inherent values of blockchain is essential as the distinction between these two ontological understandings of IT values influences the conceptualisation of blockchain technology. If decentralisation, often associated with liber tarianism, is considered an inherent value, then blockchain technologies incorporating centralised elements may be excluded from the definition. In this case, blockchain technology is inherently value-laden, embodying principles such as libertarianism, decentralisation, privacy and transparency in its design (e.g., Yin et al., 2019). This definition would challenge whether centralised and PoS blockchain uses are really ‘blockchain’. Conversely, if decentralisation is viewed as an embedded (not inherent) value, centralised and PoS blockchain uses could still be classified as ‘blockchain’. Under this ontology, decentralisation is not an inherent value of blockchain systems, but an embedded value for a desired outcome (Mueller-Bloch et al., 2022). This ontological divergence underlies the ongoing debate surrounding blockchain design and the evolving discourse on what constitutes blockchain technology. This deeper, ontologicallevel theorising is largely missing in the existing blockchain research. Consequently, the values of blockchain socio-technical systems need to be re-examined, distinguishing between embedded and inherent values. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are for example:

What are the intrinsic values of blockchain technology? How do values manifest in different blockchain socio-technical systems? What values emerge in socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use?

From an IS perspective, the discussion on embedded and inherent values of IT, IS and digital phenomena in general is also missing (Leidner and Kayworth, 2006). Investigating the values associated with blockchain technology, socio-technical systems and related phenomena can serve as a starting point for IS research to become a reference discipline in discussions of IT value. Traditionally. assessments of IT values have relied heavily on theories from economics, psychology and management disciplines, with IT values often considered to be embedded rather than inherent. In the existing IS literature, theories are lacking that could provide a powerfu explanation as to why diverse values are used to judge IT values. This provides opportunities to generate new theories. Hence, further worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

What constitutes the inherent values of IT? How do the embedded values of IT interact with its inherent values? What types of technologies have inherent values?

From a socio-technical perspective, a third, connected research opportunity is to explore the interplay between embedded values from social groups and values inherent in technology. Our framework emphasises that the values of blockchain systems are a function of both social and technical subsystems, and those interact. For example. in this case, one might wonder if blockchain is, in its inherent nature, libertarian and/or made libertarian by design choices and user behaviours, or how else these interactions and agencies can be unpacked. This is an issue that applies beyond blockchain. Hence, a further worthwhile research question to ask is as follows:

How do values from technical subsystem(s) interact with those from social subsystem(s)?

## Drivers and barriers

The first opportunity here is to develop theories on blockchain adoption that can provide a powerful explanation. Traditiona technology adoption models (e.g., the technology acceptance model [TAM]) (Davis et al., 1989) are sometimes not effective enough to explore the drivers of, or barriers to, socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. Our study’s literature review reveals that blockchain uses exhibit interpretive flexibility among different users. Users often perceive blockchain and its use as being beyond the scope of a mere technological artefact. Cryptocurrencies, for instance, are frequently analysed through financial rather than tech nological lenses, likening them to assets such as gold or stocks (e.g., Lacity, 2022; Liu et al., 2022; Mattke et al., 2019). This observation suggests that conventional IT adoption models from a technological perspective may be insufficient to fully capture the nuances of blockchain adoption and use. Our above analysis identifies three primary types of blockchain use in the existing literature. This multifaceted nature of blockchain use necessitates a more nuanced approach to studying its adoption and use. Future research should prioritise users’ understanding of blockchain use, as the diverse perceptions and interpretations of blockchain use can have varying drivers towards adoption. Hence, looking forward, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

How do current diverse perceptions of blockchain impact the validity of IT adoption models in blockchain research? In what ways can interpretive flexibility influence the development of a comprehensive model for studying blockchain use across its different categories? Is it more effective to develop distinct models for each category of blockchain use, or can a unified model adequately address the nuances of these different uses?

The second opportunity is to reconsider and re-examine the concepts in the existing IT adoption theories. Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are two major TAM constructs (Davis et al., 1989) used to explain and predict IT adoption. Perceived ease of use is defined as ‘the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort.’ (Davis et al., 1989, p. 320). Recent studies find, in unproblematic alignment with the TAM, that one of the reasons for Bitcoin adoption is the ease of acquisition (Mattke et al., 2019). However, this perception contrasts sharply with the inherent complexity of blockchain technology which as sumes understanding of cryptography (Nakamoto, 2008) (e.g., understanding the importance of ownership of keys etc). This discrepancy challenges our conventional understanding of user perceptions as valid proxies. Future research needs to reconsider and re-examine this understanding. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask here are as follows:

How do users' perceptions of ease of use/acquisition' in blockchain technologies differ from those in traditional IT systems? How can we develop more nuanced measures of ‘perceived ease of use’ in complex technological ecosystems like blockchain? How do Bitcoin users' perceptions of its ease of use/acquisition align with the actual. complex technological challenges?

The third opportunity lies in addressing the lack of research on drivers and barriers specific to various types of blockchain use. Existing literature primarily focuses on exploring factors influencing blockchain-based digital assets (Type I), while other types of blockchain use remain under-studied. To address this gap, several worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

What are the distinct drivers for, and barriers to, blockchain applications in specific use scenarios such as autonomous executable programming, supply chain management, confidential information transfer, etc.? Are certain drivers or barriers unique to specific blockchain use types and, if so, what factors contribute to this specificity? How does the interplay between technical complexity and perceived benefits influence organisational decisions regarding blockchain adoption across different use cases?

Investigating these questions would provide valuable insights into the nuanced landscape of blockchain adoption and imple mentation across various domains.

## Socio-technical systems

One problematic issue in the existing literature we reviewed is that many papers have black-boxed the technical nature of blockchain and treat blockchain as generic IT, neglecting its unique properties. This black box approach is problematic (Agarwal and Lucas, 2005; Babich and Hilary, 2020; Benbasat and Zmud, 2003; Sarker et al., 2019), as it is inadequate for producing new theories to illuminate the blockchain context in IS research. It is hard to provide suitable blockchain knowledge without accurately defining or considering the relevant specific technical foundations (Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Rossi et al., 2019).

The first opportunity is to examine the governance structures in blockchain use. One implicit assumption in most prior research has been that blockchain systems follow a decentralised structure. However, different blockchain uses have different levels of decen tralisation, with these influenced by complex interactions between technological design, economic incentives and participant be haviours. The view of blockchain systems as purely decentralised structures is an oversimplification that warrants further investigation. To address this gap, we propose two key areas for future research. Firstly, a comprehensive taxonomy needs to be developed to help us analyse the various forms of decentralisation in blockchain systems and their consequences. This taxonomy should account for the nuanced differences in governance structures across different blockchain implementations. Secondly, re searchers should investigate how specific technological design choices, economic incentive mechanisms and participant behaviours impact the level of decentralisation in blockchain systems. These studies would provide valuable insights into appropriate decen tralisation in practice, potentially leading to more effective blockchain governance models. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

In the context of blockchain use, how decentralisation is conceptualised? What are the main types of decentralisations in block chain systems and how do they differ? How do technology design, economic incentives, and user behaviour affect decentralisation in blockchains?

The second opportunity is to explore the tension between the (fully or partially) decentralised governance structure in blockchain use and the centralised regulatory framework governing this use in the socio-technical phenomena of blockchain use. For example, in blockchain uses such as DAOs and dApps, governance is decentralised and dictated by the rules specified in blockchain protocols However, the governance of blockchain applications is within current legal frameworks, regardless of the type of blockchain use case. For instance, cryptocurrencies (Renwick and Gleasure, 2021) and smart contracts (Drummer and Neumann, 2020) take place within, and may conflict with, the current contract legal framework. Governance of blockchain use should and must consider both centralised and decentralised governance, both in theory and practice. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

How do blockchain applications balance decentralised governance with legal compliance? What conflicts arise when DAOs operate under traditional regulatory frameworks? Given that rules in decentralised systems are determined by codes that may require developer or network consensus to make amendments, how can decentralised governance frameworks adapt to dynamic lega environments?

The third opportunity is to explore the tension between the immutable rules in codes (smart contracts) and dynamic, changing or flexible legal frameworks in practice. A traditional legal contract can be revised. For example, in Australia, several circumstances can occur in which a signed contract can be terminated. Australian Consumer Law provides cooling-off periods of up to 10 business days during which consumers can cancel contracts in specific contexts. Consumers may also cancel contracts within three or six months if suppliers fail to meet certain obligations. However, blockchain-based smart contracts are typically immutable once deployed, creating potential tensions with consumer protection laws. This raises important research questions such as:

What types of contracts are suitable to be digitalised as smart contracts? What types of contracts are not suitable as smart contracts?

## Consequences

Social and organisational consequences in the long term have been neglected in most reviewed papers. This overlooks blockchain’s ability to influence and constrain human and social actions in the long term. Blockchain uses are not merely additive (adding to current social structures) and passive IT tools; they are also ecologically shaping new social actions (Leonardi, 2010; Tang et al., 2019). Libertarian perspectives are supported and bolstered by blockchain diffusion, thereby exerting an impact on social and cultura structures.

The first opportunity is to explore the unique user behaviours in the socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use. For example, Ilk et al. (2021) highlight a distinctive feature, with Bitcoin users demonstrating a high level of tolerance for transaction fees. This observation challenges our existing understanding of user behaviour, especially in relation to economic rationality and costbenefit analysis (economic or efficiency). In other words, economic rationality is not the only legitimacy model when analysing blockchain use behaviours; other factors, such as belief systems (e.g., Mattke et al., 2019) and emotions (Bidwell et al., 2021, pp. e.g.,), also play important roles. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

To what extent does this cost-insensitive behaviour extend bevond Bitcoin to other blockchain uses? To what extent do these observations necessitate rethinking of economic models and cost–benefit analysis in the context of Bitcoin, cryptocurrency and blockchain adoption and use?

The second opportunity is to examine how blockchain use pertains to regulatory frameworks. Many papers in the reviewed literature were silent about the relationship (or tension) between technology design and law-making. The tension between blockchain use and existing legal frameworks is only discussed in a unidirectional manner in the literature. Almost all selected studies highlight the importance of complying with the existing legal framework (Drummer and Neumann, 2020; Schellinger et al., 2022b). However, code and law are both human-made and can be amended. Precedents across several jurisdictions show that the development of IT can cause legislation and law amendments (e.g., the Bitcoin mining ban in China and the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act of 2000 in the United States). It is not about the necessity of IT complying with the law; rather, the tension between technology design and law-making must be examined and this examination is currently lacking in the literature. Hence, worthwhile questions to ask are as follows:

How does blockchain use balance the tensions between technology and the legal framework? Will there be (or is there) a new technological form of law?

The third opportunity is to examine the long-term consequences of blockchain use. Blockchain use is commonly perceived as protecting privacy (e.g., Wang et al., 2021). As mentioned above, the privacy value of blockchain is based on the unique properties of prime numbers which form the basis of much current cryptographic security (Beck et al., 2018; Helm, 2021; Kappert et al., 2021; Schellinger et al., 2022b). However, the development of functional quantum computers, although currently limited, casts significant doubt on the security of these cryptographic methods. Advances in quantum computing will bring into question whether the specific cryptography used in a blockchain solution is as secure in a post-quantum computing world. The future is unclear but advances in this new area of computing will introduce uncertainty. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

How will the actual stability and perceived privacy of Blockchain be affected by advances in quantum computing? How wil blockchain evolve in light of such new technology? What are user perceptions of the post-quantum computing world?

The fourth opportunity extends beyond blockchain use and builds on observations by Matringe and Power (2024) and others regarding the reduction of human engagement. The increasing integration of emerging technologies, such as blockchain and artificial intelligence, in professional domains raises critical questions about the changing nature of ground-level professional understanding in terms of expertise. Hence, worthwhile research questions to ask are as follows:

How does the use of emerging technologies, such as blockchain and artificial intelligence, change the nature of ground-level human engagement? What are the long-term consequences when ground-level engagement changes via the use of emerging technologies, such as blockchain and artificial intelligence?

## Conclusion

Socio-technical phenomena involving blockchain use, which involves several different types of blockchain use, play an increasingl important role in both IS academia and practice. In this study, we have proposed a conceptual framework of socio-technical phe nomena involving blockchain use based on a literature review of 234 blockchain papers across IS, management, computer science and related disciplines. The conceptual framework comprises five themes: values (inherent values and value judgements); drivers and barriers; social subsystem(s); technical subsystem(s); and consequences (intended or unintended). This has allowed us to capture the relevant, research-based knowledge about blockchain use available today.

This framework advances scholarly understanding by consolidating fragmented insights across disciplines, providing a cohesive lens to analyse the socio-technical interplay inherent in blockchain applications. It serves as a foundational platform for future research, enabling scholars to efficiently map existing knowledge landscapes and identify critical gaps as mentioned in Section 5.

For practitioners, the framework offers actionable guidance to navigate implementation challenges. For example, deploying blockchain-based smart contracts may require balancing technical innovation with compliance risks or addressing unintended con sequences like environmental criticisms stemming from energy-intensive consensus mechanisms. By foregrounding these in terdependencies, the framework empowers stakeholders to anticipate trade-offs.

As emphasised throughout this study, blockchain technology and its uses evolve at an unprecedented pace, continuously giving rise to new socio-technical phenomena and providing research opportunities for future IS researchers. The future of blockchain research is not just about understanding technology—it is also about architecting the very fabric of our society.
