# The Algorithm Discount: Explaining Consumers’ Valuation of Human- versus Algorithm-Created Digital Products

Jennifer Rix <sup>a</sup>, Benedikt Berger <sup>b</sup>, Thomas Hess <sup>a</sup>, and Christine Rzepka <sup>a</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Institute for Digital Management and New Media, LMU Munich, Munich, Germany; <sup>b</sup>Department of Information Systems, University of Münster, Münster, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Owing to advances in generative artificial intelligence (AI), machines can now create digital products like software applications or media content, evoking calls to label such products as “AI-made.” Research on the handmade efect and algorithm aversion suggests that consumers react negatively to digital products that have been created by generative AI systems instead of humans. It is unclear why consumers show this reaction, which we refer to as “algorithm discount.” To answer this question, we conducted a mixed-methods study in the context of digital news oferings, comprising 41 qualitative interviews and a choice-based conjoint analysis with 421 respondents. The results show that consumers’ beliefs about the love and efort imbued in the product, their curiosity about algorithmically generated products, and specific product characteristics, such as the type of news article, determine the algorithm discount. These findings extend our understanding of the emergence of consumers’ aversion to algorithm-created products and ofer providers of such products insight into potential countermeasures.

## KEYWORDS

GenAI; generative AI; performative algorithms; algorithm-created products; digital products; digital news; algorithm discount; mixed-methods research

## Introduction

Progress in artificial intelligence (AI) enables generative AI (GenAI) systems such as ChatGPT or Midjourney to create marketable digital products, such as media content or software applications. Examples include an AI-created painting that was sold at an auction for USD432,500, the AI-written novel 1 the road, and algorithm-created news [62, 105]. Accordingly, firms that offer digital products can use generative AI systems to automate the production process. Following these developments, impending legislation and ethical deliberations call for the transparent disclosure of algorithmically sourced digital products instead of humanly created ones [37, 67], for example through technical measures such as watermarking [92].

Research on consumers’ valuation of physical products shows that labels indicating the source of a product are relevant for purchase decisions [33]. Propagating the source of a physical product as exclusively human (as opposed to manufactured by a machine) increases purchase intentions and willingness to pay (WTP), which is referred to as the handmade effect [33, 40, 55]. Because human creation of a product serves as a quality signal, the disclosure of an algorithmic source may not resonate well with consumers and labeling a digital product as fully algorithm-created may harm its valuation. In line with this rationale, the literature on algorithm aversion suggests that humans exhibit a preference for human instead of algorithmic task conduct [25, 51]. Because algorithms take over innately human tasks in digital product creation, it stands to reason that the prevalence of algorithm aversion translates into a devaluation of algorithm-created digital products [18]. We refer to this devaluation as the algorithm discount.

Research on the handmade effect and algorithm aversion indicates that the valuation of algorithm-created digital products may depend on consumers’ characteristics and beliefs [33]. It is still unclear, though, which consumer-related antecedents specifically explain differences to the valuation of human-created digital products. In addition to the consumerrelated antecedents, the extent of the algorithm discount may also depend on product characteristics. Because digital products like media content or software applications are experience products, consumers can assess their quality only by purchasing and consuming them [1, 46].

In such situations, according to signaling theory, consumers reduce their purchase uncertainty by taking relevant product characteristics into account as signals [22]. Since algorithms as sources of digital products introduce a new form of uncertainty for consumers, product characteristics other than the source may serve as signals to mitigate this uncertainty [7, 102]. These signals present product-related antecedents to consumers’ valuation of algorithm-created digital products. Based on these contentions, we ask:

## Research Question (RQ): What are consumer- and product-related antecedents to differences in the valuation of human- vs. algorithm-created digital products?

To answer this research question, we employed a mixed-methods study design in the context of news articles. Mixed-methods designs combine qualitative and quantitative research methods to deliver in-depth insight into a research topic [90] and allow scholars to address confirmatory and explanatory research questions simultaneously to evaluate and generate theory. In this paper, we first derived potential consumer-related antecedents to the algorithm discount from the literature on the handmade effect and algorithm aversion. Thereafter we conducted qualitative consumer interviews guided by signaling theory to identify potential product-related antecedents to the algorithm discount (Study 1). We employed signaling theory because prior studies successfully applied it to explain how consumers reduce their product uncertainty by inferring information from observable product characteristics [23].

The product-related antecedents identified in the interviews built the foundation for the product configurations in a subsequent choice-based conjoint analysis (CBC) experiment (Study 2). In an additional questionnaire to the CBC, we measured the potential consumerrelated antecedents identified in the literature as explanations for the algorithm discount. The CBC and the measurements in the questionnaire permitted us to assess the algorithm discount and simultaneously test the deductively derived hypotheses on the consumerrelated antecedents as well as the inductively generated hypotheses on the product-related antecedents.

Our study confirms that, on average, consumers ascribe a higher value to human-created than algorithm-created digital products (i.e., the existence of the algorithm discount). This finding extends research on the handmade effect from the physical realm to digital products. The algorithm discount adds to the literature on algorithm aversion by demonstrating that consumers may not only be averse to algorithmic compared to human task conduct but also to the outcome of this task conduct, such as a digital product. We show evidence that beliefs about the love and effort integrated into the product and productrelated antecedents, such as the content paradigm and information transparency of a news article, drive the algorithm discount.

In addition to the theoretical implications, the results enhance our understanding of the prevalence, magnitude, and causes of a potential algorithm discount, which is relevant for multiple stakeholders. Consumers becoming aware of this effect may avoid buying decisions to the detriment of their welfare owing to shunning algorithm-created digital products. Policymakers demanding labels for algorithm-created digital products get an idea of how consumers perceive such labels and their economic consequences. Lastly, providers of algorithm-created digital products receive indications of potential remedies for negative revenue effects upon disclosing an algorithmic source of a digital product.

## Prior Research and Theoretical Foundations

## Consumer Responses to Products of Performative Algorithms

According to Baird and Maruping [5], progress in AI yields a new type of algorithm with a higher level of agency that can independently take over task conduct as it embraces the capabilities of information gathering, decision-making, and executing tasks [5]. In line with Komiak and Benbasat [53], we refer to algorithms that take an independent and active role in task conduct as “performative algorithms.” Among those algorithms are those that enable digital product creation and are commonly referred to as GenAI [62]. In organizations, performative algorithms can perform support activities, such as recruiting [20], as well as primary activities, such as service delivery or product creation.

In this study, we focus on product creation, which typically consists of three stages: ideation and design, production, and commercialization [105]. Performative algorithms can, for example, design physical products, which is especially salient in the fashion segment but also occurs in the design of cars [101], vase motifs [55], paintings [42], or posters [40]. For digital (as opposed to physical) products, performative algorithms can also take over the production process. This applies to news articles [39], poetry [52], or music [66]. Regarding the commercialization of the product, chatbots and voice assistants can at least partly automate sales and customer service [63].

Because of the increasing employment of performative algorithms, prior research investigates the question of how consumers respond to the resulting products. Table 1 provides an overview of prior empirical research studies on this question. For algorithm-designed physical products, the disclosure of an algorithmic source mostly negatively affects evaluations of the products and reduces purchase intentions and WTP [40, 42, 49, 55, 66, 101, 105]. In line with this finding, disclosing an algorithmic source of a digital product negatively influences its perceptive evaluations, such as the perceived accuracy or readability of news articles or the awe attributed to musical pieces [39, 62, 66], but not necessarily the products’ credibility [47, 58].

Yet, studies in the context of algorithm-created digital products seldom investigate consumers behavioral instead of evaluative responses to the disclosure of an algorithmic source. To the best of our knowledge, the sole study in this regard reveals that purchase intentions for algorithm- as opposed to human-created travel articles are higher [84]. This revelation of algorithm appreciation stands in contrast to the rather negative effects of an algorithmic source on the valuations of digital products and purchasing behavior given algorithmdesigned physical products.

While Table 1 provides an overview of the existing research on the effects of disclosing a product’s algorithmic source, further research sought to determine why these effects emerge. For algorithm-designed physical products, the effects of revealing the algorithmic source of the design depend on human involvement in the product’s creation [49], the consumption context (more vs. less symbolic or hedonic vs. utilitarian) [40, 105], the product’s emotional value (emotional vs. functional) [101], consumers’ product expertise [42], and their AI knowledge (high vs. low) [105].

Research on the commercialization of products by performative algorithms suggests that differences in customer reactions to algorithmic versus human service and sales agents can be attributed to algorithmic agents being perceived as less knowledgeable and less empathetic [63]. Yet, customers’ prior AI experience and disclosure timing mitigate such differences [63]. Another reason for negative effects of disclosing the artificial identity of an agent is that customers attribute the use of chatbots to cost-cutting motivations in firms [17]. Customers’ reaction to the disclosure of an algorithmic source also depends on whether firms disclose that a human was involved before or during the interaction [36].

In contrast to this knowledge regarding differences between product design and commercialization by performative algorithms and humans, knowledge on consumers’ valuation of algorithm-created digital products is scarce. Prior research suggests that the valuation of algorithm-created news articles appears to be independent of the medium the articles are embedded in (social media vs. news websites) [47], information on the algorithm’s processes (available vs. not available) [47], the article’s stance (neutral vs. evaluative) [58], the topics (i.e., finance vs. sports) [39], and the consumption context (hedonic vs. utilitarian) [84]. The only antecedent of the valuation of algorithm-created digital products we could identify in the literature is human involvement in music production [49]. We therefore barely understand how consumers valuate products that are created by performative algorithms. Our study seeks to close this gap by building on the literature on algorithm aversion and the handmade effect as well as on signaling theory.

## Algorithm Aversion and the Handmade Efect

Algorithm aversion refers to a “biased assessment of an algorithm which manifests in negative behaviours and attitudes toward the algorithm compared to a human agent” [51:4]. As a result, humans prefer human to algorithmic task conduct under certain conditions, even if the algorithm is evidently identical or superior in performance [8, 25]. While some studies focus on the emergence of algorithm aversion when algorithms err [8, 25], others find this aversion even before humans become familiar with the algorithm [12, 18, 61]. Most studies on humans’ attitudes toward algorithms suggest aversion, but algorithm appreciation may manifest as well: humans’ preference for algorithmic over human conduct [24, 104]. The existing evidence on whether humans prefer humans or algorithms for distinct tasks is therefore inconclusive.

The causes of algorithm aversion or appreciation are diverse and include humans’ desire for control over outcomes [26] and the algorithm’s capabilities [8]. In their review of the literature on algorithm aversion, Jussupow et al. [51] differentiate between two types of antecedents to algorithm aversion: human agents’ characteristics and algorithms’ characteristics. The human-related antecedents include individuals’ task expertise [42], confidence in their own abilities [50], desire for control [15], and uniqueness motives [40]. The most important algorithm-related antecedent of algorithm aversion is its performance: Witnessing an algorithm err increases aversion, while good performance can alleviate aversion or reinforce appreciation [15, 25, 26, 50]. Different forms of human involvement in the task also reduce algorithm aversion [12, 49, 76]. Yet, there is conflicting evidence on how decision makers’ control over an algorithm or the inputs [26, 50] and the algorithm’s process transparency [15, 47] influence algorithm aversion.

Besides the characteristics of the human agent and the algorithm, task and context characteristics can affect algorithm aversion or appreciation. Context-related antecedents include the domain, such as medicine or human resources [12, 49, 76]. Among the taskrelated antecedents to algorithm aversion are monetary incentives [15] and time pressure, which increase individuals’ algorithm appreciation [50]. Furthermore, algorithm aversion wanes if the outcomes of decisions have a positive valence [12]. Online Supplemental Appendix 1 contains a comprehensive overview of seminal influences on algorithm aversion toward performative algorithms.

We seek to transfer these insights to human preferences for the outcome of human versus algorithmic conduct of the creation of digital products, as opposed to the task conduct itself. Research on the handmade effect shows that consumers care about how a product was created. Marketing a product as handmade can boost its value and attractiveness, even if the source has no impact on the product’s functional performance or aesthetic quality [33, 40, 55]. Fuchs et al. [33], for instance, show that consumers are willing to pay 17% more for a soap bar declared as handmade (vs. machine-made).

The advent of performative algorithms demands an extension of existing research on the handmade effect along two dimensions. First, this research focuses on products manufactured by machines but ideated and designed by humans, such as greeting cards, jewelry, scarves, knives, tattoos, and eyeglasses [33, 40]. Performative algorithms can, however, take over product ideation and design as well. Second, the handmade effect focuses on the machine production of physical products, while performative algorithms can produce digital products. Our study seeks to combine insight from algorithm aversion research with insight from research on the handmade effect to evaluate consumers’ reactions to the outputs of performative algorithms taking over innately human tasks, like product creation.

## Signaling Theory

Consumers make purchase decisions based on the information available in the market. Because sellers usually have more information about their products’ quality than buyers due to environments with incomplete information, information asymmetry is intrinsic to market transactions [4]. In such situations of uncertainty, consumers may leverage observable actions as signals that convey unobservable information to solve the adverse selection problem and reduce information asymmetry [21, 43, 89]. Thereby, consumers consider peripheral cues to guide their cognitive assessment of the underlying product and their purchase decisions [21].

There are two types of signals for products. First, intrinsic signals stem from the product itself and, if altered, change the product’s nature, such as a laptop’s graphical processing unit or a car’s horsepower [99]. Second, extrinsic signals are not inherent to a product but are assigned to it by the seller or third parties [83]. Typical examples include brand names, prices, or manufacturer reputation [23]. Consumers consider these signals concurrently in their purchase decisions. Extrinsic cues are usually easier to comprehend, as they do not require specific product knowledge and are more readily available [23, 83].

Purchasing digital instead of physical products magnifies product quality uncertainty, as consumers cannot trial products before making a purchase decision and can assess product quality only ex-post [1, 46, 99]. The underlying product’s materiality is therefore relevant to determine effective signals. Research on the effectiveness of extrinsic signals in reducing purchasing uncertainty of digital products finds that a movie’s advertising budget is positively correlated with its performance [6, 11], that promoting a journalistic article in a firm-owned newsletter positively impacts the return likelihood on a pay-per-article platform, and that the number of likes an article receives on such a platform is positively correlated to its sales [88].

Among intrinsic signals, the presence of complementary products can be a positive signal: For e-books or movies, the availability of a sequel can signal quality [1, 2, 6, 11]. The type of textual content can also be a signal to enhance sales: Journalistic articles with information orientation are less frequently returned on a pay-per-article platform than those with opinion orientation [88]. Furthermore, a reputable source presents a positive intrinsic signal in the context of movies or newspaper articles [1, 30, 38, 88]. Accordingly, signaling theory provides a lens to explain differences in consumers’ responses to digital products created by performative algorithms. Online Supplemental Appendix 2 contains a comprehensive overview of signals applied to digital products.

## Mixed-Methods Research Design

Due to advances in generative AI and a scarcity of prior literature on the valuation of algorithm-created digital products, we applied a mixed-methods research design to answer our research question. Mixed-methods designs combine qualitative and quantitative research methods to deliver extensive insights [90], offering three main advantages to gain a more global view on a topic [95, 96]: First, researchers can address confirmatory and explanatory research questions simultaneously to evaluate and generate theory. Second, combining two methods provides stronger inferences than a single method. Third, researchers can combine divergent and complementary views.

For the design of our study, we followed guidelines by Venkatesh et al. [96]. A mixedmethods design is particularly suitable because we investigated a new phenomenon and there was little existing evidence for our research question. While fragmented studies have investigated how consumers value human-created compared to algorithm-created digital products, we could not explain which consumer- and product-related antecedents affect this valuation.

Our study follows a sequential research design because our research model contains hypotheses we deducted from existing theory and hypotheses we had to develop inductively. We proceeded in three steps (Figure 1). First, we deducted hypotheses on the existence of the algorithm discount and potential consumer-related antecedents, which comprise source-related beliefs and personal characteristics, from the literature on algorithm aversion and the handmade effect. We did so under the assumption that consumer-related antecedents of the algorithm discount are independent of the product to be evaluated.

Second, we sought to identify potential antecedents of the algorithm discount that are related to the product. According to signaling theory, product characteristics can serve as intrinsic signals that guide consumers’ product valuation if they are observable or are otherwise communicated. Such intrinsic signals constitute potential product-related antecedents of an algorithm discount. By definition of the concept, the potential product-related antecedents to the algorithm discount differ from product to product and therefore depend on the research context. Consequently, the product-related antecedents must be empirically identified in this context instead of being theoretically deducted. We did so by means of qualitative interviews in Study 1. Third, we tested all our hypotheses in Study 2, a quantitative CBC experiment with a post-experimental questionnaire. We discuss the methodological approach and design choices for each study in the respective sections.

## Deductive Hypotheses Derivation

## Algorithm Discount

While research on algorithm aversion suggests that humans prefer algorithmic over human task conduct under certain conditions, the literature on the handmade effect reveals that humans prefer handmade physical products over those made by machines. The comparative character of algorithm aversion research matches research on the handmade effect, as inherently human tasks are transferred to algorithms [18]. We suggest that the findings from these two literature streams extend to a preference for human-created digital products over algorithm-created digital products for two reasons.

First, the product source constitutes a highly relevant signal for digital products owing to their experience good characteristic [1, 46]. Reputable sources help to resolve product quality uncertainties of experience goods ex-ante. Movies, for example, sell better with wellknown producers or actors, while established authors can lift the sales of newspaper articles [1, 38, 88]. Second, an important driver of algorithm aversion is the level of agency an algorithm exhibits [51, 76]. As performative algorithms can assume the responsibilities and rights to conduct a task independently upon humans’ delegation, these algorithms exhibit a higher level of agency [5].

Existing studies on consumers’ evaluation of algorithm-created digital products support this reasoning. Longoni et al. [62] and Graefe et al. [39] find negative effects of disclosing an algorithmic source on the evaluation of news articles in quality-related dimensions, such as accuracy or readability. Similarly, disclosing the algorithmic source of paintings or music negatively impacts consumers’ purchase intentions [66]. These insights also transfer to product commercialization: Disclosing the algorithmic identity of a sales agent reduces sales [63]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the handmade effect for physical products also holds for digital products. We refer to this potential effect as the algorithm discount.

Hypothesis 1 (H1): Consumers apply a discount to algorithm-created instead of human-created digital products.

## Consumer-Related Antecedents of the Algorithm Discount

Following research on algorithm aversion and the handmade effect, explanations for differences in consumers’ valuation of digital products created by humans and algorithms may reside in consumers’ characteristics and beliefs (i.e., consumer-related antecedents). Consumers hold different source-related beliefs (i.e., expectations about algorithmic and human conduct) and vary in their personal characteristics (i.e., personality traits, such as attitude toward technology).

Fuchs et al. [33] show that (a) the perceived love imbued in the product, (b) the effort exerted to create the product, and (c) the product’s quality are significant drivers of the handmade effect. Humans can put an emotional investment into the production process, which, through contagion, makes consumers viewing the product to be imbued with love in a symbolic way [33]. Artwork that is in direct physical contact with the artist, for instance, is perceived to be of higher value because consumers believe that an artist’s immaterial qualities can be transposed through it [70]. Accordingly, the handmade effect is stronger in more symbolic consumption contexts [40]. Human involvement also reduces algorithm aversion because humans are believed to embed their human qualities into the task conduct [12, 49, 76]. When the producer is perceived as being less capable of bringing emotional values to fruition in the product, consumers perceive it as less valuable. We therefore hypothesize that the extent of unique emotional essence attributed to algorithms in the form of love is a driver of the algorithm discount.

Hypothesis 2a (H2a): The algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute more love to be imbued in digital products created by humans than those created by algorithms.

In a similar notion, the effort heuristic suggests that the more time is spent producing a product, the more attractive it becomes [33, 56]. Kruger et al. [56] show that among two perceptually identical paintings, consumers value the painting that is stated to have been more time-consuming to draw as higher. Similarly, consumers reward organizations that take extra time to create their products [68]. Herein, the positive effect of effort is also independent of its effect on quality [68]. This effort heuristic may reverse, however, when a product has an algorithmic source, as algorithms can complete tasks faster without exerting the same physical and cognitive effort as humans do. We therefore hypothesize that exerted effort during the product creation as perceived by consumers is also a driver of the algorithm discount.

Hypothesis 2b (H2b): The algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute more effort to the creation of digital products by humans than by algorithms.

Another source-related belief that drives the handmade effect is the perceived quality [33]. It is no surprise that higher quality leads to a higher valuation of a product. Accordingly, the extent to which a product source influences consumers’ valuation of the product depends on the extent to which the source serves as a signal for quality. Prior studies show that disclosing an algorithmic instead of a human product source of digital products has a negative effect on perceived product quality and corresponding subdimensions such as accuracy, authenticity, readability, expertise, and credibility [39, 47, 49, 58, 62, 66]. While information on the quality of algorithmic agents, such as their past performance, certainty indicators, and successful peer use, can mitigate algorithm aversion [25, 32, 91], disclosing an algorithmic instead of human source may create a negative backlash on a digital product’s perceived quality [39, 49, 62]. These quality perceptions can translate into purchase behavior [58, 66]. We therefore hypothesize the attributed product quality to be a driver of the algorithm discount.

Hypothesis 2c (H2c): The algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute higher quality to digital products created by humans than those created by algorithms.

In addition to the source-related beliefs, the literature on algorithm aversion and the consumption of physical and digital products suggests that personal characteristics influence consumer behavior [51, 71, 105]. In the context of algorithm-designed physical products, Zhang et al. [105] highlight curiosity as an important influence on consumers WTP. Curiosity describes individuals’ information-seeking behavior, either based on novelty or complexity [28]. The underlying reasoning is that gaining information about novel stimuli is intrinsically rewarding [10] and that individuals seek for information when aiming to resolve uncertainty and to fill information gaps [60]. Algorithm-created physical and digital products create a sense of novelty and mystery because they represent the results of latest technological advances [45, 105] and receive widespread media attention. Similarly, performative algorithms are typically of substantial complexity, making them hard to interpret and understand. Individuals strive to resolve this information dissonance out of curiosity, often regardless of potential negative consequences or costs [45]. As a result, more curious consumers should be keen to experience such products and therefore should be less likely to discount digital products because they are created by an algorithm. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3a (H3a): The algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who are less curious about digital products from an algorithmic source.

In addition to curiosity regarding algorithm-created products, algorithmic literacy is a known determinant of consumers’ valuations of algorithmic output [105]. Algorithmic literacy describes how experienced and knowledgeable consumers are in their use of AI systems [81]. Whether such knowledge enhances the aversion or appreciation of algorithms and their outcomes is debated, though. Pinski and Benlian [81] suggest increasing algorithmic literacy as a solution to algorithm aversion and Luo et al. [63] find AI experience to be a mitigation against the negative impact of disclosing the identity of chatbots (vs. humans) in sales calls.

In contrast, consumer knowledge dissolved the positive effect of AI design (versus human design) on the WTP for products in a study by Zhang et al. [105]. The authors explain this effect with the interplay between consumer knowledge and curiosity. Since curiosity is rooted in the desire to close information gaps and triggers knowledge-seeking [103], high levels of consumer knowledge reduce the positive impact of curiosity on the WTP for AI-designed products [105]. We follow this reasoning and additionally argue that consumers with higher knowledge about AI may be more aware of its weaknesses, such as the emergence of hallucinations in generative AI [57]. Based on the integration of these findings, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis 3b (H3b): The algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who are more knowledgeable about AI.

## Inductive Hypotheses Generation (Study 1)

## Research Setting

To inquire into the valuation of digital products created by humans or performative algorithms, we explored consumers’ attitudes toward textual products, specifically news articles. Advancements in natural language processing and generation have made automated text generation a hotbed of progress and innovation in generative AI. The advent of large language models like ChatGPT demonstrates the capabilities of performative algorithms in this domain. These capabilities are also reflected by the increasing prevalence of algorithm-created news articles, which consumers get more and more in touch with. Algorithm-created news therefore presents an example of algorithm-created digital products that is already widely known to consumers, making it a realistic and valuable setting in which to explore a potential algorithm discount.

We conducted a qualitative study consisting of consumer interviews to explore which product-related antecedents determine consumers’ quality assessments and related valuation of algorithm-created compared to human-created news articles. In line with signaling theory, unobservable actions in the form of signals can reduce consumers’ adverse selection concerns due to seller and buyer product quality information asymmetries inherent to market transactions [22]. The perceived product quality in turn constitutes an important driver of consumers’ purchasing decisions, both in general [99] and in the context of algorithm-created news [39]. When the characteristics of a product change, so should the capabilities required to create it [71, 101, 105]. Since humans and performative algorithms differ in their capabilities, changes in the capabilities required to create the product affect the fit to the capabilities humans and performative algorithms can offer—or at least the fit as perceived by consumers [12, 18].

In the interviews, we asked consumers about the antecedents of their consumption of digital news in general and, given that algorithm-created news emerged fairly recently, specifically algorithm-created digital news (Online Supplemental Appendix 3 contains more information on the interview guideline). The interview questions were partly based on two literature reviews summarized in Online Supplemental Appendix 1 and 2.

## Data Collection

We conducted 41 semi-structured consumer interviews, resulting in 858 minutes of interview data. We applied purposeful sampling following Patton [79] that, first, allowed for a broad demographic distribution, as represented by age (21 to 76 years) and gender (34% female). Second, this approach allowed us to interview consumers who vary in their news consumption and expertise in the news industry. Online Supplemental Appendix 4 provides an overview of the interviewees and their demographics. During the last five interviews, we noticed that the interviews did not reveal any additional insights and concluded theoretical saturation [35].

## Data Analysis

To analyze the collected data, we used inductive coding methods common to qualitative research. We specifically used in-vivo codes as the foundation for iteratively developing structural and pattern codes for relevant product characteristics, which we formed following the principle of constant comparison [85].

During the first coding cycle, we derived structural codes that served as a categorization technique for further, more in-depth data analysis and are especially useful in explorative inquiries [85]. These codes differentiate signals (i.e., actionable measures that organizations can take in the form of product-related antecedents) from other influential factors on news consumption (i.e., beliefs or characteristics that organizations cannot actively influence, such as a consumer’s trust in the company). For example, the in-vivo code “But I think after generating the article, someone should read it again and approve it and say: Yes, okay, it’s worded correctly, it hits the tone I want to hit and it doesn’t maybe have an undertone you didn’t really want to hit” (P2) was classified as the structural code “signal,” while the in-vivo code “[. . .] If I trust the source, I also trust the message. I would assume that a source I trust would apply the same standards to an [algorithm-]generated message as to a handwritten message” (P28) was classified as the structural code “belief or characteristic.”

In the second coding cycle, we aggregated the structural codes into distinct, mutually exclusive pattern codes based on commonalities, such as the pattern code “positive effect of quality control” based on P2’s previously noted claim. These pattern codes identified emergent themes and aimed to reveal relevant product characteristics. During both coding cycles, we took several measures to ensure the rigor of the analysis process and the trustworthiness of the interpretations following Gioia et al. [35]. First, two researchers coded the data independently and iteratively to allow applied multi-researcher triangulation. Second, the analysis was discussed in a workshop with an expert in the field of interactions with AI systems.

## Hypothesizing Product-Related Antecedents

As previously outlined, product-related antecedents represent signals to reduce the information asymmetry in market transactions. In the context of this study, these signals relate to the magnitude of information asymmetry between seller and buyer in general as well as the delta in the magnitude between the different digital product sources (human vs. algorithm). In the research setting of digital news, consumers face uncertainty about credibility (e.g., trustworthiness, reliability), expertise (e.g., conciseness, comprehensiveness), and readability (e.g., comprehensibility, vividness) [39]. Accordingly, we investigate product characteristics that reduce information asymmetry in these three dimensions from the perspective of our interviewees as signals.

By contrasting the interview results with extant literature, we identified four relevant pattern codes. These codes describe the four product characteristics consumers consider most relevant in reducing asymmetry when purchasing digital news articles, including intrinsic as well as extrinsic signals [83]. We used the codes to hypothesize the productrelated characteristics of the algorithm discount. Online Supplemental Appendix 5 provides a detailed overview of exemplary quotes that illustrate the derivation of our hypotheses.

First, interviewees considered quality checks as relevant to their purchase decisions. Such quality checks refer to a four-eye principle for articles written by humans or a human quality control of algorithm-created articles, reducing asymmetry regarding the reliability and comprehensiveness of information (e.g., “Before something is published, at least one other person looks at it again” (P40)). Interviewees expressed a strong desire for human checks before an article is published, not only approving the credibility of the content but also the tone and way in which the article is written. This can alleviate readability concerns.

Also, interviewees voiced that the quality checks should be conducted by experienced and independent individuals that enhance the trustworthiness of the information. An experienced review consortium and credible legitimacy are exemplary characteristics of a quality control entity. These quality checks accordingly primarily alleviate interviewees concerns and uncertainties about a lack of filter for populistic content or fake news and about a suitable tone. These concerns may strengthen the algorithm discount because interviewees feared that algorithms lack human compassion and may misinterpret and miscontextualize data. The instantiation of a human control entity should therefore weaken the algorithm discount by reducing information asymmetry regarding credibility and expertise, which is in line with prior research on algorithm aversion [49]. We subsequently refer to this product-related antecedent of the algorithm discount as quality control and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4a (H4a): The algorithm discount is stronger for news articles lacking formal quality control.

Second, interviewees considered the exclusion of elements of opinion and interpretation in news articles as a decisive factor in their consumption of digital news articles, especially those written by an algorithm (e.g., “I would prefer that articles are written neutrally, in which all facts are broadly addressed and if you want to spread an opinion, then you must also state that clearly” (P36)). This demand corresponds to a well-established journalistic classification of news articles: A hard news article encompasses only fact-based accounts of news stories (i.e., who, what, where, when, why, and how) [29], such as a news reports. In contrast, interpretive news articles additionally provide elements of opinion, interpretation, and context (i.e., so what), such as editorials or op-eds.

Interviewees saw less potential for uncertainty regarding a hard news article’s credibility and expertise, as interpretation requires more expertise and conciseness regarding the reliability of information (i.e., clear separation between facts and opinion). In the context of the algorithm discount, interviewees were concerned that algorithmic agents have limited interpretive capabilities and exert an influence on society by creating opinionated articles, increasing the asymmetry regarding the article’s expertise. Furthermore, interviewees were skeptical that contrasting and critical voices may disappear if algorithms create interpretive news. Therefore, they tended to disregard news of this content paradigm by algorithms. Interpretive news spiraling the algorithm discount corresponds to a higher aversion toward algorithmic conduct in more subjective tasks [18]. Accordingly, we included this distinction of news articles as a product-related antecedent called content paradigm and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4b (H4b): The algorithm discount is stronger for interpretive than for hard news articles.

Third, interviewees desired transparency over the data used for creating an article, as it can reduce information asymmetry regarding the credibility of information (e.g., “Source information to see where the data is, where it comes from – that’s actually now the norm” (P41)). Interviewees did not consider algorithmic agents as capable as humans of assessing sources trustworthiness or (political) bias and impartiality. Providing references for the information in a news article allows consumers to make these assessments themselves, satisfying the desire for information transparency to reduce credibility information asymmetries and building trust in the algorithmically created content. References can therefore reduce interviewees caution about biased text, which weakens the associated algorithm discount. We therefore added the product-related antecedent information transparency and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4c (H4c): The algorithm discount is stronger for news articles lacking information transparency.

Fourth, interviewees wanted to receive information that allows them to understand the process behind creating the article (e.g., “It is always easier if you understand the processes in advance to read and evaluate the output accordingly” (P22)). Interviewees mostly addressed the relevance of this information for an algorithmic author, as they worried about bias in algorithmic models, which may increase the information asymmetry regarding an article’s credibility. They were concerned that developers may misuse their power and deliberately introduce bias to algorithms creating news articles. Information on the creation process gives consumers or experts the ability to assess the appropriateness of this process, or at least the perception of being able to do so. This notion recently received attention in journalism research, which shows that the inclusion of an “explain your process” box enhances the credibility of news [64]. Our interviewees confirmed the literature in this regard and wished for the possibility of accessing more information about the algorithm. In support of this reasoning, individuals who perceive higher process transparency of an algorithm are less averse to the algorithm in a loan evaluation setting [72]. We therefore considered process transparency as a product attribute that reduces uncertainty about credibility and expertise and hypothesize:

Hypothesis 4d (H4d): The algorithm discount is stronger for news articles lacking process transparency.

Taken together, the analysis of the 41 interviewees’ statements shows that these four product-related antecedents qualify to serve as effective signals in reducing information asymmetries regarding important factors in determining consumers’ consumption and valuation of digital news articles. Further evidence of the relevance of the derived antecedents and individuals’ desires regarding the specific design of each antecedent is available in Online Supplemental Appendix 5. ## Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis

To attain our research goals, we relied on conjoint analysis (CA), which “is interesting to use in IS whenever one explores user preferences about [. . .] new emerging IS” [69:135]. In this study, we conducted a CBC, a subtype of CA [75]. A CBC differentiates itself from other CA methods by requiring consumers to choose between product configurations rather than ranking product characteristics (i.e., attributes). This allowed us to investigate how consumers decide between news articles with varying attributes and to explore consumers preferences. A CBC is closer to real-world purchase decisions and cognitively less challenging than a traditional CA [75].

A CBC is especially suitable for this study, as it allows investigating interactions among product attributes, such as an article’s source and price [86]. A CBC assumes that consumers view each product as a bundle of attributes that can take on different values, called attribute levels [41]. Consumers assign individual utilities (i.e., part-worths) to the attribute levels, which reveal their preference structures within an attribute. The sum of the partworths for a specific product configuration represents its total utility to a consumer. Accordingly, CBC is a decompositional approach, which implies that the preferred level in one attribute can make up for less favored levels of another one. When deciding between different product configurations, consumers trade off their preferences for the attribute levels. By observing several choices between product configurations, CBC allows inferring the individuals’ part-worths. The overall utility of product configuration t for an individual i based on the part-worths $\beta$ can be modeled as follows:

$$
u _ {t, i} = \sum_ {j \in J} \sum_ {m \in M} \beta_ {i, j, m} * x _ {t, j, m} + \beta_ {i, p r i c e} * p _ {t} \text {   with   } (t \in T, i \in I)\tag{1}
$$

where $\beta _ { \mathrm { i , j , m } }$ is the part-worth of level m of attribute j for respondent I;

$\mathbf { X } _ { \mathrm { { t } , j , m } }$ is the dummy variable indicating whether product t features level m of attribute j; $\mathrm { p _ { t } }$ is the price of product t;

M is the index set of levels; and

J is the index set of attributes without price.

We integrated the investigation of the algorithm discount with the investigation of the potential product-related antecedents of the source in the form of signals. Signals, such as quality control, can exhibit direct effects on consumer behavior as well as enhance or weaken the effect of other signals [82]. We therefore operationalized the product-related antecedents as attributes alongside the product source (human vs. algorithm) and the price. The difference in the part-worths for a human and an algorithmic source served as a measure of the algorithm discount.

At the same time, interactions with the other attributes allowed testing the productrelated antecedents to this effect. Additionally, we measured the potential consumer-related antecedents using latent psychometric constructs. The CBC covers three main phases: (1) defining product profiles, i.e., identifying relevant attributes and corresponding levels; (2) consumer evaluation, i.e., creating an experimental scenario and set-up; and (3) analysis techniques, that is, deriving part-worth utilities and overall product value from the choice data [69].

## Phase 1: Defining Product Profiles

We carefully and systematically derived the relevant attributes and corresponding levels for consumers’ purchase decisions of news articles in addition to source and price following previous CBC studies [34, 100] and best-practice guidelines for CBC in an IS context [69]. We defined the product profiles in addition to the source and price based on Study 1. Following established guidelines for CA attribute formulation [98], we conducted a market review to translate these theoretical concepts into more easily comprehensible terms for the U.S. citizens partaking in our CBC experiment. We extracted existing features of digital news articles from news websites and combined our findings with insights from market reports (Online Supplemental Appendix 6) [67].

First, the review confirmed the practical relevance and viability of the attributes derived during the interviews. Second, the review, for example, induced us to use the terms “journalist” and “robo-journalist” to describe the article source, where the latter presents a term frequently used in practice to specify an algorithm-created news author. It further prompted us to distinguish between hard and interpretive news more intuitively by presenting hard news articles as “reports” and interpretive news articles as “op-eds.” In addition, we translated the constructs information and process transparency into “information on data sources” and “information on article creation process” to mirror the phrases used on news websites. The market review also helped define appropriate price points, as we adapted them from an existing pay-per-article platform. ## Phase 2: Consumer Evaluation

Based on the formulation of the product attributes and their levels, we designed the CBC experiment using Sawtooth Software Lighthouse Studio 9.14.9. The CBC experiment started with (i) a vignette that asked participants to imagine a 20-minute wait at the airport during which they decide to read newspaper articles on a pay-per-article platform to pass the time. The participants should further imagine wanting to inform themselves about current news topics to facilitate small talk with the business partner they were about to visit. Finally, participants got to know that the articles on the pay-per-article platform stemmed from reputable broadsheet and tabloid newspapers and were of the same linguistic quality and interest to the participants to account for potential influences of outlet reputation and readability.

Subsequently, we introduced participants to (ii) the six attributes as per Table 2. Following Naous and Legner [69], we included a prototype of a news article illustrating each attribute. The explanations of attributes, the prototype, and an exemplary choice task are displayed in Online Supplemental Appendix 7. The sample article’s layout was inspired by the pay-per-article platform Blendle to enhance ecological validity [88]. Thereafter participants had to complete three comprehension checks to ensure that they engaged with the scenario and the attribute level explanations.

In the (iii) CBC exercise, participants made fifteen choices between three product profiles displayed to them. Each product profile encompassed one level from all six attributes (Figure 3). For twelve of these choices, we generated the product profiles randomly using the balanced overlap method. This method allows a degree of overlap between the profiles but simultaneously rules out choices between duplicate profiles, which is useful for gathering preference information even if participants have must-have requirements for particular attributes [86]. As a CBC comprises multiple choice tasks, the configuration of the product profiles introduces the randomization that is inherent to experiments. Next to the twelve randomly created choice tasks, we added three hold-out choice tasks that we created manually and kept fixed for all participants [41]. We used these hold-out tasks to assess the reliability and validity of the observed preference structures.

![](/api/attachments/235KGKTN/fulltext/images/d1143d8da654f8f367cfd646b3da79e4b926e0b04cf7eb60b8dcaf30864d7ce7.jpg)  
Figure 3. Exemplary choice task in choice-based conjoint analysis (CBC) exercise.

We refrained from including an option not to choose any of the displayed product profiles (i.e., a none option) for conceptual and methodological reasons [9]. First, including a none option shifts individuals’ decisions from a comparative judgment approach (i.e., processing based on attributes) to an evaluative judgment approach (i.e., processing based on alternatives) [78]. While evaluative judgments are important in real purchase scenarios, our study aimed to find comparative effects. Second, given that a strong free mentality prevails in online media, the competition from free alternatives by including a none option may have been detrimental to our study [71]. Third, the none option can be used to avoid making cumbersome decisions [16, 75], but these complex trade-off decisions are pivotal for answering our research question.

After the CBC exercise, the participants proceeded to a (iv) post-experimental questionnaire in which we measured potential consumer-related antecedents of the algorithm discount, as hypothesized in section “Consumer-Related Antecedents of the Algorithm

Discount.” Additionally, we integrated control variables already applied in AI and media contexts: attitude toward AI, trust in media, past payment for online news, and news consumption [9, 65, 87, 93]. We also collected the demographic variables age, gender, education, and income. All measurements are based on established scales and were carefully adapted to the study context (Online Supplemental Appendix 8).

For the constructs love, effort, product quality, and curiosity, we employed a comparative 11-point Likert-type scale from 1 (“much less”) to 6 (“exactly the same”) to 11 (“much more”), asking participants to compare their beliefs about algorithm-created articles to those about human-created articles (“In comparison to human-created articles, the statement applies to robo-created articles . . . ”) [56]. The higher participants rated these items, the more they thought algorithmic-created articles exhibit these characteristics compared to human-created articles. For example, when individuals rated the item “The articles are timeintensive to create” with 1, they suggested the algorithmic creation of news articles is much less time-intensive than the human creation of news articles. Analogously, respondents could either indicate that they are much less curious about algorithm-created than humancreated articles (1 = “much less”), similarly curious (6 = “exactly the same”), or much more curious (11 = “much more”). For all other constructs, we applied 7-point Likert-type scales from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). Figure 4 summarizes the full choice experiment process.

## Phase 3: Analysis Techniques

The CBC experiment provided us with choice data that we analyzed using the Hierarchical Bayesian (HB) estimation method to determine the attribute levels’ part-worths. The partworths constituted the basis for our subsequent analyses. Compared to the aggregated analysis of choices based on a multinomial logit model, the HB estimation delivers more precise estimates, as it can account for heterogeneity on an individual level [3, 41]. This also allows deriving individual-level part-worths, which is necessary to determine the influence of consumer-related antecedents. HB estimation also elicits interactions more proficiently than other methods [86]. We needed to determine these interactions to evaluate how the derived product-related antecedents affect participants’ preferences for news articles written by humans or algorithms.

The HB estimation returns the part-worths for the levels of all attributes. The part-worths present interval data, meaning that they do not possess a meaningful origin, and adding any constant does not change the estimates’ meaning [13]. To generate these part-worth utilities based on the collected data (i.e., the randomly generated choice tasks and the first hold-out task), we performed HB estimation using 100,000 burn-in iterations in the Markov-Chain-Monte-Carlo simulation [9]. We used the following 10,000 iterations for parameter estimation and converted the resulting part-worths to effects coding for a more intuitive interpretation, i.e., the sum of all levels’ utilities in each attribute was scaled to zero. The part-worths show whether participants prefer one level over another but do not reveal the respondents absolute preferences. A negative part-worth therefore does not represent an outright rejection of this attribute level but a lower preference than for a level with a higher part-worth. To allow comparisons between individual respondents, we rescaled the part-worths such that the difference between the worst and best level in one attribute summated across all attributes was equal to the number of attributes times 100 (i.e., 600) [75].

![](/api/attachments/235KGKTN/fulltext/images/763eb5544a8b2df582587f85187b0fa3d2d4ba6be3f07b2b1d95327d1ae0537c.jpg)  
Figure 4. Overview of choice experiment.

To analyze the main effects of the product attributes on the product valuation by the participants, we calculated the change in utility $( \Delta u _ { j , m } )$ between all levels in one attribute. Additionally, we calculated the relative importance $R I _ { j }$ of each attribute $j ,$ which is the difference between the highest and lowest part-worths in one attribute divided by the sum of these differences across all attributes (formula 2):

$$
R I _ {j} = \frac {\left(\max _ {m} \beta_ {i , j , m} - \min _ {m} \beta_ {i , j , m}\right)}{\sum_ {j = 1} ^ {J} \left(\max _ {m} \beta_ {i , j , m} - \min _ {m} \beta_ {i , j , m}\right)}\tag{2}
$$

The resulting are $R I _ { j }$ interval-scaled and add up to 100%. They allow drawing inferences on the weight of each attribute in consumers’ decisions. The higher the $R I _ { j }$ of an attribute for a respondent, the more weight it presents in the consumer’s choice assessment.

While the part-worths allow calculating utility differences between product configurations, these utility differences are hard to interpret. We therefore computed the marginal WTP $( M W T P _ { i , j } )$ for the change from one level m to another level n in attribute j [13]. The marginal willingness to pay (MWTP) is characterized by an exchange rate between utility and price, as summarized in formula 3:

$$
M W T P _ {i, j} (m, n) = \frac {\left(\max p _ {j} - \min p _ {j}\right)}{\left(\max _ {m} \beta_ {i , p r i c e , m} - \min _ {m} \beta_ {i , p r i c e , m}\right)} * \left(\beta_ {i, j, n} - \beta_ {i, j, m}\right)\tag{3}
$$

Following the formula, the MWTP presents the marginal rate of substitution between price and non-price attributes. Yet, the MWTP approach assumes a strictly linear part-worth function across the full range of the attribute price. We used a piecewise-linear approach to account for this and determined the upper and lower bound of monetary equivalents [14, 54, 100].

We explored the interactions between the attribute source and the other attributes to determine the influence of product-related antecedents on the valuation of the article source. Interactions are present “when the net utility effect of levels from two separate attributes is significantly more or less than what would be predicted by summing their main effect parts” [86:4]. We estimated the interaction effects in line with the HB estimation. The derived interaction effects are zero-centered and can therefore be interpreted orthogonally with the main effects [74]. We determined the significance of the main and interaction effects based on the population mean draws after convergence. Following Orme and Chrzan [75], we analyzed the consistency of the effects in the last 10,000 alpha draws. To estimate the impact of consumer-related antecedents on the valuation of the article source, we performed a multiple linear regression.

## Results

## Reliability and Validity

We performed several robustness checks to ensure the reliability and validity of our estimations. First, hold-out tasks one and three showed the same product profiles, allowing assessing the test-retest reliability (i.e., the consistency of participants’ choice behavior) [41]. About three quarters (74%) of respondents chose the same product profile across these two identical choice tasks, which is close to the values of a similar CBC study [44]. Second, we predicted the aggregated shares of choices in the second hold-out task based on the elicited part-worths using the Sawtooth Lighthouse Market Simulator and analyzed the mean absolute error (MAE) in the predictions [73]. An MAE of 1.07 indicates a very high aggregate predictive validity, as it suggests that the absolute difference between predicted and actual choice shares is around one percentage point [77]. Third, to measure individuallevel predictive validity, we used the estimated part-worths to calculate the predictive hit rate on the hold-out choice tasks that did not enter the overall estimation (i.e., hold-out tasks two and three) [48]. Almost three quarters (73%) of the predictions were correct, which lies close to values derived in comparable studies [44, 77]. Further, a root-likelihood (RLH) of 70% indicated an acceptable fit of the derived utility function [13, 34]. We therefore assumed that our estimations were reliable and valid.

## Algorithm Discount

Table 4 contains the main effect results of the CBC experiment. Next to the average part-worths and their standard deviations (SDs), we quantified the change in utility $( \Delta u _ { j , m } )$ from one level to another in one attribute and tested the null hypotheses that the part-worths were equal using paired sample t-tests [14, 54, 100]. All part-worths in the attributes differed significantly (p < 0.001). The results show that consumers on average value an algorithm-created news article significantly less than one written by a human, which confirms the existence of an algorithm discount and supports H1. Similarly, interpretive news is of significantly lower utility for consumers than hard news. In contrast, quality control, information transparency, and process transparency all significantly increase consumers’ utility. The attributes’ relative importance show that respondents consider quality control and source as most important, followed by price, information transparency, content paradigm, and process transparency.

## Consumer-Related Antecedents

To examine the consumer-related antecedents to the algorithm discount in the context of algorithm-created news, we performed a multiple linear regression of the constructs included in the post-experimental questionnaire. Before running the regression, we analyzed the reliability of the latent constructs by computing Cronbach’s Alpha α. The α-values all exceeded 0.9, suggesting a high reliability of all constructs [80]. We further tested for violations of the assumptions of linear regression models. We utilized the modified Breusch-Pagan test to investigate potential heteroscedasticity, given that the residuals of our models were normally distributed based on the central limit theorem [31]. Heteroscedasticity did not present an issue $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 0 . 8 4 )$ . Based on the variance inflation factor (VIF) values, multicollinearity was also not an issue (max(VIF) = 1.84). A negative value of the difference implies that a human source is preferred over an algorithmic one (analogous to the main effect and the potential productrelated antecedents) and vice versa. Decreases in the dependent variable therefore suggest that consumers’ preferences change in favor of the human, strengthening the algorithm discount. The regression results show that higher love attributed to algorithm-created news articles is associated with an increase in the source preference in favor of the algorithm, meaning there is a lower preference for human- over algorithm-created content $( \mathtt { B } = 0 . 1 6 , \mathtt { p }$ $< 0 . 0 1 )$ . This means, vice versa, that the algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute relatively more love to be imbued in human-written news articles. This supports hypothesis H2a.

In contrast, the higher the effort attributed to creating algorithm-created content, the lower the preference for algorithm- over human-created content $( \ B = - 0 . 1 4 , \ p < 0 . 0 5 )$ . This means that the algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute relatively less effort to human-written news articles. The influence of effort on the algorithm discount is therefore significant in the opposite direction of H2b, which we consequently reject. Product quality, in turn, exhibits the hypothesized influence on the algorithm discount: The higher consumers expect the quality of algorithm-created news articles (compared to human-created ones) to be $( \ B = 0 . 3 1 , \ \mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , the lower the preference for human- over algorithm-created content. In other words, the algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who attribute relatively more quality to news articles written by humans. This supports H2c.

Furthermore, we tested the effect of consumers’ personal characteristics on the algorithm discount. The more curious consumers are about algorithm-created news articles $( \ B = 0 . 2 1$ $\mathrm { p } < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ , the lower their preference for news articles written by humans over those generated by algorithms. As hypothesized in H3a, this means that the algorithm discount is stronger for consumers who are less curious about news articles created by algorithms. In contrast, we did not find evidence for H3b, because consumers’ self-stated knowledge about AI does not significantly influence the valuation of algorithm-created content compared to human-created news articles.

## Product-Related Antecedents

The interactions between the source attribute and the other attributes, except price, revealed the product-related antecedents of the algorithm discount. The interaction effects of quality control and process transparency with source are not significant $\left( \mathtt { p } > 0 . 0 5 \right)$ : These signals therefore do not influence the algorithm discount and do not constitute product-related antecedents to the algorithm discount. We consequently reject hypotheses H4a and H4d.

In contrast, the interaction effects of content paradigm $\scriptstyle ( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 1 )$ and information transparency $\left( \mathtt { p } < 0 . 0 5 \right)$ with source are significant and can be considered product-related antecedents. As hypothesized in H4b, the algorithm discount is stronger for interpretive than for hard news. The part-worths for human and algorithm as article source are 53.02 and -53.02 for hard news and 63.12 and -63.12 for interpretive news. Moreover, the algorithm discount becomes stronger when information becomes more transparent. The part-worths for human and algorithm amount to 53.63 and -53.63 in the absence of information transparency but to 62.51 and -62.51 if it is present. Because this effect has the opposite direction as hypothesized in H4c, we reject this hypothesis. These effects are visualized in Figures 5 and 6.

## Discussion

In this mixed-methods research study, we conducted qualitative interviews with 41 news article consumers and a CBC experiment with 421 individuals to investigate consumers’ valuation of algorithm- vs. human-created digital products. Our results show that the source of a digital product significantly affects its valuation by consumers: We show that consumers value algorithm-created digital products lower than human-created digital products, applying an algorithm discount. This algorithm discount partly depends on other product characteristics, such as the content paradigm and the information transparency of a news article. We find that consumers’ source-related beliefs about the love, effort, and quality imbued in a product and their curiosity about algorithm-created products contribute to the emergence of the algorithm discount. In this section, we discuss the results against the insights of prior research.

H1’s results indicate that the handmade effect for physical products extends to digital products in the form of the algorithm discount. The existence of an algorithm discount is in line with research on the handmade effect and algorithm aversion [40, 51, 66] and adds to the mixed results in the existing body of research on consumers’ responses to digital products given an algorithmic instead of human source [39, 47, 58, 62, 66]. Algorithmcreated digital products, like their physical counterparts, are of lower value to consumers than those created by humans and are therefore subject to a lower WTP [40, 101].

Regarding the deductively derived consumer-related antecedents in H2, we find that the algorithm discount is driven by the source-related beliefs of love, effort, and quality imbued in algorithm-created as opposed to human-created products. The primary drivers of the handmade effect therefore also drive the algorithm discount [33]. Our findings imply that although digital products cannot be literally handmade, the love and quality consumers associate with the human or algorithmic origin of such products enhance their valuation.

This does not hold for the perceived effort of creating digital products, which even has a negative impact on the product valuation in our study. This effect contradicts the human effort heuristic [56]. It may be explained by individuals’ anticipated complexity of the underlying algorithm: The more effort consumers associate with algorithmically creating a news article, the more complex they consider the underlying algorithm to be. Complexity is closely related to opacity, which generally exacerbates the acceptance of AI systems [19]. The effort needed to create an article may also be related to the effort needed to read it, which is why consumers may tend to prefer articles from the source they associate with lower effort in the creation process, particularly in the scenario sketched in our experiment.

Our investigation of personal characteristics’ influence on the algorithm discount in H3 shows that the more curious consumers are about algorithm-created news, the smaller the algorithm discount becomes. Since curiosity incites consumers’ intention to explore, it makes sense that consumers prefer articles from the source they are more curious about. Since curiosity will fade as algorithm-created products become more prevalent, the positive effects of curiosity may however also wane.

In contrast, we could not identify an impact of AI knowledge on the algorithm discount. This stands in contrast to Zhang et al. [105], who identified AI knowledge as a moderator of the differences in WTP for physical products designed by humans versus algorithms. Their explanation is that low knowledge induces consumers to be curious about algorithmcreated products, thereby enhancing WTP. Since we evaluate curiosity about AI simultaneously, it makes sense that AI knowledge has no additional influence on the algorithm discount.

Among the inductively derived product-related antecedents of the algorithm discount as part of H4, the interaction effect of the content paradigm with the product source corroborates insights on the emergence of algorithm aversion in subjective tasks [18]. Given that creating articles with a hard content paradigm can be considered a rather objective task, the machine heuristic may apply. This heuristic implies that people favor machines for tasks corresponding to the machine’s perceived capabilities, including objective tasks [18].

In contrast, information transparency strengthens the algorithm discount, although we expected the opposite. An explanation for this interaction effect may be that individuals consider it impossible to trustfully report the sources of algorithms. For example, ChatGPT frequently exhibits hallucinations by making up facts and sources [57]. Consumers may consequently distrust the sources algorithms provide. Another potential explanation is that individuals want to ensure that the articles are free of human authors’ opinions and slant that cannot be ascertained by a specific source. While prior studies suggest that quality control in the form of a human-in-the-loop setting positively affects the valuations of algorithmic conduct [49], we could not establish that consumers value quality control of human- and algorithm-created articles differently. Apparently, consumers believe that quality control is similarly beneficial for digital products created by humans and algorithms, as both possibly err during the production process. Similarly, process transparency does not affect the valuation difference between human- and algorithm-created articles. This is in line with a prior study that could not find a significant effect of process transparency on the credibility of algorithm-created news articles [47].

## Theoretical and Practical Implications

From a theoretical perspective, our findings add to and connect three distinct research streams. First, we add to knowledge on the effect of a product source on consumers’ evaluation of the product. Prior studies have shown that consumers are willing to pay a premium for products created by humans [33]. As existing research “provides little insight into how consumers react to today’s robotic revolution” [40:72], our study investigates a new type of agent capable of taking over the full product creation process: performative algorithms. In line with the contentions of Baird and Maruping [5], we add to the nascent literature on delegation to more agentic IS. Studying the outcomes of delegating product creation to performative algorithms allows us to extend the literature on the handmade effect by transferring its knowledge to a new context, specifically digital products. Our findings indicate that consumers also prefer digital products with a human source over those with an algorithmic source, even though digital products are not literally handmade but created using information technology systems. Accordingly, we refer to this effect as the algorithm discount. These insights enhance our understanding of consumers’ valuation of product sources, showing that products’ materiality does not limit consumers’ preference for human-created products. We show that most of the core drivers of the handmade effect, which constitute consumer-related antecedents, also prevail in the context of digital products [33].

Second, we add to the algorithm aversion literature in two ways: On the one hand, we extend the investigation of consumers’ preferences for algorithmic vs. human task conduct to the outcomes of this conduct. Prior research investigates individuals’ decisions to delegate tasks to performative algorithms [59] and the antecedents for their algorithm aversion or appreciation [94]. Most of this research takes a “producer” perspective on algorithmic task conduct. What is missing in the current discussion are insights into consumers’ evaluation of tasks that have been delegated to algorithms, such as algorithmgenerated products as the result of delegation. Our study integrates the algorithm aversion literature with the nascent literature on performative algorithms and ‘next generation of research on IS use’ theorizing [5]. We provide insights into how individuals assess the outcomes of delegations to algorithms. Apparently, the aversion against algorithmic task conduct translates into an aversion against the results of this conduct. Our study reveals that consumers assign a lower value to digital products created by algorithms than those created by humans, introducing the “algorithm discount” as a new phenomenon in IS research.

Conversely, our delineation of antecedents to the algorithm discount contributes to understanding why algorithm aversion emerges [24]. Our study adds to a growing body of research that aims to understand the underlying processes that lead to aversion or appreciation of algorithms. Our results show that there are multifaceted antecedents, including various consumer- and product-related ones. Future research can build on these results to gain a deeper understanding into the factors that explain the aversion and appreciation of algorithms, particularly the algorithm discount.

Third, our insights add to the multifaceted discourse on the valuation of digital products. We leverage signaling theory to understand how product-related antecedents affect the valuation of digital content [102]. While these antecedents are per definition productspecific, we show that the algorithm discount is not independent of intrinsic signals, such as specific product characteristics (i.e., the content paradigm of news articles in our study), and extrinsic signals, such as information on the resources employed in the production process (i.e., the sources of information used in a news article). Herein, we address two gaps in signaling research: the neglect of (unintentional) negative signals (i.e., by integrating the source as a signal) and considering signals as solitary constructs (i.e., by considering a portfolio of signals, including their interaction effects) [22]. The interaction effects between the product source and two additional product characteristics on the products’ valuation revealed in our study demonstrate that signals do not operate in isolation but exhibit joint effects.

For practitioners, our findings contribute to answering emerging questions when delegating (parts of) the creation of digital products to performative algorithms. First, we provide insights into the potential revenue effects of leveraging algorithms for product generation. By revealing the existence of an algorithm discount, organizations can anticipate a potential negative revenue impact in generating a business case. Second, our insights on mitigating the algorithm discount can help organizations monetize algorithm-created content effectively. On the one hand, firms should evaluate for which kinds of their digital products the algorithm discount is more or less pronounced, such as hard news and interpretive news as in our study. On the other hand, firms should provide signals that can offset the algorithm discount, such as human quality control and information on the production resources and processes.

Even though creating these signals is costly, our results on the MWTP across attributes suggest that the value gained by the sum of such signals can compensate for the loss in value owing to the algorithm discount. In addition, the effect of love on the preference for a human over an algorithmic product source highlights that human strengths are relevant dimensions in the assessment of media goods. As a consequence, anthropomorphizing algorithms may positively affect the aesthetic valuation of algorithm-created digital products.

Third, our market research reveals that many organizations only provide one of the investigated signals. Our study raises awareness for the multiplicity of the signals and that ideally the full signal portfolio should be applied. Overall, we provide actionable guidance for managers to understand and assess the potential of implementing performative algorithms for digital product creation.

## Limitations and Opportunities for Future Research

Despite the careful design of our study, our work is not without limitations. First, we leveraged the prominent use case of algorithm-created digital news as a well-known application of performative algorithms for digital product creation. Yet, it stands to question for future research how the insights derived in this study transfer to other types of digital (media) products, such as software or video content.

Similarly, we validated our hypotheses in a U.S. sample. Extant research has shown that the perception of digital products is culture-dependent [1, 44]. We controlled for trust in media but did not conduct our exercise in two fundamentally different cultures with distinct perceptions of journalism. Accordingly, repeating the study in a setting with a different journalistic reputation may provide an interesting avenue for future research.

Second, the pay-per-article scenario exists in a journalistic context, such as the platform Blendle [88], and allowed us to examine the valuation of algorithm-created digital products particularly well. Subscription-based payment models are, however, more common in the market [102]. Herein, the algorithm discount may manifest differently as news portfolios, for which subscriptions can be purchased, may contain a mix of algorithm- and humancreated content. Future research may address news portfolios with differing shares of algorithm-created content alongside human-created content.

Third, humans and algorithms may work in tandem to jointly create news articles. Considering that human quality control has a positive impact on the valuation of algorithmcreated news articles, higher degrees of human involvement or even equal co-creation may enhance the value of the digital product. Future research should inquire into the magnitude and persistence of the algorithm discount given tandem authorship.

Concerning methodological limitations, even though CBC is a popular and wellestablished measurement instrument that mimics real purchase decisions, the choices in a CBC remain hypothetical. The artificial character of the decision tasks may be considered a drawback of CBC analysis. Forcing experimental participants to buy the object under consideration if their inferred WTP is larger than a randomly chosen purchase price has been suggested to address this issue [27]. Purchase obligations are, however, ineffective for news articles when their content is not disclosed. Besides, our study focuses on the incremental comparative valuation of products rather than deriving absolute values that correctly position the WTP for news articles [13]. We therefore think our results provide valid insights into consumers’ valuation of algorithm-created digital products even though we did not align incentives.

Lastly, we may overestimate the size of the algorithm discount for two reasons. The algorithm discount can only prevail when the source is actively recognized, but the recall of the source of news articles is often low [97]. The algorithm discount size may therefore be smaller in practice, as not all consumers will recognize the algorithmic instead of human source. This bias also depends on whether regulatory measures will force providers of algorithm-created digital products to label them as such. Our study scenario defined that all articles are of the same interest to participants, which is a necessary measure to isolate the algorithm discount. In practice, however, consumption decisions are also driven by other influences, such as the interest in a topic or news in general, as well as the brand of a news provider [71]. Future studies may test the algorithm discount in a field setting.

Nonetheless, our study provides early indications of the prevalence of an algorithm discount, which bears important implications for research and practice. Given that performative algorithms will increasingly diffuse into everyone’s day-to-day lives, society will be increasingly confronted with the outcomes of these novel types of algorithms. Because our study reveals that individuals take an interest in whether an algorithm or human has accomplished a task or generated an outcome, inquiring into the effects of delegating to performative algorithms is of high relevance also beyond their application to generate (digital) products.

## Conclusion

Our research provides new insights on consumers’ valuation of algorithm- vs. human-created digital products. Prior literature suggests that consumers assign higher value to digital products created by humans than by performative algorithms—a phenomenon we name algorithm discount. To understand why this is the case, we amalgamated research on the handmade effect and algorithm aversion, deriving five potential consumer-related antecedents of the algorithm discount. We built on signaling theory and conducted qualitative interviews, which revealed four potential product-related antecedents to the algorithm discount. Using a choice-based conjoint analysis, we tested the potential antecedents and found evidence that consumers’ beliefs about the love and effort imbued in the product as well as their curiosity about digital products from an algorithmic source determine the algorithm discount. In the context of our study, which concerns news websites, the algorithm discount depends on a news article’s type (report versus op-ed) and information transparency. These results indicate that the handmade effect is not limited to physical products but translates into the algorithm discount for digital products. Similarly, the aversion against algorithmic task conduct translates into an aversion against the results of this conduct. This implies a negative impact of labeling algorithmically created products as such on their monetization. However, providers of such products can leverage the impact of consumers’ beliefs and product characteristics on the algorithm discount to dampen this impact.
