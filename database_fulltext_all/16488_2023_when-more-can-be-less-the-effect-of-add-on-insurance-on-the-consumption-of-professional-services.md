# When More Can Be Less: The Effect of Add-On Insurance on the Consumption of Professional Services

Abstract. The emergence of online platforms for professional services (e.g., cosmetic pro cedures) represents a natural progression of e-commerce from search and experience goods to credence goods. Because of the deeply consequential nature of professional services and the large information asymmetries between customers and service providers, designing effective risk-reduction strategies is crucial for facilitating digital transactions of professional services. In this paper, we study whether and how the introduction of a novel risk-reduction strategy, the add-on insurance covering the potential cost of negative consequences (e.g., complications and unsatisfactory outcomes), affects the demand for professional services in online platforms. We leverage a policy change in an online platform for cosmetic procedures, which started to offer the add-on insurance for a subset of cosmetic procedures in 2016. Our empirical analysis shows that this risk-reduction strategy has asymmetric effects on low- and high-risk procedures. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the introduction of insurance increases the sales of low-risk procedures, but not those of high-risk ones. More importantly, the insurance has a negative spillover effect on uninsured competitors of insured procedures, regardless of their risk levels. The negative spillover effect of insurance on high-risk procedures is noteworthy because it hurts the sales of their uninsured competitors without increasing their own sales, suggesting that the negative spillover effect goes beyond the typical demand cannibalization explanation and can decrease the overall demand for high-risk procedures. We further corroborate our <sup>fi</sup>ndings and investigate the mechanisms behind the asymmetric treatment effects and the negative spillover effect using an online controlled experiment. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for plat forms to design, deploy, and evaluate their risk-reduction strategies

## 1. Introduction

Professional services are highly skilled services that require professional training or licenses in the respective areas, such as medicine, law, and accounting. The global market for professional services reached \$5 trillion in 2020 and is expected to surpass \$7 trillion by 2025.<sup>1</sup> With the rapid market growth, more platforms have started to sell professional services online, such as Coursera (courses) and Soyoung (cosmetic procedures). As typical types of credence goods, the outcomes of professional services are often highly consequential, and yet it is dif<sup>fi</sup>cult for customers to foresee the outcomes before purchase, because of a lack of expertise, or even judge the quality of the services after they are performed (Dulleck and Kerschbamer 2006). Consequently, the perceived risk of professional services is much higher than that of the common search and experience goods sold on e-commerce platforms. Because of the high perceived risk, the customer acquisition cost of professional services is extremely high. For instance, the average customer acquisition cost for cosmetic procedures falls in the range of CNY 4,000–6,000 (roughly USD 600–900) in China.<sup>2</sup> It is therefore crucial for the providers of professional services to develop strategies to alleviate the perceived risk of customers in order to increase sales.

The emergence of online platforms for professional services greatly facilitates the design and deployment of risk-reduction strategies on a large scale and has thus attracted increasing attention from information systems (IS) researchers (Gao et al. 2015, Lu and Rui 2018). Two common types of risk-reduction strategies exist in online platforms: ex ante and ex post strategies.

Ex ante risk-reduction strategies refer to the practice of disclosing or facilitating the disclosure of additional information through which consumers can better assess the quality of services before a purchase, such as online reviews and assurance seals (Ba and Pavlou 2002, Duan et al. 2008, Ozpolat et al.<sup>¨</sup> 2013, Chen et al. 2018). Recent studies have shown that online reviews for professional services can provide important information about the quality of physicians (Lu and Rui 2018) and can reduce perceived risk by mitigating information asymmetries (Gao et al. 2015). Nevertheless, these studies have not examined whether and how risk-reduction strategies affect the demand for professional services in online platforms, which are critical to the design and customization of risk-reduction strategies.

Ex post risk-reduction strategies refer to the practice of helping consumers recover from the unexpected loss incurred after a purchase. Product warranty and return policies are two common ex post risk-reduction strategies for conventional goods (Anderson et al. 2009, Jindal 2015), but they are often not applicable to professional services. The main reason for this inapplicability is that the various contingencies of the outcomes of professional services are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to list beforehand, and the negative outcomes of professional services can be irreversible (e.g., one may die if the surgery fails), rendering it hard to operationalize the warranty policy. Moreover, professional services such as surgery and education typically cannot be returned. A widely adopted risk-reduction strategy for professional services in online platforms is add-on insurance, which covers the added expenses due to unforeseen issues. For example, some online platforms for cosmetic procedures (e.g., Gengmei and Soyoung) offer optional add-on insurance that compensates for negative outcomes, such as operational failures and complications. However, the impact of add-on insurance, and ex post risk-reduction strategies in general, on the demand for professional services in online platforms is not yet well understood.

To <sup>fi</sup>ll in this research gap, this paper investigates whether and how the introduction of an ex post riskreduction strategy affects the demand for professional services in online platforms. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we study how the introduction of add-on insurance (see Online Appendix A for an example), a novel feature designed to cover the potential cost of unexpected issues, affects the sales of cosmetic procedures. We address the following three research questions:

1. How does the introduction of add-on insurance affect the demand for insured procedures on the platform? The answer to this question helps us understand whether this ex post risk-reduction strategy is effective for professional services.

2. Does the impact of add-on insurance on demand differ across insured procedures with different levels of risk? This investigation reveals whether the effectiveness of this ex post risk-reduction strategy is universal and, if it is not, how it varies across different types of professional services.

3. Does the introduction of add-on insurance have any spillover effect on competing procedures without insurance? The examination of the spillover effect is critical for understanding the impact of this new riskreduction strategy on the overall demand on a platform.

Our research context is one of the largest online platforms for cosmetic procedures in China, which offers 250 categories of procedures with different levels of risks. The platform initially did not provide any insurance for cosmetic procedures. In 2016, the platform started to offer optional add-on insurance underwritten by a third-party insurance company for a subset of procedures, and the selection of these procedures was determined by the platform and the insurer, rather than service providers. This policy change allows us to investigate the effect of insurance on the demand for insured and uninsured procedures in a difference-in-differences (DID) setting. We focus on procedures that were already available before the introduction of the insurance and divide them into three groups, that is, treatment, control, and spillover groups. The treatment group includes all the procedures that started to have the insurance option after the policy change, whereas the spillover group includes the uninsured procedures that are in the same categories as the treatment procedures (i.e., procedures serving the same purpose) and are thus competing directly with them. The control group includes procedures in the categories where none of the procedures have the insurance option.

Our DID analysis of the three groups generates two main <sup>fi</sup>ndings. First, while addressing our <sup>fi</sup>rst and second research questions, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the add-on insurance increases the demand for insured low-risk procedures, but not that for insured high-risk procedures. The asymmetric treatment effects of insurance suggest that insurance as a risk-reduction strategy can be effective on the demand for certain insured procedures but the effectiveness is not universal. Second, in regard to our third research question, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the introduction of insurance has a negative spillover effect on the demand for uninsured competitors in the same category, regardless of the risk level of the insured procedures. In particular, the two <sup>fi</sup>ndings taken together demonstrate that, when introduced for high-risk procedures, the insurance only decreases the demand for uninsured competitors without increasing the demand for insured procedures. Accordingly, this negative spill over effect goes beyond the typical demand cannibalization explanation, suggesting that the introduction of insurance can reduce the overall demand for high-risk procedures on the platform, which is further con<sup>fi</sup>rmed in our pseudopopulation-level analysis.

To address the concern that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings may be driven by unobserved confounders, we conduct an online controlled experiment among prospective consumers of cosmetic procedures in which the availability of insurance is randomized. We continue to <sup>fi</sup>nd that the availability of insurance has asymmetric effects on the purchase intention for high- and low-risk insured procedures and a negative spillover effect beyond demand cannibalization on uninsured substitutes.

We further investigate the mechanisms behind the asymmetric treatment effects and the negative spillover effect using participants’ perceived levels of risk and hedging that are measured in the experiment. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that the participants perceive a markedly higher level of hedging from insurance when the risk of procedures is low than when the risk is high, likely because the physical risk of high-risk procedures is harder to hedge against with insurance that focuses on <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk. The participants also perceive a higher level of risk for a procedure when the insurance is offered, echoing the cueing effect (Acquisti et al. 2017, Liang et al. 2022) that the presence of insurance reminds consumers of the potential risk. However, the increase in perceived risk is similar for high- and low-risk procedures. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the asymmetric treatment effects are driven by asymmetric hedging effects, rather than asymmetric cueing effects. Moreover, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the cueing effect also applies to uninsured competitors of insured procedures, which partially explains the negative spillover effect. Our further analysis shows that the expectation gap effect may also contribute to the negative spillover effect. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, the availability of insurance for insured procedures increases consumers expectations on the service level of similar procedures, which renders their uninsured competitors that do not live up to their new expectations less desirable.

Our work makes several important contributions to the IS literature. First, this paper addresses an emerging phenomenon in the natural progression of e-commerce from search and experience goods to credence goods. One prominent feature of credence goods is that the outcomes are often deeply consequential, and yet consumers lack the expertise to evaluate the outcomes even after purchase (Dulleck and Kerschbamer 2006). The increased complexity and uncertainty on the part of consumers yield new challenges that were previously unaddressed in e-commerce. Prior studies in the IS literature have investigated different risk-reduction strategies that enhance and facilitate the transaction of search and experience goods online, such as online reviews (Ba and Pavlou 2002, Li and Hitt 2008, Zhu and Zhang 2010), assurance seals (Ozpolat et al.<sup>¨</sup> 2013), and free product sampling (Lin et al. 2019). This paper contributes to the literature by empirically investigating whether and how a novel riskreduction strategy dedicated to credence goods affects the demand for professional services online.

Second, this paper documents a boundary condition on the effectiveness of insurance as a risk-reduction strategy that is empowered by online platforms to facilitate the digital transactions of credence goods. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that offering insurance signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly increases the sales of low-risk procedures, but not those of high-risk procedures. This <sup>fi</sup>nding highlights the importance of considering the nature of professional services when designing risk-reduction strategies. It is hard to come up with a one-size-<sup>fi</sup>ts-all strategy, as the scope and extent of risk associated with different services are different. To improve effectiveness, platforms may want to tailor their riskreduction strategies based on the speci<sup>fi</sup>c risks of different types of professional services.

Third, this paper reveals an intriguing “more can be less” phenomenon where adding a new riskreduction strategy to a subset of products on a digital platform can potentially elevate consumers’ perceived risk and expectation and reduce the overall demand for related products on the platform. Prior studies in the IS literature have investigated the common mechanisms behind the spillover effect of introducing a new feature to a subset of products, such as the cannibalization effect (Zheng et al. 2019, Krijestorac et al. 2020) and the awareness effect (Liang et al. 2019, Krijestorac et al. 2020). Nonetheless, these common mechanisms cannot explain the negative spillover effect we discover—the reduction in the overall demand on the platform. This paper sheds light on less-known mechanisms for negative spillover effects that can result in a reduction in overall demand, namely, the cueing effect and expectation gap effect rooted in consumer psychology. Platform operators should be aware of related effects that may yield unintended side effects while designing and evaluating risk-reduction strategies.

## 2. Literature Review

Perceived risk has long been recognized as a crucial factor in<sup>fl</sup>uencing a consumer’s product evaluation and purchase decision (Gemunden¨ 1985). To facilitate consumers purchase decisions, online platforms have taken a variety of measures to reduce consumers’ perceived risk, including both ex ante and ex post risk-reduction strategies.

Ex ante risk-reduction strategies operate by disclosing and facilitating the disclosure of information that can help consumers assess the quality of goods before purchase. One ubiquitous ex ante risk-reduction strategy is online reviews. It has been shown that online reviews can effectively reduce consumers’ perceived risk for conventional goods by providing information about other consumers’ experiences (Ba and Pavlou 2002, Zhu and Zhang 2010). Online reviews also work for professional services that are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to evaluate by individual consumers. For example, Lu and Rui (2018)

suggest that physicians with high ratings have signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly lower in-hospital mortality rates. Gao et al. (2015) <sup>fi</sup>nd that online ratings for high-quality physicians tend to be exaggerated, whereas low-quality physicians are less likely to be rated online. Two other examples of ex ante risk-reduction strategies are assurance seals and free product sampling. Ozpolat et al. (<sup>¨</sup> 2013) <sup>fi</sup>nd that the presence of assurance seals on e-commerce platforms increases consumers’ likelihood to convert. Lin et al. (2019) show that engaging in free product sampling can lead to higher product ratings.

Ex post risk-reduction strategies operate by compensating the loss or unsatisfactory experience of consumers after purchase. Some common examples of ex post strategies are warranty and return policies. It is well documented that warranty and return policies can reduce the perceived risk and in<sup>fl</sup>uence product demand (Anderson et al. 2009, Jindal 2015). In particular, Anderson et al. (2009) demonstrate that the value of a return policy is higher for products with higher uncertainty. However, because various contingencies of the outcomes of professional services are dif<sup>fi</sup>cult to list beforehand and the negative outcomes of professional services can be irreversible, it is hard to operationalize the warranty policy for professional services. The return policy does not apply either because professional services typically cannot be returned.

A third widely used ex post risk-reduction strategy is insurance. Insurance can be roughly classi<sup>fi</sup>ed as mandatory and optional based on whether it is required by law. For example, among the different components of auto insurance, liability insurance is typically mandatory, whereas roadside assistance and rental car reimbursement insurance are optional. This paper focuses on optional insurance that is designed as a choice, rather than a mandate. Optional insurance is also called add-on insurance, such as <sup>fl</sup>ight insurance for air travel (Eisner and Strotz 1961) and return insurance for online shopping (Zhang et al. 2021). Prior research on the relationship between insurance and the consumption of services focuses on the moral hazard problem (Einav et al. 2013), factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumers’ choice of insurance (Mehta et al. 2017), and the signaling effect of insurance (Zhang et al. 2021). However, the effects of add-on insurance, as an optional feature, on the demand for insured and uninsured products have not been examined yet. This paper strives to investigate whether and how add-on insurance can in<sup>fl</sup>uence the demand for products with versus without insurance.

## 3. Theoretical Background and Research Questions

In this section, we discuss the mechanisms through which the introduction of insurance can affect the demand for insured procedures and, importantly, their uninsured competitors.

## 3.1. Treatment Effect of Insurance on Insured Procedures

3.1.1. Hedging Effect. Similar to other types of professional services, cosmetic procedures are risky in nature and may lead to posttreatment side effects, infections, or even functional loss of a body part. Fixing or mitigating these negative outcomes may incu signi<sup>fi</sup>cant <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses to consumers. Naturally, add-on insurance can reduce the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk of consumers, as it is designed to protect consumers from potential <sup>fi</sup>nancial losses. According to a survey by an online platform for cosmetic procedures, most consumers buy insurance so that they can afford corrective cosmetic procedures if a procedure fails.<sup>3</sup> By increasing consumers’ ability to hedge against the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk associated with a cosmetic procedure, the insurance has the potential to increase the demand for the procedure.

3.1.2. Cueing Effect. The presence of the insurance may also cue people to think about the potential risks associated with the procedure, which can increase their perceived risk about the procedure. In particular, while examining the coverage of the insurance, consumers may obtain more detailed information about the potential negative consequences, such as complications, infections, and even loss of life, which they may otherwise pay less attention to. The elevated perceived risk due to these contextual cues can in turn lower consumers’ purchase intention for the insured procedures. The effects of contextual cues have been extensively studied in the literature on privacy (Acquisti et al. 2017, Liang et al. 2022).

Although the insurance is designed to increase consumers’ ability to hedge against the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk associated with cosmetic procedures, it is unclear whether this risk-reduction strategy is effective in boosting demand, given that it may also cue consumers about the potential negative consequences. Our <sup>fi</sup>rst research question thus examines whether the introduction of addon insurance on an online platform can indeed enhance demand for insured procedures on the platform.

## 3.2. Asymmetric Treatment Effects for High- and Low-Risk Procedures

As we discussed earlier, the impact of insurance on the demand for cosmetic procedures can be manifested through the hedging and cueing effects. Below, we argue that the strength of these two effects may vary with the risk level of the procedures, which can potentially lead to asymmetric treatment effects on high- and low-risk procedures.

On the one hand, the hedging effect of insurance is expected to be weaker for high-risk procedures than for low-risk procedures. Consumers typically consider risks from multiple dimensions, such as <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk and physical risk (Jacoby and Kaplan 1972). In our context, <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk results from the cost of the cosmetic procedures and the potential <sup>fi</sup>nancial burden to <sup>fi</sup>x unforeseen issues with the procedures, whereas physical risk refers to the health and safety concerns with cosmetic procedures. For low-risk procedures such as teeth whitening, the worst outcome is a waste of money, but these procedures are unlikely to negatively affect the appearance or cause physical damages to the consumers. That is, the physical risk of low-risk procedures is limited. However, for high-risk procedures such as breast augmentation, surgical failure or complications can negatively affect appearance and even lead to functional loss of a body part, suggesting that high-risk procedures involve signi<sup>fi</sup>cant physical risks. Although insurance can increase consumers’ ability to hedge against the <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk, it has no bearing on the physical risk. As a result, the hedging effect of insurance is likely weaker for highrisk procedures than for low-risk procedures.

On the other hand, the cueing effect is likely to be more pronounced for high-risk procedures than for low-risk procedures. Compared with low-risk procedures (e.g., teeth whitening), high-risk procedures (e.g., breast augmentation) are generally more complicated, have higher probabilities of failure, and can lead to more consequential negative outcomes. Therefore, the terms of the insurance for high-risk procedures involve more severe issues than those of low-risk procedures. As such, the presence of the insurance may lead prospective consumers of high-risk procedures to become aware of potential consequences, increasing their perceived risk, which in turn discourages them from attempting these procedures.

The above two arguments combined suggest that the insurance is expected to have a stronger hedging effect (increasing the ability to hedge and thus demand) and a weaker cueing effect (increasing the perceived risk and thus decreasing demand) for low-risk procedures than for high-risk procedures. Therefore, whereas the insurance may have a positive effect on the demand for low-risk procedures, the effect can be smaller, insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant, or even negative for high-risk procedures. This motivates our second empirical question of how the risk level of a procedure moderates the effect of add-on insurance on the demand for insured procedures.

## 3.3. Spillover Effect on Competing Uninsured Procedures

The introduction of add-on insurance not only affects the demand for insured procedures but may also affect that for competing uninsured procedures. Previous studies have shown that marketing efforts on a focal product may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the demand for its competitors, leading to a negative or positive spillover effect (Balachander and Ghose 2003, Chae et al. 2017). Below we discuss several mechanisms through which the introduction of insurance can affect the demand for uninsured competitors.

3.3.1. Cannibalization Effect. Conventionally, cannibalization refers to the reduction in demand due to the introduction of a new product from the same producer (Moorthy and Png 1992). Recently, it has also been used to explain the reduction in demand for competitors when the appeal of a focal product increases via marketing activities, such as promotions and seeded word of mouth (Dawes 2012, Chae et al. 2017). As insurance can increase the appeal of insured procedures to prospective consumers by reducing their perceived risk (as a risk mitigator), the insured procedures may cannibalize the demand for their uninsured competitors, resulting in a negative spillover effect.

3.3.2. Awareness Effect. Consumer awareness is the <sup>fi</sup>rst step in the consumer decision-making process. Inexperienced decision makers tend to choose brands they are aware of (Hoyer and Brown 1990). In practice, marketers often use advertising and promotions to increase the awareness of their products (Erdem and Sun 2002, Balachander and Ghose 2003). These marketing efforts can increase the awareness of products from the same brand (Erdem and Sun 2002, Balachander and Ghose 2003), as well as competing products with similar characteristics (Janakiraman et al. 2009). Similarly, the increased appeal of insured procedures because of the new insurance option may increase the general awareness of the respective procedure categories, through increased search activities and/or increased volume of word of mouth, driving up the demand for uninsured procedures in the same categories.

3.3.3. Expectation Gap Effect. According to prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky 1979), the value of a product depends on not only its absolute value, but also its deviation from a reference point. When con sumers are making purchase decisions, they often use one product as a reference product and evaluate the alternatives against this reference product (Hardie et al. 1993). In our context, with the emergence of addon insurance as a risk mitigator for one procedure, consumers may use this procedure as a reference product for similar procedures and form a new expectation (Koszegi and Rabin¨ 2006). Therefore, procedures that do not offer the insurance may not live up to consumers’ new expectations, suppressing their demand, and leading to a negative spillover effect. Different from the cannibalization effect, the expectation gap effect only decreases the demand for uninsured competing procedures and does not increase the demand for insured procedures.

3.3.4. Cueing Effect. The cueing effect mentioned earlier may also in<sup>fl</sup>uence the demand for the uninsured competitors of insured procedures, as the introduction of insurance to insured procedures may broadly remind consumers of the risk of all similar procedures, including their competitors without insurance. As a result, the demand for uninsured competitors may decrease with the introduction of insurance because of an elevated perceived risk. Similar to the expectation gap effect, the cueing effect can also suppress the demand for competitors without increasing the demand for insured procedures. In fact, the cueing effect is supposed to reduce the demand for both insured procedures and their uninsured competitors.

Among the four mechanisms discussed above, the cannibalization and awareness effects associated with a new product design have been well studied, whereas the expectation gap and cueing effects have received little attention in the context of digital platforms. The latter two effects, however, are critically important because they may yield unintended side effects and overall lower demand for the platform.

Considering these mechanisms with mixed effects on demand, our third research question examines whether the add-on insurance has any spillover effect on competing uninsured procedures and, if it does, what drives the spillover effect.

## 4. Research Context and Data 4.1. Research Context

To understand whether and how add-on insurance affects the demand for professional services, we leverage a policy change in one of the largest online platforms for cosmetic procedures in China. The platform allows customers to make appointments for cosmetic procedures online without visiting the service providers (hospitals or clinics), for which they have to pay a deposit that varies with the cost of the procedure. In developing countries like China, because of the lack of an effective medical liability insurance system, hospitals/clinics typically do not purchase any liability insurance and hence are not incentivized to take responsibility for negative consequences of their medical services (e.g., unsatisfactory outcomes, complications, infections, and malpractice; Liebman 2013, Li et al. 2014). As a result, consumers undertake substantial risks when shopping for cosmetic procedures online.

To protect customers from potential negative consequences, the platform started to offer insurance as an add-on service for a subset of procedures in three broad categories (eye-related surgeries, nose-related surgeries, and injection of Botox and hyaluronic acid) in March 2016.<sup>4</sup> This add-on insurance is a risk-reduction strategy exclusive to online customers because of the absence of similar insurance products in the of<sup>fl</sup>ine clinic setting. The platform partners with a third-party insurance company that serves as the underwriter of the insurance. The subset of eligible procedures was chosen by the platform and the insurance company, rather than by any individual hospitals/clinics providing the procedures. The categories of procedures eligi ble for insurance remain unchanged in our observation window. The insurance is optional, and customers may purchase it as an add-on while making an appointment for a procedure offering this option. The cost of the insurance is CNY 19.9 (about USD 3) for nonsurgical procedures and CNY 99 (about USD 15) for surgical procedures.<sup>5</sup> Insurance claims are <sup>fi</sup>led directly to the insurance company, and the payout depends on the severity of negative consequences. Typically, the maxi mum payout is enough to cover the cost of the procedure or that of a remedy procedure, if any. Note that the platform realizes revenues from information services (e.g., advertising) and the sales of cosmetic proce dures, but not from the sales of insurance.

We collected all the procedures listed on the platform and their transaction logs (including the time of each appointment as well as a partially masked ID of the consumer making the appointment) on the platform retrospectively in January 2018. There are 12,747 procedures with at least one appointment. To ensure that the procedure is available on this platform before and after the introduction of the add-on insurance, we focus on 1,321 procedures with at least one appointment both before and after the introduction. Note that these procedures represent 97% of procedures that were available on the platform prior to March 2016. We construct a panel data set with monthly sales of each procedure 12 months before and after March 2016, namely, over a 25-month window from March 2015 to March 2017. The panel is unbalanced, as not all procedures were available on the platform at the beginning of our observation window. The resulting data set is an unbalanced panel with 25,204 observations, which cov ers procedures from 140 different categories (e.g., laser teeth whitening, fat transfer breast augmentation, and mini-incision double eyelid). On the platform, a category is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as a set of procedures with the same functional objective and operation method (e.g., injection, laser, or surgery) but offered by different providers. Therefore, procedures in the same category are directly competing with each other.

## 4.2. Variables

The introduction of add-on insurance for a subset of procedures allows us to investigate the effect of insurance on the demand for insured and uninsured procedures in a DID setting. To answer our three research questions, we construct the following variables in our analysis:

Sales. The demand for each cosmetic procedure is measured as its monthly sales, namely, the number of new appointments made each month. This variable is our dependent variable.

After. We create a dummy variable on a monthly basis, which is one after March 2016 and zero before March 2016, to indicate whether the add-on insurance is available on the platform in a given month. Because we do not know exactly on which day of March 2016 the insurance was introduced, the dummy variable is not well de<sup>fi</sup>ned for the month of March 2016. In our analysis, we remove data for this month.

Treatment and spillover dummies. We divide procedures into three groups: treatment, control, and spillover groups. The treatment group includes all the insured procedures (i.e., those procedures having the insurance option when it became available on the platform), whereas the spillover group includes uninsured procedures that are in the same categories as the insured procedures, namely, the competitors of the insured procedures. The control group includes uninsured procedures in the categories that do not have any procedures insured. Given that the procedures in the control group are not directly competing with those in the treatment or spillover group, the control group is a good benchmark to capture the trend of sales in the absence of insurance. We use two dummy variables to indicate whether a procedure belongs to the treatment or spillover group, respectively.

Risk. Given that procedures in the same category have identical functional objectives and operation methods, their perceived risk levels by customers are likely to be similar. Therefore, we measure the risk of procedures at the category level. We construct two measures for the risk of each category. First, following the theory on categorical perception of risks (Lloyd 2001), we consider surgical and nonsurgical procedures as procedures with high and low risks, respectively. This two-level classi<sup>fi</sup>cation is widely used in the industry, including on the focal platform. In general, surgical procedures are considered to be riskier than nonsurgical ones because they typically involve more operations during the treatment and are more demanding in posttreatment care. In addition to this binary measure, we also invite users on a forum for cosmetic procedures, who might well be prospective customers, to rate the perceived risk level of each category on a <sup>fi</sup>ve-point Likert scale (very low to very high). We obtain ratings from 59 users on average for each category and then use the average rating, a continuous variable, to measure the risk of the category (please refer to Online Appendix B for more details about the survey).

Other control variables. We use the number of reviews for each procedure to control for the impact of online reviews as an ex ante risk-reduction strategy. We do not include the ratings of procedures as a control because there is little variation in the valence of ratings (more than 90% of ratings are <sup>fi</sup>ve on a scale of one to <sup>fi</sup>ve). We use the age of a procedure (i.e., the number of months since it was <sup>fi</sup>rst available on the platform) to control for the potential temporal pattern in sales over a procedure’s life cycle. In addition, we use the number of com petitors (i.e., procedures in the same category with the focal procedure), the average sales of competitors, and the average number of reviews of competitors to control for the potential competition effect. 5.2.3. Overall Effect for the Platform. Although our sample includes nearly all the procedures that were available on the platform at the time the insurance was launched, the number of procedures on the platform grew by more than 10 times by the time we collected the data. A natural question is whether our <sup>fi</sup>ndings on the 1,321 procedures (selected procedures) extend to new procedures that were added to the platform after the launch of insurance (unselected procedures). By comparing the summary statistics of procedure-level covariates, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the unselected procedures were not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different from the selected procedures overall (see Online Appendix C, Section C.1, for details).

To provide some insights into the economic value of insurance to the platform, we run additional analyses to estimate how the monthly sales and revenues of procedures on the platform change because of the introduction of insurance, where the monthly revenue of a procedure is de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the number of sold units multiplied by its price. ## 6. Controlled Experiment

The empirical analysis of the observational data documents two intriguing <sup>fi</sup>ndings (i.e., the asymmetric treatment effects and the negative spillover effect beyond demand cannibalization) but does not explain why they occur. To shed light on the underlying mechanisms behind these <sup>fi</sup>ndings, and also to address the concern that these <sup>fi</sup>ndings from observational data may be driven by unobserved confounding factors, we design a randomized online controlled experiment. The participants of our experiment are recruited from Weibo, the largest microblogging platform in China, with over 500 million monthly active users.<sup>7</sup> Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we used the paid promotion feature of Weibo (akin to promoted tweets on Twitter) to push our recruiting material to users who are interested in cosmetic procedures, namely, users who have followed the focal platform, famous cosmetic surgery physicians, or reputed cosmetic surgery clinics/ hospitals on Weibo. Ninety-three percent of recruited participants indicated that they have visited online platforms for cosmetic procedures before, suggesting that they are indeed prospective customers.

## 6.1. Experiment Design

In the experiment, we ask participants to evaluate two paired procedures that are presented to them on the same page. For ease of exposition, we refer to the <sup>fi</sup>rst procedure in each pair as the focal procedure (shown at the top) and the second procedure as the companion procedure (shown at the bottom). For the focal procedure, we randomly manipulate whether the add-on insurance is available. For the companion procedure, we randomly manipulate whether it is a substitute for the focal procedure. This manipulation allows us to mimic the DID design in the observational study in an experimental setting and demonstrate that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are not driven by contaminated control procedures. In addition, we further manipulate whether a participant is exposed to high-risk or low-risk procedures. Table 7 summarizes the $\phantom { - } 2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial design, which leads to eight experiment groups. Participants are randomly assigned to one of the eight groups and are presented two pairs of procedures as per the design for the group they are assigned to.

To ensure that the paired focal and companion procedures in Groups 1, 2, 5, and 6 are substitutes for each other, each pair of procedures displayed in these groups includes two identical procedures offered by different service providers. The focal procedures used in Groups 3, 4, 7, and 8 are the same as those in Groups 1, 2, 5, and 6, but the companion procedures are replaced by noncompeting procedures that focus on a different body part compared with the focal procedures.<sup>8</sup> The only difference between the odd and even groups is that the focal procedures in the even groups have the add-on insurance.

Within the 2 2 2 factorial design, we further consider two additional randomizations. First, for every pair of substitutable procedures, which of them is chosen as the focal procedure is randomized. Second, to address the concern that the treatment effect of insurance may be confounded by the insurance premium in the observational study, we prepare three price designs for the insurance and randomly select one of them when insurance is offered for the focal procedures in the even groups. In the <sup>fi</sup>rst design, the insurance is offered for free. In the second design, the price information of the insurance is hidden. In the third design, the price of the insurance is a <sup>fl</sup>at rate (i.e., CNY 19.9) regardless of the risk level of the procedure. With the two additional randomizations, each of the four odd groups has two subgroups, whereas each of the four even groups has six subgroups. Participants are randomly assigned to the eight groups and then further randomly assigned to the subgroups within each group.

## 6.2. Treatment and Spillover Effects Estimated from the Experiment

In our experiment design, the treatment and spillover effects of insurance can be estimated by two different empirical strategies. The <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical strategy identi<sup>fi</sup>es the treatment and spillover effects by exploiting the randomization of insurance availability between odd and even groups (e.g., Group 1 versus Group 2 for high-risk procedures, and Group 5 versus Group 6 for low-risk procedures). The second empirical strategy uses a two-period DID analysis on the eight experimental groups. We focus on the <sup>fi</sup>rst empirical strategy because it relies only on the randomization being properly implemented, and the interpretation of results is straightforward. In Online Appendix E, Section E.3, we report consistent results from the DID analysis.

Next, we explain how we identify the treatment and spillover effects of insurance using the randomized availability of insurance in odd versus even groups. Take Groups 1 and 2 as an example. For a focal proce dure shown to the participants in Groups 1 and 2, the only difference is that it has the add-on insurance in Group 2, with everything else being identical, including the position of the procedure and the paired companion procedure. Consequently, any difference between the purchase intention for this focal procedure in Groups 1 and 2 must be caused by the availability of insurance. Similarly, for a companion procedure shown to the participants in Groups 1 and 2, the only difference is that the focal procedure paired with it has the insurance in Group 2, with everything else being identical. Hence, any difference in the average purchase intention for the same companion procedure in Groups 1 and 2 can be attributed to the spillover effect of the insurance added to the focal procedure. The same logic applies to Groups 3 and 4, Groups 5 and 6, and Groups 7 and 8.

Therefore, we can estimate the average treatment/ spillover effect of insurance by regressing the purchase intention for the focal/companion procedures over the insurance availability of the focal procedures (HasInsurance), controlling for procedure-level <sup>fi</sup>xed effects and the demographic attributes of participants.

## 6.3. Mechanisms

6.3.1. Mechanisms for Asymmetric Treatment Effects. In Section 3.2, we provide two potential explanations for the asymmetric treatment effects of insurance on the demand for cosmetic procedures: (1) the hedging effect is weaker for high-risk procedures than for lowrisk procedures, and (2) the cueing effect is stronger for high-risk procedures than for low-risk procedures. If the <sup>fi</sup>rst explanation is true, the increase in the perceived level of hedging due to the availability of insurance should be lower for high-risk procedures than for low-risk procedures. If the second explanation is true, the increase in the perceived level of risk due to the availability of insurance should be higher for high-risk procedures than for low-risk procedures. To investigate which explanation is plausible, if not both, we conduct a mediation analysis with our experimental data, using the self-reported perceived risk and perceived hedging as mediators. To facilitate comparison, we report the results separately for highand low-risk procedures, as summarized in Table 9.

The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of HasInsurance in columns (1) and (5) con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the insurance is effective only for lowrisk procedures. The coef<sup>fi</sup>cients of HasInsurance in columns (2) and (6) are very close to each other, suggesting that the addition of insurance has similar effects on the perceived risk for high- and low-risk procedures. A formal test using interaction analysis in the pooled sample with both high- and low-risk procedures shows that the effects of insurance on the perceived risk are not signi<sup>fi</sup>cantly different for high- and low-risk procedures (see Online Appendix E, Section E.5). Therefore, the explanation that the asymmetric treatment effects are driven by asymmetric cueing effects is not supported by the data.

The expectation gap effect resulting from the reference point theory is likely another driver for the negative spillover effect; that is, the introduction of insurance to insured procedures increases customers expectations of their uninsured competitors, rendering the latter less desirable because of un<sup>fi</sup>lled expectations. Because it is hard to measure consumers’ expectations, we cannot directly test this explanation. In Online Appendix F, leveraging the observational data, we provide indirect evidence for the expectation gap effect by showing that the negative spillover effect is weaker when customer expectation is supposed to play a less prominent role in the decision-making process. Speci<sup>fi</sup>- cally, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the negative spillover effect diminishes for procedures whose perceived risk varies substantially across prospective consumers, a situation where it is hard for consumers to form a clear expectation of the risk and utilize that to aid decisions.

6.3.3. Summary of Empirical Findings and Identified Mechanisms. Tables 11 and 12 summarize our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings from both the observational study and experiment and the identi<sup>fi</sup>ed mechanisms through the mediation analyses on the experimental data. In a nutshell, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the hedging effect and the cueing effect are both in play. The asymmetric hedging effects lead to the asymmetric treatment effects on high- and low-risk procedures, whereas the symmetric cueing effect partially explains the symmetric negative spillover effects on high- and low-risk procedures. The analysis in Online Appendix F provides indirect evidence for another possible driver behind the negative spillover effect, the expectation gap effect.

## 7. Conclusion

The emergence of online platforms for professional services (e.g., cosmetic procedures) represents a natural progression of e-commerce from search and experience goods to credence goods. One prominent feature of credence goods is the potential for highly negative consequences when acquired, which is magni<sup>fi</sup>ed by the uncertainty of the outcome consumers face before purchase and their lack of expertise to evaluate the outcomes even after purchase. The increased complexity and uncertainty on the part of consumers yield new challenges that previously have been unaddressed in e-commerce. Therefore, designing effective risk-reduction strategies is crucial for facilitating online transactions of professional services.

In this paper, leveraging the introduction of a new feature of an online platform for cosmetic procedures, we set out to investigate whether and how the addition of a risk-reduction technique (i.e., add-on insurance) in<sup>fl</sup>uences the demand for professional services (i.e., insured and uninsured procedures) on the platform. Interestingly, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the insurance is effective only for low-risk procedures and not for high-risk procedures, which reveals a limitation of this add-on insurance as a risk-reduction strategy. More importantly, the insurance for focal procedures has a negative spillover effect on their uninsured competitors and the resulting demand reduction goes beyond demand cannibalization, at least for high-risk procedures. Using an online controlled experiment, we <sup>fi</sup>nd consistent results, con<sup>fi</sup>rming that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings from the observational study are unlikely driven by unobserved confounders. Moreover, the results from the experiment further show that the asymmetric treatment effects of the insurance on high- and low-risk procedures arise from the asymmetric hedging effects of the insurance on procedures at different risk levels. The negative spillover effect partially results from the increased perceived risk on uninsured procedures when their competitors become insured (i.e., the cueing effect). Our additional analysis of the observational data suggests that the expectation gap effect may also contribute to the negative spillover effect.

Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings have important implications for professional service platforms to design, deploy, and evaluate their risk-reduction strategies. Although our observations for the empirical analysis are collected from a speci<sup>fi</sup>c online platform, the consistency between the results from the observational data and those from the online controlled experiment that abstracts away platform-speci<sup>fi</sup>c information suggests that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings can be generalized beyond speci<sup>fi</sup>c platform settings. The identi<sup>fi</sup>ed mechanisms underlying the asymmetric treatment effect and the negative spillover effect that originate from consumer psychology have the potential to transcend beyond context and illuminate valuable insights for other platforms for professional services, such as business or legal consultancy, personal health services, <sup>fi</sup>nancial services, and education. When these platforms design riskreduction strategies to facilitate online transactions, it is important for them to recognize not only the intended hedging effect that these strategies are designed for but also the counterproductive cueing and expectation gap effects that may be triggered by these strategies unintentionally. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings provide the following practical implications.

First, the asymmetric treatment effects of insurance on high- and low-risk cosmetic procedures that we identify suggest that the effectiveness of a riskreduction strategy such as insurance may not be universal. The design of risk-reduction strategies may need to be tailored to the speci<sup>fi</sup>c risks of different professional services to be effective. For services with multidimensional risks (e.g., <sup>fi</sup>nancial risk and physical risk), platforms may want to combine multiple risk-reduction instruments that can alleviate different types of risk.

Second, the negative spillover effect of insurance that we discover demonstrates that the introduction of a risk-reduction strategy to a subset of professional services may negatively affect the demand for other uncovered competing services. Furthermore, the negative spillover effect may go beyond redistributing demand from uncovered services to covered services and may yield a demand contraction for uncovered services without increasing the demand for covered services, like what we <sup>fi</sup>nd for cosmetic procedures in the high-risk categories. Overlooking the negative spillover effect beyond demand redistribution while introducing new policies can be detrimental for the platform. To avoid the potential negative spillover effect, the platform may consider offering new policies indiscriminately to all services competing in the same categories or focus on service categories with little demand overlap with other categories.

Third, our platform-level analysis shows that the introduction of insurance to a subset of cosmetic procedures in selective categories increases the overall sales and revenues of all procedures in these categories by 11.3% and 15.3%, respectively, which suggests that the insurance is a successful risk-reduction strategy overall. However, in contrast to low-risk categories that bene<sup>fi</sup>t from the insurance, the overall sales and revenues of high-risk categories decline with the introduction of insurance because of the absence of a positive treatment effect on insured procedures and the presence of negative spillover effect on uninsured procedures. This <sup>fi</sup>nding suggests that when a platform introduces a risk-reduction strategy, it is possible that not all categories respond positively to its addition. The platform may want to expand the coverage of the strategy in selective categories that immediately bene<sup>fi</sup>t from its addition and seek ways to improve the effectiveness of the strategy in other categories to overturn the possible negative effects.

Last but not least, by investigating the mechanisms behind the negative spillover effect, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the add-on insurance, as a risk-reduction strategy, may unintentionally increase the perceived risk of prospective customers on both insured and uninsured professional services, by reminding consumers of the risks associated with these services. In addition, the introduction of insurance to certain services may increase consumers expectations of like services and make competing services that are uninsured less desirable. These mechanismlevel insights call attention to potential unintended side effects of new features that may in<sup>fl</sup>uence consumer perception and urge platforms to evaluate these side effects and explore ways to avoid them when designing and testing new features, as otherwise more new features can potentially mean less value to consumers.

Our work has its limitations. The reliability of our empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings based on observational data hinges on the parallel trend assumption, which may not be true in the posttreatment period. We alleviate this concern by conducting several robustness analyses including CEM, the synthetic control method, and a controlled online experiment, all of which produce consistent <sup>fi</sup>ndings. Moreover, although the platform uses both ex ante and ex post risk-reduction strategies (i.e., online reviews and the add-on insurance), we do not compare the effectiveness of these two types of strategies. Future research can examine the differential effects of these two risk-reduction strategies through experimentation. In addition, we do not observe whether customers of insured procedures bought the insurance while making appointments. When such data become available, it might be interesting to further study the differential effects of this optional risk-reduction strategy on consumers who purchase the insurance and those who do not purchase the insurance. Finally, our experiment tests only two speci<sup>fi</sup>c mechanisms underlying the asymmetric treatment effects and the negative spillover effect (i.e., the hedging effect and the cueing effect). Future research may further investigate the other mechanisms that may be at work, such as the awareness effect and the cannibalization effect, and their relative contributions.
