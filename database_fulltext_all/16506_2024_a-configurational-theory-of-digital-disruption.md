# A configurational theory of digital disruption

Sha Huang | Andrew Burton-Jones | Dongming Xu

UQ Business School, the University of Queensland, Brisbane, Australia

Correspondence Sha Huang, UQ Business School, the University of Queensland, Brisbane, Australia. Email: s.huang@business.uq.edu.au

## Abstract

Digital Disruption (DD) has become a hot topic in recent years, yet detailed research is surprisingly lacking. The literature offers almost no insights into how DD occurs at the industry level and what industry factors influence it. This paper advances knowledge of DD by developing and testing a configurational theory. Using a multimethod research design, we identify two types of DD and four industry factors (downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict, transferability of core competitive elements, and industry player size) that contingently lead to the different types of DD. We integrate those findings into a configurational theory that describes causal recipes of how these factors or conditions combine to produce the outcome of transformational DD and destructive DD. The theory offers important implications for researchers and practitioners. The research also contributes methodologically by demonstrating the merits of combining grounded theory with qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) to expand the theory-building potential of QCA.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Digital disruption (DD) is rewriting every aspect of our lives, including how we work, socialise, and communicat (Park. 2017: Weill & Woerner. 2018). DD not only affects firms: it sweeps across industries (Productivity Commission, 2016). Streaming services such as Netflix have revolutionised the entertainment landscape, leading to a significant decline and eventual collapse of the traditional video rental industry (Kelly, 2020). Ride-sharing companies such as Uber not only introduced a new and competitive transportation option but also drasticall affected the traditional taxi industry (Cannon & Summers, 2014). Electric mobility and advancements in self driving technologies by companies such as Tesla have likewise reshaped the automotive industry (McKinsey & Company, 2021).

Although DD has gained attention from both researchers and practitioners in recent years, detailed research is still surprisingly lacking. To date, DD research has mainly taken a firm-level perspective (Bughin & Van Zeebroeck, 2017; Tan et al., 2015; Templeton et al., 2019; Utesheva et al., 2016). There is a paucity of DD research from an industry-level perspective (Riemer & Johnston, 2019; Skog et al., 2018). It is critical to develop a bette understanding of DD at the industry level because DD not only topples individual incumbent firms but also cause upheaval across industries. Strategies that lead to an individual firm's survival (e.g., niche marketing or diversification) do not necessarily work for an industry as a whole. Despite the success of a few individual firms in a traditiona industry, the industry itself may be largely swept away if too many of its constituent firms are unable to survive (Kmia, 2018).

An important but overlooked facet of DD is that its effect across industries can vary. Some industries embrace the fundamental changes wrought by digital innovations far better than others. However, researchers know very lit tle about how DD occurs at the industry level and what industry factors influence it. Not only does this gap constrain scholarship, but it also means that researchers are ill-equipped to assist organisational leaders and industry-leve policy-makers. To fill this gap, this study addresses the following research questions:

RQ1. How does digital disruption unfold amongst different industries?

## RQ2. What are the patterns or configurations of industry factors that bring about different types of digital disruption?

We address this new inquiry using a multi-method, three-stage research design. We first conducted a grounded the ory study (Glaser & Strauss, 2017), using a sample of 21 digitally disrupted industries in Australia to identify key concepts of DD from an industry perspective. Next we performed a qualitative comparative analysis (QCA) to study the configurations of different types of DD, and build a configurational theory with causal recipes that explain how the relevant factors or conditions combine into configurations that produce two types of DD. Finally, we carried out subsequent theory testing, using a holdout sample, to validate our hypothesized causal recipes. Configurational theories are a highly suitable system of inquiry for studying complex digital phenomena (Benbya et al., 2020; Bharadwa et al., 2013; El Sawy et al., 2010; Nambisan et al., 2017; Park & Mithas, 2020; Yoo et al., 2012). Adopting the config urational approach enabled us to better identify the patterns (i.e., specific configurations of industry-level causal con ditions) that exist across different industries that have undergone DD, thus allowing for more complex causa relationships to surface.

This research makes several contributions. First, we develop a novel, “native” theory of digital disruption. It is the first theory of its kind that explains how different types of DD can be achieved through configurations of indus try factors. The framework provides a foundation for further theoretical development of DD. Second, we offer methodological contribution by demonstrating the merits of a novel multi-method design that uses grounded theor with OCA to develop and test an inductive configurational theory, which would be difficult to achieve using conven tional approaches. Third, our research contributes to practice by providing executives and policy-makers with means to learn how DD can unfold in an industry, revealing the industries in which DD is likely to be problematic and offering useful guidance for learning how to take advantage of, or respond to, different types of DD

The paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2, we provide relevant background concepts and literature on both DD and our theory-building approach. In Section 3, we then explain our overall multi-method, three-stage researc design. In Section 4, we describe our grounded theory study (Stage 1) and its findings. In Section 5, we discuss th analysis of multiple configurations of DD using QCA (Stage 2) and suggest our theoretical framework and proposi tions. In Section 6, we present the results of testing our emergent theory (Stage 3). Finally, in Section 7, we addres the research's contributions, implications, and limitations

## 2 | CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Digital disruption

The term “digital disruption” was first introduced by Schmidt and Cohen (2010), with the DD concept beginning to emerge as a prominent phenomenon in recent years. DD is generally perceived by information systems (IS) scholars as a type of digital technology-induced environmental turbulence capable of producing industry-level upheava (El Sawy et al., 2010; Karimi & Walter, 2015; Tan et al., 2015). However, the fragmented literature on DD lacks com mon agreement on exactly what DD is and what it encompasses (Baiyere & Hukal, 2020; Strohmeier, 2020).

To conceptually clarify the nature of DD and to evaluate extant research on the topic of DD, we conducted a systematic review of the growing DD literature. We reviewed 108 relevant articles, and synthesised existing knowledge concerning DD into six levels of analysis: artefact, individual, organisational, industry, economic, and societal. More details of the literature review are presented in Supplementary Appendix A.<sup>1</sup> Based on our analysis of explicit definitions of DD, as well as our literature review on the essential properties and characteristics of the DD phenomenon, we propose the following definition of DD tailored to the industry level of analysis:

The sociotechnical process by which digital innovations come to fundamentally alter the established logics of value creation and/or the established relations of value creation of an industry.

Industry here refers to a group of companies related through their primary business activities. DD is a sociotechnica process as opposed to a purely technical process, such as digitization, which refers only to the technical conversion of analogous information into binary digits (Strohmeier, 2020). DD integrates both technical (digital technologies and human phenomena, thus constituting the broader sociotechnical concepts. Digital innovations refer to the creation of (and consequent change in) market offerings, business processes, or models that result from the use of digita technology (Nambisan et al., 2017). This definition emphasises both the digital and innovative properties of DD to distinguish it from other related concepts (Baiyere & Hukal, 2020).

The digital property indicates that the disruption is induced, enabled, or triggered by the unique attributes of dig ital technology (Baiyere & Hukal, 2020). Thus, DD differs from the related phenomenon of disruptive innovation because the latter can be triggered by non-digital innovations (Christensen, 1997). In addition, only a small portion of the digital disruptions follow the process explained in Christensen's (1997) theory (Downes & Nunes, 2013; Skog et al., 2018). Although DD and disruptive innovation have some commonalities, disruptive innovation is neither suffi cient nor necessary for DD to take place.

The innovative property indicates that DD must introduce something new into the domain being disrupted. It is also clear that digital innovation is a necessary but not sufficient condition for DD to occur. All DDs must come from digital innovation but not all digital innovation results in DDs. For example, digital innovations that provide incremen tal improvements do not result in DD (Baiyere & Hukal, 2020)

DD suggests fundamental alterations in how the industry operates before and after the disruption. The primary alterations, we argue, consist of the re-establishment of the dominant logics of value creation and/or the dominant relations of value creation of the affected industry. In recent years, the term “value logics” has increasingly been linked to DD. Researchers have suggested that digital product architectures create new dominant logics for organising innovation (Skog et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010). We expand on the conceptualization of value logics (Skog et al., 2018) as a key element of DD, defining an industry's “logics of value creation” as the foundational rationales underlying solution conceptualization and critical resource allocation decisions to meet customer needs in exchange for economic benefit. Therefore, a change in an industry's dominant logics of value creation implies a change in the solutions required (or perceived by stakeholders to be required) to meet customer needs (Thornton et al., 2012). For example, in the context of photography, customers have a need to retain images of the past for safekeeping. To meet this need, the old logics of value creation offered customers chemically processed prints of photographs in physica form and charged customers on a per-item basis. The new logics of value creation offers customers images in a digital form that are saved on a cloud storage platform in exchange for a monthly fee.

Fundamental change can also be achieved by altering the dominant relations of value creation in an industry. The term “relations of value creation” is based on the related concept of “relations of production,” which is com monly used in economic and sociological theory (Edwards, 1978; Seddon, 1978). The term “relations of production” refers to the relationships between people in the process of production. Any socially productive process involves the relations that people form with one another to fulfil their material needs. Examples of such relations include those between bosses and workers, capitalists and the proletariat, landowners and farmhands, and members of a cooperative (Ritzer, 2004). We modify and extend this concept as a dimension of value creation and define the “relations of value creation as the relationship structure that determines how the actors and tasks within an industry ecosystem are organised around the production of something valuable for the customers (which may be individuals or an organi sations) in return for economic benefit

To illustrate how DD can alter the dominant relations of value creation, consider the emergence of ride-sharing platforms such as Uber in the taxi industry. In this case, the logics of value creation did not change with the emergence of these platforms, as both ride-sharing and traditional taxi services have continued to offer customers a simi lar solution: driving them individually in a vehicle along a road from point A to point B. However, the old relationship structure, where services were organised around a taxi company, has been significantly altered by ride-sharing plat forms. These disruptive platforms have created a substitutive relationship structure and networks of providers out side of the taxi industry. As a result, consumers no longer need to rely on taxis for door-to-door transport at shor notice.

Although the logics and relations of value creation are not the only factors that undergo profound changes fol lowing DDs, our review of the literature utilising abductive theorising logics (Sætre & Van De Ven, 2021) suggest they can be used as the foundation from which to understand how other things have also changed (e.g., custome expectations, technological infrastructure, and approach to market).

With respect to profound changes, DD is often confused with another term —digital transformation. These two concepts have many similarities as they are both used to describe significant digital changes. However, they differ in several ways.

First, the two concepts differ in their mode of change. Changes can occur continuously or episodically. An epi sodic change is infrequent and intentional, whereas continuous change involves ongoing, evolving, and cumulativ change (Hanelt et al., 2021). Digital transformations can occur episodically or continuously, whereas DD is only episodic.

Second, compared to digital transformation, DD has a more threat-oriented overtone. While digital transformation is often used in a positive way, to describe a journey to improve organisations across an industry, DD represents a clear threat because it can render current value creation approaches obsolete in ways that digital transformatio need not (Hanelt et al., 2021; Strohmeier, 2020; Vial, 2019)

Third, although DD must involve a fundamental change in historically sustainable logics and/or the relations of value creation, a digital transformation does not have to drive such a change. For example, the dental services indus try has been digitally transformed by the introduction of computer-based operational software but has not under gone DD because neither the logics nor the relations of value creation have been altered in a major way. In this case, what has been substantially altered is the “method of value creation,” from a mostly manual process to a digitalenabled process. Although the logical conceptualization of the final solution (various dental services carried out by dentists) has not changed much, the workflow of dentists and the tools used by dentists have been significantl transformed by the introduction of digital technologies. This type of digital transformation is usually voluntarily and intentionally initiated by the industry players to improve performance (Vial, 2019), rather than to respond to the exis tential threat posed by DD.

Nonetheless, while these two concepts are different, they are not mutually exclusive. For instance, DD and digital transformation can overlap when incumbents strategically respond to changes in an industry and engage in the process of transformation to survive in the new business environment. In other words, DD and digital transformation are not entirely different. They are both associated with the change caused by digital technologies. Digital transfor mation need not have all elements of DD and vice versa

It is clear from our literature review (see Supplementary Appendix A at https://osf.io/kpajx) that DD has been understudied at the industry level. The extant literature has heavily focused on firm-level analysis, emphasising the outcomes of, and responses to, DD for individual incumbent firms. There is still much to learn regarding DD from an industry-level perspective. We need to see both the forest and the trees because DD does not only topple individua incumbent firms but upends whole industries. Strategies that lead to an individual firm's survival (e.g., niche marketing or diversification) do not necessarily work for industries as a whole. For instance, while Fujifilm has been able to survive individually at the firm level by diversifying into other industries such as pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, th photographic film manufacturing industry is still unable to survive as a whole and is edging toward obsolescence (Kmia, 2018).

Through the literature review, we identified a key, overlooked facet of DD, which is that its effects across industries can vary. Some industries embrace the fundamental changes wrought by digital innovations far better than others. However, existing literature offers little to no explanation of what industry factors influence such different outcomes. To the best of our knowledge. there is no previous study that differentiates DD into categories or typolo gies. The few existing industry-level studies are usually confined to one industry (Karimi & Walter, 2015), thus offer ing few insights into what factors are important across all industries

The dominant theory in the DD literature is the disruptive innovation theory(Christensen. 1997: Christensen et al., 2018). However, it has been criticised for failing to reflect the reality of DD (Downes & Nunes, 2013; Skog et al., 2018). For instance, Christensen's (1997) theory suggests that entrants achieve disruption by entering the mar ket with cheap, inferior offerings. However, digital disruptors' profiles and processes differ dramatically from thos of Christensen's accounts. Apple's iPhone was, from its introduction, a superior and more expensive alternative to the mobile phones produced by market leaders, such as Nokia, Motorola, and Blackberry. The iPhone, together with smartphones using the Android operating system, proceeded to disrupt the mobile phone market (Downes & Nunes, 2013).

In addition, as an organisational-level theory, disruptive innovation theory focuses on the interplay between individual disruptors and the decisions of individual incumbent firms. Although this theory has profound implications at the firm level, the structural changes in an industry's environment where DD has major impacts have not been considered. Furthermore, disruptive innovation theory assumes homogeneity of the DD phenomenon, and thus does not distinguish types or subclasses of DD.

Overall, our review of the literature suggests that existing perspectives for understanding DD do not fully recog nise and acknowledge the differences in DDs that are observed empirically at the industry level. This is significant because the extent to which digital innovations have disruptive impacts on established industries depends not onl on the innovations' intrinsic characteristics but also on the industry's characteristics. Consequently, existing theorie are ill-suited to addressing this overlooked facet of DD.

In short, there is a need to develop a new theory of DD that can explain how and why DD unfolds differentl across industries, with an emphasis on the role of industry-level factors (as opposed to firm-level or artefact-level) i explaining the DD outcomes. Motivated by this insight, we now turn to how we chose a theory-building approach to tackle this problem.

## 2.2 | Configurational approach to theory building

To build a theory of DD, we adopted a configurational approach to theory building. Configurational theories are based on the recognition that patterns of conditions, rather than individual conditions, are stronger determinants o outcomes and that multiple condition patterns may yield the same outcome (Fiss, 2007; Ragin & Rihoux, 2009).

Configurational theory approaches use different assumptions regarding causality from those used in the mor common variance theory approaches (El Sawy et al., 2010; Fiss, 2007). First, configurational approaches assume conjunctural causation. In other words, each condition usually does not have its own unique impact on the outcome— rather, it may instead only have an impact in combination with other conditions. This differs from variance theoreti cal models where each variable is assumed to have an individual impact on the outcome. Variance theory relies on correlational analysis to identify the most powerful variable or predictor for explaining variance in the outcome net of the explanatory power of other variables (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012; Singleton & Straits, 2018)

Second, configurational theory assumes equifinality. Equifinality refers to different, mutually non-exclusiv explanations of a phenomenon (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Simply put, more than one path (i.e., multiple combi nations) may lead to the outcome. Variance theory however assumes a single path, resulting in a single optimal mode based on statistically estimated best fit with the available data (Fiss, 2007; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012).

Third, configurational theory assumes causal asymmetry. This indicates that a causal role attributed to a condition always refers to only one of the two qualitative states: presence or absence. The explanation of the occurrence of an outcome does not necessarily help explain its non-occurrence (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). In contrast, variance theory assumes causal symmetry, wherein the explanation of the dependent variable Y automatically implies the explanation of not Y because the occurrence and non-occurrence of a phenomenon are both explained with th same equation (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012).

In summary, configurational theory approaches assume a type of causal complexity that is defined by conju nctural causation, equifinality, and causal asymmetry (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). While all significant condition in variance theory are necessary and sufficient, in configurational approaches they may be necessary, sufficient, both INUS (a condition that is an insufficient but a necessary part of a condition which is itself unnecessary but sufficient for the result), or SUIN (a condition that is a sufficient, but an unnecessary part of a factor that is insufficient, bu necessary for the result) (Rivard & Lapointe, 2012; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012)

Although variance theory approaches are the dominant approaches to theory building in IS research, configurational theory approaches have been recommended as very well-suited to studying IS phenomena (El Saw et al., 2010; Park & Mithas, 2020). DD is a complex emergent phenomenon that requires the use of study approaches that take into consideration sociotechnical systems that operate far from equilibrium conditions as wel as the complex nature of causality (Benbya et al., 2020; Nambisan et al., 2017).

The assumptions and mental frames associated with variance theories are arguably insufficient for studying complex digital phenomena such as DD (El Sawy et al., 2010; Park & Mithas, 2020; Yoo et al., 2010). As Nambisan et al. (2017, p. 231) note: “Classic variance-based studies are often too static and make too simplistic assumption about the nature of the phenomenon. so the use of such methodologies does not easily get at the heart of the matter.”

Moreover, variance theories are not well suited to studying disequilibrium conditions, in which the elements and their relations change, as in the case of DD (Benbya et al., 2020; El Sawy et al., 2010; Meyer et al., 1993).

We join those who argue that configurational theories are well-suited as a system of inquiry for studying complex digital phenomena (Benbya et al., 2020; Bharadwaj et al., 2013; El Sawy et al., 2010; Nambisan et al., 2017; Park & Mithas, 2020; Yoo et al., 2012). Adopting a configurational approach to theory building allows us to better identify the patterns that exist across different industries that undergo DD (i.e., specific configurations of industrylevel causal conditions), thus allowing for more complex causal relationships to surface.

## 3 | OVERALL RESEARCH DESIGN

We employed a multi-method research design using QCA as our main method. QCA, a set-theoretic method (Ragin, 2008), is the dominant configurational approach in the literature. It is widely used for its ability to handl causal complexity (Fiss, 2007, 2011; Park et al., 2017; Rivard & Lapointe, 2012; Tan et al., 2016). QCA allows the analysis of causal complexity and multiple interaction effects associated with equifinality, conjunctural causation and causal asymmetry that are difficult to capture using conventional research methods (Ragin, 2008; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). In addition, DD's frequency of occurrence indicates that it belongs to medium-N situations (i.e., situations typically involving 12–50 cases), to which QCA is highly suited (Fiss, 2007; Park & Mithas, 2020 Ragin, 2008).

While we use QCA as the overarching method, QCA alone is not able to fully address our research questions and develop a configurational theory of DD. This is because QCA requires that theoretical constructs already developed in prior literature be used as input conditions for the analysis (Ragin & Rihoux, 2009). DD research is too nascent to have such detailed theories available. We overcome this limitation by using a multi-method design, combining a grounded theory approach and QCA to inductively build a configurational theory that explains how various factors or conditions come together to produce different types of DD.

It has been recommended that rigorous theory development should establish both formative validity and sum mative validity (Lee & Hubona, 2009). Formative validity concerns the process by which a theory is formed or built and its interim outputs, while summative validity concerns the sum result or product of the process, namely, the the ory. A theory gains formative validity by following accepted procedures in the process of its being formed. A theory, once formed, achieves summative validity by surviving an empirical test that uses the fundamental deductive logic of modus tollens (Lee & Hubona, 2009). To establish summative validity, we then use QCA with a hold-out sample to test and validate the emergent theory. Table 1 summarises the overall research design.

Overall, the design in Table 1 follows Park, Fiss, and El Sawy's (2020) principles and guidelines for applying QCA, with appropriate modifications to accommodate the multimethod design.

As the table shows, we first conducted a grounded theory study (Stage 1) to understand the phenomenon of interest (i.e., DD). Grounded theory approaches are designed to enable a theory to be induced (Glaser, 1992; Glaser & Strauss, 2017). They are especially relevant in our research as the first step in developing a set of theoretical constructs.

## 3.1 | Data sources

Recall, this study's objective is first to identify a distinct category of DD, and then to identify industry conditions and patterns of those conditions that lead to that category of DD. To meet this objective, we needed rich, descriptive industry-level data. As our study is not only concerned with one or two specific industries, but rather to examine pat terns across all industries, primary data collection methods (e.g., field studies) and experiments are not viable options due to limited resources and impracticality. As an appropriate alternative, we used IBISWorld, an industry market research database, as the main data source to conduct our analysis. IBISWorld contains professional industry research reports with insights into and analyses of all Australian industries. We chose this context because Australia the country where we reside, is a typical modern, democratic, capitalist economy that can be generalised to man other Western economies (Stevens, 2009). IBISWorld encompasses the entire Australian economy and produces th most comprehensive selection of industrial analysis, statistics, trends, and forecasts available

IBISWorld analyses industries at the class level, offering up-to-date information on approximately 500 industries Each industry report consists of 40–50 pages of text—a rich source of qualitative data. Each report contains exten sive text written by industry experts recounting and analysing what has happened to an industry over the preceding few years. While these reports were our primary data source, we also conducted triangulation wherever possible by cross-referencing them with secondary information, such as published academic case studies, annual reports, and news articles, about each digitally disrupted industry. Nevertheless, we found no conflicting evidence in the othe secondary sources that would challenge the conclusions presented in the IBISWorld reports

Content analysis was performed on all 503 industry reports published in 2020 and 2021, which encompassed 474 individual industries across 20 sectors. We thoroughly evaluated each industry to identify those that had been significantly affected by DD in the previous 10 years. Appendix A provides an overview of the number of disrupted industries in each sector and outlines the procedures used to identify digitally disrupted industries. In total, 54 industries were identified as having undergone DD. Of these, 21 were used in the theory-building phase (Stage 1 and Stage 2), while the remaining 33 served as the holdout sample for the theory-testing phase (Stage 3), as outlined in Table A1.

## 4 | EXPLORATORY ANALYSIS OF DIGITAL DISRUPTION ACROSS INDUSTRIES (STAGE 1)

Stage 1 was the first part of the inductive theory-building process. By inductive, we mean theoretical concepts are discovered empirically, without preconceived ideas or extant theoretical frameworks. An inductive approach to developing a novel theory often begins with an exploration of the new phenomenon and the discovery of condition that lead to general claims (Park, Fiss, & El Sawy, 2020). The novelty and complexity of DD implies that an explor atory approach is well-suited for our research question. In this stage, we aim to learn as much as possible about how DD unfolds across industries and what influences this process.

## 4.1 | Stage 1 method

We adopted the grounded theory approach along with Gioia et al.'s (2013) data analysis technique. The grounde theory approach is especially relevant for research on issues for which limited prior research has been conducted and for which theory building is needed (Bryant & Charmaz, 2007; Urquhart, 2013; Wiesche et al., 2017). We tak the broader meaning of the term “grounded theory,” in which it is more than a specific methodology, but a practice paradigm of emergent theory generation as discovered in empirical data (Levina, 2021; Walsh et al., 2015). Although we employed Gioia's technique in data analysis, we did not adopt Gioia's philosophical position of interpretivism. We follow those who argue that grounded theory is a flexible approach that can be employed in conjunction with divers ontologies and epistemologies (Glaser & Strauss, 2017; Urquhart, 2013; Walsh et al., 2015).

We followed the three core principles of grounded theory in our data collection and analysis (Glaser & Strauss, 2017; Walsh et al., 2015). First, we followed the principle of emergence, by remaining open to what was dis covered empirically, without preconceived ideas or theoretical frameworks from extant theory. Second, we followed the principle of theoretical sampling as opposed to probability sampling. Third, we followed the principle of joint data collection and constant comparison, where new data are continuously compared with previously analysed data for similarities and differences. Each new case was analysed to see whether the data continued to support emerging concepts (Gehman et al., 2018; Gioia et al., 2013; Glaser, 1992; Walsh et al., 2015). Given the importance of th grounded theory approach to this study, we provide further detail in Appendix B

## 4.2 | Stage 1 findings

Our primary insight induced in Stage 1 was that industry-level DD can take one of two forms: transformational or destructive. We also induced that four factors or conditions influence the type of DD an industry is likely to undergo: downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict, transferability of core competitive elements (CCEs), and industry player size. In the next subsections, we present these findings and highlight the influence of the four factors.

In transforma tional disruption, an affected industry is not necessarily in decline, as most of the incumbents tend to survive th changes.

The old way of doing business is fundamentally changed. A a result, the affected industry faces the consequence of becoming less relevant to customers. This is manifested in the industry's long-term decline in total revenue or business numbers. Destructive DD produces a marginalised industry whose overall size is smaller than prior to the disruption. Many incumbent businesses must close and exit the industry, while the remaining incumbents are often forced to operate at a less profitable level. In extreme cases destructive DD results in an industry's complete obsolescence

A good example of an industry subject to destructive DD is the video and DVD rental outlets industry. Th affordability and convenience of online streaming options like Netflix made the historically dominant logic of renting out physical videos unattractive. As the video and DVD rental outlet industry became less relevant to customers many existing businesses became financially unviable and were pushed out of the industry. The IBISWorld 2020 report on video and DVD rental outlets notes:

The Video and DVD Hire Outlets industry has been profoundly affected by technological disruption over the past decade. The widespread adoption of digital transfer technology has increasingly rendered the industry technologically obsolete… Demand for the industry's services has plummeted due to the popularity of online streaming options… The industry is deeply entrenched in the decline phase of its economic life cycle and approaches total obsolescence (Kelly, 2020, p. 40).

## 4.2.2 | Factors influencing type of digital disruption

After identifying these two types of DD, we sought to understand why one type occurs rather than the other—i other words, why some industries can survive and thrive while others are marginalised or even reach tota obsolescence. The analysis of factors was an iterative process of constant comparison in which every slice of dat was compared with existing concepts to see if it enriched an existing category or pointed in a new direction. Through this process, we learned that many of the factors identified at the very early stage (e.g., market concentration, information intensity, strategic variety) were not as relevant as more cases were compared and thus we eliminated them Based on our analysis, we discovered four factors that can affect the type of DD an industry is likely to experience downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict, transferability of CCEs, and industry player size

## Downstream digital disruption

We define downstream DD as the presence of DD in a focal industry's significant downstream markets. For example, viewing the newspaper publishing industry as the focal industry, newspaper and book retailers would be a significant downstream market, because newspaper and book retailers are the major first-tier buyers of newspapers. Insofar as the newspaper and book retailing industry has undergone DD and is downstream of the newspaper publishing industry, we identify the newspaper publishing industry as having downstream DD.

Pulp, paper, and paperboard manufacturing is another example of an industry with downstream DD. This industry's downstream markets include the paper product wholesaling industry, the printing industry, and the news paper publishing industry. All three of these downstream industries have undergone DD. Therefore, we consider th pulp, paper, and paperboard manufacturing industry as having downstream DD.

Of course, not all industries undergoing DD exhibit downstream DD. For example, the video and DVD renta outlets industry has no downstream industry because it sells directly to customers. Also, a focal industry can poten tially have multiple downstream industries, with some downstream industries having DD but not others. Ever IBISWorld industry report has a designated section listing all upstream and downstream industries with regard to th focal industry. Hence, we were able to compare the downstream industries listed in the IBISWorld database with the list of digitally disrupted industries that we had earlier identified

Our analysis revealed that the presence of downstream DD is a contributing factor to destructive DD. The IBISWorld reports clearly show the consequences of downstream DD, as demonstrated by the following statements:

Weaker activity in the downstream Printing industry has constrained industry [printing support services] growth (Feller, 2020, p.7).

The Pulp, Paper and Paperboard Manufacturing industry has declined over the past five years, as downstream printing and publishing markets have consolidated, reducing domestic demand for paper products. (Kyriakopoulos, 2020a, p.10).

The tendency for downstream DD to result in destructive DD of the focal industry can be understood as collatera damage. When the downstream industry faces DD, it must fundamentally change its means of doing business or it industry market share will be significantly reduced. This then reduces the demand and revenue of the upstream sup plier industry. Put another way, if a company's buyers change their ways and no longer require a service or product or if the number of buyers significantly shrinks, then it is likely that the company's revenue and market relevance wil also shrink.

## Digitally enabled structural conflict

We use the term digitally enabled structural conflict (or simply structural conflict ) to refer to situations in which the historically sustainable industry structure and established ways of doing business become incompatible with the new dominant mode of value creation enabled by digital innovations. When the new logics or relations of value creation are in major conflict with an incumbent industry, they are likely to substitute the functions of that incumbent industry. This dilutes the customer base and results in destructive DD of the affected incumbent industry. Th effects of this factor can be seen in the IBISWorld reports in statements such as:

The growing use of online sites to arrange sexual meetings among consenting strangers stands as a substantial external threat to long-term industry [brothel keeping] viability. (Allday, 2021, p.32).

Advances in computer hardware and software products that better connect end users and manufac turers have had a detrimental effect on the industry [kitchen and diningware wholesaling], as this has aided growth in wholesale bypass. This process essentially eliminates the wholesale function from the supply chain. (Kyriakopoulos, 2020b, p.34).

It is also worth pointing out that not all logics and relations of value creation introduced by digital disruptors conflict with existing industry structures. For example, Uber Eats complements rather than substitutes the restaurant industry: “Uber Eats has integrated its system into restaurants … As online food delivery services have expanded, many restau rants have had to adopt this technology to remain relevant compared with competing restaurants”(Barry, 2020, p. 38) The introduction of a new logic or relation of value creation that complements the existing industry structure allows incumbent industries to transform and incorporate new ways of doing business, thus resulting in transformational DD.

## Transferability of core competitive elements

We define the transferability of CCEs as the degree to which an industry's CCEs under the historically sustainable logics or relations of value creation can be transferred or passed on as the CCEs of the new industry ecosystem. By CCE, we mean an important or characteristic feature that is required as a basis of competition for players in an indus try to stay competitive in that industry. For example, access to utility infrastructure is one of the CCEs of the pulp paper and paperboard manufacturing industry because large-scale paper manufacturing has significant gas, electric ity, and water requirements. Manufacturers must have access to reasonably priced energy and water resources, or they will be unable to remain competitive in the industry. Another CCE of this industry is economies of scale because paper manufacturers that have large-scale mills and make good use of capacity can better compete wit other manufacturers. An important competitive element of one industry may be irrelevant in another industry; thus different industries have different sets of CCEs.

Nevertheless, it is not the CCEs themselves that matter for the outcomes of DD but rather their transferability which is a measure of how much the competitive landscape has or will be changed after the introduction of new ways of doing business arising from digital innovations. Are the CCEs of the old industry ecosystem still relevant in the new ecosystem? Or has the basis of competition changed so much that the previously critical CCEs for industr players are no longer significant?

When CCE transferability is high, the overall basis of industry competition will not be dramatically chan ged by digital innovations. For example, a CCE in the restaurant industry is the quality of the food. This has not changed with the introduction of food delivery platforms such as Uber Eats because the convenience o online ordering and food delivery has not made meal quality a less critical factor. In contrast, when transfer ability is low, many CCEs are no longer relevant under the new basis of competition. The video and DVD renta outlets industry illustrates low transferability because previously important CCEs, such as the availability of car parking, the ability to control stock on hand, and visible location, have become irrelevant. The rise of video streaming dramatically changed the competitive landscape and rendered previously important factors obsolete.

The data analysis revealed that when the transferability of CCEs is high, incumbents of the affected industry are more likely to maintain their competitive advantage under the new way of doing business. This allows incumbent sufficient time to transform and integrate into the new logics or relations of value creation and stay competitiv post-disruption. For example, it is evident from the IBISWorld reports that universities and other higher educatio institutions, given their high CCE transferability, have undergone transformational disruption rather than becoming obsolete as a result of online education alternatives:

A university's reputation is highly important in the industry … student preferences for face-to-face education and a lack of recognition of these courses from employers limit the threat MOOCs [massive open online courses] pose to the industry. (Caldwell, 2020, p.31).

The core basis of competition in the higher education industry has not been dramatically changed by the convenience of online course offerings. Factors crucial to success in this industry, such as good reputation, ability to attract research funds, and ability to provide relevant courses, remain important

Another example is the watch and jewellery retailing industry. Although online-only retailers can offer cheaper prices because of lower overheads, incumbent jewellery retailers (e.g., Tiffany & Co.) have remained competitive b transforming themselves into omnichannel operators that offer both online and in-store options. This transformatio has been successful in part because the basis of competition was not fundamentally changed. Brand reputatio remains a critical competitive element:

While price is an important factor, it is not necessarily the primary basis of competition in the indus try. Consumers will often pay more for watches and jewellery they perceive to be of high quality. For example, consumers may be willing to pay more for a diamond ring that comes from a trusted and independent jeweller rather than get a cheaper ring with an alternative retailer. Consequently, reputation is a critical factor. (Oo, 2021, p.31).

## Industry player size

Our analysis suggested that the size of industry players also affects how DD occurs. “Industry player size” refers to the size of the average business in a given industry as measured by annual turnover and number of employees Industries made up of mostly large players, such as the banking industry, respond differently from industries consisting of mostly small players, such as the photographic film processing industry. Our analysis of the IBISWorl reports revealed that large players are more likely to invest heavily in upgrading their capabilities to operate in envi ronments with new business logics:

Major banks have made significant investments in developing their online and digital banking platforms and payment systems to keep up with the technological disruptions across the finance sector (Wu, 2020, p. 38).

Larger industry players tend to have the necessary resources to transform themselves. In contrast, industries consisting of mainly small players lack the resources to build new capabilities that are crucial for competing in the new business environment. As a result, most players choose to leave the industry instead, and the industry diminishes a a consequence:

Many smaller operators have been unable to remain competitive and have exited the [photographic film processing] industry. (Chapman, 2021, p 7).

## 4.3 | Putting it all together: A preliminary configurational theory of digital disruption

At the beginning of grounded theory research, researchers try to avoid their prior knowledge from leading them to preformulated theoretical ideas as this could hinder the emergence of new concepts from data. However, as th grounded theory approach proceeds, and emergent concepts have been identified, it is helpful to use abductive logic to make sense of the emergent concepts, provide plausible explanations for relationships among the emerging con cepts, and increase the generalisability of the emergent theory (Gioia et al., 2013; Ketokivi & Mantere, 2010

Urquhart et al., 2010). Abduction here refers to the movement from the unexplained toward plausible explanations (Sætre & Van de Ven, 2021).

As noted above, we induced two types of DD and four industry factors that contingently lead to the different types of DD using inductive logic. After that, in the quest to better understand the phenomenon under study, we came to realise that the analogy of biological ecosystems was insightful in explaining the relationships between th four industry factors and the mechanisms underlying the empirical findings. Analogical reasoning is a compound form of reasoning that integrates both abduction and induction (Magnani et al., 2010)

Similar to how biological organisms survive in their ecosystems, industries (organisms of the business world must survive in their economic ecosystems (Moore, 1996). DD can be viewed as a change in an economic ecosystem from the historically sustainable non-digital ecosystem to a new digital economic ecosystem. Before DD occurs, the historically sustainable non-digital economic ecosystem (of a nation) is in a state of quasi-equilibrium, where the ecosystem's activities, actors, positions, and links are quite stable. Incremental innovations occur regularly, and new products and services are launched through established channels (Adner, 2017). This historically sustainable ecosystem dynamic is destroyed by the onset of DD, replacing it with a new ecosystem structure and dynamics induced b digital innovation. DD changes the value proposition to customers as well as the structure of economic relationship in the old ecosystem. The DD process essentially replaces the old ecosystems that affected industries belonged to with the emergence of a new digital economic ecosystem. Two foreseeable fates emerge for affected industries When faced with a change in the ecosystem, some industries can transform themselves to secure a role in the new ecosystem (similar to the evolution of species to adapt to the new biological ecosystem), leading to transformationa DD. Other industries are unable to secure a role in the new ecosystem (similar to the extinction of species in biologi cal ecosystems), leading to destructive DD.

We theorised that the four factors discovered in our study (downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict transferability of CCEs, and industry player size) are essentially factors that affect an industry's ability to secure its role in the new digital economic ecosystem. Downstream DD and digitally enabled structural conflict determine if an industry is sufficiently compatible with the new digital economic ecosystem to sustain itself. In biology, it is almost impossible for an anaerobic organism to exist in an oxygen-based ecosystem because of its incompatibility with such an ecosystem. Just as in a biological ecosystem, it is difficult for industries with low compatibility to exist in the new digital economic ecosystem because it does not provide sufficient extrinsic value to the other entities within the eco system. For example, the paper industry was part of a product ecosystem of physical books and is not compatibl with the new ecosystem based on e-books. The industries that were central in the old economic ecosystem includ the paper, book publishing, and book retailing industries, whereas the industries that are relevant in the new ecosys tem include Amazon and other digital platforms, e-book publishers, e-book makers, and self-publishers. Anothe example is the taxi industry, which is not compatible with the new ecosystem occupied by digital ride-sharing plat forms and participant drivers

Transferability of CCEs and industry player size determine whether an industry can maintain its competitive advantage and thus secure its position in the new ecosystem. An industry might find it difficult to secure its role i the new ecosystem over time if it lacks competitiveness. For example, if the average industry player size is ver small, such as in the financial planning industry, it is not favourable for industry players to invest heavily in resource to acquire robo-advisers on top of human advisers. As a result, it is harder for the financial planning industry to sta competitive in the new AI-driven ecosystem. If an industry cannot compete effectively in the new ecosystem, it wil diminish over time.

As the figur shows, we theorised that digital economic ecosystem compatibility factors (downstream DD or digitally enabled structural conflict) and digital economic ecosystem competitiveness factors (transferability of CCEs or industry playe size) combine to produce the two types of DD (transformational or destructive).

We now operationalise Figure 1 in the form of hypotheses that focus on explaining the occurrence of each type of DD. ## 5 | EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS USING FUZZY-SET QUALITATIVE COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS (STAGE 2)

Based on the constructs and model from Stage 1, we analysed 21 cases of digitally disrupted industries in Stage 2 with the goal of identifying any configurations that are reliably associated with each type of DD and building a configurational theory with new causal recipes and hypotheses.

## 5.1 | Stage 2 method

We used fsQCA (Ragin & Rihoux, 2009), a set-theoretic method, to explore how the four explanatory condition identified in Stage 1 combine into configurations. QCA originated in sociology and political science to extend crosscase comparative analysis (Ragin, 2008). fsQCA is a type of QCA that is especially well suited for our inquiry. Due to its use of set theory, Boolean algebra, and counterfactual analysis, fsQCA can capture complex relationships among multiple elements more effectively than other conventional statistical methods, such as cluster analysis and regres sion analysis (Fiss, 2011). fsQCA's focus is not on identifying the net effects of individual independent variables on an outcome but on identifying causal recipes associated with an outcome (Park, Fiss, & El Sawy, 2020 Ragin, 2008). In this case, fsQCA allows us to show how downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict, trans ferability of CCEs, and industry player size combine to produce transformational DD or destructive DD. Appendix C lists the key concepts and steps of fsOCA that relate to this study.

## 5.2 | Stage 2 findings

Figure 2 shows two configurations of transformational DD and two configurations of destructive DD that we obtained from our analysis.

Following Park, Fiss, and El Sawy (2020), we present our fsQCA results in Figure 2 for intermediate and parsimo nious solutions. The elements in the parsimonious solution are embedded in the intermediate solution. Core condi tions appear in both parsimonious and intermediate solutions while peripheral conditions only appear in th intermediate solutions. Bigger symbols represent the core conditions compared to peripheral conditions. Appendix C presents the counterfactual analysis conditions that were used based on the emergent theory.

## 5.2.1 | Configurations for transformational DD

Our analysis revealed two pathways to transformational DD. Configuration 1 (T1) suggests that transformationa DD is achieved when the CCE transferability of the industry is high and both downstream DD and digitally enable structural conflict are absent. Note that the absence of downstream DD is a peripheral condition, while the absenc of digitally enabled structural conflict and the presence of high CCE transferability are both core conditions. An example of this configuration is the restaurant industry, which was able to transform and integrate into the new rela tions of value creation introduced by food delivery platforms such as Uber Eats

Configuration 2 (T2) suggests that the combination of no downstream DD, no digitally enabled structural con flict, and mostly large industry players is sufficient to result in transformational DD. In this configuration, all three conditions are core and there are no peripheral conditions. An example of this configuration is the telecommunica tions services industry, where the industry was able to transform and adopt the new logic of value creation from fixed lines to wireless telecommunications.

Coverage measures also support the researcher in determining the percentage of an outcome that is covered b a solution (Ragin & Rihoux, 2009). We reported the overall solution coverage (how much is covered by the solution term), raw coverage (the share of the outcome explained by a certain alternative path), and unique coverage (th share of the outcome exclusively explained by a certain alternative path) (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Of th two configurations, T1 has the higher raw coverage (0.75), meaning that it is empirically more relevant for transfor mational DD than T2, which has a much lower raw coverage (0.38).

In addition to the sufficiency analysis, we conducted a necessary condition test (see Appendix C for the details) Results showed that the conjunction of both ‘NOT downstream DD’ and ‘NOT structural conflict’ is a highly consis tent necessary condition for the outcome (Kahwati & Kane, 2020; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012; Thomann et al., 2018). This indicates that the relation between the two conditions and the higher-order concept of “favorable digital economic ecosystem compatibility” is a non-exclusive OR relation (Hackett, 2015), whereby the absence of downstream DD or the absence of digitally enabled structural conflict, or the absence of both can be understood a favourable digital economic ecosystem compatibility conditions (Goertz, 2012)

The results also indicate that the presence of high transferability of CCEs and large industry player size can be considered as INUS conditions, in that they are an “insufficient, but necessary part of a factor which is itself unnec essary but sufficient for the result (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012, p. 79). Simply put, both high CCE transferabilit and large player size are neither sufficient nor necessary to produce the outcome on their own, but they are needed as ingredients in the causal recipe, i.e., they lead to the outcome of transformational DD when working together wit other factors or conditions.

Thus, the empirical configurations derived from the QCA are closely tied to the equifinal and conjunctural char acteristics of DD's causal complexity.

## 5.2.2 | Configurations for destructive DD

Our analysis identified two configurations for the outcome of destructive DD. Configuration 1 (D1) indicates that destructive DD is achieved when there is digitally enabled structural conflict. Examples include the photographic film processing industry, in which the established ways of doing business become incompatible with digital photograph and cloud storage platforms. Configuration 2 (D2) suggests that destructive DD occurs when the industry's transfer ability of CCEs is not high, coupled with the presence of downstream DD. An example of this configuration is th printing support services industry, where the downstream printing industry also faced DD. In addition, the competi tive landscape has also changed dramatically, rendering existing CCEs (e.g., access to niche markets such as book binding) irrelevant in the digital economic ecosystem.

Among the two configurations, D1 has the higher raw coverage (0.69), meaning that it is empirically more rele vant for destructive DD compared with D2, which has a lower raw coverage (0.44). The results imply that digitall enabled structural conflict holds greater influence than downstream DD. This observation aligns with our in-dept analysis of cases. For instance, in the free-to-air television broadcasting industry, structural conflict with onlin streaming services exerts more influence on the outcome than other factors. Thus, for the outcome to occur, down stream DD needs to be in combination with unfavourable digital economic ecosystem competitiveness conditions (i.e., the absence of high transferability of CCEs), whereas the presence of digitally enabled structural conflict alon is sufficient for the outcome.

None of the conditions appear in all configurations, which indicates that there is no single necessary condition for destructive DD to occur. However, the combination (superset) of the presence of downstream DD or the presence of digitally enabled structural conflict is necessary for the outcome. This is also confirmed by the necessary con ditions test (see Appendix C). The findings support the higher-order concept of “unfavorable digital economi ecosystem compatibility.” This indicates that the relation between the two conditions and the higher order concept of “unfavorable digital economic ecosystem compatibility” is a non-exclusive OR relation (Hackett, 2015), whereby the presence of downstream DD or the presence of digitally enabled structural conflict, or the presence of both can be understood as the unfavourable digital economic ecosystem compatibility conditions (Goertz, 2012)

## 5.3 | Putting it all together: Toward a configurational theory of DD

The observed configurations require careful analysis in light of the underlying emergent theory and a deep understanding of individual case details to enhance the interpretation of their significance (Park, Fiss, & El Sawy, 2020 Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). Thanks to our manageable number of cases, we could cross-reference these configu rations with the specific cases. This process of going back to the actual cases played a pivotal role in the formatio of our hypotheses, as opposed to solely relying on the results from Stage 2 for the hypothesis development.<sup>3</sup> Leveraging in-depth case knowledge enabled us to make sense of the emerging patterns and interpret our findings.

When formulating the refined hypotheses, we took into account both the preliminary hypotheses based on the emergent theory and the empirical results from Stage 2. While we assume that the Stage 2 sample adequately repre sents the population, we acknowledge its limitations. Specifically, the Stage 2 results might not encompass all poten tial solutions for the population, and some results, or parts of the results, may lack theoretical significance on their own.<sup>4</sup> Consequently, we conducted a thorough analysis to determine whether the empirical results supported or contradicted the emergent theory and refined the theory as necessary (Locke et al., 2008)

Recall the configurational model developed from Stage 1 (see Figure 1). At this stage of theorising, we refine the emergent configurational theory and propose the following:

Transformational DD occurs when an industry is able to transform and secure its role in the new digital eco nomic ecosystem. Configurations with favourable new ecosystem integration conditions are likely to be associated with transformational DD. Based on the analysis of configurations sufficient for transformational DD, we propose the following:

Hypothesis 1. Transformational DD of an industry is associated with the presence of both favourable digital economic ecosystem compatibility conditions (i.e., the absence of both downstream DD and digitally enabled structural conflict) and at least one favourable ecosystem competitiveness condition (i.e., the presence of high transferability of CCEs or the presence of large industry player size).

When an industry is unable to transform and secure its role in the new digital economic ecosystem, it will most likely experience destructive DD. Configurations with unfavourable new ecosystem integration conditions are likel to be associated with destructive DD. Based on the analysis of configurations sufficient for destructive DD, we pro pose the following:

Hypothesis 2. Destructive DD of an industry is associated with configurations of unfavourable digita economic ecosystem compatibility conditions (i.e., the presence of downstream DD or the presence of digitally enabled structural conflict, or the presence of both).

The effect of downstream DD is weaker than the effect of digitally enabled structural conflict such that:

H2a. The presence of digitally enabled structural conflict in any configuration results in destructive DD.

H2b. The presence of downstream DD combined with at least one of the unfavourable digital economic ecosystem competitiveness conditions (i.e., the absence of high transferability of CCEs or the absence of large industry player size) results in destructive DD.<sup>5</sup>

## 7 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

Digital technologies have disrupted a variety of organisations in many industries and have greatly affected the mod ern economy (Bughin & Van Zeebroeck, 2017). Yet, despite the importance of DD, it remains understudied, espe cially at the industry level (Riemer & Johnston, 2019). This paper advances knowledge of DD by addressing th research questions: (1) How does digital disruption unfold among different industries? (2) What are the patterns or configurations of industry factors that bring about different types of digital disruption?

This paper contributes through its development and test of a configurational theory of digital disruption. The paper also contributes to the methodological advancement of QCA through its combination with the grounded theory method (GTM).

First, we contribute through the answers we obtained to our two research questions by identifying two types of DD and four factors that contingently lead to the two different types of DD. More importantly, we integrated those insights into a configurational theory that describes causal recipes of how these factors or conditions combine to produce the out come of transformational DD and destructive DD. Likewise, we explained how the causal recipes can be viewed as alter native ways in which industries can secure their roles in the new competitive ecosystem wrought by DD.

The new theorising developed in this paper advances knowledge of DD and opens up a new area of inquiry. Extant literature largely treated DD as homogenous (Christensen et al., 2018; Riemer & Johnston, 2019), not fully recognising the differences among DDs that can and do occur empirically. To our knowledge, our paper is the first to explicitly cat egorise industry-level DD into different types. This new conceptualization not only offers valuable insights but also reveals new research opportunities by emphasising not just the commonalities among DDs but also differences among them. Specifically, subsequent work can choose to focus on one of the two types of DD for deeper analysis or compare the two types. Specific opportunities for research may involve studying: (1) whether additional factors are important for transformational DD, (2) whether additional factors influence the outcome of destructive DD, (3) how the proces of transformational DD differs from destructive DD, (4) what key events or indicators signal the trajectory of a DD t be transformational or destructive, (5) how firms should respond when their industry faces destructive DD, and (6) what strategies and capabilities should firms develop when their industry faces transformational DD.

In addition, this work not only provides important and timely clarification of DD, but also finds a way to operationalise it to the point where a comparison across industries is feasible. To the best of our knowledge, thi paper is the first to explicitly examine industry factors and their roles in influencing DD outcomes. Prior literature focuses on the firm-level analysis and treats industry as a general contextual matter. The few industry-level studie are usually confined to one industry, Thus, they provide only a limited understanding of what factors are importan: across industries. This research is the first to have examined all industries in a nation (in our case, Australia). Future research could use our work as a basis to further refine and/or develop new measurable constructs to compare DD across different industries. Our research could also be used as a foundation to further investigate other industry level inquiries such as How can digital disruption be managed at the industry level? and How can industry struc tures be designed to protect against destructive DD?

The proposed theory also shows a relationship between DD and economic ecosystems. Future research could extend this by further investigating the relationship between DD and innovation ecosystems (Wang, 2021), such as learning how digital ecosystems can be leveraged by entrepreneurs to produce the desired DD outcome.

Our research also contributes methodologically by demonstrating the merits of expanding QCA's theory-building potential with grounded theory. Although QCA is recognised as an important method for IS research (El Saw et al., 2010; Park, Fiss, & El Sawy, 2020), studies using QCA have largely focused on topics that have been exten sively studied. In other words, the QCA approach in IS research has tended to focus on expanding or revitalising exis ting theory rather than building substantially new theory. This is because, for QCA to produce causal recipes, it must have a list of theoretically based conditions to use as inputs. Naturally, researchers find the conditions based on exis ting research and build the configurational theory abductively (Dwivedi et al., 2018; Park et al., 2017; Rivard & Lapointe, 2012). Yet, this limits the applicability of QCA in building configurational theories for new phenomen “from scratch” without drawing on theoretical frameworks from existing perspectives

This paper addresses this limitation by combining the grounded theory method and QCA to inductively develop a novel configurational theory. This multi-method approach can significantly increase the types of theories that can be developed using QCA or grounded theory method alone. It is difficult to produce configurational theory using th grounded theory method alone because it is very hard to manually code interactions among multiple constructs QCA likewise heavily relies on prior knowledge and conditions, which limits the ability to discover new factors. Com bining the grounded theory method and QCA creates the opportunity to develop more interesting and robust config urational theories.

## 7.1.1 | Our approach does not rely on a priori theoretical framework

As noted earlier (see also Appendix E), conventional QCA papers often rely on a priori theories for selecting condi tions or as overarching lenses for more granular concept development. We suggest that the usefulness of this con ventional approach depends on the nature of the phenomenon being investigated (see Figure 5). When phenomenon has been studied extensively with many prior papers on the topic, the conventional QCA approach i suitable for shedding new light on a well-established phenomenon. For example, Dawson, Denford, Williams, et al (2016) used the legal view of agency theory to discover new insights into the well-established information technol ogy governance phenomenon.

The conventional QCA approach is also appropriate when the phenomenon of interest is new but can b explained using a more general well-established theory. For example, Maier, Laumer, Joseph, et al. (2021) used job embeddedness theory and a priori concepts of shocks and job dissatisfaction as the basis for selecting conditions to explain the recent phenomenon of rehiring information technology professionals, which they conceptualised as ‘turnback intention.

Although conventional approaches provide interesting and useful insights, they tend to result in expanding or revitalising existing theories rather than producing a substantial new theory. The usefulness of the conventiona QCA approach is limited when a new phenomenon appears for which little prior literature exists and for which new concepts and novel theoretical perspectives need to be developed. The grounded theory informed QCA approach i useful in such situations and addresses this limitation of the conventional QCA approach by combining grounded theory and QCA to inductively develop a novel configurational theory. A grounded theory informed configurationa approach does not need to rely on prior theoretical framing. Rather, conditions emerge systematically from th grounded theory analysis as opposed to being selected from prior literature or expert knowledge. ## 7.1.2 | Sample size requirement

The grounded theory informed QCA approach also differs from conventional approaches in terms of the required sample size. Although QCA was originally developed for small-N (<12 cases) and medium-N (12–50 cases) settings its applicability was later extended to large-N studies (>50 cases) (Greckhamer et al., 2013; Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). In this regard, most QCA papers published in IS journals are large-N studies

Our approach is more suitable for small-N and medium-N rather than large-N studies. QCA has occasionally bee applied to samples of up to 12 cases (Iannacci et al., 2022). In such cases, however, a systematic comparison of th necessity or sufficiency of attribute combinations depends on substantial in-depth cross-case and within-case ana lyses. QCA can be used to formalise cross-case comparisons, but care must be taken with this approach. If not com bined with in-depth cross-case and within-case analyses, QCA analyses of up to 10 cases do not provide sufficient evidence to construct robust causal models (Greckhamer et al., 2013). The grounded theory method provides the indepth cross-case and within-case analysis needed to achieve meaningful inferences and, thus is compatible wit small sample sizes.

As we have shown in our research, the grounded theory informed QCA approach is also compatible with medium-N situation. The number of cases for the grounded theory method to reach theoretical saturation usuall falls within the medium-N range (12–50 cases). This medium-N range enables the researcher to be closer to the data and more engaged in the interpretation of results while providing enough data points and evidence to construct robust causal models (Greckhamer et al., 2013)

The compatibility of large-N situations with the grounded theory informed QCA approach remains uncertain. The grounded theory method requires in-depth analysis of each case, which would be infeasible with a large set of cases. As the number of cases increases, researchers are less able to find and correctly incorporate in-depth informa tion about each. In addition, large-N QCA studies are often based on different assumptions, objectives, and method ological implications that may not be compatible with the grounded theory method. Nonetheless, instead of attempting to gather in-depth information about all cases, researchers may select only the most typical cases from the large-N sample to generate their emergent theory and use the other cases for theory testing

## 7.1.3 | Configurational multiplicity but not theoretical multiplicity

Another interesting characteristic of the grounded theory informed QCA approach is that it results in configurationa multiplicity but not theoretical multiplicity. Theoretical multiplicity refers to “a situation where the phenomenon under consideration is best understood using more than one theoretical perspective, where each theoretical perspec tive determines what factors to consider, how they are related, and why these causalities exist” (Park, Fiss, & E Sawy, 2020, p. 1496). For example, theoretical multiplicity can be seen in Lee et al.'s (2019) paper, where multiple theoretical perspectives (transaction cost economics, resource dependency theory, and social exchange theory) wer combined to select the relevant conditions and explanations of configurational patterns. Configurational multiplicit refers to a situation where even within a particular theoretical perspective there may by different configurations or factors such that there is not one best way but in fact several effective ways to organize” (Park, Fiss, & E Sawy, 2020, p. 1496). For example, Fedorowicz et al. (2018) based their selection of all conditions on one a prior theory (the theory of network effectiveness) and analysed different configurations of public services network gover nance practices that enable high levels of network governance effectiveness. Although the grounded theor informed QCA approach has different factors and configurations, the patterns of configurations are explained by one theory, not multiple theories.

Researchers who use conventional QCA approaches can choose to use one or multiple theories. Given the nature of the theory-building approach, the grounded theory informed QCA approach is only in line with configura tional multiplicity based on a single theoretical perspective. This theoretical perspective (the emergent theory) i grounded in data systematically gathered and analysed. Thus, this approach may not be suitable for researchers who wish to examine how competing theoretical perspectives affect the observed patterns of configurations.

## 7.1.4 | Embracing the assumption of causal complexity ex-ante

Our grounded theory informed QCA approach differed from conventional QCA approaches in that we assumed the causal complexity of the world ex-ante. In other words, we began with the fundamental assumption that, in reality phenomena typically demonstrate causal complexity (i.e., causal simplicity would be a rare occurrence).

We can describe our position as aligning with an innocent until proven guilty legal approach versus a guilt until proven innocent’ approach. Under the grounded theory informed QCA approach, similar to ‘innocent unti proven guilty’, we assumed a causal complexity of the world and embraced the configurational approach before starting to build the theory. In contrast, conventional QCA studies have taken a ‘guilty until proven innocent’ view. Although not explicitly communicated, conventional QCA approaches assume that the world is based on causal sim plicity (and that causal complexity is the exception rather than the norm), and the onus is on the researcher to show evidence of causal complexity prior to conducting QCA. Under this assumption, before conducting QCA, researcher must often justify why configurations need to be considered in the first place and discuss theories that explicitl state an underlying mechanism that comprises a complex interplay between multiple conditions (Greckhame et al., 2013; Mattke et al., 2022)

A potential weakness of our approach is it cannot be definitively known that the factors identified in th grounded theory study will adhere to the conjunctural causation logic before the induction of data occurs. It is possi ble (though unlikely) that following the grounded theory informed approach, the QCA analysis will confirm a non complex causality (i.e., each of the factors is necessary and sufficient on its own). In this case, a variance theor model rather than a configurational theory model would be sufficient to explain causality

In our research, we were reasonably confident that DD exhibits causal complexity based on our literature review and the first author's personal industry experience. If a researcher is unsure of the nature of causality of the phenom enon under study, a pilot study may be conducted to obtain evidence before heavily investing in this approach.

## 7.1.5 | Theory testing as a part of theory development

Theory development in the IS field has been criticised for lack of rigour because many theories only satisfy formativ (not summative) validity (Lee & Hubona, 2009). Our 3-stage research design incorporates theory testing in the third stage to establish summative validity

While the roles of inductive and abductive reasoning in generating theory from data can be readily accepted their roles in validating the resulting theory are questionable. The logic of validation requires a deductive process, wherein the prerequisite of independent testing necessitates that a hypothesis is not tested with the same empirica material from which it is developed (Bryant & Charmaz, 2007). A further prerequisite is that hypotheses are formu lated with sufficient rigour to allow for a systematic confrontation with evidence. For instance, if a theory emerges from data, then that same data can hardly provide the independent evidence required to test the theory. Conse quently, in the third stage of our multi-method research design, we used a separate holdout sample to test an validate our emerging theory. This involves using the QCA method in a top-down deductive style of theory testing as opposed to the bottom-up inductive approach of theory building

Our use of a holdout sample differs from previous QCA studies that use it as a way to ensure the predictive validity of findings (Soto Setzke et al., 2021). Specifically, we use the holdout sample to help validate the newly developed theory rather than for testing predictive validity. We also place more emphasis on the emergent theory in our approach than the empirically observed individual configurations (which are limited by sample size, and thus can be idiosyncratic) (see Figure 6). In other words, the individual configurations are only meaningful when considered together with the emergent theory, and it is the overall emergent configurational theory that is the focus of th research and validation.

The requirements of theory discovery and validation differ. To discover theory, we can employ a flexible, itera tive, and adaptive approach to generating and utilising data. In testing theory, we need to specify in advance what theoretical claims we are advancing in the form of a hypothesis and what data would count for their refutation or validation. In our case, based on deductive logic, we consider a configurational theory to be validated when the empirically observed configurations from the test sample are subsets of the hypothesized configurations. In othe words, hypothesized configurations are supersets of the observed configurations. Empirical samples might not con tain all theoretically possible configurations, and different empirical samples can contain different subsets of the hypothesized supersets.

Conventional applications of QCA differ from our approach in that they place less emphasis on the development of the higher-level emergent theory and instead focus on the empirically observed individual configurations. Becaus conventional QCA studies tend to use well-established, high-level, a-priori “meta” theory/or theories, the focus is not to increase the level of abstraction, but to rather contextualise and lower the level of the abstraction of the “meta” theory to be more appropriate for the phenomenon at hand. As a result, more emphasis is placed on the indi vidual configurations and less on the higher-level theory.

Both approaches have their merits depending on the maturity of the phenomenon under study and the aims of the researcher. Conventional QCA sheds new light on the well-established phenomenon and provides new insight that are rich and contextualised. While the grounded theory informed QCA approach does provide rich, detailed insight, the emphasis is on new theory development and validation that celebrates the inclusivity of researc paradigms.

Also, by having a holdout sample, researchers need to be aware of the trade-off between cases used for theory generation and cases used for theory validation and ensure that both samples contain a high number of logically possible configurations.

Overall, we contend that a grounded theory informed QCA approach complements the conventional QCA approach and provides an alternative QCA method that can be useful to advance configurational theory development.

Before concluding, it is important to highlight that the theory developed and tested in this study is eminently practical, exemplifying another strength of configurational research. In particular, the theory provides a means of identifying how DD is likely to play out in an affected industry—knowledge that would benefit any company execu tive, industry association, or industry policy-maker. This paper also presents a method for identifying the opportuni ties and risks an industry is facing and offering useful guidance to executives to inform their decision-making. Over time, we hope that the proposed theory can help business executives, venture capitalists, and government official focus on what matters most in DD, thereby reducing economic waste and increasing business success.

This paper does, however, have limitations. We developed our frameworks based on the Australian economy; further empirical work is needed to validate the emergent theory's usefulness in other economies. Moreover, although this research has provided a deeper understanding of DD, our focus on the configurational perspective meant that we did not consider a variety of other perspectives. We hope future research will do so.

## APPENDIX A

## A.1 | Overview of disrupted industries and selection procedure

To be selected as a digitally disrupted industry, strong qualitative and/or quantitative evidence from the industr reports are required to show that the industry met the proposed definition of DD. DD was defined as the socio technical process by which digital innovations come to fundamentally alter the established logics of value creation and/or the established relations of value creation of an industry. That is, we had to see evidence that the industr underwent disruption caused by digital innovation.

In addition, the selected industry must involve the re-establishment of the dominant logics of value creation and/or the relations of value creation. Recall from the main paper, that an industry's logics of value creation is th foundational rationale underlying solution conceptualization and critical resource allocation decisions to meet customer needs in exchange for economic benefit. An industry's relations of value creation is the relationship struc ture that determines how the actors and tasks within an industry ecosystem are organised around the production of something valuable for customers (which may be individuals or organisations) in return for economic benefit

Apart from meeting the definition of DD, we required selected industries to contain strong evidence from the industry reports. As an example of quantitative evidence, video and DVD rental outlets had an average of 28.1% revenue growth and 62.1% profit growth per annum between 2015 and 2020. Sample qualitative evidence includes quotes like the following: “The motel industry, similar to the overall accommodation sector, has been sub stantially disrupted by the emergence of Airbnb” (Cloutman, 2021, p.35).

When analysing the data, we noted that, in some industries, digital innovations were used as supporting tools to assist industry players in increasing efficiency. In these cases, the digital innovation could be transformative but not necessarily disruptive. For example, the dental services industry has been digitally transformed by the introduction of computer-based operational software that increases efficiency. Nonetheless, industry reports suggested that it had not undergone DD, because neither the logic nor the relation of value creation had been fundamentally altered Industries that have undergone digital transformation but not disruption are outside our research scope and thus excluded from our sample. Our unit of analysis is industry—that is, each industry is treated as one data point in ou analysis. Overall, 54 industries were identified as having undergone DD (total population of digitally disrupted indus tries in Australia).

## APPENDIX B

## B.1 | Additional details on our application of the grounded theory approach in Stage 1

Section 4.1 of the main paper provides an overview of our use of the grounded theory approach. In this appendix, we provide more details on our theoretical sampling and data analysis

## B.1.1. | Theoretical sampling

As noted earlier, we identified 54 digitally disrupted industries in the IBISWorld database. We then applied theoreti cal sampling to this population of Australian industries that had undergone DD. Theoretical sampling is a form of non-probability sampling and is a defining characteristic of grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 2017 Urquhart, 2013). In theoretical sampling, data samples are selected based on their theoretical purpose and relevance The concepts that emerge from the data guide the direction of subsequent data collection. This sampling strateg ensures that the emergent theory is grounded in data, not extant theory, and that the theory is conceptually elabo rated rather than logically deduced (Gehman et al., 2018; Urquhart, 2013).

In the context of this study, we started off with the most extreme case—that is, industries in which DD had th greatest impact—to uncover prominent characteristics. Guided by the emerging concepts, we further developed the emerging theory by adding more cases to maximise industry heterogeneity. When following the joint data collection and constant comparison principle of grounded theory, the emergence of concepts is both sequential and iterative. At the earlier stage of case selection, saturation on the DD types was achieved. This formed the basis of further theoreti cal sampling of the next “slices of data,” where the decisions were made on analytical grounds to look for more factors influencing types of DD to enrich the existing category. We continuously compared the additional industries with pre viously analysed industries as we looked for similarities and differences. We stopped adding more industries when the oretical saturation was reached at 21 cases and no new factors could be identified anymore (Gioia et al., 2013 Urquhart, 2013). Consequently, the final sample included 21 cases of digitally disrupted industries

## B.1.2. | Data analysis

Over the years, the grounded theory approach has evolved into Glasserian and Straussian variants (Urquhart et al., 2010). We found Glaser's variant particularly amenable, especially his view that when a coding process is too restrictive, it may “strangle any emergent conceptualizations” and force the concepts through preconceptions (Glaser, 1992; Urquhart et al., 2010). To achieve a good balance of “allowing the data to speak” and providing rigour in qualitative data analysis in the eyes of others, we adopted Gioia et al.'s (2013) data analysis technique, which is widely used (Gehman et al., 2018).

Following Gioia's technique, we categorised the data into first-order and second-order codes. We later incorporated these codes into a more structured model (Gioia et al., 2013). In the first-order analysis, we prioritised term used in the industry reports and made fewer attempts at grouping data, which resulted in many categories in this ini tial stage. Once we extracted the first-order terms and codes into second-order themes, we analysed the emerging themes to see if they could provide explanations for our research questions. Once a workable set of themes and concept surfaced, we sought to refine the emergent second-order themes into second-order aggregate dimensions, using inductive as well as abductive logics. ## C.1 | Additional details on our application of fsQCA in Stage 2

## C.1.1. | Data sample

To explore the configurations associated with the outcomes of interest, we continue to use the sample of 21 cases of digitally disrupted industries from Stage 1.<sup>6</sup> We checked that the 21 cases are not only appropriate for the grounded theory approach but are also appropriate sample for the configurational approach. The 21 cases included at least one case from each industry (to maximise heterogeneity across industries) and the remaining cases reflec different configurations of factors within certain industries (to maximise configurational heterogeneity). The sample have achieved a high number of logically possible configurations. When analysing these cases with fsQCA, the focu shifts from discovering novel concepts to identifying causal recipes of how these conditions combine into configura tions to produce the two types of DD

## C.1.2. | Calibration process

The first step of fsQCA requires a calibration process, which assigns the membership of each case to explanator sets (i.e., downstream DD, digitally enabled structural conflict, transferability of core competitive elements (CCEs) industry player size) and an outcome set (i.e., type of DD) by transforming the value of each variable for a case into a set-membership score.

Consistent with previous research, we coded memberships of conditions with binary attributes using the value of 1 and 0 (i.e., fully in or fully out). We used four-value fuzzy sets (i.e., fully in, more in than out, more out than in fully out) to code conditions derived from qualitative data (Crilly et al., 2012; Dwivedi et al., 2018; Misangyi & Acharya, 2014). Note that fsQCA can process both crisp sets (i.e., binary value) and fuzzy sets. We relied on existing guidelines and substantive case knowledge to establish all calibration thresholds.

The two outcomes are transformational DD and destructive DD. Each digitally disrupted industry is assigned as either transformational DD or destructive DD based on the evidence in the IBISWorld reports. In other words, every case has either full membership in the transformational DD set (non-membership of destructive DD) or full member ship in the destructive DD set (non-membership of transformational DD). Full membership is numerically represen ted as 1, and non-membership as 0 (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). The assignment was based on an in-dept understanding of individual cases, incorporating the definition of DD types developed in Stage 1 and considering both quantitative and qualitative evidence. For example, the Video and DVD rental industry was designated as destructive DD. The substantial reduction in industry size and relevance to customers was reflected in a 28.1% 5-year per annual revenue growth, 62% 5-year per annual profit growth, 22.7% 5-year per annual business growth, and 26.5% 5-year per annual employment growth (Kelly, 2020, p.40). The assimilation of incumbents wa also deemed unsuccessful, leading to the discontinuation of many businesses. As noted in the report: The Video and DVD Hire Outlets industry has been profoundly affected by technological disruption over the past decade. The widespread adoption of digital transfer technology has increasingly rendered the industry technologically obsolete” (Kelly, 2020, p.40).

In contrast, the restaurant industry was assigned transformational DD through extensive case knowledge Technology disruption has significantly affected the restaurant industry over the past decade, mainly through onlin food delivery services…many restaurants have had to adopt this technology to remain relevant compared with com peting restaurants, further intensifying competition in the industry” (Barry, 2020, p. 38). Despite the upheaval cause by digital disruption, incumbents in the restaurant industry responded by transforming themselves to assimilate th new way of doing business, preventing a major decline in the industry as a whole. This is evident in the industry's metrics, such as a 1.3% 5-year per annual revenue growth, 7.2% 5-year per annual profit growth, 0.6% 5-year per annual business growth, and 1.3% 5-year per annual employment growth (Barry, 2020). An intercoder reliabilit test was conducted. A randomly selected sample of 10 cases was coded by an independent coder who was provided with in-depth knowledge of the cases. 100% consistency was reported, indicating the reliability of the calibration.

The four explanatory conditions were calibrated using crisp sets or four-value fuzzy sets, depending on appropri ateness. Downstream DD membership was assigned according to the definitions outlined in Stage 1. Cases with DD in the significant downstream markets with respect to the focal industry were assigned full membership (1), while cases without the presence of downstream DD were assigned non-membership (0). In every IBISWorld report, there is a des ignated section that lists which industries are downstream of the focal industry. We compared the list of downstream industries from the IBIS report against our list of disrupted industries to determine the calibration of each case.

Similarly, membership in digitally enabled structural conflict was assigned in a binary manner according to our definition. Cases with the presence of structural conflicts were assigned full membership (1) and cases with th absence of structural conflicts were assigned non-membership (0). The intercoder reliability test resulted in 100% consistency in the calibration of both downstream DD and digitally enabled structural conflict.

The calibration of the transferability of CCEs condition used four-value fuzzy sets (Crilly et al., 2012; Misangyi & Acharya, 2014). Levels included fully in (1), more in than out (0.66), more out than in (0.33), and fully out (0), with 0 indi cating non-membership, 1 indicating complete membership, and 0.33 and 0.66 representing levels in between. A valu of 0.33 suggests that a case is more out of the set than in, and a value of 0.66 suggests that the case is more in the set than out. We assigned complete membership to industries with high transferability, where most of the CCEs are still rel evant under the new competitive landscape. We assign non-membership if most of the CCEs are considered irrelevant.

In the IBISWorld report on each industry, a designated section explains key success factors and why these fac tors are important to success in that particular industry. The key success factors provided in the report are used as a proxy for calibrating the transferability of CCEs. For example, having a good reputation, the ability to raise revenue from additional sources (e.g., research grants), the ability to provide relevant courses that respond to the needs of students and their potential employers, and the use of specialist equipment or facilities (e.g., strong delivery systems resources, and support systems) are listed as the key success factors for the higher education industr (Caldwell, 2020). It is important to note that different industries have different sets of key success factors. We first compared the key success factors from 2020 to those in the 2015 reports to see if and how they changed with th increased adoption of digital technologies. We also analysed the list of key success factors in the 2020 reports to see which of them would become irrelevant in the new digital economic ecosystems. Although the key success factors are used as guidance, we also take other relevant information from the report in determining the CCEs of an industry. If more than half of the CCEs are still relevant, the industry is considered to be more in the set of industries with high transferability than out. Likewise, if fewer than half of the CCEs are still relevant, the industry is considered to be more out of the set of industries with high transferability than in. The assignment of memberships was based on the careful analysis of changes in the relevance of key success factors together with in-depth knowledge of th individual cases (Ragin & Rihoux, 2009). The results of the intercoder reliability test were 95% consistent, affirming the reliability of the coding

Industry player size was operationalised into the binary categories of “large” and “not large” based on informa tion from the Australian Taxation Office regarding company size and our in-depth knowledge of the cases under study. We consider industries with average annual revenue equal to or greater than AUD\$20 M or an average of 20 or more employees to be full members of “large industries” (a value of 1). Industries with average annual revenu of less than \$20 M and average employee numbers of less than 20 were assigned non-membership (a value of 0). Average annual revenue and number of employees were calculated using data provided in the IBISWorld reports The independent coder reported 100% consistency.

## C.1.3. | Truth-table algorithms

The second step used a truth-table algorithm to identify the configurations of causal conditions (i.e., the four explan atory conditions) that sufficiently produce the outcome of interest (i.e., the two types of DD). Tables C1 and C2 below present truth tables for transformational DD and destructive DD. With the results of the calibration, each cas is allocated into one of several possible combinations, corresponding to one row in the truth table. Because we hav four causal conditions, there are 16 (i.e., 2<sup>4</sup>) theoretically possible ideal combinations.

Because this study has only a limited number of cases, we set the minimum acceptable number as 1 (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). This means the fsQCA software considers all combinations with at least one case for subsequent analysis in the truth-table algorithm. The truth-table algorithm then calculates a consistency score to reflect how reli ably a combination produces the outcome. This is similar to the significance level in regression analysis (Park, Fiss, & El Sawy, 2020). We use a consistency cutoff of 85% in the truth-table analysis, well above the 0.75 minimum rec ommended by Ragin and Rihoux (2009) to filter out natural noise
