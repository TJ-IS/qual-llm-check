# Flow of the Game: A Hidden Markov Model of Player Engagement in Online Mobile Games

Jiaying Deng,<sup>a</sup> Stephanie Lee,<sup>b</sup> Yong Tan<sup>b,</sup>\*

Abstract. With the rapid development of mobile technology and consumers’ increasing demand for portability, the mobile gaming market has experienced huge growth. To ensure the success of mobile games, game publishers need to keep their players engaged and, at the same time, seek monetization strategies that do not interrupt players’ engagement. This paper examines how in-game challenge-related factors (i.e., perceived challenge and fluctuation of perceived challenge) and a novel mobile game monetization strategy— reward ads—affect players’ engagement. Reward ads, a popular in-app advertising monetization model in which players can voluntarily watch an ad in exchange for a reward within the game, can help publishers generate revenue without breaking the flow of the game. We develop a Hidden Markov Model and employ detailed tap-stream data from a mobile game app to study how challenge-related factors and reward ads affect players dynamic engagement state evolution. First, we find that players’ perceived challenge level has a positive, but diminishing, marginal effect on players’ engagement state transitions. Second, a higher fluctuation of perceived challenge increases the probability that players move to a higher engagement state. Third, the scaffolding earned by watching reward ads can help players transition to a higher engagement level. Furthermore, we study the moderating effect of perceived challenge on reward ads, and we find that reward ads are more helpful in engaging players when the perceived challenge is higher. The findings enhance the understanding of players’ engagement with mobile games and provide design guidance for mobile game publishers.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid development of mobile technology and consumers’ increasing demand for portability, mobile gaming has become a prominent component of not only the video game market, but also the entire entertainment industry. The value of the global mobile gaming market was 131.2 billion U.S. dollars in 2021 and is projected to reach 173.4 billion U.S. dollars in 2026.<sup>1</sup> However, high attrition rates and low engagement are notorious problems faced by mobile game publishers. Mobile game apps, on average, only retain 25% of users after a single day and around 2.3% of users after a month.<sup>2</sup> Moreover, when monetizing their games, mobile game publishers face challenges in ensuring the profitability of mobile games (e.g., advertisement revenue) without losing the players’ engagement and negatively affecting players game experience. Although most mobile game apps rely on advertisement revenue, traditional advertisements are often obstructive. Therefore, to ensure the success of games, game publishers need to keep their players engaged and, at the same time, seek monetization strategies that do not interrupt players’ engagement.

To create successful mobile games, it is important to create a gaming environment that enhances players engagement, as higher engagement leads to prolonged game-play time, increased player retention, amplified network effects attracting new users, and higher revenue for game publishers. The concept of flow is often used to examine factors that lead to enhanced engagement, where flow refers to a peak engagement state in which a person is completely immersed and focused on the activity at hand (Csikszentmihalyi 1975, 1990). Game developers, publishers, and managers seek to design a mobile game that enhances players’ flow experiences and choose a mobile game monetization strategy that does not break or inhibit the flow experiences. This paper examines how different game design factors (i.e., perceived challenge and fluctuation of perceived challenge)

and a novel mobile game monetization strategy— reward ads—affect players’ engagement. Reward ads are a popular in-app advertising monetization model where users are offered in-game rewards, such as virtual currency, in exchange for voluntarily watching an advertisement.

To examine how different factors affect players’ engagement, we extend flow theory (Csikszentmihalyi 1975, 1990), which posits that an appropriate level of challenge is essential for experiencing flow. If a task is overly challenging, individuals might feel anxious, whereas if it is too easy, they may experience boredom. As adjusting the challenge levels within games can be an important strategy for game publishers in engaging players and fostering an enjoyable player experience (Li et al. 2023), we extend flow theory to examine how challenge-related factors and reward ads affect players engagement with mobile games. Specifically, we address the following two main research questions: (1) How do different challenge-related game design factors (i.e., perceived challenge and fluctuation of perceived challenge) affect mobile game players’ engagement state transitions? (2) How do the reward ads affect players’ engagement state transitions?

To address the first main research question, this paper examines how the perceived challenge level of the game affects players’ engagement. Then, we study the effect of the fluctuation of perceived challenge on players’ engagement evolution, which has not been empirically studied in the previous literature. Although most prior literature mainly considers game playing as a static process, we posit that it is important to examine dynamic fluctuation of players’ perceptions in challenge levels. If a mobile game is either consistently difficult or easy for players, the players may be more likely to quit the game because they can easily predict the challenge levels of the next few rounds and feel bored. Instead, fluctuation in players’ perceived challenge levels (e.g., “easy-difficulteasy” fluctuations) can keep them in suspense (Chane et al. 2008) and, at the same time, provide players with both the sense of stimulation and the sense of restoration (Hockey 1997).

In addition to the challenge-related game design factors, it is important to address the second main research question and adopt a mobile game monetization strategy that does not hinder players’ flow experiences. Delivering advertisement content for third parties via in-app advertising often creates a dilemma for game publishers; on the one hand, in-game advertisements generate revenue, but, on the other hand, the advertisement can annoyingly interrupt the flow of the game, discourage players from playing the game, and lower players engagement. Reward ads can offer a potential solution to this dilemma. Instead of forcing players to watch ads, reward ads are opt-in, meaning that players voluntarily choose whether to watch reward ads in exchange for in-game incentives or rewards, such as virtual coins, puzzle hints, extra lives, or other premium features that help them progress in the game. Reward ads are described as a win-win-win for players, game publishers, and advertisers; game players can choose to watch ads when they wish to, game publishers can increase revenue while keeping users engaged, and advertisers can enjoy a higher return on ad spending.<sup>3</sup> Although the reward ad is an innovative and popular advertisement format, the empirical literature on reward ads and their effect on players’ engagement has been limited.

To examine how reward ads affect players’ engagement with mobile games, we integrate flow theory with scaffolding theory (Wood et al. 1976, Vygotsky 1978). Scaffolding theory posits that individuals can reach higher levels of engagement when provided with scaf folding, which refers to temporal support provided so that the individual can complete challenging tasks (Wood et al. 1976). We hypothesize that in-game benefits earned from reward ads can act as scaffolds to provide support and guide anxious and frustrated individuals back into the flow, as hints from reward ads can help lower the perceived challenge level of puzzles and provide players with an opportunity to further explore the game. Furthermore, the scaffolding can be more valuable to players when the perceived challenge level of the game is higher. Therefore, we examine how players’ perceived challenge level moderates the effect of reward ads on players’ engagement.

We build a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) to examine how the aforementioned factors affect players’ engagement levels and to investigate their heterogeneous effects on players’ dynamic engagement transitions. The HMM framework provides a flexible way to account for possible changes in players’ latent engagement levels and observed activities (outcomes) by explicitly modeling the structural dynamics. The HMM assumes there are two processes, where one process is unobserved (hidden) and stochastically determines the observed outcome process (Netzer et al. 2008). In our context, players’ engagement, which refers to their immersion in the game, is a latent unobserved mental state, and playing behavior (i.e., the number of puzzles played) is the observed activity, which is closely related to and determined by players’ latent engagement states. We set the number of played puzzles as the observed outcome because it is closely related to game publishers’ success. We calibrate the HMM using a longitudinal data set obtained from a large mobile game company in the United States. The mobile game is a single-player puzzle-solving game, and reward ads are an important revenue source for the company.

Our findings can be summarized as follows. First, we find that perceived challenge levels have a diminishing positive effect on players’ engagement state transitions. The second-order effect of perceived challenge is negative, which indicates a diminishing marginal effect. Hence, excessively challenging games may potentially overwhelm players and hinder their engagement. Second, a higher fluctuation of perceived challenge levels increases the probability that players move to a higher state, and the positive effect is most effective for gamers who are in a high engagement state. Third, the scaffolding earned by watching reward ads can help players transition to a higher engagement level. Furthermore, we study the moderating effect of perceived challenge on reward ads, and we find that reward ads are more helpful in engaging players when the perceived challenge is higher.

The paper makes several important contributions, especially to the extant literature on flow theory and scaffolding theory. First, we contribute to the literature on flow theory by examining how perceived challenge levels and fluctuation of perceived challenge affect players’ engagement evolution. By using detailed and granular tap-stream data from a mobile gaming app, we provide empirical evidence on how theory-based challenge-related factors affect players’ engagement. In particular, the effect of the fluctuation of perceived challenge on players’ engagement has not been empirically studied in previous literature. Second, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to employ scaffolding theory to provide empirical evidence on how reward ads affect mobile game players’ engagement transitions. Although challenge is an important element in game design, few studies examine how to guide players back to the flow state when a game is very challenging. This paper bridges this important gap in the literature by providing a theoretical framework that integrates flow theory and scaffolding theory to understand how reward ads can provide access to scaffolds, which can help players keep engaged and enter the flow state. Furthermore, although scaffolding is a concept that is traditionally employed in the context of student learning and education (e.g., Anghileri 2006, Basawapatna et al. 2013), we apply this concept in mobile gaming to offer novel perspectives on examining the effect of reward ads on players’ engagement. Finally, we note that investigating the effects of challenge-related factors on engagement through observational data is often challenging due to omitted variable concerns. Fortunately, our single-player setting has no social interactions (e.g., connecting with friends through other social media channels or having a ranking system among players), which alleviates the concern of confounders such as peer effects, helps to identify the effect of challengerelated factors, and extends flow theory.

## 2. Related Literature

## 2.1. User Engagement in Online Games

This paper relates to prior literature on user engagement in online games, which spans several fields, including human-computer interaction (HCI), information systems, and marketing. The success of online games heavily depends on user engagement and continuous interactions; the more engaged the players are, the more revenue the game publishers can generate. According to the HCI literature, engagement is defined as the interaction between a user and a system (Attfield et al. 2011), and high engagement can be characterized by active participation, a high level of energy, and full absorption (Schau feli et al. 2002). Prior HCI literature employs surveys to examine how different game design features, such as the usability of software (Dyck et al. 2003), challenge levels (Malone 1980, Denisova et al. 2017), gamification (Deterding et al. 2011), and the values of progress (Barr et al. 2007), can relate to user engagement and contribute to flow. For example, Kang et al. (2020) examine how technical features, such as perceived control and perceived ease of use, and social interaction affect user engagement in multiplayer online battle arena games. Recent HCI literature also examines how recent advances in technologies, such as highly immersive visualization systems like the Oculus Rift, can lead to a higher level of engagement (Brondi et al. 2015). A potential limitation of prior HCI lit erature lies in how it primarily relies on surveys and interviews to measure user engagement and other game features, which could limit the real-world applicability of findings (Zhang et al. 2019). Prior information systems and marketing literature explore potential strategies to increase players’ engagement, where players’ engagement is measured by their participation levels. For exam ple, Liu et al. (2013) conduct an experimental study to examine how competition in games affects players engagement and emotions, and Gu et al. (2022) conducts randomized field experiments to examine how the crowd-sourcing feature affects players’ engagement.

This paper differs from and extends prior literature on game engagement in several aspects. First, most prior lit erature employs surveys and laboratory experiments to examine players’ engagement (e.g., Brondi et al. 2015, Kang et al. 2020). However, the simulated environments and study designs may not fully capture players’ real world decisions (Epstein and Harackiewicz 1992). We use granular game-playing data from a real-world mobile game to empirically examine players’ engagement. Second, most prior literature treats players’ engagement as a static process. For example, although the prior HCI literature recognizes that challenge levels can affect en gagement, most literature overlooks how players’ perceptions of challenge levels and their effects on engagement dynamically evolve. Online games are an experiential good that is consumed over time, with players engaging in continuous interaction with the game (Yang and Mai 2010). Because players’ engagement should be examined as a dynamic process, we employ detailed data and the HMM to examine how different factors affect players engagement transitions. Related to our examination of the dynamics of players’ engagement is Huang et al. (2019), which develops a two-stage data-analytic modeling approach to propose a matching algorithm to maximize video game players’ game-play. Different from Huang et al. (2019), which investigates a multiplayer video game where players compete with others for a higher ranking, we study a single-player mobile game and examine how different challenge-related factors and reward ads affect mobile game players’ engagement. Furthermore, our research setting does not offer a ranking system or social interactions among players, which helps us rule out other potential confounders, such as peer effects. Third, there has been a lack of empirical literature on how reward ads affect players’ engagement, and this paper addresses this gap in the literature.

## 2.2. Online Advertisement

The paper also relates to the prior literature on online advertisements by examining reward ads, which is a novel and popular, yet understudied, advertisement format. For many mobile apps, advertisements are an important source of revenue, especially given the low in-app purchase rates. There is an extensive prior literature on online advertisements (Liu et al. 2012, Zhang and Katona 2012, Teixeira et al. 2014), especially on the topics of advertisement effectiveness and content design (Lee et al. 2018a, Todri et al. 2020). However, although significant efforts have been made to improve advertisements effectiveness, the tension between advertisements and users remains pronounced, especially in mobile apps. Reward ads are an innovative ad format that can miti gate tensions between publishers and game players by providing a choice for players to watch ads in exchange for rewards in the game. Prior theoretical literature employs analytical models to examine when a platform should provide the option of reward ads (Guo et al. 2019, Sheng et al. 2022). For instance, Guo et al. (2019) provide an analytical model of reward advertising by considering three possible strategies: a premium contentselling strategy, a pure reward ads strategy, and a hybrid strategy. They derive the rule for determining which strategy is most profitable and the corresponding optimal reward rate. Sheng et al. (2022) find that whether to provide reward ads in the game depends on the degree to which incentivized actions distract players from making in-app purchases (i.e., cannibalization effect). However, there has been limited empirical literature on them. To the best of our knowledge, Chiong et al. (2017) is the only other empirical literature on reward ads; yet, they address a different research question and examine the effectiveness of reward ads on ad conversion (i.e., whether reward ads lead to more installations of the advertised app when compared with nonincentivized ads). There has not yet been literature that empirically examines the dynamic relationship between reward ads and mobile game players’ engagement. This paper employs individual-level mobile app tap-stream data to empirically examine how reward ads, along with other factors, dynamically affect mobile game players engagement.

## 3. Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses Development

## 3.1. Players’ Engagement and Flow Theory

We employ the theoretical framework of flow theory to examine how challenge-related factors and reward ads affect players’ engagement with mobile games. When individuals increase their engagement to a very high level, they can achieve a peak engagement state, which is called flow (Aubrey et al. 2022). According to flow theory, flow refers to a mental state where the individual is fully absorbed and involved in a task,<sup>4</sup> and a crucial element for a flow state is an appropriate level of challenge (Csikszentmihalyi 1975, 1990).

In examining how to elevate an individual’s engagement to a high level, prior literature employs the concept of flow and flow theory in various settings, including education (Carli et al. 1988, Shernoff et al. 2003), work (Ceja and Navarro 2011, Ilies et al. 2017), information technology usage (Agarwal and Karahanna 2000), social media (Kim et al. 2020), sports (Jackson 1995), and online games (Jones 1998, Cowley et al. 2008, Liu 2017). In this paper, we develop and estimate an HMM with granular game-play data to examine the impact of challengerelated factors and reward ads on player engagement. Specifically, we extend flow theory to examine how (i) perceived challenge levels and (ii) fluctuations of perceived challenge levels affect players’ engagement. Furthermore, we examine how (iii) reward ads, which can provide access to scaffolds and whose effect can be mod erated by the perceived challenge levels, affect players engagement.

## 3.2. Hypotheses Development

3.2.1. Perceived Challenge. First, we extend upon flow theory to posit that perceived challenge levels significantly affect players’ engagement with mobile games. Following flow theory, if a game is easy and simple, it will not require much effort or concentration from players, leading to a state of boredom rather than flow, which leaves players disengaged. At the same time, if the game is overly challenging and exceeds the optimal challenge level, it may hinder players’ progression and diminish their engagement with the game. Therefore, we hypothesize that the effect of perceived challenge is nonlinear, such that (a) some levels of perceived challenge have positive effects on players’ engagement transition (i.e., a positive linear term), whereas (b) too high levels of perceived challenge result in a reduced marginal effect and impede players’ engagement transition (i.e., a nega tive squared term). Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 1. Perceived challenge has a positive, but diminishing, marginal effect on players’ engagement state transitions.

3.2.2. Fluctuation of Perceived Challenge. We additionally examine how the fluctuation of perceived challenge affects players’ engagement. In the prior literature, the challenge-related factors are generally examined in static laboratory settings, and the dynamic fluctuations in gamers’ perceptions have been mostly ignored. However, the multiple periods of interactions between players and mobile games necessitate a dynamic view of players’ experiences in the game (Eliashberg and Sawhney 1994). In fact, flow is conceptualized as a dynamic process wherein a continued engagement is sustained through continuous back-and-forth interaction between the individual and the environment (Csikszentmihaly 1975, Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi 2002). Therefore, dynamic fluctuations in gamers’ perceptions, which have been largely overlooked in prior literature, should not be dismissed, as they can affect players’ flow experiences.

We posit that the fluctuation of perceived challenge levels can affect players’ flow experiences via the following mechanisms. First, fluctuations in challenge sequence can meet people’s variety-seeking demand (Sevilla et al. 2019); fluctuations can prevent monotonous experiences and sustain mental involvement, which is critical for keeping players absorbed in the activity, a key condition for flow. People get bored with doing the same thing, such as eating the same foods or listening to the same music. In the context of games, playing the same level of challenge several times can bring about boredom (Chanel et al. 2008), but variations (i.e., fluctuation) can have a positive effect on players’ engagement and help players feel more excited and stimulated over time (Rolls et al. 1981, Berger et al. 2021). Second, fluctuations in challenge levels can balance stimulation with cognitive restoration. Completing a difficult puzzle can be stimulating and foster flow by challenging players to exert mental effort (Groening and Binnewies 2019, Li et al. 2023). At the same time, alternating with an easier task afterward can prevent cognitive overload, which allows individuals to maintain emotional stability and recharge (Hockey 1997), keeping them in the flow state. Third, the fluctuation can keep players uncertain about the future challenge level, creating a feeling of suspense and heightening attention. In flow, focused attention is crucial; suspense is an effective tool to attract individuals’ attention, foster curiosity, and sustain engagement (Ely et al. 2015). Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2. The fluctuation of perceived challenge has a positive effect on players’ engagement state transitions.

3.2.3. Reward Ads. Reward ads are an innovative and unique advertisement format that consists of two distinct components: the advertisement itself and the reward provided to players after watching it. Because of this unique nature, the effect of reward ads on players engagement may be unclear and remains an empirical question. On the one hand, the advertisement aspect of reward ads can distract or annoy players and diminish their engagement with the game. On the other hand, the reward component can provide a channel for players to earn virtual currency and access the scaffolding, which can lower the challenge level of the puzzle and enhance their engagement. In this paper, we integrate the concept of flow with scaffolding theory (Wood et al. 1976, Vygotsky 1978) to posit that reward ads have overall positive effects on players’ engagement. According to scaffolding theory, individuals should be provided with scaffolds to complete challenging tasks, where scaffold refers to temporary support provided so that the individual can reach higher levels of engagement (Wood et al. 1976). The concepts of flow and scaffold can be complementary, as scaffolding can help guide frustrated and anxious individuals back into the flow state (Basawapatna et al. 2013). Therefore, we propose that reward ads can provide access to scaffolds and help keep players engaged. Specifically, when online games become too difficult, players may experience frustration and not be able to move forward for a long time, which can result in a game-quit decision and decrease players’ engagement. However, players would be less likely to feel frustrated if they were given sufficient scaffolding. In particular, rewards that players receive after voluntarily watching the reward ads allow them to buy premium hints that can lower the challenge level of puzzles and help them proceed to the next round. Furthermore, players can independently decide whether and when to watch the reward ads. Hence, reward ads can offer access to nondisruptive scaffolds that do not interfere with players flow experiences. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3. Reward ads, which provide access to scaf folds, have a positive effect on players’ engagement state transitions.

3.2.4. Moderating Role of Perceived Challenge. We examine how players’ perceived challenge level moderates the effect of reward ads on players’ engagement. Game players enjoy playing games when they feel that they are adequately challenged because it keeps them engaged and stimulated (Li et al. 2023). However, when the game becomes excessively challenging, players may experience frustration, leading to disengagement. In such instances, reducing perceived challenge levels can enhance both players’ engagement and long-term retention (Ascarza et al. 2024). Reward ads can provide a means for players to access scaffolding, which can help lower the perceived challenge level of the game. Therefore, the value of scaffolding would be higher when the perceived challenge level is higher, as scaffolding can help reduce the challenge level, alleviate the frustration of players, and enable players to continue making progress within the game without feeling overwhelmed. Conversely, when the perceived challenge level of the game is low, players would not require as much scaffolding and comfortably navigate the challenges on their own. Therefore, we posit that when perceived challenge levels of games are higher, the positive effect that reward ads have on players’ engagement would be greater. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 4. Perceived challenge positively moderates the effect of reward ads on players’ engagement state transitions.

3.2.5. Dynamics of Players’ Engagement. In examining players’ engagement with mobile games, it is important to note that players’ engagement exhibits a dynamic relationship. As players interact with the mobile game over time and progress through the game, players may be differently affected by different factors, including challenge-related factors and the scaffolding provided by reward ads. Therefore, a player’s engagement should be treated as a dynamic process because one factor may be more or less effective as players move to different engagement levels. In other words, even though the challenge-related factors and scaffolding from reward ads affect players’ engagement, their effectiveness may vary, depending on players’ engagement levels (Singh et al. 2011, Yan and Tan 2014). Therefore, we place emphasis on players’ dynamic playing behavior, and we adopt the HMM to capture and examine the dynamics in players’ engagement transitions and in-game activities.

## 4. Model

## 4.1. Model Setting

To empirically examine mobile game players’ engagement, we employ individual-level game-playing data from an anonymous mobile game app company in the United States. The company has over 100 million downloads across its portfolio of games. The game that we focus on is a single-player puzzle-solving game with over 200,000 monthly active users. The puzzle game genre is an extremely popular mobile game genre; in fact, the puzzle game is the most popular mobile game genre in the United States and the United Kingdom, with over 60% of mobile game players playing games in the puzzle genre.<sup>5</sup> The total revenue of the puzzle mobile games market reached 18.38 billion U.S. dollars in 2022 and is expected to reach 29.71 billion U.S. dollars by 2027, with a projected annual growth rate of 9%.<sup>6</sup>

In the puzzle game that we examine, players need to spell the right word based on hints from two pictures, and they earn a fixed number of virtual coins after successfully solving a puzzle. Players can use virtual coins to buy premium hints, such as revealing one letter, revealing one word in the picture, or skipping the current puzzle. Players obtain coins mainly in two ways: by solving the puzzle or by watching the reward ads.<sup>7</sup> The reward ad is a 30-second video advertisement, which is administrated by a third party, and the reward ad usu ally advertises other mobile apps. Players receive a fixed number of virtual coins after watching a reward ad. Watching a reward ad is a completely voluntary action. In other words, a player needs to click on a reward ad button to watch reward ads and receive virtual coins as a reward.<sup>8</sup> Players can choose to watch as many reward ads as they need to earn enough coins. Reward ads are a popular in-app advertising ad format that many other puzzle mobile games employ; a survey finds that 82% of mobile game app developer respondents use reward ads.<sup>9</sup>

## 4.2. Hidden Markov Mode

To examine our hypotheses on the effects of challenge related factors and reward ads, we introduce a Hidden Markov Model that identifies a player’s engagement as a latent state, and the transition across different engagement states is a Markovian process. The HMM that we adopt is widely used in the marketing and information systems fields (Netzer et al. 2008, Singh et al. 2011, Lee et al. 2018b, Zhang et al. 2019). An HMM is a model of a stochastic process that is not directly observed, but can be observed through another set of stochastic processes that produce a set of observations (MacDonald and Zucchini 1997). The HMM is suitable for our context because players’ engagement level is not directly observed, but is rather a reflection of the process which leads to the observed activities (i.e., game-play decisions). Moreover, the HMM allows us to segment players according to their engagement levels and investigate the varying impacts of challenge-related factors on engagement evolution. In the HMM, the number of puz zles completed by the player is set as the observed out come that is stochastically determined by hidden engagement states. Figure 1 presents the framework of our HMM. The figure illustrates how players transition between different levels of engagement through gameplay activity and how their observed outcomes depend on the latent engagement states. Similar to other HMMs, the proposed HMM in our paper comprises three elements: the initial state distribution, ; the state-transition matrix, Q; and the observed outcome distribution, P.

where $\mu ( s , m )$ is the threshold for the transition from state s to state m, and $\mu ( s , n ) \geqslant \mu ( s , n - 1 ) \geqslant \cdots \geqslant \mu ( s ,$ $s + 1 ) \geqslant \mu ( s , s - 1 ) \geqslant \cdots \geqslant \mu ( s , 1 )$ ). $X _ { i t }$ is a vector of covariates that affect state transition, $\pmb { \alpha } _ { s }$ is a vector of corresponding coefficients, and $\eta _ { i }$ is the player-specific random effects to capture the individual unobserved heterogeneity that may affect state transition.<sup>10</sup>

## 4.4. State-Dependent Outcome

Given the player’s state, the observed outcome is assumed to be conditionally independent. In our model, the state-dependent outcome is the number of puzzles a player played; players initially decide whether to play the game in each period, and then, conditional on play ing the game, they decide how many puzzles to play. Therefore, the number of puzzles a player completes is modeled using a hurdle model, which is composed of two parts (Feng 2021). The first part is a logit model that governs whether a player plays the game or not. We denote $d _ { i t } = 1$ if a player i plays the game and solves at least one puzzle on day t. If a player i does not solve any puzzle on day $t ,$ we set $d _ { i t } = 0 .$ . The probability of observing $d _ { i t } = 1$ is modeled as follows: $\textstyle P r ( d _ { i t } = \mathbf { \dot { 1 } } \mid S _ { i t } = s ) =$ $e x \breve { p } ( \beta _ { s } ) / ( 1 + e x p ( \beta _ { s } ) )$ , where $\beta _ { s }$ is a state-dependent constant. Then, the second part of the hurdle model expresses the distribution of nonzero counts of the number of puzzles played by using a zero-truncated negative binomial model. Therefore, conditional on her engagement state $s ,$ the probability of observing that player i played $Y _ { i t }$ puzzles in period t is:

$$
P r (Y _ {i t} \mid S _ {i t} = s) = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} 1 - P r (d _ {i t} = 1 \mid S _ {i t} = s) & Y _ {i t} = 0, \\ \frac {P r (d _ {i t} = 1 \mid S _ {i t} = s)}{(1 - f (Y _ {i t} = 0 \mid S _ {i t} = s))} \\ \frac {\Gamma (Y _ {i t} + \theta_ {s})}{\Gamma (\theta_ {s}) \Gamma (Y _ {i t} + 1)} \bigg (\frac {\lambda_ {s}}{\lambda_ {s} + \theta_ {s}} \bigg) ^ {Y _ {i t}} \\ \bigg (\frac {\theta_ {s}}{\lambda_ {s} + \theta_ {s}} \bigg) ^ {\theta_ {s}} & Y _ {i t} > 0, \end{array} \right.\tag{2}
$$

where $Y _ { i t }$ is the number of puzzles that a player i played in period $t . f ( Y _ { i t } )$ is the probability mass function of the negative binomial distribution, and $f ( Y _ { i t } = 0 \mid S _ { i t } = s ) =$ $( \theta _ { s } / \lambda _ { s } + \theta _ { s } ) ^ { \theta _ { s } } ,$ , where $\theta _ { s }$ is the overdispersion rate of $Y _ { i t } .$ In addition, $\lambda _ { s } = e x p ( \gamma _ { s } Z _ { i t } + \zeta _ { i } )$ , where $Z _ { i t }$ is a vector of covariates that affect the observed outcome, $\gamma _ { s }$ is a vector of corresponding coefficients, and $\zeta _ { i }$ is the player-specific random effects to capture the individual unobserved heterogeneity that may affect the puzzle volume. We allow the player-specific random effects $\eta _ { i }$ and $\zeta _ { i }$ to be correlated by modeling the conditional distribution of the two random effects.

Considering a sequence of game-play outcomes $Y ( i ) =$ $Y _ { i 1 } Y _ { i 2 } \cdots Y _ { i T }$ for player i and a sequence of states $S ( i ) = S _ { i 1 } S _ { i 2 } \cdots S _ { i T } ,$ we can write the conditional likelihood function as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} = \sum_ {s _ {1} = 1} ^ {n} \sum_ {s _ {2} = 1} ^ {n} \dots \sum_ {s _ {T _ {i}} = 1} ^ {n} \left[ P r (S _ {i 1} = s _ {1}) \prod_ {t = 2} ^ {T _ {i}} q _ {i t - 1} (s _ {t - 1}, s _ {t}) \right. \\ \times \prod_ {t = 1} ^ {T _ {i}} \bigg (P r (d _ {i t} = 1 \mid S _ {i t} = s _ {t}) \times \frac {f (Y _ {i t} \mid S _ {i t} = s _ {t})}{1 - f (Y _ {i t} = 0 \mid S _ {i t} = s _ {t})} \bigg) ^ {d _ {i t} = 1} \\ \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \qquad \times (1 - P r (d _ {i t} = 1 \mid S _ {i t} = s _ {t})) ^ {d _ {i t} = 0} \bigg ], \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

where $s _ { t } \in { 1 , 2 , \dots , n }$ is the state that a player can possibly reside in period $t , T _ { i }$ is the length of player i’s outcome sequence. Note that we model the process from the time that players start to play the game until their last observation in our time window. Thus, different players can have different outcome lengths, and $T _ { i }$ varies across players. Finally, the likelihood of player i can be obtained by integrating over the two player-specific random effects η and $\zeta ,$ where H(ζ) is the distribution of ζ and $G ( \eta | \dot { \zeta } )$ is the conditional distribution of $\eta$ given ζ.

$$
L (Y (i)) = \int_ {\zeta} \int_ {\eta} L (Y (i) \mid \zeta , \eta) d G (\eta \mid \zeta) d H (\zeta).\tag{4}
$$

## 5. Data and Variable Construction

To examine players’ engagement evolution, we employ players’ tap-stream data of the puzzle game from a United Sates-based mobile game app company. We randomly select 1,232 players who joined the mobile game app on or after January 1, 2018. The data include players’ complete game-play tap-stream from January 1 to February 28, 2018. We aggregate in-game activities to a daily level, and time period t in the HMM refers to date t. The aggregation approach is appropriate in our setting because the average time to solve a single puzzle is around 80 seconds, and state transitions are unlikely to happen so frequently. Table 1 provides a summary of variable descriptions and statistics. As shown in the model in Section 4, the HMM has two components: state transition matrix and state-dependent outcome. Therefore, two sets of variables are constructed: variables affecting state transition and variables affecting state-dependent outcomes.

## 5.1. Variables Affecting State Transition

We first explain how variables that affect state transition are constructed. These variables allow us to examine how challenge-related factors and scaffolding provided by reward ads affect players’ engagement and flow experiences.

First, in Hypothesis 1, we examine how players’ perceived challenge levels of the game affect engagement state transitions. Hence, we construct the variable perceived challenge in the following way. We note that in a single-player mobile game context, puzzle-solving time captures how difficult the player perceives the puzzle to be. At the same time, a player’s prior puzzle-solving experiences may affect the player’s current perception of the challenge. For example, consider a scenario in which player i needed 30 seconds to solve one puzzle, whereas player j needed 90 seconds to solve a puzzle prior to period t. In such a case, even if player i and player j spend 60 seconds to solve one puzzle in period t, their perceptions of the challenge would be different. To account for this scenario, we construct the variable perceived challenge at the individual level by using each player’s cumulative average puzzle-solving time till period $t . ^ { 1 1 }$ We also include the quadratic term of perceived challenge to examine whether there is a nonlinear relationship between the perceived challenge and the propensity of moving to or staying at a higher engagement state.

Second, in Hypothesis 2, we examine the effects of round-to-round shifts in perceived challenge levels $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ fluctuation of perceived challenge), which have not been studied in the prior empirical literature. By extending upon the fluctuation measurements employed in the finance (Markowitz 1952) and marketing (Berger et al. 2021) literature, we define the fluctuation of perceived chal lenge as the standard deviation of differences in puzzle solving time between adjoining puzzles. For example, in each period $t ,$ for $k \in 2 , 3 , \ldots , K _ { i } ,$ , where $K _ { i }$ is the total number of played puzzles for player i at time $t ,$ we first calculate $\Delta _ { k } ,$ which is the difference between player $i \prime \mathrm { s }$ puzzle-solving time for puzzle k and puzzle $k ^ { ^ { \star } } - \overset { ^ { \star } } { 1 } . ^ { 1 2 }$ Then, we use the standard deviation over all $\Delta _ { k }$ for $k \in$ $2 , 3 , \ldots , K _ { i }$ to measure the fluctuation in period t. To alleviate the potential correlation between perceived challenge and fluctuation of perceived challenge, we standardize fluctuation of perceived challenge by dividing it by perceived challenge. Finally, similar to perceived challenge, we take its cumulative average till period t to represent the player’s fluctuation of perceived challenge, accounting for the player’s previous experience in the game. In addition, we note that puzzle levels in the game may become increasingly difficult or easy in some periods, and the temporary increasing or decreasing trend may affect players’ engagement transition. Therefore, we control for the trend in perceived challenge over each period by running a linear regression on the puzzles solved by player i in period t and using the estimated coefficient from the regression to measure the increasing or decreasing trend in puzzle levels solved by player i in period t.<sup>13</sup>

Third, to examine how the scaffold provided by reward ads helps keep players engaged (Hypothesis 3), we construct the variable reward ads, which measures the number of reward ads watched in period t. We note that reward ads provide a channel to earn virtual currency to access scaffolding. Because players watch reward ads to collect coins to purchase hints, the number of reward ads watched reflects the amount of scaffolding accessed in period t.<sup>14</sup> Furthermore, we include the interaction terms between reward ads and players’ perceived challenge to examine its moderating effect (Hypothesis 4). We examine the moderating effects because reward ads can help lower the perceived challenge levels of puzzles and provide players with an opportunity to further explore the game. Finally, similar to most other mobile apps, there can be a satiation or addiction effect among players as they solve more puzzles and become more experienced in playing the mobile game (Han et al. 2016, Kwon et al. 2016). Therefore, we include the variable cumulative puzzles, which measures the cumulative number of puzzles solved until period t, to control its effect on engagement state transitions; the control variable can account for players’ achievement, experience, satiation, and addiction levels since they joined the platform. We note that all variables that affect state transitions are constructed at the individual level by using the detailed tap-stream data.

## 5.2. Variables Affecting Observed Outcome

As discussed in Section 4, in our HMM, the statedependent observed outcome is the number of puzzles that the player played. In each period, a player first decides whether to play the game and then decides how many puzzles to play, conditional on playing the game. A state-dependent constant β<sub>s</sub> is used to model the binary playing decision, and we adopt a normalization requirement that β is ordered, which ensures the identification of the states. This indicates that, without any stimulus, players in a higher engagement state are more likely to play the game than players in a lower engagement state.

Certain variables can directly affect the number of puzzles played, and we include such variables as variables affecting observed outcomes. We include the variable soft currency uses, which measures the number of virtual coins that a player spends to purchase premium hints to solve more puzzles. In-app purchases measures the number of in-app purchases the player made to help the puzzle-solving process. Furthermore, to capture how historical game-playing behaviors relate to current play ing activity, we construct two variables that describe players’ historical game-play patterns: historical intensity and irregularity. The variable historical intensity is constructed as the total number of puzzles solved divided by the number of active days before the most recent active period. The variable irregularity is defined as the standard deviation of time intervals among active days until time $t . ^ { 1 5 }$ The higher the standard deviation, the more irregular the player’s playing pattern. Finally, we include two indicator variables, weekend (i.e., a variable

equal to one if period t is Saturday or Sunday, and zero otherwise) and work hour (i.e., a variable equal to one if the playing time is between 9 a.m. and 5 p.m., and zero otherwise), to control for the time availability of players.<sup>16</sup>

## 6. Estimation Results and Findings 6.1. Model Selection

In the HMM, the number of hidden states is unknown, and the model needs to find the number of hidden states that best fits the data. We use maximum likelihood estimation to estimate the HMM parameters. Table 2 shows that the model with three hidden states outperforms all other specifications because it has a smaller Bayesian information criterion (BIC) and Akaike information criterion (AIC), as highlighted in bold. The three states represtart with a latent class model to estimate the initial distribution for players’ hidden engagement states. To avoid the label switching, we add constraints on the intercept in the logit probability of playing, such that $\beta _ { L } < \beta _ { M } < \beta _ { H } ,$ to ensure that players in higher engagement states have a higher intrinsic propensity to play the game.

We follow Heckman and Singer (1984) and use a nonparametric approach to estimate the random effects, ζ and η. The approximation process for the underlying unknown probability distribution is evaluated by a finite set of supporters associated with their corresponding probability mass functions. We relate two normalizing constants, $C _ { \zeta }$ and $C _ { \eta } ,$ with the supporters and set the boundary on each of the random effects to be between zero and one. The optimal number of supporters is chosen by model fit. Online Appendix D shows the loglikelihood values with different combinations of supporter numbers; the optimal numbers for ζ and η are three and two, respectively.

## 6.3. Robustness Checks

We conduct various robustness checks to ensure the robustness of our results. First, the main HMM assumes that players stay at the same engagement level and there is no state transition when players are not playing the game. In Online Appendix A, we allow state transition to take place in periods when players have no game-play activities. Second, in the main model, the state transition process is described as an ordered logit distribution. In Online Appendix F, we use the ordered probit distribution to model transition probabilities where the error term follows a normal distribution. Third, in Online Appendix G, we show that the results are robust to employing alternative user samples. Fourth, we show that the results still hold if we use the playing time in each period as the observed outcome (Online Appendix H). Fifth, to examine whether the effects of challenge related factors are sensitive to players’ tenure, we run an additional robustness check and control for Tenure in variables affecting state transitions (Online Appendix I). In Online Appendix J, we investigate the potential mod erating effect of fluctuation of perceived challenge on reward ads. Finally, in Online Appendix K, we use alternative constructs of challenge-related factors to account for players’ previous experiences and show that the results remain robust. Overall, we find that our results are robust to alternative variable constructions, alternative model structures, and alternative assumptions.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusion

Mobile games have become an important component of the entertainment industry. Similar to other mobile apps, users’ (players’) engagement and activity levels are key performance indicators, and improving engagement levels is strongly related to a mobile game’s success in the market. At the same time, game publishers also need to monetize the app. Many mobile games adopt a freemium business model, and because of low in-game purchase rates, in-app advertising has been a significant revenue source for many mobile games. In-app advertising creates a dilemma for game publishers, as in-game advertisements generate revenue, but the advertisement can interrupt the flow of the game and harm players in-app experience. Reward ads can offer a solution that balances publishers’ needs for revenue and players engagement with the game.

We use an HMM to examine how mobile games challenge-related factors and the scaffold provided by reward ads affect players’ dynamic engagement transitions. We employ flow theory and scaffolding theory to examine factors that can enhance players’ engagement. First, we extend flow theory to empirically find that perceived challenge has a positive nonlinear effect on players’ engagement state transitions. Second, in the prior literature on flow theory, the dynamic fluctuations in gamers’ perceptions of challenge levels have been mostly overlooked. We contribute to flow theory by empirically examining the effect of fluctuation of perceived challenge in stimulating players’ engagement. Third, we contribute to scaffolding theory by providing empirical evidence that hints earned from reward ads can serve as an efficient tool to guide players to a flow state.

## 7.1. Practical and Managerial Implications

Our findings have important managerial and practical implications for the pertinent stakeholders, including mobile game developers and advertisers, as players engagement is of focal importance to them. First, as we find that challenge-related factors, including perceived challenge levels and fluctuations in challenge levels, affect players’ engagement, game developers should consider implementing a system for detecting players perceived challenge levels and dynamically adjusting the challenge levels of their games to keep players engaged. In addition, game developers should design games with fluctuations in challenge levels to provide players with a sense of stimulation and restoration, thereby keeping players engaged. Second, the finding that the scaffolding from reward ads can keep players engaged provides empirical evidence that reward ads can create a win-win situation for both game developers and advertisers. In light of these findings, game developers who have not yet adopted reward ads into their games can consider incorporating reward ads to enhance players’ engagement. Finally, as our finding reveals that the positive effect of reward ads is stronger when players face a higher perceived challenge level, game developers can adjust their game designs accordingly. For example, game developers can strategically display gentle reminders for the reward ads when players perceive a certain puzzle to be particularly challenging and players do not have sufficient in-game currency. Such reminders, which should be displayed in a nonintrusive manner when a player is stuck in the game and in need of extra help, can help players make progress and guide players back to the flow state, enhancing players’ overall engagement with the game.

## 7.2. Limitations and Future Research

This paper is not without limitations, which can help identify potential avenues for future research. First, a potential limitation of our research is that we employ data from a single-player mobile puzzle game. Hence, although the puzzle game is one of the most popular mobile game genres, our findings may not be fully generalizable to other mobile game genres. For example, in the battle-royale game genre, which is another popular game genre where a player fights against other players, players may be additionally affected by social motivation, which captures users’ needs to interact with others in online communities (Hsu and Lu 2004). Therefore, future research can examine other game genres to further investigate how challenge-related factors interact with social motivation factors to affect players’ engagement in mobile games. Second, although this paper examines the effects of reward ads on players’ engagement, we do not study the effects of reward ads on other potential outcomes, such as advertisers’ and game publishers’ revenue. Therefore, future research can empirically examine how reward ads affect not only players’ engagement, but also game publishers’ revenue and monetization strategies, as well as advertisers’ returns on investments. Finally, we study reward ads in the context of mobile games, but future research can additionally examine how reward ads have been employed in different contexts. For example, there have been trends where reward ads have been employed in e-commerce settings, where shoppers are offered shopping credit for watching the ad.<sup>18</sup> Future research can investigate how reward ads affect their users shopping experience and ad performance.
