Abstract. This study examines the impact of technological acquisitions on contributions to <sup>fi</sup>rm-sponsored community-based open-source software (OSS). We distinguish between internal contributors af<sup>fi</sup>liated with target <sup>fi</sup>rms and external contributors from the commu nity, and examine how they respond to technological acquisitions differently. Theoretically, we examine how technological acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences contributors’ uncertainty about project quality through a signaling effect and in<sup>fl</sup>uences their uncertainty about project continuity through potential resource combination. We connect uncertainties with contrib utors’ motivations to theorize their responses to acquisitions. Empirically, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that external contributors contribute more actively to both target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ sponsored projects and other projects in the OSS community after acquisitions, which contrast with the adverse ef fects of acquisitions observed in traditional corporate innovation. Although internal contributors reduce contributions to target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ sponsored projects after acquisitions, they in crease contributions to other OSS projects in the community. We also <sup>fi</sup>nd that the acquirer’s OSS experience and the project similarity between the acquirer and the target drive both external and internal contributors to shift their development efforts to the acquirer’s projects and other projects in the OSS community. By examining these effort shifts in OSS contributions, our study generates unique theoretical insights about the impacts of technological acquisitions in the OSS context and important practical implications for ac quirers, target <sup>fi</sup>rms, and the general OSS community.

## 1. Introduction

After three decades of development, open-source software (OSS) has become the status quo for many commercial <sup>fi</sup>rms to achieve technological innovation (Wurster 2011, Black Duck Software 2015, Germonprez et al. 2017). As <sup>fi</sup>rms increasingly realize the importance of OSS, technological acquisitions are becoming a key approach to access OSS resources and bolster OSS development. For example, the year 2018 alone witnessed several high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le OSS-related acquisitions, including IBM’s \$34 billion acquisition of Red Hat (one of the biggest acquisitions in tech history), Salesforce’s \$6.5 billion acquisition of Mulesoft, Adobe’s \$1.68 billion acquisition of Magento, and AT&T’s acquisition of Alien Vault (Finley 2018).

Many acquisition decisions (e.g., the IBM–Red Hat deal) were reportedly motivated by the target <sup>fi</sup>rms OSS innovation practices and prospects, such as their promising OSS projects, developer resources, and long-term collaboration potentials in the OSS communities (Baird et al. 2019). Therefore, in most cases, acquirers are expected to sustain target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OSS projects and retain or even expand their pools of OSS contributors (Whitehurst 2018). However, industry reports have shown mixed <sup>fi</sup>ndings on how OSS contributors react to acquisitions (Vaughan-Nichols 2018). Whereas high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le technological acquisitions can sometimes motivate OSS contributors to continue their efforts, they may also result in disincentives. Thus, it is important to understand how OSS contributors, especially those of target <sup>fi</sup>rms, react to technological acquisitions and how to motivate and sustain their development efforts after acquisitions.

Despite the importance of OSS and the prevalence of OSS-related acquisitions, the research on how technological acquisitions impact OSS activities remains scarce. The existing studies of OSS have primarily focused on how individual-level characteristics and project-level designs (e.g., reward systems) motivate individual developers to contribute to OSS projects (e.g., Roberts et al. 2006, Fang and Neufeld 2009, Hann et al. 2013, Spaeth et al. 2015). The understanding of OSS development from the <sup>fi</sup>rms’ perspectives remains limited (Nagle 2019). In particular, technological acquisitions have rarely been studied in the OSS context. In parallel, while the mergers and acquisitions (M&A) literature has considered how acquisitions in<sup>fl</sup>uence corporate innovation (Veugelers 2006), this literature has primarily focused on <sup>fi</sup>rms’ internal innovation activities and outputs, such as research and development (R&D) (e.g., Ahuja and Katila 2001, Cassiman et al. 2005) and patents (e.g., Aggarwal and Hsu 2014), of target <sup>fi</sup>rms or merged entities. The extant perspectives are still inadequate for understanding open innovation activities such as OSS development.

The OSS context makes the impact of technological acquisition unique in several aspects. First, whereas traditional corporate innovation is mostly driven by internal contributors, OSS projects can also be pushed forward by external contributors not af<sup>fi</sup>liated with the sponsor <sup>fi</sup>rms. External contributors with various motivations, behavior, and responsibilities are likely in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by technological acquisitions in different ways compared with internal contributors. Second, in the open space of OSS, both external and internal contributors in the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s projects can also work on outside projects not sponsored by the target. They have the <sup>fl</sup>exibility of shifting development efforts within the OSS community as a response to technological acquisition. Therefore, it is imperative to consider the implications of such effort shift for the parties directly involved in acquisition (i.e., the acquirer and the target) and the whole OSS community.

We focus on two main research questions in this study. First, we explore how external contributors and internal contributors of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS projects respond differently to technological acquisitions.<sup>1</sup> In this context, external contributors are independent outside developers, and internal contributors are developers af<sup>fi</sup>liated with the target <sup>fi</sup>rm. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we study how technological acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences the internal and external contributions to the following types of projects: (1) the target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ projects, (2) the acquirers projects, and (3) other outside projects. Distinguishing between internal and external contributors helps illustrate how the impacts of technological acquisition in the OSS context manifest differently from those in the traditional context of corporate innovation.

Second, we examine the factors that moderate the impact of technological acquisitions on OSS. We focus on the potential of resource combination between the acquirer and the target <sup>fi</sup>rm (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999), as re<sup>fl</sup>ected in two factors. The <sup>fi</sup>rst factor is whether the acquirer has its own OSS development experience. As acquisition is usually a corporate strategy to obtain external technological capabilities (Rothaermel and Alexandre 2009, Ransbotham and Mitra 2010), an experienced acquirer’s acquisition decision can inform the target’s OSS contributors dif ferently from that of an inexperienced acquirer. The second factor is the similarity between the acquirer’s OSS projects and the target’s OSS projects. Based on the literature on technological overlap (Ahuja and Katila 2001, Cassiman et al. 2005), similar technological experiences between the acquirer and the target are likely to drive the combination of their resources through acquisition (Cassiman et al. 2005).

To address these research questions, we integrate two theoretical lenses from the literature. First, we adopt a lens of signaling (Graebner 2004, 2009) to consider how technological acquisition signals the value of OSS and the acquirer’s interest and therefore resolves the uncertainty encountered by OSS contributors (Hahn et al. 2008, Maruping et al. 2019). The signaling perspective enables us to theorize how acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences the motivations for OSS contributors and how they al locate efforts after acquisition. Second, we adopt a lens of resource combination in acquisition (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999) to theorize how the motivating effect of acquisition through signaling is moderated by the potential of resource combination. Such an integrated perspective allows us to delineate the differential motivations and the different postacquisition effort reallocation behaviors of external contributors and internal con tributors of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm.

Empirically, we compile a unique monthly <sup>fi</sup>rmlevel data set that contains OSS activities and acquisition events of 10,397 technology <sup>fi</sup>rms that sponsor a total of 96,517 OSS projects on the OSS community of GitHub (github.com) from January 2010 to November 2015. GitHub is currently the largest web-based repository hosting service, offering free public repositories to individual code developers and <sup>fi</sup>rms to host their community-based OSS projects.<sup>2</sup> To obtain information about acquisition events, we use both the SDC Platinum database and Crunchbase Data Solutions, the two databases with comprehensive coverage of acquisition information on both start-ups and established <sup>fi</sup>rms (e.g., Hanelt et al. 2021). We combine both data sources and use a difference-in-differences estimation approach in conjunction with multiple matching and instrumental variable approaches to draw empirical inferences about how internal and external contributors of target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OSS projects react to technological acquisitions. We also verify our key <sup>fi</sup>ndings using a series of alternative model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, robustness checks, and additional analyses.

Our study generates several key <sup>fi</sup>ndings with signi<sup>fi</sup>cant theoretical implications. First, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, after acquisition, the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external contributors increase their contributions to both the target’s OSS projects (by 15.1%) and other projects in the OSS community (by 31.6%), which contrasts with the existing <sup>fi</sup>ndings on how acquisitions reduce innovation in the traditional internal corporate environment (e.g., Hitt et al. 1991, Veugelers 2006, Szucs ¨ 2014). We do <sup>fi</sup>nd that technological acquisitions reduce internal contributors’ contributions to the target’s OSS projects (by 28.4%), but they also increase their contributions to other projects in the OSS community (by 26.3%). As a result, technological acquisitions increase the overall activeness of both external contributors and internal contributors. A key implication is that some adverse effects of acquisitions still apply to internal contributors in the OSS context. However, the open nature of OSS provides a broader space of opportunities to realize the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of technological acquisitions.

Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that the acquirer’s OSS experience encourages both external and internal contributors to shift their development efforts from the target’s projects to the acquirer’s OSS projects and other projects. A similar effect is also observed when the acquirer and the target have more similar OSS projects. The implications of these <sup>fi</sup>ndings are twofold. First, our study illustrates how the acquirer’s own projects can bene<sup>fi</sup>t from technological acquisition. Second, by examining the shift of development efforts, the study helps better understand the implications of combination potential in acquisition for not only the acquirer and the target but also the broad OSS community. The <sup>fi</sup>ndings also generate new insights on how the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of combination potential unfold in OSS development.

## 2. Theory and Literature

## 2.1. Technological Acquisition and Firm Innovation

Examining OSS development from the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s perspective, our study is related to the literature on how technological acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation. The literature has documented mixed empirical <sup>fi</sup>ndings (Bertrand 2009, Stiebale and Reize 2011, Szucs¨ 2014). On the one hand, acquisition can create resource constraints that undermine managerial commitment to innovation (Hitt et al. 1990), cause organizational complexity, increase costs that impede innovation (e.g., Stiebale and Reize 2011, Szucs¨ 2014), and result in employee resistance (Ernst and Vitt 2000). On the other hand, acquisition bene<sup>fi</sup>ts <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation through synergizing resources to increases economies of scale and scope (Cassiman et al. 2005) and through reducing undesirable technological spillovers (Szucs¨ 2014).

The extant literature has largely theorized innovation as <sup>fi</sup>rm-controlled activities, where the acquirer or the merged entity acts as the central decision maker to determine innovation. Such a theoretical perspective is not suf<sup>fi</sup>cient for understanding the implications of acquisition in the open innovation space, since it does not account for external contributors’ in<sup>fl</sup>uences on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS innovation or the contributions of internal developers to the OSS community. Since individual developers have much <sup>fl</sup>exibility in allocating their contribution efforts across various projects in the open space of OSS, we aim to incorporate the broader implications of technological acquisition for different types of OSS contributors and the whole OSS community.

## 2.2. Uncertainty in Continued OSS Contribution

The OSS literature has extensively studied various intrinsic and extrinsic motivations that drive contributions to OSS (e.g., Lerner and Tirole 2002, Fang and Neufeld 2009, Von Krogh et al. 2012). Recent research has adopted an uncertainty perspective (Hahn et al. 2008, Maruping et al. 2019). The main proposition from this perspective is that the motivations are in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by OSS contributors’ uncertainty about contribution processes and outcomes (Maruping et al. 2019). Continued OSS contribution would occur when contributor uncertainty decreases, which is consistent with the general lens of uncertainty reduction theory (e.g., Ramirez et al. 2002). Because the uncertainty is multifaceted, contributors seek informational cues from various sources, for example, their observations of community environment (Maruping et al. 2019) and interactions with other parties (Hahn et al. 2008), to resolve their uncertainties.

In this study, we adopt a theoretical lens of signaling to consider how technological acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences the uncertainty of the target’s OSS contributors and their resulting OSS contribution behavior. The main proposition here is that acquisition signals the value of the target’s OSS projects to the latter’s contributors, especially external ones.

## 2.3. Signaling and Combination Potential in Technological Acquisition

The general concept of signaling refers to employing various mechanisms to reduce information asymmetry (e.g., Spence 1974). Interorganizational interactions constitute a key signaling mechanism to inform external stakeholders and reduce their uncertainty in eval uating <sup>fi</sup>rm innovation (Reuer et al. 2012). For example, venture capital’s involvement signals the quality of the start-up’s innovation (Brav and Gompers 1997, Blevins and Ragozzino 2018). External stakeholders can also infer the innovation quality from <sup>fi</sup>rms’ alliances with prominent partners (Stuart et al. 1999). Likewise, in technological acquisition, the acquirer’s decision to engage in the acquired innovation domains can signal the prospects of these domains (Graebner 2009).

The OSS literature has adopted the signaling perspective to examine contribution activities (Head 2016, Ho and Rai 2017). Since OSS contributors, especially external ones, are typical external stakeholders who do not observe all organizational details (Ho and Rai 2017), they seek signals to address the uncertainty about the prospects of their OSS projects and ascertain the value of their efforts (Arantes and Freire 2011). In technological acquisition, as the acquirer usually expends substantial time and effort in due diligence and negotiations, its acquisition decision can signal the value of the target’s OSS projects. Individual contributors can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by this signal in determining their future contributions.

Whereas the acquisition signals the value of the target’s OSS projects, the response in development efforts from the target’s contributors can be in<sup>fl</sup>uenced by how the acquirer deals with acquired projects. The combination of technological resources to realize integrational advantages is a key factor that drives acquisition decisions (Blonigen and Taylor 2000, Phillips and Zhdanov 2013, Bena and Li 2014). Therefore, combination potential, the extent to which the acquirer can consolidate the target’s resources with those of its own (Lubatkin 1983, Larsson and Finkelstein 1999), is another relevant factor that in<sup>fl</sup>uences the impact of acquisition on OSS contributions. Whereas combination through acquisition generates synergistic bene<sup>fi</sup>ts for the merged entity, the combination of similar operations often elicits resistance from the employees of the target, because their positions and career opportunities are potentially jeopardized (Datta 1991, Chatterjee et al. 1992, Larsson and Finkelstein 1999). Therefore, in the OSS context, the effect of value signal in acquisition on the incentives of the target’s individual contributors should also be dependent on the effect of combination potential. We take an integrated perspective to consider how the signaling effect of acquisition is moderated by the combination potential. This integrated perspective facilitates the better understanding of how contributors, under development uncertainties, choose to shift their efforts across different types of OSS projects.

We consider two observable factors relevant to combination potential in the OSS context. First, the behavioral learning perspective (Haleblian and Finkelstein 1999) suggests that the acquirer’s experience determines how resources and capabilities are effectively combined in acquisitions. We therefore consider the observable OSS experience of the acquirer as an indicator of combination potential. Second, from a resource perspective, the higher similarity between the acquirer and the target in product markets and operational arrangements, the greater potential that their resources would be combined in acquisition (e.g.,

Stimpert and Duhaime 1997, Pehrsson 2006, Meyer and Altenborg 2008). This is also in line with the view of strategic <sup>fi</sup>t, which suggests that related businesses that share similarities in management styles, organizational culture, and administrative processes can integrate resources and capabilities more effectively (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999, Bauer and Matzler 2014). We therefore consider the project similarity between the acquirer and the target, which is usually publicly observable in the OSS space, as another indicator of combination potential.

The open nature of OSS in our research context plays a nontrivial role in differentiating our consideration of signaling and combination potential from that in the traditional research of acquisition. First, the traditional research of acquisition that focuses on in-house innovation has paid little attention to external contributors (Oberg<sup>¨</sup> 2016), which are an integral part of OSS. Second, in the OSS context, whereas the target’s internal employees may resist the combination activities in the acquisition, they have options in the open space of OSS to choose between their existing projects, the acquirer’s projects, and other projects within the OSS community (Hahn et al. 2008). The OSS environment’s unique features would add complexity to the implications of combination potential in technological acquisition.

## 3. Hypothesis Development

## 3.1. Technological Acquisition and External OSS Contributors

The signaling effect of technological acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences various motivations of the target’s external contributors. For example, the extrinsic motivations of external contributors to join OSS projects include learning high-quality software development practices (Ho and Rai 2017) and working with high-pro<sup>fi</sup>le developers (Bagozzi and Dholakia 2006, Mehra et al. 2011). Acquisition can signal the acquirer’s positive evaluations about the project quality and developer resources in the target <sup>fi</sup>rm and ascertain the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts of engaging in the target’s projects. Therefore, from a signaling perspective, technological acquisition can motivate external contributors to increase their development efforts in the target’s OSS projects.

Likewise, the acquisition decision can signal the acquirer’s interest in the OSS domain that the target engages in, making external contributors more con<sup>fi</sup>dent about the prospect of this domain. Based on this signaling logic, external contributors would also be motivated to work more actively on other related OSS projects in the community, even though they are not sponsored speci<sup>fi</sup>cally by the target.

In addition, since external contributors are not formally af<sup>fi</sup>liated with the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, they are less affected by various disincentives in acquisitions than internal contributors. For example, the target’s internal employees often resist the new organizational culture and have concerns about their future career in the merged entity (Bertrand 2009, Stiebale and Reize 2011, Szucs¨ 2014). These issues should be less relevant to external contributors. Although project discontinuity may be a potential concern, compared with internal contributors, external contributors have less at stake (e.g., privileges in projects and employment bene<sup>fi</sup>ts). External contributors can choose to wait (rather than immediately abandon the existing projects) to resolve the uncertainty about project discontinuity. They also have the option to switch to other related projects of the acquirer or within the OSS community to cope with project discontinuity. We thus expect that, overall, technological acquisition would motivate external contributors to be more active in OSS contribution. We hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 1. Technological acquisition makes the target’s external contributors (a) more active in contributing to the target’s sponsored OSS projects and (b) more active in contributing to other projects in the OSS community.

## 3.2. Technological Acquisition and Internal OSS Contributors

For the target’s internal contributors, whereas the acquisition signals the value of their development, the uncertainty about their future career within the merged entity is likely a more salient factor (e.g., Ernst and Vitt 2000, Bauer et al. 2016). Internal contributors of the target can be discouraged by this uncertainty in working on the target’s OSS projects (Datta 1991, Chatterjee et al. 1992). The acquirer may consolidate the target’s quality projects with its own development or discontinue un<sup>fi</sup>tted projects from the target, both of which can depreciate the role of the target’s internal contributors (Ernst and Vitt 2000) and undermine their bene<sup>fi</sup>ts (e.g., career development and associated economic returns) of continuing to work on the target’s existing projects. In practice, for example, after the IBM–Red Hat deal, Red Hat’s employees expressed serious concerns about their future careers (Vaughan-Nichols 2018).

Despite their disincentives to work on the target’s existing OSS projects, internal contributors are unlikely to lose faith in the promise of general OSS development because, from the signaling perspective, acquisition ascertains the value of the OSS domain that they devote to. The open space of OSS provides internal contributors opportunities to apply their experiences to other projects in the same OSS domain (Von Krogh 2003, Singh et al. 2011), to increase their job mobility, and to better position themselves on the external job markets (Riehle 2007, Hahn et al. 2008, Zhang et al.

2013). Therefore, while reducing contributions to the target’s existing projects, internal contributors can reallocate their efforts and work on other related OSS projects in the OSS community. We, therefore, hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 2. Technological acquisition makes the target’s internal contributors (a) less active in contributing to the target’s sponsored projects and (b) more active in contributing to other projects in the OSS community.

## 3.3. Acquirer’s OSS Experience

The acquisition research has recognized that the acquirer’s experience facilitates postacquisition resource combination (Haleblian and Finkelstein 1999). Extending this lens to the OSS context, an acquirer with OSS experience is more likely to combine the target’s OSS resources with its own. Therefore, the combination potential from an experienced acquirer increases the uncertainty about the continuity of these projects, which may be depreciated or marginalized (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999). For external contributors, the increased uncertainty about project continuity can undermine their contribution motivation. For internal contributors, they would be more concerned about their future careers (Greenwood et al. 1994) and the potential con<sup>fl</sup>icts with the acquirer’s workforce (Walter 1985). In contrast, an acquirer without OSS experience likely relies on the target’s resources to enter the OSS domain and continue the target’s existing projects. Then external contributors have less concerns about project termination, and internal contributors would also <sup>fi</sup>nd more room to continue leveraging their OSS expertise in the merged entity. In this regard, the acquirer’s OSS experience can weaken the positive impact of the acquisition on external contributors and make the negative impact of acquisition on the target’s internal contributors more salient. We hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 3. When the acquirer has OSS experience, (a) the positive impact of acquisition on external contributions to the target’s OSS projects becomes weaker and (b) the negative impact of acquisition on internal contributions to the target’s OSS projects becomes stronger, compared with the case when the acquirer has no OSS experience.

Whereas an experienced acquirer may discontinue some OSS projects of the target, it should be more capable, compared with an inexperienced acquirer, in promoting development in the OSS domain of the acquisition. Relevant experience enables the acquirer to better support the OSS community, although not necessarily through the target’s existing projects. The potential resource combination by an experienced acquirer should also allow the merged entity to vitalize the related OSS domain more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciently. Therefore, when the acquisition signals the promise of the OSS domain and the acquirer’s interest, the acquirer’s OSS experience should help further convince contributors about the prospect of the related general domain of OSS. Both external and internal contributors of the target can still stay in the related OSS domain and continue or even increase their contributions to other projects in the community. We thus hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 4. When the acquirer has OSS experience, the positive impacts of technological acquisition on both external contributors’ and internal contributors’ contributions to other OSS projects (not sponsored by the target firm) become stronger, compared with the case when the acquirer has no OSS experience.

When the target’s contributors reallocate their development efforts, they likely increase their contributions to acquirer’s projects, when the acquirer also engages in OSS development. Such reallocation bene<sup>fi</sup>ts the target’s contributors in the following ways. First, the acquirer’s projects are likely to be more sustainable than the targets’ projects, and contributors bene<sup>fi</sup>t from engaging in the projects with long-term promises. Second, the OSS experience would facilitate the acquirer to absorb OSS knowledge from the target, making it more likely for the target’s contributors to continue utilizing their relevant expertise in the acquirer’s projects. Third, the target’s contributors can learn from the acquirer’s OSS experience and build collaboration relationships with the acquirer’ other developers. The knowledge and social capital accrued from working on the acquirer’s projects can bene<sup>fi</sup>t the target’s contributors in future career development. Therefore, we hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 5. When the acquirer has OSS experience, technological acquisition encourages the target firm’s external and internal contributors to increase their contributions to the acquirer’s OSS projects.

## 3.4. Project Similarity Between the Acquirer and the Target

Project similarity re<sup>fl</sup>ects technological similarity between the acquirer and the target, which facilitates assimilating expertise from the target by the acquirer, as recognized by the literature (e.g., Ahuja and Katila 2001, Cassiman et al. 2005). When acquisition signals the value of the target’s OSS projects, project similarity helps strengthen the signal effect and con<sup>fi</sup>rm the value, because the related knowledge in the same domain should enable the acquirer to make a more rational judgment for acquisition. However, project similarity also makes it more likely for the acquirer to combine the target’s projects with its own development, because technological similarity not only lowers the barriers of combination but also facilitates the target’s contributors to join the acquirer’s projects. As a result, whereas the strengthened signaling effect of acquisition should motivate the target’s contributors to continue working in the same OSS domain, the latter is likely to respond to acquisition by reallocating thei efforts from the target’s projects to other projects, especially the acquirer’s projects. The traditional disincentives of technological similarity, namely, knowledge overlap (Cassiman et al. 2005, Chondrakis and Farchi 2014), should be less salient in the OSS context. This is because the consolidation of similar OSS projects can lead to more ef<sup>fi</sup>ciency to scale up the comple mentary contributions from external contributors, which helps address knowledge overlap. Therefore, we expect that, in the OSS context, project similarity would strengthen the signaling effect of acquisition by motivating the target’s internal and external contributors to reallocate more of their development efforts to the acquirer’s projects and other projects in the OSS community. We therefore hypothesize the following.

Hypothesis 6. When the acquirer and the target have more similar OSS projects, the technological acquisition makes the target’s external and internal contributors shif more of their development efforts from the target’s project to the acquirer’s projects.

Hypothesis 7. When the acquirer and the target have more similar OSS projects, the technological acquisition makes the target’s external and internal contributors shif more of their development efforts from the target’s projects to other OSS projects (not sponsored by the target or the acquirer).

## 4. Research Method 4.1. Data and Variables

We combine two data sources on OSS development and technological acquisitions to construct a rich panel data set. First, the data on OSS development is collected from the largest code repository worldwide, GitHub. We obtain all entities listed in GitHub under the “organizations” category and extracted all projects hosted by each organization.<sup>3</sup> Since its debut in 2008, GitHub has become a major free public repository for individuals and organizations to host their communitybased OSS projects. By 2019, the platform had attracted over 40 million developers and over 2.9 million organi zations, making it the largest code repository worldwide. GitHub data have been used in previous studies as a representative source of measures on OSS activity (e.g., Lindberg et al. 2016; Medappa and Srivastava 2019). Second, we track technological acquisition events recorded in the Crunchbase and Securities Data Com pany (SDC) Platinum databases. These databases have comprehensive coverage of acquisition information on both start-ups and established <sup>fi</sup>rms and have been used in prior studies (Block and Sandner 2009, Howell 2021).

We construct our sample of target <sup>fi</sup>rms by matching the list of organization names in GitHub with the full list of <sup>fi</sup>rm names in Crunchbase and SDC and kept those that are exactly matched.<sup>4</sup> In this procedure, we take the already standardized <sup>fi</sup>rm names in each database, remove the suf<sup>fi</sup>x, and match them across data sets using an exact matching approach. The full sample from this matching procedure comprises <sup>fi</sup>rmmonth-level panel data, including 10,397 matched <sup>fi</sup>rms and spanning from January 2010 to November 2015. We then combine the sample with the acquisition events in the SDC and Crunchbase databases to examine whether each <sup>fi</sup>rm was acquired and obtain the announced acquisition dates (Gousios 2013). Out of the 10,397 <sup>fi</sup>rms in the full sample, 1,190 <sup>fi</sup>rms were acquired during this sample period.<sup>5</sup>

We measure the contributions to a <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS projects using the number of commits to its project repositories on GitHub.<sup>6</sup> A commit is a con<sup>fi</sup>rmed change to the source code of a Git project, which usually contains chunks of code, and it has been used as the meaningful measure of contribution to open-source projects (Alali et al. 2008). We <sup>fi</sup>rst obtain the monthly number of commits to each OSS project that the <sup>fi</sup>rm sponsors and then aggregate these commits by summing them up to the <sup>fi</sup>rm level.

We use two approaches to categorize commits from internal contributors and external contributors of each organization. First, since each <sup>fi</sup>rm hosting repositories on GitHub is required to disclose the list of af<sup>fi</sup>liated members prior to a policy change in November $2 0 1 5 , ^ { 7 }$ we identify a developer as an internal member of a <sup>fi</sup>rm, according to the time stamp of when the developer appears on the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s af<sup>fi</sup>liated members list.<sup>8</sup> Second, we compare each contributor’s email address with the organization’s corporate email domain and identify the contributor as an internal member of the <sup>fi</sup>rm if the contributor’s email address contains the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s corporate email domain. Since both approaches are conservative, we regard a contributor as internal if either approach indicates that the contributor is internal, and we refer to all commits from internal contributors as internal commits. All other contributors are regarded as external contributors and their contributions as external contributions.

## 4.2. Identification Strategies

To identify the effect of technological acquisitions on OSS contributions, we employ a difference-in-differences (DID) estimation approach. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, we compare the differences in various measures of OSS contribution between the treatment and control groups, before and after the acquisition event. However, this approach encounters several identi<sup>fi</sup>cation challenges. First, the acquired targets may be inherently different from the unacquired targets, which potentially confounds the DID estimation. Second, there can be unobservable factors that in<sup>fl</sup>uence both acquisitions and OSS contributions. Lastly, OSS contributions may in<sup>fl</sup>uence the likelihood of acquisition, leading to the reverse causality concern.

We use a series of identi<sup>fi</sup>cation strategies to address the potential endogeneity issues. First, we use propensity score matching (PSM) to construct a matched sample with similar attributes on all the observable measures for the treatment and control groups (Abadie and Imbens 2006). We also use coarsened exact matching (CEM) as a robustness check for the matching method (Iacus et al. 2012). Second, in addition to using <sup>fi</sup>xed effects to control the time-invariant organizational factors, we also follow the literature (Angrist and Pischke 2008) to employ instrumental variables (IVs) in a control function approach to address the potential endogeneity of acquisition events. This addresses the potential issue of unobservable time-varying variables that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the likelihood of acquisitions and confound our results.

Third, in the robustness check section, we use the relative time model to further verify our DID model’s parallel trends assumption and examine the impact of technological acquisitions on OSS contributions over time.

For PSM, we <sup>fi</sup>rst run a <sup>fi</sup>rm-level logistic regression using all the targets’ observable features to predict the acquisition probabilities. These features include <sup>fi</sup>rmlevel attributes such as size (employee number), age, business category, and funding (Aggarwal and Hsu 2014), and measures of OSS activities, such as the number of sponsored projects, the total number of commits, the number of unique contributors, and the percentage of internal commits and contributors. Then, we match the targets in the treatment group with the targets in the control group using similar predicted probabilities (i.e., the propensities) of acquisition, using one-to-one matching without replacement. For CEM, we group acquired targets and unacquired targets into different strata based on the same set of <sup>fi</sup>rm-level features. We are essentially comparing acquired and unacquired targets assigned to the same strata with CEM where the <sup>fi</sup>rm-level features are similar.

We con<sup>fi</sup>rm that the covariates are balanced with no signi<sup>fi</sup>cant differences across the treatment and control groups in both PSM and CEM after matching. The balance check of covariates is reported in Online Appendix A. Meanwhile, we also inevitably lose some observations in matching (due to the lack of match). After matching, the PSM sample contains 662 pairs of unique acquired and unacquired targets, and the CEM sample contains 679 pairs. These approaches help address selection concerns where <sup>fi</sup>rm-level features lead to differences in the probability of being acquired. We will present the main analysis on the PSM sample and report the results estimated on the CEM sample in Online Appendix B to verify that the results are not speci<sup>fi</sup>c to the matching method selected.

In addition to matching, we use instrumental variables (IVs) with a control function approach to address the potential endogeneity concerns regarding technological acquisitions. As noted by Angrist and Pischke (2008), good instruments should satisfy the relevance and exclusion restriction conditions. In our context, an ideal IV is related to technological acquisitions but is uncorrelated with OSS contributions through channels other than acquisitions (exclusion restriction). We proceed to <sup>fi</sup>nd suitable instruments that meet these conditions, from variables that are related to the acquirers but not the targets. The two IVs are the M&A experience of potential acquirers’ board members (IV\_boardexp ) and the prior non-OSS acquisitions by potential acquirers $( I V \_ n o n O S S a c q s _ { i t } )$ . Both variables are related to the acquisition events but are unlikely to affect the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS contributions. We next discuss how these IVs are constructed and their suitability in detail.

First, prior research (e.g., Field and Mkrtchyan 2017) has suggested that board members’ prior M&A experiences improve <sup>fi</sup>rm performance in future acquisitions. Therefore, more M&A experiences of board members are likely to spur additional acquisition events. At the same time, there is no obvious reason that potential acquirers’ board-level M&A experiences are directly related to a target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS activities, and, therefore, the exclusion condition is satis<sup>fi</sup>ed. Based on this literature, we construct our <sup>fi</sup>rst IV, IV\_ boardexp<sub>it</sub>. For each target i at month t, we <sup>fi</sup>rst identify potential acquirers as <sup>fi</sup>rms that had acquisitions in the same business category as target i prior to month $t . ^ { 1 1 }$ For each potential acquirer in each month, we get the list of its board members using Crunchbase and BoardEx.<sup>12</sup> By tracing each board member’s employment history, we obtain the number of M&A events each board member has experienced previously. Then we aggregate the board member’s experience to the <sup>fi</sup>rm level for each potential acquirer. For each target i at month t, IV\_boardexp is calculated as the average board M&A experience of all its potential acquirers by month t.

The rationale for the second IV is based on the merger wave literature (Rhodes-Kropf et al. 2005, Ahern and Harford 2014), which documents clustered merger and acquisition events during certain periods in certain industries, often resulting from market <sup>fl</sup>uctuations that in<sup>fl</sup>uence the assets of acquirers or valuations of target <sup>fi</sup>rms. Therefore, if the potential acquirers have more acquisitions in the past, then it is more likely that their <sup>fi</sup>nancial performance or valuation would in<sup>fl</sup>uence their probability to initiate additional acquisitions. We only count the non-OSS acquisitions when we calculate a potential acquirer’s past acquisitions and exclude all acquisitions with OSS <sup>fi</sup>rms as targets. This further ensures that the acquirers’ previous non-OSS acquisitions are not directly correlated with target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OSS contributions. Speci<sup>fi</sup>cally, for each potential acquirer in month t, we <sup>fi</sup>rst calculate its total number of non-OSS acquisitions that occurred prior to month t. Then, for each target i at month t, the IV IV\_nonOSSacqs is calculated as the average number of non-OSS acquisitions of all its potential acquirers by month t.

## 5.1. Impact of Acquisitions on External and Internal Contributions

Table 3 reports the effects of technological acquisitions on external and internal contributions to the targets projects and to other projects in the OSS community, as speci<sup>fi</sup>ed in Equation (1). We <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the impact on the target’s external contributors. On average, technological acquisition leads to an increase of 15.1% in monthly external contributions, which corresponds to about 2.62 commits. For a target with the median number of external commits, this increase would move the target up by 3.9 percentiles in the distribution of external commits for all targets. In column (2) for Ext-to-Community, the positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of 1(PostAcquired) suggests that acquisition also leads to an increase in monthly commits made by the target’s external contributors to other projects in the OSS community, by 31.6% on average (about 1.55 commits). In column (3) for Int-to-Target, the negative and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef<sup>fi</sup>cient of 1(PostAcquired) suggests that technological acquisition leads to a decrease in internal contributions to the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s OSS projects, by about 28.4% on average, which amounts to about 5.11 commits in monthly internal contributions. In contrast, in column (4) for Int-to-Community, the positive and signi<sup>fi</sup>cant coef-<sup>fi</sup>cient of 1(PostAcquired) suggests that, after acquisitions, internal contributors of the targets increase their monthly contributions to other OSS projects by 26.3% (which amounts to 0.55 commits). Therefore, part (b) of Hypothesis 2 is also supported. The estimates fo other control variables are generally in line with expectations. For example, targets with more recent projects $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ with smaller values of ProjectAge), with more contributors, or sponsoring more OSS projects generally receive more external and internal contributions.

## 6. Robustness and Additional Analyses

In this section, we present various robustness checks to verify our main <sup>fi</sup>ndings and additional analyses to generate further insights. We <sup>fi</sup>rst use a relative time model to check the parallel trends assumption in the DID estimation. We then enrich our <sup>fi</sup>ndings by examining the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s overall contributions and the source of changes. Lastly, we examine several alternative model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations, including the DID estimations on the full sample (without matching) and on the CEM sample, and the sensitivity of the matching process. Overall, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are robust throughout all these robustness checks.

## 6.2. Overall Contribution and Source of Changes

We conduct additional analyses to further examine the overall effect of the acquisition on the contribution to the whole open-source community, including the target <sup>fi</sup>rms. We use the total number of commits from the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s internal and external contributors to any OSS project (including the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s projects, the acquirer’s projects, and other projects in the OSS community) as the dependent variables and employ the same estimation framework in Equation (1). The results are reported in Table D2 in Online Appendix D. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that acquisition leads to a signi<sup>fi</sup>- cant increase in the total number of commits from the target’s external contributors and a modest increase from the internal contributors. These <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that, after the acquisition, internal contributors are more likely to shift efforts from the target to the acquirer and the OSS community while maintaining a consistent level of overall contributions. In contrast, the external contributors tend to increase their overall contributions.

We also conduct several additional analyses to obtain further insights about the source of changes in internal and external contributions. We <sup>fi</sup>rst examine the extent to which the observed impact of the acquisition is driven by a reduction in the number of unique contributors. We use the number of unique contributors (both internal and external contributors) as alternative dependent variables to replicate our main analyses. The results are reported in Table D3 in Online Appendix D. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that acquisitions lead to a significant decrease in the number of unique internal contributors and a modest increase in the number of unique external contributors. Another possible source for the changes is the commits per contributor. We examine this impact using the per-contributor commits, de<sup>fi</sup>ned as the total internal and external commits divided by the corresponding number of unique internal contributors and external contributors, respectively, as the dependent variables. Results are reported in columns (3) and (4) of Table D3. We observe that the impact of acquisitions on the average commits per contributor is insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant for both the internal and external contributors. Overall, these <sup>fi</sup>ndings suggest that the reduced number of internal contributors is a key driving force of technological acquisitions’ negative effect on internal OSS contributions.<sup>18</sup>

6.3. Alternative Samples and Sensitivity Analyses We further verify the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>nding through a series of additional analyses using alternative estimation framework and samples, including a full-sample analysis and a CEM sample analysis. The purpose is to verify that the estimated impacts of acquisitions on OSS contributions are robust to the matching methods employed. The results reported in Online Appendix B con<sup>fi</sup>rm that our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are consistent across these alternative model speci<sup>fi</sup>cations and samples.

To further alleviate the concern about selection bias (i.e., that better quality <sup>fi</sup>rms are more likely to get acquired), we conduct an additional analysis, where we use a subset of the control group that was acquired between the end of our sample period and December 2020, which consists of 1,301 unique <sup>fi</sup>rms. These subset controls are arguably more similar to the treatment group, since they were also acquired eventually, just not in the sample period. Employing a similar “lookahead” propensity score matching approach as in Bapna et al. (2018), we match the <sup>fi</sup>rms in the treatment group with this eventually acquired control group and repeat the analysis on this new matched sample. The estimation results are presented in Table D5 in Online Appendix D. We observe that the estimations on this new matched sample are all in the same direction as the results in Table 3 in the paper. This indicates that selection bias relating to the potential differences in quality for the acquired and unacquired <sup>fi</sup>rms is not a major concern in our main results, since they have been quite successfully accounted for through matching and the external instrumental variables already.

The matching methods we use are based on the assumption that observed characteristics determine the treatment (i.e., whether a target is acquired). To check the sensitivity of our results to the impact of unobservable variables on the likelihood of acquisition, we further conduct a sensitivity analysis to calculate the Rosenbaum bounds (Rosenbaum 2002, Mithas and Krishnan 2009, Oestreicher-Singer and Zalmanson 2013). This analysis evaluates how strong an unobservable factor needs to impact the odds ratio of treatment to actually alter the estimated results after matching. We <sup>fi</sup>nd that, across the different estimations, our results remain very consistent, allowing for unobservable variables to impact the odds ratio for a target to be acquired, up to a range of 70%–290%. For example, the effect of technological acquisition on the contributions to other projects (log(Int-to-Community)) only becomes insigni<sup>fi</sup>cant (p-value lower than 10%) when unobservable variables change the odds of being acquired by more than 290%. Hence, hidden bias from unobserved variables is unlikely to overturn the robustness of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings.

## 7. Discussion and Conclusions 7.1. Summary of Findings and Theoretical Contributions

This study examines how technological acquisition impacts the contributions of the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s internal and external OSS contributors. The unique OSS context allows us to develop novel insights that are distinct from those in the extant literature of acquisition, since OSS contributors are less bounded by traditional organizational boundaries. We synergize two relevant theoretical lenses in the literature. First, we adopt a signaling perspective that views technological acquisition as a signaling mechanism to inform OSS contributors about the value of the target’s projects and the related OSS domain. This signaling perspective helps explain how acquisition may positively in<sup>fl</sup>uence the motivations of the target’s OSS contributors. Second, we adopt a perspective of combination potential that recognizes the possible resource combination by the acquirer through acquisition. We theorize how the motivating effect of acquisition (from the signaling perspective) can be moderated by the consideration of combination potential. This uni<sup>fi</sup>ed view helps explain how acquisition may drive contributors to reallocate their efforts (e.g., from the target’s projects to the acquirer’s projects and other projects in the OSS community) as a response to acquisition. The theoretical contributions of our <sup>fi</sup>ndings are fourfold, as we summarize next.

First, our study reveals a distinction between the target’s external contributors and internal contributors in responding to technological acquisition. Such <sup>fi</sup>ndings generate a key contribution to the innovation literature, because the extant literature has remained silent about how acquisition in<sup>fl</sup>uences external innovation contributors. Whereas the <sup>fi</sup>nding of decreased internal contributions to the target is consistent with the existing understanding of innovation disincentives caused by acquisition (e.g., Hitt et al. 1990, Ernst and Vitt 2000), we contribute by illustrating that external contributions to the target increase after acquisition, which has not been explicated in the existing theories. The important theoretical implication is that, in the OSS context, the in<sup>fl</sup>uences of traditional disincentives caused by acquisition (e.g., career uncertainty within the <sup>fi</sup>rm) would be less salient for external contributors. Therefore, the positive effects of technological acquisition (e.g., the signaling effect to reduce value uncertainty) would unfold more signi<sup>fi</sup>- cantly to motivate external contributors. In this regard, the role of the open nature of OSS is nontrivial, and incorporating such consideration contributes to the existing theories and advances our understanding of technological acquisition in the new era of open innovation.

Second, we <sup>fi</sup>nd that, in responding to acquisitions, OSS contributors shift their efforts from the target’s projects to other projects in the OSS community, which vitalize the whole OSS community. This <sup>fi</sup>nding contributes theoretically to both the research on technological acquisition and that on OSS. For technological acquisition research, our insight reveals how acquisition can impact the whole OSS community, rather than just target <sup>fi</sup>rms. Our study also contributes by advancing the understanding of the impacts of acquisition from the perspective of individual OSS contributors, rather than from the traditional perspective of <sup>fi</sup>rm. For OSS research, we theoretically extend the understanding of organizational engagement in OSS (e.g., Feller et al. 2008, Germonprez et al. 2017, Nagle 2018). Our key contribution is to illustrate that the strategic moves of <sup>fi</sup>rm sponsors (e.g., acquisition) can motivate individual OSS contributors and vitalize the whole OSS community. The related <sup>fi</sup>ndings also contribute to the stream of OSS research that develops the understanding of what in<sup>fl</sup>uences the motivations of OSS contribution (e.g., Lerner and Tirole 2002, Roberts et al. 2006, Fang and Neufeld 2009) from the <sup>fi</sup>rm’s perspective.

Third, our study offers a unique view on how acquirers bene<sup>fi</sup>t from technological acquisition in the OSS context. Our key insight is that the open nature of OSS allows the target <sup>fi</sup>rm’s external and internal contributors to shift their efforts to the acquirer’s projects. As a result, the voluntary effort shifting of individual contributors facilitates resource combination by the acquirer. The key theoretical contribution of this insight is to reveal that the acquirer’s absorptive capacity is in<sup>fl</sup>uenced not only by its own organizational attributions but also by the dynamic choices of individual OSS contributors, which extends the understanding of absorptive capacity into the open innovation context (e.g., Cohen and Levinthal 1990). Moreover, our <sup>fi</sup>ndings illustrate that the acquirer’s OSS experience motivates the target’s OSS contributors to reallocate more of their efforts to the acquirer’s projects. The implication is that the acquirer’s experience not only facilitates the acquirer’s own postacquisition integration process (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999) but also helps retain the development talents for the merged entity and attract external contributions from the general OSS community. This insight extends the existing theoretical understanding of postacquisition integration management for the acquirer (Larsson and Finkelstein 1999).

Fourth, our study illustrates that project similarity between the acquirer and the target motivates the target’s OSS contributors to shift more of their efforts to the acquirer’s projects. This <sup>fi</sup>nding contributes to the stream of research on the impact of technological overlap in acquisitions (Ahuja and Katila 2001, Cassiman et al. 2005). The extant research has primarily identi<sup>fi</sup>ed the bene<sup>fi</sup>ts and costs of technological overlap from the perspective of either the acquirer or the merged entity. Our study provides a different and complementary perspective by considering how project similarity in<sup>fl</sup>uences individual contributors from the target to reallocate their development efforts to the acquirer’s projects. As we <sup>fi</sup>nd that individual contributors tend to shift more of their efforts to the acquirer’s projects when the acquirer and the target have more similar projects, the implication is that technological overlap in this context aids the acquirer to absorb technological capabilities from the target.

## 7.2. Limitations and Future Directions

Some limitations of this study generate directions for future research. First, our consideration of OSS contributions mainly focuses on the contribution volume measured by the number of commits. Whereas this is a reasonable and well-adopted measure to capture overall innovation activities, it may not be <sup>fi</sup>negrained enough to re<sup>fl</sup>ect the nature of the contribution. Future research can consider enriching the measures on OSS contribution, using more cutting-edge techniques (e.g., content analysis) to assess the nature of commits and related innovation activities. Second, although we focus on the dynamics of OSS contributions after acquisitions, we do not directly observe how acquirers convert certain OSS projects to proprietary ones and how target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ OSS contributors shift their efforts to proprietary development. This issue does not affect our main insights in this study. Our empirical measures capture changes in contributions to OSS projects. Converting OSS projects to proprietary development would lead to reduced contributions to OSS projects, and these changes would still be captured in our measures. However, future research with alternative data sources may develop more in-depth insights about OSS projects being converted to proprietary innovation in technological acquisitions.

Third, although we consider technological acquisitions from a general perspective, different types of acquisitions (e.g., domestic vs. international, withinindustry vs. cross-industry, etc.) probably in<sup>fl</sup>uence OSS activities differently. Our study takes the <sup>fi</sup>rst step to show that technological acquisitions can generally bene<sup>fi</sup>t the OSS community by motivating the reallocation effort of the target <sup>fi</sup>rms’ contributors. Fourth, although our study has considered the resource combination perspective, our observation is not detailed enough to reveal exactly how OSS resources and expertise are consolidated in the postacquisition stages.

Future research with available data can further explore different combination approaches and examine how the merged entities can better support various OSS contributors and reap bene<sup>fi</sup>ts from technological acquisitions. Future studies with more complete data on acquisition announcement and completion dates could also explore how the impact of technological acquisitions on OSS contributions change, from the announcement to the execution.

## 7.3. Managerial Implications

This study generates important implications for practice. Our <sup>fi</sup>ndings yield insights about how various stakeholders can bene<sup>fi</sup>t more from technological acquisitions in the OSS context. For both external and internal OSS contributors of the target, our results suggest that technological acquisitions can allow them to better reallocate their development resources and efforts. OSS contributors can use acquisition as an information source to evaluate OSS projects and increase their involvement in more promising projects. For internal contributors, even though certain adverse effects of acquisition could unfold in their career paths within the target <sup>fi</sup>rm, they can continue acquiring related technical expertise by engaging in other projects in the OSS community. For acquirers and target <sup>fi</sup>rms, the OSS community provides them an effective approach to engage individual innovators through acquisitions. Targets can take acquisitions as an opportunity to convince external contributors about the value of their projects and openly advocate their innovation agendas in the postacquisition stage. Acquirers can also attract interested contributors, both from target <sup>fi</sup>rms and from the general OSS community, to join their related development activities.

From the perspective of resource combination, our study suggests that acquirers and targets need to devise communication and coordination strategies to fully bene<sup>fi</sup>t from technological acquisitions. Through the lens of signaling, clarifying how resources and expertise would be combined for future OSS development would ease internal contributors’ career concerns and also motivate more active engagement from external contributors. Acquirers should also signal to the OSS community effectively if they intend to maintain targets’ OSS projects. They can also provide more explicit incentives to engage contributors so as to better absorb OSS expertise from both targets and the outside OSS community.
