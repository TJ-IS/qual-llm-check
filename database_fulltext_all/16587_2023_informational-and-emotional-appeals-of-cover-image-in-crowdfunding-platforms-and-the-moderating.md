# Informational and Emotional Appeals of Cover Image in Crowdfunding Platforms and the Moderating Role of Campaign Outputs

## Abstract:

The first-stage decision is critical in the decision process in crowdfunding, where the cover image plays an important role in forming first impressions and persuading click funders. This study explores how emotional visual cues (human face) and informational visual cues (visual text) in the cover image affect funders’ decisions and how the effects differ across campaigns. The results demonstrate the great persuasive power of human faces and visual text. We further illustrate the effects of joyful expressions and image-text congruency as well as the nonsignificant influence of the length and polarity of visual text. Moreover, emotional visual cues are more effective in intangible product campaigns, while informational visual cues are more effective in tangible product campaigns, and both are important in social service campaigns. Valuable insight is provided to creators about the design of the cover image.

Keywords: in, cover image, persuasive appeal, first impression, campaign output

## 1 Introduction

Crowdfunding helps entrepreneurs to collect funds from a large group of people all over the world through online platforms [42]. Funder's decision-making process on platforms generally includes two stages, as shown in Figure 1. In the first stage, the summary information of a campaign is presented on the landing page or the search page on crowdfunding platforms or shared by creators on social media. It is used to persuade potential funders to click the link, and a first impression is formed in the meantime [30]. In the second stage, potential funders read the detailed information on the campaign page, and the creator needs to persuade them to support the campaign. First-stage persuasion plays a vital role in the decision process because first impressions not only affect click decisions but also have a great influence on investment decisions [51]. Hence, it’s critical for creators to design proper summary information to attract potential funders during a brief moment.

Among the summary information, the cover image is the most eye-catching element that occupies more than half of the space [20,24]. Images are processed more quickly and automatically in humans' brains than texts [56], thereby more likely to attract viewers' attention and form first impressions during a brief moment. As a result, a well-designed cover image can persuade funders’ decisions [35]. However, the first-stage decision process has not yet received enough academic attention, especially about how the cover image affects funders’ decisions. Besides, a first impression exerts a greater effect when it matches the expectation of funders, which is related to the campaign type [20]. Prior studies consistently suggested that different campaign categories should various kinds of information to persuade funders [15]. Therefore, this research aims to explore how cover images persuade funders and how the effect differs across campaign categories.

To explore the persuasive power of the cover image, we leverage two basic persuasion strategies, i.e., informational and emotional appeals, defined as persuasive intentions which appeal to receivers’ cognitions or emotions [54,67]. The two appeals could be manifested as visual cues in images [62]. We define the visual cues that appeal to cognitions (emotions) as information (emotional) visual cues. Moreover, the superiority of the two appeals usually depends highly on the context, such as product category [54]. Different appeals in the cover image could form different first impressions, thereby bringing different persuasive effects [30].

Another research gap is that few studies have reasonably categorized campaigns yet. Although Tafesse [53] classified campaigns according to the product type, it seems not enough because products are not the mandatory output in crowdfunding, unlike the marketing context. Indeed, crowdfunding has become a particularly important and even the only available channel for social entrepreneurs, but non-profit projects face great difficulties in attracting funding [38,48]. Therefore, in light of the forms of the final output, we suggest three categories. (1) Tangible products: the output is tangible which can be seen and touched, aiming at funders’ individual benefits (phones, clothes, electrical appliances, etc.); (2) intangible products: the output is intangible which cannot be seen or touched (i.e., not made out of material objects), aiming at funders’ individual benefits as well (film, show, exhibition, etc.); (3) social services: the output brings social benefits or impacts to the society rather than specific individuals, no matter it is tangible or intangible (environment protection, human rights, etc.). The forms of campaign outputs are directly related to the forms of rewards, attracting different types of funders. Figure 2 shows examples of summary information for each category.

For our goal to guide the design of summary information, this study proposed the following research questions: (1) How do informational and emotional appeals in the cover image affect funders’ decisions? (2) How do crowdfunding campaign outputs moderate the influence of informational and emotional appeals in the cover image on funders’ decisions?

To investigate informational and emotional appeals in images, we focus on the two most salient visual cues proposed in Cerf et al. [9]’s experiments, i.e., human face and visual text. The use of human faces makes the image more subjective and emotional, while visual text (i.e., text presented within an image) makes the image more objective and informational, resulting in different persuasion effects and first impressions. Using a large dataset with all sub-categories from Indiegogo.com, we first adopt the NLP method to categorize campaigns into the three forms of outputs, then use deep learning models to automatically obtain visual cues from images. Our main findings are as follows: (1) For emotional appeal, the presence of human faces, the number of faces, and joyful expressions in the cover image facilitate funders’ decisions. (2) For informational appeal, the presence of visual text and the image-text congruency (i.e., congruency between visual text and other text in summary information) in the cover image also promote funders’ decisions. (3) The length and sentiment polarity of visual text do not matter, indicating the visual text may be processed in a holistic and fast style and not comprehensively interpreted in the first-stage decision. (4) The emotional visual cues have a larger positive impact in intangible product campaigns, while the informational visual cues have a larger positive impact in tangible product campaigns, and both of them are important in social service campaigns.

The main contribution of this study is that we extend the literature on decision-making in crowdfunding by targeting the first-stage decision-making process. To this end, we investigate how cover images persuade funders’ decisions by leveraging persuasive appeals and first impressions. We also enrich the research of visual information and moderating effects in crowdfunding. Besides, we contribute to persuasion theory by demonstrating that informational and emotional persuasive appeals can be manifested as visual cues in images.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the literature review of the study. Section 3 presents the hypotheses development. Section 4 describes the dataset, the operations of variables, and the regression results. The remaining sections of the paper discuss the findings, followed by contributions and limitations.

## 2 Literature

## 2.1 Visual information in crowdfunding

The literature about the effects of visual information in crowdfunding is summarized in Table 1. Prior studies mainly focused on the visual cues that appeal to funders’ emotions, which is hard to be achieved by texts, while the informational visual cues that appeal to funders' cognitions are scarcely documented in the literature. Several visual attributes have been shown to induce viewers’ emotions and significantly influence investment decision-making, such as colors and the complexity of visual cues [40,68].

Regarding the content of visual information, human in images was the most concerned and was analyzed from various aspects. Human in images or videos commonly arouses the interest of viewers [15,29], and the appearance of the person (race, age, gender, attractiveness, weight, etc.) has an impact on investment intentions [17,27]. The one who appears more trustworthy and passionate is more likely to have fundraising [14,17]. Korzynski et al. [32] analyzed videos and found that creators who make an impression of self-presentation or exemplification are more likely to succeed. Moreover, different facial expressions displayed in images might indicate certain signals, produce emotional contagion, and further impact funding performance [28,46,64].

The persuasion effect of images is also confirmed by studies. Dey et al. [15] divided persuasion factors in videos into product-related factors and video related factors, revealing they were predictive of the crowdfunding outcome of campaigns and moderated by campaign category. Wang et al. [62] categorized photos into three persuasive appeals: pathos (emotional-related), ethos (credible-related), and logos (rational-related), showing both pathos and ethos photos have positive persuasion effects while logos photos are negative in medical crowdfunding.

## 2.2 Crowdfunding campaign categories

The types of rewards in reward-based crowdfunding are not regulated, ranging from preordering of products, a service to even a thank letter [42]. Business campaigns offer products or services as individual incentives to extrinsically motivate funders, whereas social campaigns offer rewards mostly in the form of thanks such that the motivations are mainly altruism [7,52]. Annaleena Parhankangas [1] pointed out that the expectations of social campaigns are illinformed and difficult to anticipate, thus relying more heavily on linguistic style to attract funding, while business campaigns could convey various product, entrepreneur, and firm related signals. Different from private goods, the benefits of public goods are available to those who did not contribute to the campaign [23]. Another issue is legitimacy, such as an endorsement from a third party, which helps organizations to be perceived as predictable and trustworthy [48]. Robiady et al. [49] emphasized trust building is particularly important for social campaigns.

Within business campaigns, the fundamental difference comes from the types of products. Tangible products are physical objects, while intangible services cannot be seen, felt, tasted, or touched. Indeed, investment behavior in tangible product campaigns is similar to online shopping without guaranteed delivery [22]. Products in crowdfunding campaigns have never been published before, thus, they need to form a mental representation of the product to make funding decisions [50], which can be addressed by both concrete pictures and textual descriptions [58]. However, intangible products are not easy to be directly described by texts. Tafesse [53] showed that both objective information and positive emotion have greater influence in intangible campaigns, the former of which provides tangible details for funders to evaluate the features and benefits, while the latter might be viewed as an integral part of intangible service and experience.

Studies also demonstrated that funders pay more attention to humans in some specific intangible sub-categories. Gafni et al. [19] found that art related campaigns are more inclined to display entrepreneurs’ information which brings greater benefits. Likewise, Dey et al. [15] revealed that the utility and relevance of the product in technology campaigns attract more attention, while the presenter in the video and the video quality in design and fashion campaigns is the main focus.

In conclusion, the literature has long suggested that different categories of crowdfunding campaigns should adopt different persuasion strategies. Such differences stem from the form of the final output, that is, whether the output is for-profit and whether it is intangible. However, it’s not clear how the persuasive effects of the cover image on funders’ decisions vary across campaign outputs.

## 3 Hypothesis development

## 3.1 First-stage persuasion process

Different from prior research, this study focuses on the first-stage persuasion process. This stage has three characteristics: (1) Summary information is highly structured and limited, only containing a title, a tagline, and a cover image. (2) In the first stage, a campaign link is usually embedded in a crowded online environment, surrounded by thousands of other campaigns, where it is hard to be the main focus; while in the second stage, the campaign content is the only focal stimuli. (3) Prior study suggests consumers are generally less motivated to process ads when they are freely browsing through multiple ads, and more motivated when they intend to scrutinize them or are interested in them [18]. Similarly, potential funders may be less motivated to process the summary information of each campaign when they freely browse in a crowded environment [13]. Their average attention is usually very brief and the click decision may be made in a moment [13].

The first impression formed in this stage has a lasting effect throughout the whole decision process [30]. Such long-term effect of first impressions is called the "Halo effect" in the marketing literature, which carries over that first impression to the evaluation of other attributes of the product [39]. The phenomenon could be explained by a cognitive ‘confirmation bias’, which occurs when viewers only focus on confirmatory evidence supporting their first impressions while ignoring disconfirmatory evidence [39]. In the context of crowdfunding, first impressions formed in the first stage remain in the funders' mind even as he moves to the secondstage decision. For instance, when they feel attracted or perceive empathy by the cover image, they may downplay possible negative issues on the campaign page, like bland text or spelling errors. As a result, good first impressions could not only persuade click decisions but also promote investment decisions.

We explain how cover images may influence funders' decisions two ways. First, according to dual-system theory, human information processing mechanisms include cognitive and emotional systems, which can be activated by informational and emotional appeals in ads respectively [55]. When visual cues in cover images trigger emotional processing or cognitive processing or both together, funders are more likely to have interest and click the campaign link [55]. The informational appeal usually deploys clear and concrete terms to convey objective information about products or campaigns, decreasing the uncertainty of funders. In contrast, the emotional appeal largely contains joy, love, humor, and pride, presenting affective and hedonic information to inspire and motivate funders [54,67]. Following prior literature, text is more likely to convey informational messages, whereas human faces are easier to arouse affective response [6]. Therefore, we choose the visual text and human face as the manifestation of two appeals in the cover image respectively.

Second, due to the unique characteristics of first-stage persuasion, we argue that attractiveness is the most important and effective first impression, although other impressions may also have positive impacts. Attractiveness refers to how viewers perceive the visual appeal that arouses interest [20]. Tian et al. [55] have indicated that the click decision is decided by consumers' attention when they receive plenty of information on the website. Thus, creators should improve the salience of visual objects. An attractive cover image helps creators compete for funders' attention with other campaigns in a brief moment [24]. In this regard, experiments from Cerf et al. [9] showed that because of similar mechanisms for attentional deployment, text and faces are most effective in attracting the gaze among high-level image features, and it is difficult for viewers to avoid looking at them. In other words, when funders are attracted by human faces and visual text, they are more likely to click the campaign link for further exploration.

In summary, the persuasion process is as follows. First, human faces and visual text attract funders' attention and form several kinds of first impressions. Second, proper first impressions and the activated emotional or cognitive processing system stimulate their click decisions. The properness of impressions and persuasive appeals depend on the campaign outputs. Third, first impressions remain in their mind and influence their second-stage decisions. Next, we describe how emotional or informational visual cues form first impressions and persuade funders decisions in detail, and how campaign outputs moderate these effects.

## 3.1.1 Emotional visual cues

In prior research, advertisements coded as “emotional” typically involve humans in pictures [10]. Given that physical contact is absent online, the human face enables direct contact between the creator and the viewer, making the images more attractive and subjective than images without humans [33]. Human image produces higher levels of image appeal and perceived social presence, thus stimulating higher levels of enjoyment and subsequent positive attitudes [59]. By inducing the emotions of viewers, human image greatly influences funders' decision-making in crowdfunding [14].

Figures 2(b) and 2(c) are examples of the cover image with human faces. Human faces in cover images evoke first impressions, including empathy, attractiveness, perceived passion, perceived trust, etc. Specifically, the creator's image reflects his unobservable characteristics, some of which might affectively influence funders, such as trustworthy and passionate [14,17]. Attractive models who are displaying products enhance the attractiveness of the images and promote the generation of viewers' mental imagery in the absence of physical contact with products [2]. The image of people in need can inspire funders’ intrinsic motivation, such as altruism and empathy, which means that funders may feel compassionate about him [20].

These impressions have been shown to enhance funders' attitudes and facilitate funders' decisions in prior literature [14]. In the first stage, these positive impressions and the activation of the emotional processing system induced by human faces could stimulate funders' click decisions. The emotions won’t disappear immediately and continue to affect funders as they browse the campaign page, even when they try to think rationally [46]. Therefore, emotional visual cues could further influence investment decisions.

H1. Emotional visual cues (faces) have a positive impact on funders’ decisions.

Facial expression is also an essential emotional visual cue in the cover image, according to emotional contagion theory [46]. We focus on positive expression because it’s more prevalent and has various benefits in crowdfunding, where people need to build positive relationships with funders [28]. Specifically, we explore the effect of joyful expression, which is the most fundamental form of positive emotions. During a brief moment of decision-making, smiles are able to catch viewers' eyes and leave a better impression because smiles are powerful social forces that positively influence interpersonal judgments, making people perceived to be kinder, more sociable, and more honest [63]. Research has shown that displaying joy on campaign pages helps creators persuade funders to evaluate them more favorably and increase funding performance [8,28]. Passionate creators improve viewers’ attitudes toward the project, while creators who don’t smile lower the satisfaction of viewers [11,15]. Hence, we argue that joyful expressions in the cover image are able to induce a stronger first impression and emotions. The stronger impressions may last well through the whole decision process, impacting click decisions and investment decisions.

H2. Emotional visual cues (joys) have a positive impact on funders’ decisions.

## 3.1.2 Informational visual cues

Although text might also involve emotional appeals, such as the use of sentiment words, researchers have demonstrated that compared to the visual modality, textual modality is more likely to convey objective and concrete messages, resulting in informational appeal [5,6]. Besides, the text in the cover image is generally several words or a short sentence which is hard to achieve emotional appeal. The marketing literature discussed the effects of text within images and proposed some benefits of texts combined with images. Text provides persuasive verbal communication, and text in a known language has a larger positive power [37], further revealing the cognitive process of viewers. Text can directly convey information about the project or the product, helping viewers remember more information when watching an image [4,58]. Consequently, the visual text could evoke perceived informativeness as first impressions and persuade funders cognitively.

The text element is long believed to be key in capturing consumers’ attention in ad design [44]. The visual text could attract viewers' eyes during a brief moment, and well-designed visual text could attract more attention. On the one hand, Cui and Jiang [13] suggested that text within images could be processed much faster than text separate from images, thus being suitable for our first-stage persuasion. On the other hand, prior research has shown that text in a larger size could attract more attention [44]. Among summary information, compared with other pure texts like titles, the cover image provides flexibility in designing visual text, such that funders could attract potential funders by using visual text.

Figure 2(a) is an example of the cover image with visual text. In a large size and beautiful format, the visual text highlights the selling points of a product, including "order free", "selfcleaning", and "sealing". During a brief moment, funders could note the text, and they will feel more information is acquired compared with other campaigns that have not done that. Since providing information is an essential goal for ads [54], especially in the first stage when funders know nothing about the campaign, the perceived informativeness could persuade their click decisions cognitively. In the second stage, the first impressions of informativeness and attractiveness could remain in their mind. Even after a long time, they may still remember these unique selling points, which could affect their investment decisions.

H3. Informational visual cues (texts) have a positive impact on funders’ decisions.

Given that the first-stage decision is a fast process and funders' motivation is low, funders are likely to expect the information processing to be easy. According to processing fluency theory, stimuli that can be easily processed generally induce positive evaluations and favorable attitudes [47]. Levels of processing fluency have a subsequent impact on funders’ preference for a campaign and further impact their investment decisions [61]. When viewing summary information, funders integrate the cover image, the title, and the tagline for information processing and decide whether to click it or not. Prior research has reported that congruent information from visual format and textual format is more easily processed, thereby enhancing the formation of positive attitudes [57]. Associated semantics between texts and images facilitate cognitive processing and fluency, promoting funders’ decisions [26].

For our goal to explore the effect of visual text, we manifest the image-text congruency by the degree of semantic match between visual text and the adjacent text. For example, Figure 2(a) repeats the brand name, the product name, and its features in both title and visual text forms; while the title and visual text in Figure 2(b) are not similar. When two modalities convey similar content, viewers can remember more information [36]. Hence, in addition to enhancing processing fluency, congruency between visual text and other text also brings another benefit: a stronger first impression, which lasts during the whole decision-making process.

H4. Congruency between visual text and other text in summary information has a positive impact on funders’ decisions.

3.2 Interactions of campaign outputs and persuasive appeals

Since the superiority of informational and emotional appeals depends highly on the context

[54], we argue that campaign outputs can moderate the effects of two persuasive appeals, and

different first impressions are suitable for different campaign types. Specifically, emotional visual

cues are anticipated to be more effective in intangible and social campaigns, while informational

visual cues are anticipated to be more effective in tangible campaigns.

Tangible products provide functional, instrumental, and practical benefits; while intangible

products provide aesthetic, experiential, and enjoyment benefits [12]. Due to intangibility, it’s

difficult to rationally evaluate the products such that emotions can be seen as a part of intangible

products, playing an important role in funders’ decision-making. On the contrary, funders seek for

objective and concrete information to evaluate tangible products, while strong emotional

sentiment could be perceived as a distraction [53]. Besides, the quality of the intangible product

mostly depends on the one who offered the product, including reliability, responsiveness,

assurance, and empathy [45]. In contrast, the quality of tangible products is more relevant with

their materials, techniques, and so on. Human images in intangible product campaigns could

attract more attention than tangible product campaigns, such as directors and actors of the film,

performers of the show, and artists of the exhibition [15,19]. Because the quality of such projects

mainly depends on the creator’s characteristics or whatever key people are involved in the

campaign [21].

Compared with the other two categories, social campaigns are non-profit oriented, with

rewards mainly in the form of thanks. Driven by intrinsic motivation, the emotions such as

compassion greatly affect investment decision-making [25]. Relational and emotional bonds

between engaged individuals are important in social campaigns [49]. Emotional strategy such as

storytelling induces high positive emotions and further promote altruism during customer

engagement [41,43]. In medical crowdfunding, which is also non-profit, Wang et al. [62] showed

that emotional appeal is more effective in persuading donation behaviors than rational appeal. As

for visual cues, the appearance of creators has a great influence on charitable giving, which

funders can use to evaluate if the creator is reliable and able to fulfill his campaign promise [27].

Wang et al. [60] suggested the effects of emphasizing creators are more pronounced in socialoriented projects. Hence, it’s beneficial to display the creators to build trust or people in need to inspire compassion in social campaigns [38]. We propose the following hypothesis.

H5. Compared with tangible campaigns, emotional visual cues have a larger positive impact on funders’ decisions in intangible and social campaigns.

H6. Compared with intangible and social campaigns, informational visual cues have a larger positive impact on funders’ decisions in tangible campaigns.

## 4 Empirical study

## 4.1 Data collection

This study used a dataset collected from Indiegogo.com, which is one of the largest rewardbased crowdfunding platforms worldwide. The whole dataset includes 170,394 crowdfunding campaigns, with the observation window from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2017. We eliminated extreme values of fundraising goals and dropped the projects not written in English. To avoid the influence of data imbalance, we kept all the successful campaigns and randomly selected the same number of failed campaigns to form a new sample (n=21,974). We further removed some projects in the section 4.2.2 and 4.2.3, and finally, 14,611 projects remained.

## 4.2 Operations of variables

Table 3 presents an overview of the variables and their descriptive statistics.

## 4.2.1 Dependent variables

Since the funders’ click decisions and the first impressions have a strong relationship with the fundraising performance, we use fundraising amount and the number of backers as proxies of the outcomes of funders' decisions. Fundraising amount is closely related to the basic logic of investment intentions of potential funders, thus revealing how good the first impression is. Still, it may be affected by funding goals or product prices on the campaign page [3]. The number of backers can reflect the number of clicks in the first stage, thus revealing the persuasive effect. Besides, because the funding type is “all and more” in this dataset, success is not a suitable measure. Considering the skewed distribution and the cases of zero, we do log transformation

after adding one to both two variables.

## 4.2.2 Independent Variables

In order to use a large-scale dataset from crowdfunding platforms for empirical study, we employed deep learning models to obtain the measures of independent variables. Given that the tasks of our research are very common in computer science, we used Baidu API to avoid repeated labeling work and waste of computing resources. Baidu API supports high concurrency with millisecond level response time, and their algorithms are ranked at the forefront in worldwide AI competitions. Specifically, OCR (Optical Character Recognition) is employed to obtain the text within images, and face recognition is employed to recognize human faces in images. To improve the accuracy of OCR, some cases are considered as non-detected: (1) only a single letter, (2) spelling error, (3) and low confidence of recognition (below 0.8). We removed 47 outliers with more than 100 visual words and did a log transformation since it’s a right-skewed distribution.

We use two dummy variables, Face and Text, to measure if any human face or visual text can be detected in the image. For a reduced dataset whose Face equals 1 (n=6563), we include No. of faces to further explore the intensity of emotional visual cues and a dummy variable Joy to measure if at least half of human faces have a joyful expression, which is also detected from face recognition. For a reduced dataset whose Text equals 1 (n=7468), we include No. of words to explore the intensity of informational visual cues after removing the stop words. Moreover, the Congruency between visual text and other text (i.e., the title and the tagline) is calculated as the number of duplicated words between them divided by the number of visual words, ranging from 0 to 1. We control for the text length when examining the effects of congruency.

The steps are as follows: (1) Randomly select 500 images from the dataset collected from Indiegogo. (2) Three labelers classified the sample respectively for if images have text, human face, or joyful expression. (3) The final labels are determined by the voting method. ## 4.2.3 Moderator

In order to explore the moderating role of crowdfunding campaign categories, we take the forms of final outputs into account, i.e., tangible products, intangible products, and social service, encoding them into three dummy variables Tangible, Intangible, and Social.

To make the categorization, we first differentiate the business and non-profit projects, where the former is similar to the pre-ordering of products online [22]. Second, the outputs of business projects could be further classified into tangible and intangible products. Tafesse [53] has suggested that the effects of message strategy and vivid media use on campaign success differ by product type. It's noted that some sub-categories which are seen as intangible products traditionally also include non-profit projects and should be classified into social services. Unlike the concept of “service” in marketing, these projects collect money for charity services such as charity shows, with the funders being distinct from the target audience of the service.

The dataset covers all three categories and 28 sub-categories in Indiegogo. “Tech & Innovative” includes education, fashion & wearables, health & fitness, etc., which is clearly defined as a category with physical products, and creators are required to update the stages of product development in Indiegogo<sup>1</sup>. “Creative Works” includes art, comics, dance & theater, film, music, etc., whose sub-categories are generally seen as intangible products in other studies [53]. In the sub-categories of “Community Projects”, culture, environment, human rights, and wellness have social impacts and are usually non-profit, while local businesses might aim for individual benefit. There also a lot of unclassified “Community Projects”.

The categorization is made in several steps. First, “Tech & Innovative” projects are all classified into Tangible and the sub-categories of culture, environment, human rights, and wellness are classified into Social. Second, we use keywords to identify social campaigns from “local business” and unclassified “Community Projects”. Based on the keywords used by Annaleena Parhankangas [1], we analyze the word frequency of the classified social campaigns to identify additional words. As a result, the keywords include “social justice”, “human rights”, “economic development”, “health”, “education”, “hunger”, “patriots”, “preservation”, “climate change”, “humane society”, “historic places”, “vulnerable groups”, “nonprofit” and “not for profit”. Third, we use the keywords “nonprofit” and “not for profit” to distinguish social campaigns from “Creative works”, after which the rest campaigns are classified into Intangible. Consequently, 7,316 of “Community Projects” are excluded from this study as not identified as Social. Although these projects do not output tangible or intangible products as well, their goals are usually the creator's own benefit, such as fundraising for medical expenses, rather than public benefit. Besides, a corresponding sub-category is not provided for these projects in Indiegogo, indicating they are not supported by the platform.

## 4.2.4 Control variables

First, we control for the quality of pictures by calculating the sharpness of pictures since the quality might affect the acquisition and process of visual cues. Besides, to avoid the potential influence of sentiment of visual text, we calculated the sentiment polarity through a Python package “Textblob”, which ranges from -1 to 1, where -1 represents negative polarity, and 1 represents positive polarity. Table 3 shows the average polarity of visual text is 0.04, and the standard deviation is only 0.17, which indicates that most of the visual texts have a neutral sentiment. Actually, only 187 campaigns among 7468 campaigns containing visual text have positive or negative sentiment (the score is less than -0.5 or above 0.5). Therefore, we can exclude the situation that visual text may emotionally persuade viewers.

Second, we control for basic information of projects as previous studies, including funding goal, funding days, number of rewards, videos, geography, number of comments, number of updates, number of team members, and number of Facebook friends [69]. We also control for the amount of information, including the length of the description, the number of pictures, and the total length of the title and the tagline.

Third, we use the year-month fixed effect to control for the creating dates of projects, and there are 24 months in total. In examining the main effects of visual cues, we also use the type fixed effect to control the sub-categories of projects.

## 4.5 Robustness

We conducted two robustness tests to further confirm our hypotheses. First, we use the number of backers as the dependent variable instead of the fundraising amount and keep independent variables the same. The main effects of persuasive visual cues on No. of backers log are tested in Table 5, which are consistent with our prior results and support H1-H4 except that Joy becomes insignificant. Model 5 only contains control variables and the R-squared value climbs from 0.536 to 0.538 after including the visual cues in cover images in Model 6. Model 6 shows that both Face (0.172\*\*) and Text (0.168\*\*) have great positive impacts on the number of backers. Model 7 shows that No. of faces (0.026\*\*) also has a positive impact on the number of backers. Model 8 shows Congruency (0.584\*\*) between visual text and other text in summary information has a positive impact, while No. of words log (-0.020) and Polarity (-0.049) have no impact on the number of backers.

Models 13-16 in Table 6 and Figure 5 show the robustness test of moderating effects of campaign outputs on the number of backers, and the results are exactly consistent (H5 supported and H6 partly supported). The interaction effects between Face and Intangible (0.226\*\*), Face and Social (0.208\*\*), No. of faces and Intangible (0.082\*\*), No. of faces and Social (0.087\*\*) are significantly positive compared with tangible campaigns. ## 5 Discussion and Conclusion

## 5.1 Discussion

Although the cover image plays an important role in funders’ decision-making process, it remained unclear how it persuades potential funders to click the campaign link and further affects investment decisions. We argue that persuasive visual cues in the cover image could attract viewers' attention and form a first impression during a brief moment. Overall, our results show that both informational and emotional visual cues in the cover image have a great influence on funders’ decisions, and such influence can be moderated by the forms of campaign outputs.

For emotional visual cues, we find human face greatly facilitates funders’ decisions and more human faces bring a larger positive effect, which is in line with prior studies that examined the human images on campaign pages [15,29]. Interestingly, the joyful expression enhances funding amount but has no relationship with the number of backers, indicating that joyful expression might be effective in forming a better impression instead of persuading funders to change their decisions in the first stage. Actually, this finding is in line with Xiang et al. [65]’s argument, suggesting emotional appeal has a larger positive effect on persuading investment backers rather than consumers. The decision of how much to invest is more rational and more like an investment function, while the click decision is inspired by funders' interest which underlines the consumer role. Moreover, joyful expression is also able to leave a stronger first impression by enhancing viewers' perceived empathy according to emotional contagion theory [46].

Visual text is scarcely discussed in crowdfunding literature, yet our dataset shows a considerable number of creators display text in the cover image. Visual text can realize the informational appeal of images and attract funders’ attention during a brief moment, thereby enhancing the probability of click decisions and further affecting the fundraising outcomes [4,37,58]. We also illustrate the congruency between visual text and other text in summary information greatly affects funders’ decisions. Image-text congruency increases processing fluency, which makes viewers easier to process and remember information, especially under a condition when funders' attention is brief and motivation is low [57]. Furthermore, we surprisingly find the length and sentiment polarity of visual text does not matter. This finding is opposite to prior crowdfunding studies focused on pure text in the second-stage persuasion [e.g., 31]. The possible reason is that funders won't carefully interpret the visual text during the quick view in the first stage. It can be explained by a recent study by Cui and Jiang [13], who found viewers recognize the visual text as a local element of the picture and process it in a similar manner as pictorial features. That is, instead of analyzing the visual text word by word deliberately, they adopt a relative holistic and fast style to process it. We also exclude the alternative explanation that visual text may emotionally affect viewers. In conclusion, we can argue that the presence of visual text leaves an impression of informativeness and attractiveness, and the image-text congruency strengthens such an impression.

Our findings further illustrate that emotional and informational appeals in the cover image have different effects depending on the forms of campaign outputs. Specifically, displaying human faces and joyful expressions is more effective in intangible product and social service campaigns, while displaying visual text is more effective in tangible product campaigns. It indicates that funders are more rational and seek objective and concrete information in tangible campaigns, whereas funders are more emotional and care about the person-related factors in intangible and social campaigns. The result is contrary to Tafesse [53] who argued both two appeals are more effective in intangible campaigns, which can be explained by the different modalities of information. The textual modality is more suitable for informational appeal, whereas the visual modality is more suitable for emotional appeal [5,6]. While it is true that the objective information in textual modality may overcome intangibility, the strong emotional appeal might overshadow the effects of informational appeal in images [66]; that is, the persuasive function of the cover image in intangible campaigns is anticipated to be emotional rather than informational.

Inconsistent with our hypothesis, the effect of informational visual cues has no significant difference between tangible and social campaigns, thus indicating both informational and emotional appeals in the cover image are important in social campaigns. Annaleena Parhankangas [1] pointed out that the outputs of social campaigns are generally not clearly defined and difficult to anticipate, whereas the outputs of campaigns with products are more explicit such that various product or firm-related signals can be used to inform funders. As a result, concrete and objective texts might be useful in overcoming the defect of unclear output in social campaigns.

## 5.2 Conclusion

This study makes some theoretical contributions. We contribute to the related literature on decision support systems by revealing the first-stage decision-making process in crowdfunding. In contrast to the prior studies that mostly focus on the second-stage decision, we propose a twostage decision-making process and emphasized the unique characteristics of the first-stage decision (i.e., limited information, crowded environment, and brief attention). We further highlight the importance of the cover image under this condition, based on which two influential mechanisms are proposed. First, informational and emotional visual cues are proposed leveraging the study on persuasive appeals; second, attractiveness is suggested as the most important first impression in the first stage. Our study clarifies the first-stage persuasion process and how first impressions last throughout the whole decision process.

We also extend the crowdfunding literature in the following aspects. First, our study also extends the study of visual and textual information since the effect of visual text has not been documented yet, especially in the cover image. In addition to confirming the persuasive power of visual text, we also indicate the effect of congruency between visual text and other text according to processing fluency theory. Moreover, due to the unique characteristics of the first-stage decision and the cover image, the length and sentiment polarity of visual text have no significant impact. Second, in light of the classification by product type [53], this study systematically categorized crowdfunding campaigns from the perspective of campaign outputs (tangible vs. intangible, profit vs. non-profit), including tangible products, intangible products, and social services. Finally, our findings demonstrate the moderating effects of campaign outputs on the influence of visual information, which is scarcely discussed by prior studies. The results enrich the research of moderating effects in crowdfunding, providing a boundary condition for the use of persuasive appeals in the cover image.

Furthermore, we enrich a classic theory in IS field, namely persuasion theory, by demonstrating that informational and emotional persuasive appeals can be manifested as visual cues in images. Although prior studies made attempts to classify images into different persuasion types, such a method depends highly on the dataset and cannot be generalized, because the training set is manually coded from their own dataset. Our study revealed that an image is able to achieve two persuasive appeals if both informational visual cues and emotional visual cues coexist in images [67]. By capturing the different types of visual cues, this study realized a finegrained analysis of persuasive appeals in the cover image, which can be extended to other contexts.

This study provides significant practical implications. Crowdfunding creators should pay more attention to the first-stage persuasion and carefully design the summary information to attract funders’ eyes during a brief moment. We suggest creators adopt better information presentation strategies through proper persuasive appeals in the cover image according to their campaign outputs to promote funders’ decisions. Specifically, tangible product campaigns are better to attach some proper text (i.e., brand names, unique selling points, text congruent with the title, etc.) to the images while displaying human face is more effective in intangible product campaigns, and both two appeals are effective in social service campaigns. From the perspective of funders, it’s meaningful to be conscious of how they are influenced by the cover image on crowdfunding platforms. Due to information asymmetry in crowdfunding, funders’ decisionmaking is easy to be affected by emotions. Our study can help funders to be aware of the persuasion power of the cover image and make a more rational decision.

The limitations of this study provide directions for future work. First, despite all our efforts, this study might miss some important persuasive visual cues in the cover image, which need further exploration. For example, the product in the image might be another important informational visual cue. Second, this study assumes a two-stage persuasion process. Although fundraising performance is a good proxy of the outcomes of click decisions and first impressions, we cannot provide empirical evidence of the mechanism due to our data and methodology limit.

Future work could employ other methods, such as experiments to capture participants' actual click decisions and investment decisions; meanwhile, measure their first impressions like the degree of attractiveness through scales. In this way, we can also investigate how the characteristics of funders moderate the influence of persuasive appeals in the cover image.
