# E-mail overload: Exploring employees’ experiences using e-mail during worktime and leisure time and consequences for their subjective well-being

## Abstract:

Increased connectivity between employees and their organizations complicates managing information flows and constant availability via e-mail. We classify employees’ e-mail experiences using e-mail into groups of e-mail overload based on a boundary perspective and assess its impact and coping mechanisms, from a coping perspective, on subjective well-being. Survey results (N = 1,372) show that (1) e-mail overload during worktime increases job stress and reduces job and life satisfaction; (2) e-mail overload during work and leisure increases job stress and reduces life satisfaction; and (3) having no e-mail overload decreases job stress and improves job and life satisfaction. We formulate implications for research and practice.

Keywords: e-mail overload, coping mechanisms, well-being, survey data

## 1 Introduction

The multiplicity of information and communication devices (computers, smartphones, tablets, etc.) and digital tools (e-mails, social media, document sharing, instant messaging, video conferencing, etc.), combined with high-speed network performance and the widespread use of wireless networks, allow increasingly nomadic connection from anywhere and at any time (Eurofound & ILO, 2017). Even though instant messaging applications such as WhatsApp, Messenger, Telegram, and Microsoft Teams are popular (Bahri et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022), professional e-mail remains the most prominent and popular way to communicate while working (Russell et al., 2024; Letmathe & Noll, 2024). Checking their e-mails is the first thing done by individuals in the morning before they look at social media or anything else online (The Radicati Group, 2023). In addition, the number of e-mails received is still growing, as shown by The Radicati Group (2023), which estimates that the number of business-related emails sent and received per user per day worldwide is nowadays growing at a rate of more than 4% per year. At the same time, at the EU level, it has been estimated that the cost (overall cost, direct health cost, and loss of productivity) due to low employee well-being is above 450 billion EUR per annum (International Labour Organization, 2016).

Professional e-mail use has been shown to have mixed effects. On one hand, positive impacts such as increasing work efficiency, reducing repetitive tasks, and enhancing information and knowledge sharing create a motivational work environment beneficial for both employees and their organizations (Castellacci & Tveito, 2018; Martin, 2017). Additionally, the stakes related to e-mail use are not only at the personal level; guaranteeing the motivation, health, and cohesion of the staff to ensure continuity of production is also a major issue for organizations (Barley et al., 2011; Martin, 2017; Wang et al., 2020).

On the other hand, negative impacts appear, such as e-mail creating a culture of urgency and immediacy (Jerejian et al., 2013; Lanctot & Duxbury, 2021) or disrupting managerial relations (Mazmanian et al., 2013). E-mail is has a number of negative aspects, most prominently “email overload” (Gupta et al., 2011; Tarafdar et al., 2023). E-mail overload is defined as an individual’s perception of being overwhelmed by e-mail that they consider too much to handle (Misra et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2023). It covers information overload (volume of e-mails), communication overload (frequency of e-mail use), and cognitive overload (inability to process the flow of information). Despite authors studying ICT-mediated overload (e.g., Yu et al. 2023), little is known about how employees’ experience of using e-mails leads to e-mail overload during worktime and/or leisure time. However, boundary issues are increasingly becoming critical in the post-COVID-19 digital transformation of the work environment.

Therefore, our first objective is to understand which forms of e-mail overload emerge from employees’ experience of using professional e-mail during worktime and leisure time. We draw on a boundary perspective to have a comprehensive classification of employees’ practices when they deal with professional e-mail during their worktime and when they decide to (or have to) deal with professional e-mail during their leisure time. We draw on a coping perspective to assess the consequences of e-mail overload, which are rarely beneficial for employees. The risks relating to e-mail overload are psychological and emotional (Barley et al., 2011). According to the transactional theory of stress, in technostress research, overload, as a dimension of environmental demands or techno-stressors, is mainly appraised as stressfu (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Stich et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2007, 2019, 2023). Authors highlight work-related outcomes including lower productivity, increased work and longer working hours, and poor decision-making (Tarafdar et al., 2023). Scholars have pointed out other negative effects on work outcomes such as productivity, innovation, and performance (Yu et al., 2023). E-mail interruptions not only drain cognitive energy, but also can affect well being negatively (Wijngaards et al., 2022). Furthermore, e-mail interruptions are likely perceived as incompatible with goal progress and goal attainment and, for this reason, thwar employees’ well-being (Wijngaards et al., 2022). In this context, a body of research has tried to identify coping mechanisms to overcome these negative consequences of e-mail overload, such as the to-do list (Letmathe & Noll, 2024) or the definition of a speed of response expectation (Giurge & Bohns, 2021). Nevertheless, in existing evidence, how e-mail overload may be beneficial or detrimental to employees’ subjective well-being is only indirectly measured via specific e-mail use or misuse (regarding, e.g., volume, time dedicated to e-mail) and one or two dimensions of subjective well-being.

The increasing prevalence of remote work and flexible work arrangements, which generate a digital and hybrid work environment, especially after the COVID-19 pandemic (Barrero et al., 2021, DeFilippis et al., 2020, Kudyba, 2020), necessitate research on achieving healthy e-mail consideration at work and outside in the context of a blurred boundary between work and non work spheres (including the location and the time or moment, which separate the two). Our second objective is thus to examine a number of aspects of global work and life subjective well-being, rather than focusing solely on a few work-related outcomes (such as productivity, decision-making, or job stress). Subjective well-being is defined as an individual’s overall evaluation of their satisfaction with their work and personal life. Subjective well-being is defined by three key characteristics: It is subjective, it encompasses positive and negative measures, and it involves a comprehensive evaluation all aspects of an individual’s life circumstances (Diener, 1984). To gain insight into the impact of e-mail overload on employees subjective well-being, we thus examine three key dimensions of subjective well-being: job stress, job satisfaction, and life satisfaction.

In sum, the present empirical assessment aims to address the aforementioned gaps by proposing a novel classification of e-mail overload and providing new empirical evidence on its impact on employees’ subjective well-being, along with coping mechanisms to overcome its detrimental effects. An online survey of 1,372 employees was conducted. By identifying various forms of e-mail overload, from a work sphere and private sphere relevance perspective, and by examining the impact of these sphere-based e-mail overloads on employees’ well-being, we provide a comprehensive understanding of the nature and outcomes of e-mail overload in both worktime and leisure time contexts.

The main contributions of this paper are fourfold. First, we adopt a boundary perspective to and leisure time. We employ a coping perspective to conceptualize the disadvantages of e-mail overload and to identify potential coping mechanisms to overcome them. Thus, we contribute to various disciplines, such as communication and media research, information systems, human resources management, and personal economics.

Second, while there is a lack of consensus on good practices in using e-mail (Gupta et al., 2011; Mark et al., 2016; Szóstek, 2011), we classify employees’ experience using e-mail into various e-mail overload profiles. In addition, as the spatial and temporal flexibility of e-mail contributes to blurring the time boundary between the work and leisure spheres (Chesley, 2005, Eurofound & ILO, 2017, Yu et al., 2023), we pay attention to e-mail use during both worktime and leisure time. By doing so, we provide a classification of the forms of e-mail overload, extending the current body of research focused on some e-mail use and misuse during worktime or leisure time (Epley & Kruger, 2005; Jerejian et al., 2013; Martin et al., 2022). Furthermore, we examine the mediating role of various coping mechanisms highlighted in the literature as a means of overcoming the drawbacks associated with e-mail overload (Giurge & Bohns, 2021; Letmathe & Noll, 2024).

Third, while the majority of existing literature correlates certain e-mail use or misuse with one or two direct (or indirect) measures of job stress, job satisfaction, or life satisfaction, it never considers all three dimensions of employees’ subjective well-being, which this paper analyzes. Moreover, we examine not only the average effect across the whole sample, but also the responsibilities).

Fourth, Luxembourg is a relevant context to study and an interesting gap to fill, as many local firms provide portable devices to allow their staff to stay connected with their organizations when out of the office (in 2019, about 73% compared with 68% in the European Union, according to the Digital Economy & Society Index in the EU<sup>1</sup>).

We next present the literature background on e-mail overload causes from a boundary perspective and on e-mail overload consequences from a coping perspective, along with the existing evidence that links e-mail use and overload to subjective well-being. Following that, we present the data and the methods we used and our findings. We conclude by examining the findings in the context of the existing literature and considering their implications for research and practice. We finally identify potential limitations and avenues for future research.

## 2 Literature Background

This section presents the literature background on the causes and consequences of e-mail overload from two perspectives: that of the boundaries between work and home life, and that of coping strategies to overcome the detrimental effects of e-mail overload. Second, it provides an overview of existing empirical research that links e-mail usage, misuse, or overload with at least one of the three dimensions of subjective well-being: job stress, job satisfaction, and life satisfaction.

## 2.1 E-mail overload antecedents from a boundary perspective

For work communication and collaboration purposes, employees use several digital media (Jackson & Farzaneh, 2012; Watson-Manheim & Bélanger, 2007) that are complementary, substitutable, and superposable. Among them, e-mail remains an important—if not the most important—communication tool (Letmathe & Noll, 2024). In addition, e-mail is distinctive because it enables three specific usages: the “umbrella e-mail” (I protect myself), the “visibility e-mail” (I make myself as visible as possible), and the transfer of information that may be false (gossip, rumors, and urban legends; Kibby, 2005). As it involves written communication, it can also lead to misinterpretation (Epley & Kruger, 2005).

The misuse of e-mail generates e-mail overload. Conventional ICT-mediated overload mainly covers information overload, communication overload, system feature overload, socia overload, and interruption overload (Yu et al., 2023). Focusing on e-mail overload, we consider in this work the three most cited forms of e-mail overload: information overload (volume of e mails), communication overload (frequency of e-mail usage), and cognitive overload (ability to process received information). We also consider the distinction between the worktime and leisure time contexts of e-mail use.

Information overload occurs when an employee deals with a volume of e-mail that is too important to be treated in time (Brown, 2007). It generates the feeling of having to manage too much information in a minimum of time (Gupta et al., 2011). This is the most studied dimension of e-mail overload.

Communication overload is related to rapid frequency and/or employees having difficulty dealing with all the unsolicited messages they receive, compared with what they want (e.g., Eppler and Mengis, 2004; Reinke and Chamorro-Premuzic, 2014). The speed of communication of this asynchronous medium can create a culture of urgency and immediacy (Jerejian et al., 2013; Lanctot & Duxbury, 2021). The frequency with which e-mails enter the inbox creates interruptions, and may cause the dispersal syndrome, as e-mails generate distractions, stimuli, and the fear of missing out (FoMO; Mark et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2020). It also increases the effort to interpret e-mail content (Gupta et al., 2011; Stich et al., 2019). The recipient and the sender of e-mails are also important in emergency evaluation. For instance, employees may think they need to answer immediately if the e-mail comes from their superior or feel forced to answer even during leisure time. Communication overload that is generated by e-mail use can be amplified by use of many ICTs tools dealing with e-mail and other communication applications, such as instant messaging, social networking, shared storage systems, and video conferencing (Bahri et al., 2020; Tarafdar et al., 2023). In their work on communication overload, Stephens et al. (2017) identified seven dimensions composing communication overload: compromising message quality, having many distractions, using many information and communication technologies, pressuring for decisions, feeling responsible to respond, overwhelming with information, and piling up of messages. In our study, pressure and feeling responsible or guilty are emphasized.

Cognitive overload concerns the ability to process received information. For Reinke and Chamorro-Premuzic (2014), it occurs when the use of e-mails gets out of control—in other words, when employees are unable to find, cope with, or process the information contained in their e-mail effectively. Eppler and Mengis (2004) emphasize that cognitive overload worsens when the amount of decision time begins to decrease. This occurs (i) if the volume of the information exceeds the capacity to process the information, ii) if the time required to process the information exceeds the time available, (iii) if the decision-maker feels that they must process more information from e-mail than they can efficiently process, or (iv) if the amount of information received by e-mail exceeds the amount of energy available to digest it. These causes may vary across employees and organization characteristics, the work tasks, and the way the technology is used (Eppler & Mengis, 2004). Attention theories and cognitive load theory theorize this cognitive overload (Letmathe & Noll, 2024). The first theory assumes that the total attention that can be used for different things is limited and that interference increases cognitive load so that attention can only be focused on one activity. The second theory distinguishes three types of cognitive overload: intrinsic, extraneous, and germane. It refers to the degree of complexity, inefficient presentation of the information, and new information

Moreover, the temporal aspect of the three dimensions of e-mail overload is also crucial, since the use of e-mail reduces the time available for employees to process other work tasks by generating interruptions (Gupta et al., 2011), creating an obligation of reactivity, a permanent sense of urgency (Fitz et al., 2019), and a use that often overflows into leisure time (Martin et al., 2022).

The argument for dealing with worktime and leisure time involves considering boundaries. Boundaries are broadly defined as “physical, temporal, emotional, cognitive, and/or relational limits” (Ashforth et al., 2000, p. 474) that help people make sense of the world around them and delineate one entity from another. The boundary-blurring and constant availability of ICTs allows individuals to be “always online” without strict boundaries between private and work contexts (Chesley, 2005, Yu et al., 2023, Eurofound & ILO, 2017; Lal & Dwivedi, 2010). The balance between work and life, known as work–life balance, represents equilibrium between job demands and other life roles (Hasan et al., 2020). For Tarafdar et al. (2023), e-mail overload is positively associated with the role of overload related to work. The work–life balance can contribute to the perpetuation of an “always on” work culture. This is a prime focus in everyday discourses of life. The nature of work–life balance research is interdisciplinary, spanning social sciences, business, psychology, and economics (Verma et al., 2024).

In our study, we explore how employees’ experience using e-mail shapes the form of e-mail overload they face across a sample of employees working in Luxembourg by asking the following research question:

RQ1. Which forms of e-mail overload emerge from employees’ experience of using professional e-mail during worktime and leisure time?

## 2.2 E-mail overload consequences and adaptive solutions from a coping perspective

From a coping perspective, technostress—the stress caused by the use of digital technologies— is defined as “a problem of adaptation that an individual experiences when he or she is unable to cope with, or get used to, ICTs” (Tarafdar et al., 2007, p. 304). Various definitions of technostress include psychological, physical, and behavioral manifestations related to technostressors. Research has concentrated on the antecedents and consequences of technostress (Salo et al., 2018). Most studies on the consequences of technostress have focused on decreased job satisfaction, work engagement, or productivity (Ragu-Nathan et al. 2008; Tarafdar et al. 2007, 2010, 2015). Another reason the use of digital technologies, especially e-mail, can have a negative effect on employees’ subjective well-being is that it leads to greater workload (Barley et al., 2011; van Veldhoven et al., 2002).

According to the transactional theory of stress, in technostress research, overload, as a dimension of environmental demands or techno-stressors, is mainly appraised as stressful in a threatening way that induces adverse consequences for employees’ subjective well-being (Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Stich et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2007, 2019, 2023).

A body of research has evaluated the effectiveness of different coping mechanisms that employees can use to mitigate the negative effects of e-mail overload on their well-being. For possible), “to-do list” (keeping e-mails in the inbox as a reminder of things to do), “alertness” (keeping the inbox permanently open), “prioritization” (prioritizing the “must-dos” over the “would-be-nice-to-dos”), and “folder organization” (sorting e-mails into separate folders; Letmathe & Noll, 2024). Letmathe & Noll (2024) highlight that, among these five coping mechanisms, the zero-inbox and to-do list strategies facilitate enhanced e-mail management performance among individuals. The zero-inbox approach optimizes solely the efficacy of email use, whereas the to-do list and prioritization mechanisms enhance work effectiveness. In the same effort to identify coping strategies, Wijngaards et al. (2022) or Kushlev & Dunn (2015) investigated for whom and under what circumstances e-mail message batching (i.e., processing e-mails only at certain times of the day or frequency) is effective for reducing email interruptions and supporting well-being. Non-urgent work e-mails received during leisure time are perceived as more urgent by recipients than by senders (Giurge & Bohns, 2021). Giurge & Bohns (2021) showed that receivers overestimate senders’ response speed expectations to non-urgent e-mails sent outside regular work hours (e.g., on the week-end), a phenomenon they labelled e-mail urgency bias. Therefore, individual’s policies of reducing the e-mail urgency bias by explicitly noting their response speed expectations can be part of the solution. Nevertheless, there is a lack of consensus on best individual e-mail processing strategy (Gupta et al., 2011; Mark et al., 2016; Szóstek, 2011). The individual choice of an email management strategy may explains why some employees will check e-mail as soon as received (alertness), while some others will take their time and distinguish unforeseen demands from usual e-mails (Mark et al., 2015).

## 2.3 Existing empirical evidence relating e-mail overload and three different dimensions of subjective well-being

Subjective well-being refers to a person’s overall evaluation of how satisfied they are with their work and private life. Subjective well-being has three distinguishing features: it is subjective, it includes positive and negative measures, and it includes a global assessment of all aspects of three dimensions of subjective well-being.

The first dimension of employees’ subjective well-being we focus on is job stress. We of the existing studies focus on a limited range of e-mail-related usages, such as the volume and quality of e-mails received, the time spent managing e-mails, or the use of notifications (e.g., Barley et al., 2011; Brown et al., 2014; Kushlev & Dunn, 2015). Only a few assess the role of e-mail overload (Reinke & Chamorro-Premuzic, 2014). Furthermore, only a limited number of studies have examined the use of e-mail outside of normal working hours, and they have not identified a significant link with job stress (Martin et al., 2022).

There is a relatively limited body of research examining perceived job stress, with notable exceptions such as Kushlev & Dunn (2015), Misra et al. (2020), and Martin et al. (2022). Most studies conclude that e-mail misuse is detrimental to employees’ mental well-being and that coping mechanisms can help overcome the negative effects (Wijngaards et al., 2022).

Nevertheless, none of the existing studies provides a comprehensive assessment of the extent to which e-mail overload in a work/life context appears and influences job stress.

Building on this indirect existing evidence on how e-mail overload affects employees’ subjective well-being, we will investigate the relationships between forms of e-mail overload (RQ1) and three dimensions of subjective well-being: job stress, job satisfaction and life satisfaction. We pose the following research question:

RQ2. How do the forms of e-mail overload affect employees’ subjective well-being? Consequently, our research model (see Figure 1), based on the boundary perspective, aims, first, to distinguish employees’ experience using e-mail during worktime and leisure time into employees’ profiles of e-mail overload. Second, we investigate the link between employees e-mail overload profiles and subjective well-being. From a coping perspective, we assess the role of coping mechanism strategies in mitigating the relationship between e-mail overload and subjective well-being. Additionally, we examine not only the average effect across the sample, controlling for individual, job, and workplace characteristics, but also the heterogeneity across different sociodemographic groups (gender, age, managerial responsibilities).

## 3 Data and Methods

## 3.1 Data collection

To conduct our analysis, we use data collected in the framework of the European Social Fund (ESF) project entitled “Info Flow Savvy” led by IMS—Inspiring More Sustainability, Luxembourg<sup>2</sup>—and gathered by the Luxembourg Institute of Socio-Economic Research (LISER). IMS and LISER developed the items for the survey by drawing both on the literature on e-mail usage, misuse, and overload and on discussions with external stakeholders from the Ministry of State, the Ministry of Labor, Employment, and Social Economy, the Chamber of Commerce, and the Chamber of Employees of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. The data were collected, during the summer of 2019, with an online questionnaire available in both French and English. The final sample collected includes 1,372 employees working in 107 firms located in Luxembourg living either in the country or in cross-bordering countries (Belgium, and the gender, nationality, and age of the employees, we adopted a weighting procedure to obtain representative results for the studied working population.

In comparison with other countries, Luxembourg’s labor market is characterized by a high proportion of foreign employees. Foreign workers represent 73.3% of the total number of people employed in 2019,<sup>3</sup> compared with 8.3% in the European Union, consisting of 15 countries.<sup>4</sup> Within firms having at least 10 employees, the percentage of employees using the Internet at work was 45.8% in 2018 according to OECD data, less than in other European countries, especially border countries: 58.5% in Belgium, 58.1% in Germany, and 60.9% in France. In 2016, according to Eurostat,<sup>5</sup> more than three out of four of Luxembourg’s firms provided portable devices with mobile Internet connection to their staff, far greater than the seven out of ten provided by European firms.

## 3.2 The outcome variables: Three subjective well-being variables

We investigate the relationships that exist between e-mail overload, coping mechanisms, control variables (see Figure 1), and subjective well-being measured by three variables: job stress, job satisfaction, and life satisfaction.

To measure job stress, employees indicated the frequency with which they suffered from job stress. Employees had to choose between six levels of frequency from “never” to “every day.” In the questionnaire “never” takes the value 1, “less than once a month” the value 2; “at least once a month” the value 3; “once a week” the value 4; “more than once a week” the value 5; and “every day” the value 6.

Life satisfaction is a global evaluative judgement made by an individual about the overall state of her/his life using a 0–10 scale (from 1 for employees who are completely unsatisfied to 10 for employees who are completely satisfied). An identical format is used to measure employees’ job satisfaction. Due to low frequencies in dissatisfaction levels, we grouped some point scales (0 with 1; 2 and 3 with 4) and created studied variables that go from 1 to 8 (Table 4).

To reduce the time and cost for the survey, we opted to utilize single-item measures. It is noteworthy that all the single-item measures we used correlate highly with longer multipleitem counterparts. The efficacy of single-item measures of stress has been validated against multi-item measures in multiple studies (Gilbert & Kelloway, 2014; Littman et al., 2006). A job satisfaction single item was also validated by Pelly et al. (2022) in comparison with the Abridged Job Descriptive Index (AJDI developed by Stanton et al., 2002). Cheung & Lucas comparable to that of the multiple-item Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS developed by Diener et al., 1985).

Given that the three subjective well-being variables are ordered, the models to be adopted are ordered models such as ordered Logit or ordered Probit. Given that these two types of model can be used interchangeably because they provide similar results (Davidson & MacKinnon, 1984), we have chosen to adopt an ordered Logit model.<sup>6</sup> To gain insight into the differences between employees’ e-mail overload profiles and coping mechanisms, we run a number of independent ordered logit models. This number will be equal to the number of outcome variables (three) multiplied by the number of groups identified by the clustering analysis (see hereafter) and the introduction or not of coping versus no coping mechanisms.

In this study, the relationship between e-mail overload profiles and the three subjective well being variables with the greatest possible accuracy is measured using odds ratios. The primary objective of the odds ratio is to quantify and assess the probability of an event occurring. In more specific terms, the odds ratio is calculated by relating the number of times the event may ratio is 1.5, this indicates that the probability of experiencing the event is 50% higher than the probability of not experiencing the event. Conversely, if the odds ratio is equal to 0.7, this indicates that the probability of knowing the event is 30% lower than that of not knowing it.

## 3.3 Forms of e-mail overload derived from employees’ experience using e-mail

Grounded in the literature assessing e-mail usage, misuse, and overload in a worktime or a of employees regarding their experience using professional e-mail covering e-mail misuse in the work and leisure environments and work context for e-mail use. Details regarding the questions employed in the study can be found in Appendix A. In contrast to numerous studies that concentrate on particular elements of e-mail usage or misuse, our approach encompasses a more comprehensive range of employees’ experiences using professional e-mail.

First, we cover, as usual, the number of e-mails received (e.g., Brown et al., 2014; Jerejian et al., 2013). Second, while a lot of studies focus on working outside working hours (e.g., Arlinghaus & Nachreiner, 2013; Schieman & Young, 2013), we cover e-mail both use during worktime and during leisure time (e.g., Martin et al., 2022). Third, we cover the fear of missing out (FoMO) approach (Fitz et al., 2019) with the feeling of being forced to answer and guilt in not answering that employees have when they receive e-mail and need to answer immediately. Fourth, we examine the individual-to-group process of e-mail exchange between the receivers and senders (Addas & Pinsonneault, 2018), covering superiors, colleagues, staff, and customers, expanding the coverage of supervisors and colleagues done by Wright et al. (2014). Finally, we add the frequency of useless e-mails and the difficulty in managing e-mails. For the work context of e-mail use, the variables cover the reception of too much information during the day, the pace of work affected by unforeseen demands, and the time employees get to work without being interrupted. The consistency of the Cronbach’s  of all 19 measures together is good (0.829; Hair et al., 2006).

To identify employees’ e-mail overload profiles, we synthesized the information from the 19 practices of employees’ experience using e-mail by clustering them, using the K-means clustering algorithm.<sup>7</sup> We chose the number of clusters or groups using the dendrogram (see Appendix B, Figure B1) and the “elbow” method by identifying a slope reduction (or a stable slope or an increase) at a point on the graphic of the sum of the squared distances (see Appendix B, Figure B2).<sup>8</sup>

## 3.4 Coping mechanisms

A survey question on employees’ actual professional e-mail management behavior was employed to identify the coping mechanisms used by employees, as identified in the literature that analyzes employees’ coping strategies (Letmathe & Noll, 2024; Giurge & Bohns, 2021) Based on the correlation between items, we grouped survey items related to different coping mechanisms. Details of the survey items used are provided in Appendix A. The zero inbox strategy refers to employees’ willingness to have an informative overview of e-mails without accumulating information by using an inbox-clearing activity. The to-do list strategy corresponds to using the inbox as a reminder of things that need to be done. The alertness strategy involves employees’ willingness not to see e-mail accumulate at the expense of being and folder organization strategy. Both are used to structure work-related e-mails in a rigorous and structured way. Finally, we measure whether senders explicitly state their response speed expectations, as a strategy to reduce the e-mail urgency bias (Giurge & Bohns, 2021). To ensure consistency, the coping mechanisms have been standardized to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1.

## 3.5 Control Variables

In our regressions, we control for a broad range of individual characteristics: gender, age, nationality, being in a couple or not, having child(ren) or not, education level, transport mode to the workplace, and commuting time. We also control for other job characteristics that can be related to employees’ subjective well-being: occupation, working full time or not, management responsibilities and having direct contact with people outside the firm, and workplace characteristics: firms’ business sectors and sizes. Appendix C, Table C1 presents the control variables introduced in the analyses. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of our results is assessed by considering the variables of gender, age, and management responsibilities.

## 4 Main Results

The presentation of the empirical results is divided into two parts. The first part presents the identification of the different groups of employees sharing similar forms of e-mail overload. The second part presents the analyses of the relationships between employees’ belonging to ones of the groups defined in part one (three groups) and their subjective well-being.

## 4.1 Research Question 1: Which forms of e-mail overload emerge from employees’ experience using professional e-mail during worktime and leisure time?

When we classify employees according to the 19 variables that describe employees’ experience using e-mail (covering e-mail misuse practices and work context around the use during worktime and leisure time), we obtain three groups (cf. Appendix B, Table B1). A first group of employees covers 43.2% of the studied population who share similar behavior in e-mail misuse and work context of e-mail use. They are close to the average of the whole sample in their number of e-mails received, the time dedicated to managing e-mails, the frequency of useless e-mails received, and the number of difficulties they encounter in finding information in their e-mails. They are above average on variables that measure e-mail use during work time. Conversely, they are below average on variables that capture e-mail use during leisure time. Compared with the average, they face more unforeseen demands at work, have received too much information, and are more frequently interrupted in their work. In sum, these employees face e-mail overload only during their worktime. We called this group “Worktime e-mail overload.”

A second group consists of 18.7% of the studied population. Compared with the average these employees take more time to manage their e-mails, receive more e-mails, more frequently receive useless e-mails, and face more difficulties in finding information in their e-mails. Compared with the average, they face more unanticipated work demands, agree more that they receive too much information, and are more frequently interrupted in their work. They are overrepresented for all variables that are linked to behavior in responding to e-mails during both worktime and leisure time. Indeed, these employees face e-mail overload all of the time. We called this group “All time e-mail overload.”

The third group comprises 38.1% of the studied population. Compared with the average and the other two profiles, employees in this group are less concerned about e-mail misuse work. In other words, for these employees, e-mail use is not a critical situation. For instance, they do not feel forced to answer e-mails without delay. These employees do not face e-mail overload. We called this group “No e-mail overload.”

Therefore, our results identify three profiles of e-mail overload: Worktime e-mail overload, All-time e-mail overload, and No e-mail overload.<sup>9</sup>

## 4.3 Heterogeneity effects related to sociodemographic characteristics

Little existing research provides evidence on how different sociodemographic groups experience using e-mail, face different forms of e-mail overload, and are impacted in their subjective well-being. As underlined by Martin et al. (2022) or Gui & Büchi (2019), gender, age, and supervision role are important factors to consider.

## 5 Discussion and Implications

## 5.1 Discussion

As the intensification in e-mail use we observed in 2019 increased during the lockdown(s) induced by the COVID-19 pandemic (DeFilippis et al., 2020; Kudyba, 2020)<sup>10</sup> and is expected to persist post-pandemic (Rigotti et al., 2021), it is crucial to understand the consequences of e-mail overload for employees’ subjective well-being. The present study contributes to this understanding.

Based on a sample of 1,372 employees working in Luxembourg, our study confirms two mechanisms through which e-mail overload affect employees’ subjective well-being. The first mechanism is positive. For example, employees who deal with a manageable volume of professional e-mails during worktime and do not face e-mail overload have beneficial wellbeing outcomes. The second mechanism is negative. Employees who deal with e-mail overload during worktime and leisure time have detrimental outcomes in terms of stress and job and life satisfaction. These two mechanisms offer a greater understanding of perceived e-mail overload and its effects on subjective well-being.

Our main results show that (1) e-mail overload during worktime is negatively related to job and life satisfaction and positively related to job stress; (2) e-mail overload during both worktime and leisure time is negatively related to life satisfaction and positively related to job stress; (3) having no e-mail overload is negatively related to job stress and positively related to job and life satisfaction.

In particular, with regard to job stress, our results echo research that emphasizes that the risks relating to e-mail overload are psychological and emotional in nature (Barley et al., 2011). For some individuals, specific e-mail misuse contributes to the development of stress factors linked to the feeling of urgency and of pressure (Jerejian et al., 2013; Lanctot & Duxbury, 2021). When employees suffer from e-mail overload, they feel that they are not able to cope with the flood of e-mails that falls on them to deal with. They then enter a phase of stress or emotional exhaustion, linked to a dual feeling of inefficiency and guilt (Brown et al., 2014; Reinke & Chamorro-Premuzic, 2014). It also induces some extreme consequences of suffering job stress such as emotional exhaustion (Brown et al., 2014), sleeping disorders (Brown et al., 2014; Fitz et al., 2019; Kushlev & Dunn, 2015), and burnout (Ter Hoeven et al., 2016). E-mail is indeed the very symbol of workplace stress, a scapegoat to be blamed for all workplace stress, because the inbox serves as a constant reminder of how much work remains to be done (McMurtry, 2014).

In regard to job satisfaction, our findings corroborate those of prior research indicating that, for instance, e-mail addiction reduces employees’ efficiency (Marulanda-Carter & Jackson, 2012; Pirkkalainen & Salo, 2016). Marulanda-Carter & Jackson (2012) show indeed that fiveminute e-mail interruptions cause a task to take one-third longer than completing a task withou e-mail interruptions. The intrusive use of e-mails has also been shown to induce interruption and impair the pursuit of focused work (Karlsen and Ytre-Arne, 2022), which can be detrimental to job satisfaction. E-mail overload is said to be at the root of the deterioration of relationships between co-workers and may cause a loss of job satisfaction due to the decrease in face-to-face meetings and the lack of time available for time off for informal interactions, particularly around the coffee machine (Waber et al., 2010).

In terms of life satisfaction, our findings are in alignment with the existing work showing that e-mail enables work to be carried out from anywhere at any time (Eurofound & ILO, 2017; Lal & Dwivedi, 2010), thereby blurring the border between work and family (Chesley, 2005) and generating work–family conflict (e.g., Schieman and Young, 2013).

In addition, we provide results on how employees from different sociodemographic groups are affected in their subjective well-being, based on the forms of e-mail overload they face. While the elimination of e-mail overload would undoubtedly benefit all employees, we identified certain vulnerable groups that are particularly affected. Women who belong to the group suffering from All time e-mail overload exhibit higher job stress levels and lower life satisfaction levels than their men counterparts. Senior employees who belong to the group suffering from Worktime time e-mail overload display higher job stress levels and lower job satisfaction levels than their younger counterparts. Although long working hours and presenteeism are considered integral aspects of the role of managers (Ang et al., 2015), younger managers (below the age of 50) have been found to experience a reduction in job and life satisfaction. Furthermore, managers report elevated stress levels compared with non-managers when they have at least one child. This is despite the fact that having at least one child is associated with enhanced job satisfaction for both managers and non-managers.

In regard to coping mechanisms that can mitigate the adverse effects of e-mail overload, our findings substantiate the efficacy of explicitly defining response time expectations, as previously proposed by Giurge & Bohns (2021) to mitigate e-mail urgency bias. This coping mechanism is shown to enhance job satisfaction across all employees, irrespective of the nature of their e-mail overload, and to improve life satisfaction among those experiencing worktime e-mail overload or no overload. In contrast, the to-do list (keeping e-mails in the inbox as reminder of things to do) increases job stress for those suffering from one of the two forms of e-mail overload. In addition, the worst strategy is the “alertness” one (keeping the e-mail client permanently open), as it exacerbates the job stress of those facing Worktime e-mail overload while simultaneously reducing the job and life satisfaction of all employees regardless of the e-mail overload they face. This is in line with research showing that checking e-mail less frequently reduces stress (Kushlev & Dunn, 2015).

## 5.2 Implications for Research

Our study contributes to exiting research on e-mail overload in several ways.

First, we mobilized a boundary perspective to conceptualize the understanding of employees experience of using e-mail during both working and leisure time. The coping perspective is employed to conceptualize the potential detrimental consequences of e-mail overload and to identify coping mechanisms to overcome them.

In this way, our approach goes beyond those of authors who distinguish several sources of digital overload—digital sources of information, communication, and stimulation (Misra et al., 2020)—or focus only on specific use or misuse of e-mail, rather than e-mail overload. Additionally, building on the work of Yu et al. (2023), we propose to enrich their concept of congruence. The authors distinguish between the perception of overload based on the content of the information (mainly professional or not). We propose to add a congruence based on the sphere (professional or not) and more specifically based on the location and the time or moment (professional location versus home; professional time versus off hours; professional moment versus leisure). By doing so, our research further illustrates the importance of studying boundary management at the sphere level (work versus leisure) in order to mitigate misuse of e-mail and to turn its benefits—asynchrony and flexibility—into techno-distress.

Second, we present a novel approach to the classification of e-mail overload in relation to employees’ experience of using e-mail. In contrast to existing studies that focus on specific technologies (e.g., mobile technology), or specific e-mail use or misuse (e.g., volume, time dedicated to e-mail), our study emphasizes the relevance of e-mail overload in the context of work or leisure time. Furthermore, we investigated the mediating role of various coping mechanisms identified in the literature as a means of overcoming the detrimental consequences associated with e-mail overload.

Third, we contribute to the literature on e-mail overload by examining the relationships between perceived e-mail overload and three subjective well-being measures of job stress, job satisfaction, and life satisfaction, rather than just a few work outcomes. Furthermore, our analysis considers not only the overall mean effect across the entire sample, but also the heterogeneity between various sociodemographic groups (gender, age, management responsibilities).

Fourth, we present empirical evidence for Luxembourg, which provides a relevant context for the study given the notable proportion of local firms that provide mobile devices to facilitate their staff’s ability to remain connected with their organization when outside the office. Eurostat<sup>11</sup> data indicate a growing trend among enterprises to provide mobile devices (including mobile phones, tablets, and laptops) to their staff. In 2019, 68% of firms in the

European Union with at least 10 employees provided their staff with mobile devices with Internet access. In Luxembourg, this figure stands at 73%.

## 5.3 Implications for Practice

Our findings have several implications for practice. First, employees, organizations, policy makers, and technology designers should acknowledge the dual aspect of e-mail overload in terms of both work and leisure time. To avoid this phenomenon, it is necessary for them to either take or provide corresponding actions.

First, digital skills should become a necessary personal resource for dealing with e-mail overload, and the ability to disconnect can be considered a digital skill in itself (Nguyen et al., 2022). As e-mail management strategies are context-dependent, our study implicitly offers some support for suggestions to have some coping mechanisms that influence e-mail management performance positively, especially the explicitly noted response speed expectations.

Second, our empirical evidence suggests that senior management should consider addressing e-mail overload, as a large proportion of employees are facing this problem (60% in the studied population). Since e-mail overload is detrimental to well-being and happier employees are shown to be more creative and productive (Mark et al., 2015; 2016) and to sustain companies performance (Wood et al., 2012), low well-being of employees will be detrimental to companies. Organizations can use our insights to improve workplace well-being programs. Managers should, at least, respect existing laws and regulations. Nevertheless, if no regulations are available, they should seek to take a proactive approach and go beyond the legal framework to put in place rules for sending and handling e-mail in order to mitigate e-mail overload. Managers should spread good practices of e-mail management to limit the risks of e-mail overload. They should indeed develop an information systems ethic by focusing on e-mail monitoring in the workplace (Kiser et al., 2011) and combat information “pollution” (Cai & Zhang, 1996). E-mail batching should be integrated into the culture and core practices of an organization (Wijngaards et al., 2022), specifically for employees facing higher volumes. They should invite their staff to undergo training on existing inbox features to better manage incoming and outgoing e-mails, as well as semi-automatic deletion of e-mails considered unnecessary (e.g., e-mails from suspicious sources). They should consider e-mail management methods, such as the Eisenhower matrix (Bratterud et al., 2020) that organizes e-mails according to their degree of importance and of urgency and makes it possible to deal better with e-mails to be treated, planned, transferred, delegated, or deleted. Another solution may be to test alternative communication options, such as instant messaging, web conferences, involved.

It will be easier for managers to implement good practices regarding the use of e-mail during worktime, but they can also set a good example by not soliciting their subordinates during their leisure time and encouraging all to do likewise. Some organizations make it advisable or even compulsory to write “You do not have to reply to this message outside of office hours.”

Third, policy makers should introduce public policies that can also play a role in reducing email overload, especially during leisure time. For instance, providing a right to disconnect after regular working hours (as in France) will help employees to limit the external pressure of the flow of information, communication, and work during leisure time. More generally, with more studies such as the present one, an increasing number of governments will become aware of the risks of e-mail overload. This will in turn lead to the formulation of more robust policies aimed at preventing it.

Additionally, our results bring the opportunity for technology designers to enrich e-mail management tools that help employees manage e-mail overload according to their multidimensional contexts. For example, designers should offer the possibility for employees to enter parameters such as the maximum number of e-mails received during a specific time interval.

## 5.4 Limitations and Future Research

Our results have some limitations that need to be considered. Our analyses rely on cross sectional data. Therefore, our results highlight relationships between employee e-mail overload and subjective well-being. In a more confirmatory approach, panel data and structural equation modeling would be relevant to assess a causal model globally. Indeed, longitudinal studies that track the long-term effects of e-mail overload on employees would provide valuable insights into the phenomenon in dynamics and identify further evidence-based policies.

While existing research on e-mail overload focuses primarily on certain e-mail use and misuse, especially receiving too much information via e-mail, future research should also consider the fact that employees could receive too little, or poor-quality information. In this case, the users find themselves in a situation of deficiency. This can result in repetitive and sometimes unconscious behavior, such as continuous, addictive checking of e-mail notifications. This constitutes another avenue for future research.

Technostress embodies “good stress” and encompasses challenge stressors and positive consequences, whereas techno-distress embodies “bad stress” and encompasses hindrance stressors and negative consequences (e.g., Ragu-Nathan et al., 2008; Stich et al., 2019; Tarafdar et al., 2023). For example, some researchers conclude that technology overload has a positive effect on productivity (Yu et al., 2023). Thus, further research is necessary to investigate stress outcomes by nuancing good and bad stress.

The COVID-19 lockdowns have led to a boom in working from home, highlighting the growing reliance on digital collaboration and communication tools by workers. While e-mail use has remained high and constant, instant messaging via Microsoft Teams has increased (Yang et al., 2022). Hauret et al. (2024) also note that the use of other digital collaboration and document-sharing platforms (groupware), and web conferencing during the COID-19 lockdown(s). Furthermore, these different tools can easily complement each other. Focusing specifically on e-mail and instant messaging, it has been shown that they serve distinct purposes (Iskandar et al., 2017). E-mail is an asynchronous form of communication that allows employees more time to organize their thoughts before sending, while instant messaging is a near-synchronous form of communication that facilitates quick information access and knowledge sharing, similarly to face-to-face communication. Investigating how the growing number of digital collaboration and communication tools used by workers, and exploring how these tools can be associated to enhance employees’ well-being, offers another area for future research.

It is pertinent to question the extent to which the findings from this study can be generalized to other contexts. It can be argued that the use of e-mail for work purposes is a universal phenomenon, irrespective of the national context. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the adoption of digital collaboration and communication tools may vary between developed and developing countries due to significant differences in their digital infrastructure.<sup>12</sup>

However, an interesting avenue for future research is the need for comparative studies to understand the impact of cultural, societal, and institutional factors on e-mail overload perceptions and strategies.

To conclude, insights gained from this research are timely, considering the recent resort to hybrid work (Bloom et al., 2022), increasing the use of digital collaboration and communication tools. Understanding the link between employee well-being and e-mail (mis)use has never been more important. Since technology has facilitated the emergence of boundaryless organizations with the possibility to connect to one’s work anywhere and anytime, it is important to understand complex work/non-work interactions and how maintaining boundaries may be necessary for well-being. Finally, our study provides some suggestions on how disciplined employees and organizations can mitigate the negative impact of e-mail overload on employees’ well-being.
