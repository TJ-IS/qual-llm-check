# Digitalization and financial performance: A supply chain perspective

## Abstract

Digitalization is widely regarded as a strategic approach to gaining competitive advantage. However, some firms have suffered from the digitalization paradox, meaning that investments in digitalization cannot always achieve the expected performance improvement. This study examines how the digitalization of a firm affects its financial performance and the moderating effect of digitalization proximity between the firm and its supplier. The results suggest an inverted U-shaped relationship between a firm’s digitalization and financial performance. Furthermore, the positive moderating role of digitalization proximity in this nonlinear relationship suggests that alignment with suppliers in digitalization allows a firm to materialize the benefits of digitalization. This study provides evidence of the complex relationship between digitalization and financial performance. It empirically demonstrates the importance of aligning with supply chain partners for digitalization to enhance performance benefits. It also offers practical guidance for managers in navigating the evolving digital landscape.

Keywords: digitalization, digitalization proximity, financial performance, supply chain

## 1. Introduction

Digitalization involves investing in information technologies, assets, and tools to transform business processes and activities (Fitzgerald et al., 2014; Matarazzo et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024). In this study, we focus on the digital technologies that drive business transformation, leverage the value of data, facilitate the decision-making process, and create new market opportunities (Jovanovic and Rousseau, 2005; Dalenogare et al., 2018; Gebauer et al., 2020). Specifically, we concentrate on artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, cloud computing, big data analytics, and digital applications, which are widely adopted and extensively studied and have generated significant economic value (e.g., Mithas et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021). AI technology can assist in identifying patterns, enabling automated and intelligent production and operation processes, and providing scientifically validated recommendations (Choi et al., 2022). Big data analytics and cloud computing technology offer enhanced capabilities for realtime response, analysis, and monitoring (Frank et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022a). Blockchain technology disrupts traditional transaction models, enabling trust-free economic transactions (Upadhyay, 2020). Digital application technologies translate foundational technologies into practical solutions, lowering the barrier to entry and enabling businesses to harness the benefits of digital innovation swiftly (Zhou and Li, 2023). These technologies help firms to invent and produce new products or processes and enable ongoing technical improvement and innovation (Jovanovic and Rousseau, 2005; Bresnahan, 2010). Researchers argue that digitalization is critical for firms to improve performance outcomes (Vial, 2019). However, despite substantial investments in digitalization, firms may not achieve the expected performance enhancements, a phenomenon known as the digitalization paradox (Gebauer et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2023). For example, research has shown that only 17% of Chinese firms have a digital edge that translates into improved financial performance, and the average increase is approximately 11% (Accenture, 2022).

The literature shows inconsistent results regarding how digitalization influences firm performance. Some studies find that digitalization is positively associated with performance outcomes (e.g., Ardito et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a; Yang and Yee, 2022; Tian et al., 2023), whereas others argue that addressing various challenges is necessary to achieve performance improvement through digitalization (e.g., Wimelius et al., 2021; Smith and Beretta, 2021; Guo et al., 2023). Recent research has revealed inverted U-shaped relationships between digitalization and innovation performance (Gong et al., 2023), profitability (Xiang and Jiang, 2023), environmental information disclosure (Chen et al., 2024), and environmental performance (Li, 2022). Studies have also reported mixed findings on the impact of digitalization on financial performance (Appendix 1), highlighting the need for further empirical research. For example, Kohtamäki et al. (2020) identified an inverted U-shaped relationship based on a questionnaire survey of Swedish manufacturing firms. In contrast, Bhandari et al. (2023) found a U-shaped relationship based on a sample of US-listed manufacturing firms. Therefore, our first research question is the following: How does digitalization affect a firm’s financial performance?

Digitalization has evolved from an internal focus to a supply chain perspective (Holmström et al., 2019; Son et al., 2021). Previous research has highlighted the significance of supply chain digital collaboration and the alignment of digitalization among supply chain partners in achieving competitive advantages (Li et al., 2023a; Lin et al., 2023). Digitalization proximity refers to the alignment of digitalization between a firm and its supplier (Benner and Waldfogel, 2008; Song et al., 2023). It denotes the similarities in investments in specific digital technologies between a firm and its supplier. Thus, increases in digitalization proximity are expected to facilitate supply chain digital collaboration between firms and their suppliers (Wang and Hu, 2020). However, there is limited empirical evidence on the performance implications of the alignment of firms and their suppliers in digitalization (Son et al., 2021). Therefore, our second research question is as follows: How does digitalization proximity affect the impact of digitalization on financial performance?

We employ a two-way fixed effects model complemented by robustness checks to investigate the research questions. We construct a panel data set encompassing 788 listed Chinese manufacturing firms and their 1448 suppliers from 2010 to 2020. We find that an inverted U-shaped impact of digitalization on financial performance is well supported at the firm level, with the turning point located at the extreme end of our samples. The results further reveal a positive moderating role of digitalization proximity in the digitalization–financial performance linkage.

This study contributes to the literature by clarifying the complex relationship between digitalization and financial performance. The result is consistent with Kohtamäki et al. (2020), providing further empirical evidence of the inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance. In addition, it corroborates the existence of the digitalization paradox (e.g., Yang and Yee, 2022; Gong et al., 2023). It also investigates digitalization within a supply chain context. A firm can derive greater financial benefits from digitalization when its efforts align with its suppliers. This finding not only aligns with previous studies highlighting the beneficial effects of technology proximity (e.g., Song et al., 2023) but also highlights the pivotal role of supply chains in determining the success of a firm's digitalization strategy (e.g., Son et al., 2021; Lerman et al., 2022). Our findings remind managers to exercise caution, avoiding indiscriminate increases in digitalization investments.

It is also imperative for managers to recognize that aligning digitalization efforts is instrumental in reaping the comprehensive benefits.

## 2. Literature Review and Hypotheses Development

## 2.1. Digitalization and financial performance

Digitalization is the process by which firms integrate and utilize advanced digital technologies to optimize operational processes, improve supply chain management, and ultimately reach strategic goals and achieve competitive advantages (Holmström et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2023). It allows firms to embrace new approaches in management, offering novel avenues for value creation (Nasiri et al., 2022). Proponents emphasize that implementing digitalization enhances operational performance (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2023), innovation performance (Loebbecke and Picot, 2015; Nambisan et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2023), environmental performance and sustainability (Dubey et al., 2019; Cai et al., 2023), and productivity (Brynjolfsson and Hitt, 1995).

Researchers have adopted different approaches, such as questionnaire surveys (Zomer et al., 2020; Abou-Foul et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022a; Li et al., 2022b; Li et al., 2024; Kohtamäki et al., 2020), text mining (Zeng et al., 2022; Bhandari et al., 2023), and field experiments (Bar-Gill et al., 2024), to measure and empirically investigate the impact of digitalization and report a positive association between digitalization and financial performance. For example, based on a survey of U.S. and European manufacturers, Abou-Foul et al. (2021) showed that digitalization has a direct and positive effect on a firm’s financial performance. Using text mining and samples from Chinese listed companies, researchers find that digitalization can significantly improve stock liquidity and firm performance (Zeng et al., 2022). Moreover, studies have shown that adopting data-driven decision-making can improve firm productivity (Brynjolfsson and McElheran, 2016) and help small and medium-sized enterprises establish a competitive advantage (Bar-Gill et al., 2024). However, achieving financial success through digitalization remains challenging and risky (Vial, 2019; Nasiri et al., 2022). An increasing body of research has revealed paradoxes in the impact of digitalization on firm performance, such as the paradox of asymmetrical costs and benefits (Sjödin et al., 2020) and the paradox of flexibility and stability of the organizational structure (Warner and Wäger, 2019). Researchers have also examined the nonlinear relationship between digitalization and firm performance. For example, Kohtamäki et al. (2020) reported an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance, which is moderated by servitization. Brynjolfsson et al. (2021) argued that there is a J-curve between investment in AI and productivity growth. Li (2022) identified an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and a firm’s environmental performance. Gong et al. (2023) found an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and radical innovation performance. Xiang and Jiang (2023) found that digitalization first decreases and then increases the performance of commercial banks. Bhandari et al. (2023) confirmed that digitalization has a U-shaped link with a firm’s financial performance. Hence, ongoing investment in digitalization may not necessarily result in the anticipated revenue growth.

We argue that after the initial boost to financial performance, the positive effect of digitalization investment gradually decreases until it flips over to a negative effect. Specifically, digitalization enhances firms’ information processing capacity (Zhou and Li, 2023). It assists firms in broadening their knowledge boundaries, allowing them to leverage a broad spectrum of informational resources (Vaio et al., 2021). This knowledge can be used to optimize production processes and improve operational efficiency (Loebbecke and Picot, 2015). However, the marginal benefits of digitalization are diminishing, as digital technologies may yield redundant information. A firm may also lack an appropriate management system to process and apply the vast amount of data acquired from advanced digital technologies to improve operational efficiency. Hence, a sustained increase in digitalization will result in diminishing returns on investment (Ma and Zhu, 2022). Continuous digital investment crowds out resources to develop capabilities that improve operational efficiency, such as business process reengineering and employee training (Wamba et al., 2017). Empirical evidence shows that operational efficiency is associated positively with financial performance (Cao et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023; Gao and Wan, 2022). Thus, the improvement in financial performance diminishes as digitalization continues to increase.

Moreover, the costs associated with digitalization are expected to increase, negatively affecting operational efficiency and hence financial performance. As digitalization progresses, the demand for various digital technologies increases, requiring firms to acquire new capabilities to drive business development. For example, digitalization increases data collection, storage, and management costs. In addition to acquisition costs, digitalization requires firms to upgrade their operational processes and systems (Gong et al., 2023). Indeed, during digitalization, firms must reassess their strategy, organizational structure and business ecosystem (Zomer et al., 2020), increasing hidden management costs. Firms must employ and train specialized personnel to implement digital technologies fully, increasing human resource costs (Cappa et al., 2021). Hence, the effect of digitalization on financial performance involves diminishing marginal benefits and increasing costs. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1: Digitalization has an inverted U-shaped relationship with financial performance.

## 2.2. The moderating role of digitalization proximity

Recent studies have viewed digitalization as a strategic initiative involving supply chain members (Nambisan et al., 2017; Vial, 2019; Holmström et al., 2019; Son et al., 2021). The effectiveness of the digitalization of firms can be influenced by their suppliers' digital strategies and capabilities (Li et al., 2025). Data-driven collaboration in supply chains helps reduce information asymmetry and coordination barriers, enhancing the efficiency and performance of the supply chain (Lerman et al., 2022; Bar-Gill et al., 2024). However, due to technological and financial constraints, the extent to which firms have adopted digital technologies varies considerably (Somohano-Rodriguez et al., 2020). Such variations may result in supply chain members adopting different digital technologies (Son et al., 2021). This misalignment increases the difficulty of collaboration between supply chain members. Thus, it may undermine the efficiency and effectiveness of collaboration among supply chain partners. When a firm invests in digitalization, considering its suppliers’ digitalization strategies is crucial.

Digitalization proximity captures the alignment in digitalization between firms and their suppliers and reflects the closeness in their investment choices regarding digital technologies (Benner and Waldfogel, 2008; Song et al., 2023). It is related to the strategic focus and the allocation of investments in digital technologies (Kindermann et al., 2021). Digital technologies empower supply chain members to engage in real-time information sharing, collaborative planning, and enhanced responsiveness, thus strengthening supply chain transparency, flexibility, and agility (Yang et al., 2021). However, digital technologies have diverse attributes, functions, and applications, making them difficult to integrate. Effective collaboration cannot be achieved if a firm and its suppliers apply different technologies and tools during digitalization.

We argue that digitalization proximity between firms and their suppliers enhances the impact of digitalization on financial performance in two ways. First, it reduces transaction costs. The proximity of digitalization between a firm and its suppliers breaks “data silos,” enhancing information transparency and reducing costs of communication and information searching (Yang et al., 2023). A firm that maintains close digital ties with its suppliers is more likely to alleviate coordination barriers in supply chain processes and reduce the likelihood of suppliers opportunistic behavior (Song et al., 2023), thereby reducing default costs. Second, digitalization proximity improves coordination benefits. Digitalization proximity establishes an accurate and rapid data exchange channel between a firm and its suppliers, enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of the firm’s transactions (Kaplan and Norton, 2006). This alignment also promotes consistency among organizational strategies, production, and operations within the supply chain through standardized data sharing and interoperability (Yang et al., 2023). Hence, a firm can benefit from streamlined transactions caused by greater digitalization proximity. Therefore, as digitalization proximity increases, the benefit of digitalization is strengthened and its cost is reduced, leading to a steepening of the inverted U-shaped curve between digitalization and financial performance (Haans et al., 2016). Hence, high digitalization proximity implies that a firm can benefit more from digitalization investments, causing the inflexion point of the nonlinear curve to shift rightward. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

H2: Digitalization proximity moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance positively.

## 3. Methodology

## 3.1. Sample and data

We select a sample of listed manufacturing firms from the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database from 2012 to 2023. Then we collect data on each firm's supply chain relationship from the FactSet database, which provides transaction information between a firm and its supplier based on the actual purchase (Culot et al., 2023). If a purchasing transaction occurs between a firm and a supplier in a year, we view this as a supply chain relationship. We construct a panel dataset of listed Chinese manufacturing firms paired with their suppliers. Firms with missing values and labeled special treatment are removed. The final sample comprises 4472 firm–supplier–year observations, including 788 firms and 1448 suppliers over 12 years. The analysis is based on the firm–supplier dyad because digitalization proximity is defined at the individual supplier relationship level. The firm–supplier dyad also allows us to preserve the supplier’s idiosyncratic effects.

## 3.2. Variables

## 3.2.1. Digitalization

Following Tao et al. (2023), we measure the level of a firm’s digitalization using the ratio of the firm's digitalized intangible assets and digitalized construction in progress to total intangible assets and total construction in progress. We construct a feature lexicon based on the literature and research reports (Appendix 2). We classify all feature words into five categories: AI, blockchain, cloud computing, big data analytics, and digital applications (Zhou and Li, 2023; Li et al., 2023b). We subsequently screen the intangible assets and construction-in progress items from the firm’s notes to the annual financial report manually. We set them as digitalized intangible assets or digitalized construction in progress if they contain the keywords. Digitalization (DIGIT) is computed as

$$
D I G I T _ {i, t} = \frac {D I G I N T A N _ {i , t} + D I G C I P _ {i , t}}{I N T A N _ {i , t} + C I P _ {i , t}},\tag{1}
$$

where $D I G I N T A N _ { i , t }$ denotes firm i’s digitalized intangible assets at the end of period t and $D I G C I P _ { i , t }$ denotes firm i’s digitalized construction in progress at the end of period t. $I N T A N _ { i , t }$ and $C I P _ { i , t }$ denote the total intangible assets and total construction in progress of firm i at the end of period t.

## 3.2.2. Financial performance

We assess a firm’s financial performance using its annual return on equity (ROE), computed as the ratio of net profit to net assets.

## 3.2.3. Digitalization proximity

Following Benner and Waldfogel (2008), we use the Euclidean distance to measure digitalization proximity (PROXIMITY). This approach can capture the similarity between a firm and its supplier in terms of digitalization (Stein et al., 2015). The measure is calculated via the equation

$$
P R O X I M I T Y _ {i, j, t} = - 1 * \sqrt {\sum_ {k = 1} ^ {5} (d _ {i k t} - d _ {j k t}) ^ {2}} ,\tag{2}
$$

where $P R O X I M T Y _ { i , j , t }$ represents the similarity in digitalization between focal firm i and its supplier j during period t. We classify investment in digital technologies into five categories: AI, cloud computing, blockchain, big data, and digital applications. $d _ { i k t }$ denotes the ratio of intangible assets and construction in progress related to technology in category k to the total amount of intangible assets and construction in progress at the end of period t for firm i. Similarly, $d _ { j k t }$ denotes the ratio of intangible assets and construction in progress related to technology in category k to the total amount of intangible assets and construction in progress at the end of period t for supplier j. The Euclidean distance between $d _ { i t }$ and $d _ { j t }$ reflects the difference in digitalization between firm i and supplier j at the end of period t. We then take the negative transformation of the Euclidean distance to calculate the digitalization proximity.

## 3.2.4. Control variables

We incorporate several control variables to accommodate firm-level $( F _ { i t } )$ , supplier-level $( S _ { j t } )$

and firm–supplier dyad-level $( D _ { i j t } )$ heterogeneity. First, we control for variables that embody a firm’s resources. Firm size $( S I Z E ^ { F } )$ is measured by the natural logarithm of the number of employees of a firm. Firm age $( A G E ^ { F } )$ is measured as the logarithm of the number of years from the establishment year to the reporting year. Large and old firms tend to accumulate more knowledge and experience. We also control for the cash turnover ratio $( C A S H ^ { F } )$ , which is determined by the ratio of total sales generated by operating activities to the average cash balance, and the sales growth rate $( S A L E G R ^ { F } )$ ), which is the change in total sales for the current period relative to the prior period (Steinker et al., 2016). The cash turnover ratio and sales growth rate capture the firm’s financial resources.

Second, we control for a firm’s financial risk, which influences financial performance. We use leverage $( L E V ^ { F } )$ , measured as the ratio of total liabilities to total assets, as a control variable for measuring the debt repayment capability of a firm (Tao et al., 2023). Moreover, a firm’s accounts payable turnover $( P A Y ^ { F } )$ is controlled to capture its bargaining power, computed by dividing net operating costs by the average accounts payable balance.

Third, a firm's capital market performance is also expected to affect its financial performance. We thus control for stock return volatility $( S R V ^ { F } )$ and the shareholding ratio $( S H R ^ { F } )$ . The former is calculated as the annualized standard deviation of monthly stock returns, and the latter is measured as the proportion of shares held by the largest shareholder (Bagirov and Mateus, 2019). Furthermore, we include Tobin’s Q $( T Q ^ { F } )$ , which captures corporate value in the stock market. It is calculated as the ratio of the market value of the asset to the replacement value of the asset (Vizcaíno and Chousa, 2016).

We include control variables for suppliers’ resources and capabilities that affect a firm's financial performance (Chu et al., 2019; Barker et al., 2022), including suppliers' size (SIZE<sup>S</sup>), leverage $( L E V ^ { S } )$ , and sales growth rate $( S A L E G R ^ { S } )$ , which are measured in the same way as the focal firm’s size, leverage and sales growth rate. At the firm–supplier dyad level, we introduce the tenure of the relationship between a firm and its supplier $( T E N U R E ^ { D } )$ into our model to control for the focal firm–supplier relationship strength following Song et al. (2023).

The means, standard deviations, and correlations for all the variables are shown in Table 1. We test for multicollinearity via variance inflation factors (VIFs). The mean VIF is 1.88 and the maximum VIF is 4.32, both of which are below the recommended threshold of 5.0, which indicates that multicollinearity is not a concern in the following analysis. Additionally, we Winsorize all continuous variables at the 1% level to address outliers.

## 3.3. Model

The following model was developed to test H1:

$$
\begin{array}{r l} & R O E _ {i, t} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D I G I T _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} D I G I T _ {i, t} ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} S I Z E _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {4} A G E _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {5} L E V _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {6} C A S H _ {i, t} ^ {F} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {7} S A L E G R _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {8} S R V _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {9} P A Y _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 0} S H R _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 1} T Q _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 2} S I Z E _ {j, t} ^ {S} \\ & \qquad + \beta_ {1 3} L E V E R A G E _ {j, t} ^ {S} + \beta_ {1 4} S A L E G R _ {j, t} ^ {S} + \beta_ {1 5} T E N U R E _ {i, j, t} ^ {D} + u _ {i} + v _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, j, t} (3) \end{array}
$$

where $R O E _ { i , t }$ denotes the financial performance of firm i in year t and $D I G I T _ { i , t }$ denotes the digitalization level of firm i in year t. u and v are used to control for the firm and year fixed effects, respectively. Controlling for these two fixed effects can eliminate the influences of time-invariant firm characteristics and common shocks and trends at the time level, reducing omitted variable bias and effectively mitigating endogeneity issues. Finally, $\beta _ { 0 }$ is a constant, and $\varepsilon _ { i , j , t }$ denotes the error, which is clustered at the dyad level.

Furthermore, to test H2, the interaction terms among digitalization, the quadratic terms of digitalization and digitalization proximity are incorporated into Eq. (3) to obtain

$$
\begin{array}{r l} R O E _ {i, t} & = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} D I G I T _ {i, t} + \beta_ {2} D I G I T _ {i, t} ^ {2} + \beta_ {3} P R O X I M I T Y _ {i, j, t} + \beta_ {4} D I G I T _ {i, t} \times P R O X I M I T Y _ {i, j, t} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {5} D I G I T _ {i, t} ^ {2} \times P R O X I M I T Y _ {i, j, t} + \beta_ {6} S I Z E _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {7} A G E _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {8} L E V _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {9} C A S H _ {i, t} ^ {F} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {1 0} S A L E G R _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 1} S R V _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 2} P A Y _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 3} S H R _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 4} T Q _ {i, t} ^ {F} + \beta_ {1 5} S I Z E _ {j, t} ^ {S} \\ & \quad + \beta_ {1 6} L E V E R A G E _ {j, t} ^ {S} + \beta_ {1 7} S A L E G R _ {j, t} ^ {S} + \beta_ {1 8} T E N U R E _ {i, j, t} ^ {D} + u _ {i} + v _ {t} + \varepsilon_ {i, j, t} \end{array} \tag {4}
$$

In Eq. (4), the interaction terms capture how digitalization proximity moderates the nonlinear effect of firm digitalization on financial performance.

We also use the instrumental variable (IV) method with two-stage least squares (2SLS) estimators, which are frequently utilized in the literature. This method can address reverse causality and mitigate the interference of the second significant endogeneity problem, namely omitted variables. Specifically, following Larcker and Rusticus (2010), we choose the average digitalization level of firms in the same year, city, and industry (excluding the designated focal firm; AVED) as the IV of digitalization. AVED fulfils two criteria for being an IV: correlation and exogeneity. That is, a firm’s digitalization is correlated significantly with the average digitalization level of firms in a particular year and the city and industry in which it is located. This is because digitalization is influenced by technological developments over time. Institutional pressure also drives firms in the same industry and city to mimic each other. However, the average digitalization level in the same city, industry, and year does not affect the firm's financial performance directly because AVED is related to the institutional, technological, and business environments of a city and industry in a year; however, financial performance is determined by a firm’s resources, capabilities, operations, and strategies.

Specifically, AVED is calculated via the equation

$$
A V E D _ {r, k, t} = \frac {\Sigma_ {p = 1} ^ {n} D I G I T _ {p , t} - D I G I T _ {i , t}}{n - 1}, i, p \in (r, k),\tag{5}
$$

where r denotes the city, t is the year, and k is the industry. Then p represents the peer firms of focal firm i in the same year, industry, and city, and n is the total number of firms in the sample for the same year, industry, and city. As shown in Table 4, the results are consistent with those for the main models. Moreover, the IV passes the under-identification test and the weak identification test.

## 4.3. Post hoc analyses

## 4.3.1. Firm–supplier digitalization asymmetry

Power reflects a firm’s ability to influence its partner’s actions and decisions in a supply chain (Zhao et al., 2008). It has been viewed as a key form of relational capital that influences supply chain behavior and performance (Zhang et al., 2022). A firm may use non-mediated or mediated power in supply chain relationships. Thus, we further explore how the power structure in the supply chain influences the relationship between digitalization and financial performance.

When a firm has the knowledge, expertise, or skills desired by its supply chain partner, it gives the firm non-mediated power over the partner (Zhao et al., 2008). Digitalization asymmetry reflects the difference in digital capabilities between a firm and its supplier. Thus, it influences the power structure in the supply chain and provides non-mediated power to parties with greater digital knowledge and capabilities (Zhang et al., 2022; Son et al., 2021). We divide the sample into two groups according to the difference in the digitalization level between firms and their suppliers. We introduce a binary variable, DA, that takes a value of 1 when the digitalization level of a firm exceeds that of the corresponding supplier and 0 otherwise. Thus, the DA reflects the power structure in the supply chain. Specifically, DA = 1 indicates that the focal firm has non-mediated power over its supplier, and DA = 0 indicates that the supplier has non-mediated power over the focal firm.

## 5. Discussion and Conclusions

## 5.1. Discussion of findings

The empirical analyses document how a firm’s digitalization affects its financial performance and the impact of digitalization proximity on the relationship. First, the results clarify the complex relationship between digitalization and financial performance (Gong et al., 2023; Bhandari et al., 2023). We find an inverted U-shaped relationship, which reveals that moderate levels of digitalization can lead to the best financial performance. This finding coincides with digitalization's “too-much-of-a-good-thing” argument (Gong et al., 2023; Feliciano-Cestero, 2023). It also provides a possible explanation for the digitalization paradox phenomenon (Zeng et al., 2022; Sjödin et al., 2020; Warner and Wäger, 2019).

Second, the results suggest that suppliers play critical roles in helping firms benefit from digitalization. Increasing digitalization proximity between a firm and its supplier steepens the inverted U-shaped curve between the firm’s digitalization and financial performance and shifts the inflexion point to the right. The findings reveal that proximity in digital technology investment between a firm and its supplier plays a complementary role in leveraging the performance-enhancing effects of digitalization. These findings corroborate the argument that strategic fit with supply chain partners in the digitalization investment is essential for benefiting from digital technologies (Kazantsev et al., 2022).

Third, the post hoc analyses reveal that when a firm has greater digital capabilities than its suppliers or when supplier concentration is low, there is still an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance, which is moderated positively by digitalization proximity. A firm with a higher digitalization level has non-mediated power, which is relational and associated with a positive attitude toward supply chain relationships (Zhao et al., 2008). Its influence is exerted indirectly and is less manipulative. Suppliers can decide whether and how much to be influenced by non-mediated power. Moreover, when supplier concentration is low, major suppliers do not have the mediated power to influence firms’ operations and decisions. Thus, the power structure in the supply chain does not influence the main findings in these two situations.

When a supplier has greater digital capabilities than a firm, the supplier has non-mediated power. The extent to which a firm is influenced by a supplier’s use of non-mediated power is determined by the firm (Zhang et al., 2022). Thus, a firm can learn from the supplier’s expertise and skills and invest in digitalization when it benefits its operational and supply chain processes. A firm’s continued investment in digitalization can improve the efficiency and effectiveness of collaboration in supply chains, enhancing its financial performance. This finding indicates that a firm can benefit from a supplier’s use of non-mediated power and learning from the supplier, as digitalization proximity strengthens the positive impact of digitalization on financial performance. Thus, collaborating with suppliers with greater digital capabilities could be one method of addressing the digitalization paradox.

Mediated power can be exerted according to the intention of the power holder (Zhang et al., 2022). When supplier concentration is high, major suppliers decide whether, when, and how to use mediated power to influence a firm’s decisions on digitalization. Thus, major suppliers may use mediated power to push the firm to invest in digitalization even when its employees do not have the skills and knowledge or when it does not have a management system to support the implementation of the technologies. As a result, digitalization may reduce financial performance. The firm can accumulate digital knowledge and skills, hire employees with digital capabilities, and develop innovative processes during its digitalization journey, allowing it to benefit from digitalization. Hence, there is a U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance. Mediated power is manipulative and instrumental. It is a competitive use of power. Thus, a firm tends to reduce its dependence on major suppliers by adopting digital technologies different from those used by major suppliers. Moreover, major suppliers may use mediated power to push a focal firm to adopt similar digital technologies. As a result, the moderating effect of digitalization proximity becomes insignificant.

## 5.2. Research implications

This study contributes to the literature by investigating the relationship between digitalization and financial performance. Research has reported positive effects of digitalization on performance outcomes (Ardito et al., 2021; Nasiri et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Tian et al., 2023). However, some studies have acknowledged the potential risks of digitalization (Kohtamäki et al., 2020; Smith and Beretta, 2021; Wimelius et al., 2021), such as the digitalization paradoxes (Sjödin et al., 2020; Warner and Wäger, 2019). Unexpected empirical evidence on digitalization investment has prompted scholars to examine the complex relationship between digitalization and performance (Gong et al., 2023; Li, 2022; Xiang and Jiang, 2023; Bhandari et al., 2023). Using a sample of Chinese listed companies, we contribute to this line of literature by providing empirical evidence of an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance and revealing the mechanism through which digitalization affects firms' financial performance. We show that digitalization could be a double-edged sword and that mindlessly investing in digital technologies could influence a firm’s competitive advantage negatively. Thus, we suggest that researchers consider nonlinear relationships when investigating the impact of digitalization on performance outcomes.

Our study also contributes to the literature by taking a supply chain perspective and linking digitalization proximity to suppliers with the performance outcomes of a firm’s digitalization. We find that digitalization proximity is a critical factor influencing the inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance. This finding aligns with those of previous studies that focused on the positive role of technology proximity (e.g., Song et al., 2023). Studies have focused on understanding how digitalization influences firm performance from an internal perspective (Yang et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023). This discovery highlights the significance of supply chains affecting the success of a firm’s digitalization strategy (Huang et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2023) and reveals that the effectiveness of digitalization is contingent on alignment with suppliers’ digitalization strategies (Son et al., 2021; Tiwari et al., 2024). Moreover, the results of the post hoc analyses enhance the understanding of the boundary conditions of the digitalization–financial performance relationship. The findings reveal that the power structure in supply chains influences the impact of digitalization on financial performance. We also find that the mediated and non-mediated power between a firm and its supplier play different roles. The results provide additional evidence that a supply chain perspective is essential for firms to benefit from digitalization. The findings offer insights into why digitalization paradoxes exist. We suggest that researchers consider supply chain factors such as digitalization proximity, firm–supplier digitalization asymmetry, and supplier concentration when investigating the impact of digitalization on financial performance empirically.

## 5.3. Managerial implications

The finding of an inverted U-shaped relationship between digitalization and financial performance offers valuable insights into Chinese firms’ digitalization strategies. This highlights the importance of managing digitalization efforts strategically to maximize financial benefits while avoiding potential pitfalls. During the initial phases of digitalization, firms can improve their financial performance significantly. According to the analysis, approximately 90 of the firms in the sample are below the inflection point. Thus, most Chinese firms can still benefit from digitalization. We suggest that firms invest in advanced digital technologies such as AI, blockchain, cloud computing, and digital applications. However, managers must recognize that digitalization efforts may reach a point of diminishing returns, where further investments can lead to diminishing financial returns or even reduced performance. We suggest that firms monitor their investment in digital technologies carefully (e.g., AI, cloud computing, and digital applications) and consider the tradeoffs between benefits and costs when developing digital strategies. The analysis reveals that the best digitalization level for Chinese firms is 0.6563, which indicates that 65.63% of intangible assets and construction in progress are digitalized intangible assets and construction in progress. Thus, we suggest that Chinese firms increase investment in digital technologies if the ratio of digitalized intangible assets and construction in progress to total intangible assets and total construction in progress is lower than 0.6563 and reduce investment otherwise.

We suggest that firms consider suppliers’ investment in digital technologies. Managers must recognize that digitalization proximity to suppliers enables firms to realize the benefits of digitalization fully. Thus, we suggest that firms collaborate with suppliers to align digitalization strategies and investment in digital technologies. Additionally, we suggest that firms select suppliers that have adopted a similar digitalization strategy when making sourcing decisions. Firms should also design supplier development programs to help suppliers adopt and implement digital technologies aligned with firms. Additionally, we recommend that managers involve suppliers in discussions and management of digital strategies.

We suggest that Chinese firms consider the power structure within the supply chain when designing a digitalization strategy. Managers should be aware that digitalization asymmetry influences the digitalization–financial performance relationship. Firms should select and collaborate with suppliers that have high digitalization capabilities. Managers should be aware that collaborating with those suppliers could provide a solution to the digitalization paradox. We also recommend that firms increase their investment in digital technologies when they have a high level of supplier concentration. Managers should be aware that even if the initial investment in digital technologies may be negatively associated with financial performance, they should invest continuously in digitalization. They can benefit from it after they achieve a certain level of digitalization.

## 5.4. Limitations and future research

This paper has the following limitations that lead to future research opportunities. First, our findings are based on a sample of Chinese manufacturers. These findings may be influenced by China's business, institutional, and cultural environment. Thus, future research could generalize and validate the findings in other countries. Second, this study focuses on digitalization proximity to suppliers. Other supply chain stakeholders, such as customers, could also influence the impact of digitalization on financial performance. Future research could take a network perspective and explore how digitalization proximity at the network level influences a firm’s digitalization. Third, the analysis explores only the impacts of the power structure on the relationship between digitalization and financial performance. Future studies could take a relational perspective and investigate how supply chain relationships, such as trust, influence the performance outcome of digitalization. Fourth, this study focuses on a supply chain perspective when investigating the contingencies of the impact of digitalization on financial performance. Future studies could investigate how the heterogeneity of firm characteristics, such as ownership, size and R&D intensity, influences the relationship between digitalization and performance outcomes. Fifth, this study focuses on the impact of a firm’s digitalization on its financial performance. Future research could extend this study by investigating how a firm’s digitalization affects supply chain performance such as resilience and sustainability.
