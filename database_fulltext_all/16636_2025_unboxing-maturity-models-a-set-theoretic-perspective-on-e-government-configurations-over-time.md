# UNBOXING MATURITY MODELS: A SET-THEORETIC PERSPECTIVE ON E-GOVERNMENT CONFIGURATIONS OVER TIME

Abstract. This study conceptualizes e-Government maturity from the theoretical lens of strategic change. Drawing on a multiplicity of theories, it undertakes a fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis of the drivers of e-Government maturity over the 2010-2020 decade. It bypasses partially conflicting findings about the contribution of human capital to high levels of e-Government maturity by showcasing instead two configurations of conditions where human capital investments become a non-trivial necessary condition over time. Theoretical, methodological, and practical implications are discussed by zooming in on the configurational patterns emerging over time and, by extension, refining the notion of ‘turning point’ discussed in the literature.

Keywords: e-Government Maturity; Change Models; Theoretical Multiplicity; Configurational Theorizing; fsQCA.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

E-Government maturity is a concept traditionally used by scholars to denote the progress of public sector organizations through incremental stages of e-Government development (Klievink & Janssen, 2009). Since the foundational work of Nolan and colleagues (e.g., Gibson & Nolan, 1974; Nolan, 1979), maturity models have been widely applied in both public and private sectors to stimulate the development of capabilities required to advance from one stage to another. Despite the extensive literature on maturity models and their recognized practical value (e.g., Deloitte Research, 2000; Gartner, 2017; OECD, 2020; United Nations, 2001), two theoretical issues remain unresolved, necessitating a deeper understanding of the drivers and underlying processes of e-Government maturity.

First, there are contradictory findings regarding the factors contributing to e-Government maturity. For example, Das et al. (2017, p. 422) argued that “Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Information & Communication Infrastructure (ICT) may be sufficient conditions for e-Government maturity,” suggesting that e-Government can develop or even flourish without significant reliance on other factors such as human capital and good governance. Conversely, other scholars have proposed that “investments in human capital will ensure the growth and maturity of e-Government systems” (Krishnan et al., 2017, p. 308), echoing earlier studies that emphasized the disproportionate benefits of human capital investments, particularly in developing countries (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019; Kraemer et al., 2005; Zhao et al., 2014a/b). The disparity between the significance of human capital in e-Government maturity in different studies highlights the need for a deeper examination of the drivers of e-Government maturity.

Second, there is wide disagreement over how to characterize the evolution of e-Government maturity. Early conceptions of e-Government maturity alluded to an incremental process of change (Coursey & Norris, 2008; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011; Renteria et al., 2019). However, recent theorizing points to a punctuated equilibrium model of e-Government development, with sudden bursts of change altering the trajectory of e-Government development in either a progressive or regressive fashion (Iannacci et al., 2019; Pittaway & Montazemi, 2020). This conceptual ambiguity suggests that treating e-Government maturity as a linear progression may be overly simplistic. Instead, the potential for e-Government development to regress indicates a need to revisit how e-Government maturity is theorized.

Resolving these two issues is critical for developing a rigorous theory of e-Government maturity and aiding policymakers in making informed decisions about the strategic allocation of resources to foster e-Government development. Although 'policy' and 'policymaking' are often equated with 'strategy' and 'strategy making' in the public sector (cf. Mintzberg, 1987, p. 23; footnote 20), maturity models tend to underestimate the complexity of policymaking. They often assume the public sector is a stable domain where policymakers can plan the development of dynamic capabilities "at the right time" to advance from one stage of e-Government development to the next (Klievink & Janssen, 2009, p. 277).

However, the public sector is characterized by high levels of uncertainty due to conflicting stakeholder interests and its vulnerability to exogenous shocks and disruptions (Hodgkinson & Hughes, 2024). Accordingly, policies must be continuously reformulated to achieve predefined goals and require the development of improvisational capabilities “to address urgent, unpredictable, and novel environmental situations” (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010, p. 444). Considering that public sector strategizing demands rigorous attention to temporal patterns (Hodgkinson & Hughes, 2024; Peppard et al., 2014), in this paper we ask:

How are portfolios of investments strategically configured to achieve high levels of e-Government maturity over time?

Moreover, there are known discrepancies in the evolutionary trajectory of e-Government development between developed and developing countries (Joshi & Islam, 2018; Liu & Yuan, 2015). This divide is attributed to the differences between developed and developing countries (Dewan & Riggins, 2005; Vassilakopoulou & Hustad, 2021) and the constraints of resource-poor environments, which make long-term strategic planning particularly challenging, especially in the least developed countries. To shed light on this issue, we raise an additional question:

Are there differences over time in the evolutionary trajectories of e-Government maturity in developed versus developing countries as evidenced by portfolios of innovation?

To address these questions, we draw on data from the e-Government Development Index (EGDI) since the EGDI provides a foundation for comparative and longitudinal studies of e-Government development. The EGDI effectively captures the evolving performance of both developed and developing countries, enabling us to track configurational patterns over time and, by zooming in on these patterns, to analyze the evolutionary trajectories of the most typical developed and developing countries.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the extant literature on maturity models to provide the theoretical background for this study. Section 3 proposes a new configurational maturity model of e-Government development that integrates research on e-Government maturity with socio-technical drivers of change. Section 4 employs set-theoretic configurational methods in a longitudinal fashion as a suitable fit between theories and methods. Section 5 presents our findings for high and low levels of the EGDI. Section 6 highlights the trajectories of high EGDI for the most typical developed and developing countries over time. Section 7 discusses the theoretical, methodological, and practical implications of our findings. Section 8 concludes with the limitations of our work, offering a launching pad for future research.

## 2. THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

In this section, we review focal concepts that constitute the theoretical background of our empirical study. First, we review extant literature on maturity models that illuminates distinct stages of e-Government development with the assumption that these stages capture a sequential and irreversible change process aimed at reaching a desired end state. We then ground the notion of maturity in the strategic change literature, taking a hybrid view of e-Government maturity that reconciles incremental (continuous) and radical (discontinuous) change (Street & Denford, 2012).

## 2.1 E-Government Maturity Models: An Overview

There has been considerable research on e-Government development in general (e.g., Andersen & Henriksen, 2006; Janowski, 2015; Layne & Lee, 2001; Lee, 2010; Lee & Kwak, 2012) and on maturity models in particular (Lasrado et al., 2015; Pereira & Serrano, 2020; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011). Academics, consulting firms, and international organizations have all proposed their own versions of e-Government maturity models. As illustrated in Table 1, there are varying views on how to characterize the evolution of e-Government maturity, including the number and nature of phases and the focus on front-end versus back-end processes. However, there is growing consensus that e-Government maturity models outline a path toward an envisioned end state, typically following a series of stages where each stage is a precursor to the next (Iannacci et al., 2019; Lasrado et al., 2015; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011).

For instance, Layne and Lee (2001) describe a four-stage model starting with basic online cataloging, progressing to transaction capabilities, then to vertical and horizontal integration within government systems. Focusing on citizens, Hiller and Bélanger (2001) outline a five-stage model beginning with information posting, advancing through two-way communication and transactions, leading to comprehensive integration and culminating in citizen participation through activities like online voting. Shifting the focus from back-end to front-end processes, Andersen and Henriksen (2006) propose a four-stage model that goes from cultivation through extension, maturity, and finally revolution. Similarly, the United Nations suggests a four-stage model marking the evolution from basic informational services to fully connected services that integrate various citizen interactions with the government.

On his part, Lee (2010) proposes a five-stage maturity model that begins with presenting information and progresses through assimilating, reforming, morphing, and finally achieving e-governance where services are managed simultaneously in both physical and digital domains. Lee and Kwak (2012) further develop this idea with stages that emphasize government transparency, participation, collaboration, and ubiquitous engagement with the public. Janowski (2015) presents a model that includes digitization, transformation, engagement, and contextualization of government services, emphasizing the government's role in broader societal development. Meanwhile, the most recent model by the OECD (2020) details a six-stage process focusing on making government processes digital by design, datadriven, and proactive in anticipating and meeting public needs.

Regardless of the number and nature of phases or the focus on front-end versus backend, these models depict e-Government maturity as progressing toward an envisioned end state in a sequential and irreversible fashion. We argue that one way to advance the theorizing of maturity models is to remove these linear assumptions. By extending previous work on change models (Iannacci et al., 2019; Lasrado et al., 2015; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011), we conceive of e-Government maturity as comprising both an incremental process of change and rapid bursts of change that mark more radical shifts either in the direction or pace of e-Government development.

Accordingly, we conceptualize e-Government maturity as a continuous evolutionary process whose development at any given moment is derived from the accumulated effects of causal factors that contribute to e-Government development over time. Nevertheless, exogenous shocks may produce radical and unexpected shifts in direction or pace at particular points in time. Hence, we contend that conventional stage models may not fully capture the true nature of e-Government maturity as a process of development that alternates between continuous and episodic change.

## 2.2 Conceptualizing e-Government Maturity from the Theoretical Lens of Strategic Change

The word "development" (or "maturity") usually implies a process of growth (Roztocki & Weistroffer, 2016, p. 542). However, this process is inextricably linked with the notion of change because it denotes progress through incremental stages of e-Government development (Klievink & Janssen, 2009). While continuous improvement can be characterized by incremental changes, more radical changes may be required when shifting from "one stage to the next" (Ibid, p. 276). Change itself may be conceived as a process construct that includes both temporal and causal influences. Arguably, the successful development of e-Government initiatives revolves around multiple and interdependent factors that work holistically in a systemic fashion (Iannacci et al., 2022). Governments should not only endeavor to deliver online services efficiently, but also ensure that citizens have physical access to technology (e.g., the internet, broadband technology) and the requisite skills to use this technology effectively. Hence, both social and technical drivers contribute to e-Government maturity. While the skillful use of technology falls under social drivers, technical drivers encompass both physical access to technological infrastructure and the provision of online services.

Aware that there are both social and technical drivers of e-Government maturity (Iannacci et al., 2019), we theorize that e-Government maturity encompasses three distinct aspects: investments in human capital, online services, and telecommunication infrastructure (United Nations, 2010-2020). Accordingly, we investigate the complex interactions among these three elements to identify cross-case patterns of e-Government maturity over time.

Admittedly, there are significant disagreements in the literature regarding the driving forces of e-Government maturity. While the United Nations has focused on the human and technological development of a country, as well as the provision of online services, both academics and practitioners have called for additional drivers of e-Government maturity, including the GDP of a country (e.g., Dias, 2020) and good governance (Kaufmann & Kraay, 2023). In this study, we focus on a few critical elements, rather than attempting to incorporate as many explanatory conditions as possible (Sydow et al., 2020). By doing so, we provide a more nuanced perspective of e-Government maturity, emphasizing the United Nations' (2010-2020) drivers of e-Government development. The United Nations' (2010-2020) methodology is a fitting approach to measure governments' capacity to supply online services (see the Online Service Index in Appendix A), as well as citizens' ability to access

telecommunication infrastructure (see the Telecommunication Infrastructure Index in Appendix A) and effectively use these online services thanks to their level of education (see the Human Capital Index in Appendix A). Extensions of the more parsimonious, threecondition United Nations’ model confirm the robustness of the United Nation’s (2010-2020) approach, as they are a subset of the solutions obtained with this model. These extensions highlight the importance of online services combined with human capital and/or telecommunication infrastructure in the very core of the more complex recipes for high EGDI.<sup>1</sup>

## 3. CONFIGURATIONAL MATURITY MODEL OF E-GOVERNMENT DEVELOPMENT

Conceptualizing e-Government maturity from a strategic change-management perspective allows us to study the reciprocal interactions between social and technical drivers in a configurational fashion based on set-theoretic concepts (Iannacci et al., 2022). Accordingly, we define e-Government maturity as a set-theoretic concept captured by the EGDI, which is proxied by the non-causal conjunction of the Online Services Index (OSI), the Human Capital Index (HCI), and the Telecommunication Infrastructure Index (TII) (cf. Goertz & Mahoney, 2005). The EGDI is a fitting measure to capture the performance of both developed and developing countries because it is "a benchmarking tool for e-Government development" (United Nations, 2020, p. 11). By definition, it is a relative, composite index that highlights the temporal and contextual nature of e-Government development (Andersen et al., 2020; Dias, 2020).

Figure 1 shows the configurational maturity model of e-Government development in set-theoretic terms, where e-Government development alternates between continuous

(incremental) and episodic (radical) change. From a set-theoretic perspective, the EGDI is the non-causal conjunction of OSI, HCI, and TII. However, the configurational patterns may not always include all three components due to countries experiencing resource constraints over time. Consequently, there can be a discrepancy between the EGDI in theory (i.e., the EGDI as the non-causal conjunction of these three components) and the EGDI in practice (i.e., how these three components combine over time). To account for this discrepancy, the United Nations calculates the EGDI by computing the weighted average of the OSI, HCI, and TII (see Appendix A), allowing high scores to compensate for lower scores (cf. Ragin, 2000, p. 321-328).

Considering that the EGDI is monitored by the United Nations on a bi-annual basis (Stier, 2015), our model shows that e-Government development at time t+2 depends on e-Government development at time t, thus assuming an intertemporal path dependence between the two (Sydow et al., 2009). The notion of path dependence is a fitting approach to study this change process, particularly within developing countries, thanks to the recursive nature of e-Government development wherein e-Government development at a particular point in time can spawn future e-Government development at a later time (Xiao et al., 2013).

Our model acknowledges that e-Government development may alternate between continuous and episodic change “with discontinuous change punctuating periods of stability” (Meyer et al., 1993, p. 1178). This is because it combines the endogenous process of incremental change with an “exogenous perspective, that is, an activity that is not under the regime of path dependence” (Sydow et al., 2009, p. 702). That substantial change may be required when shifting from one stage of e-Government development to the next (Klievink & Janssen, 2009) calls for a punctuated equilibrium theory of change where long periods of stability are interrupted by short bursts of radical change (Street & Denford, 2012). Accordingly, technological entities (e.g., social media, cloud computing, AI, etc.) may change over time and capture macro level events, that is, “discontinuous occurrences that represent qualitative shifts” (Van de Ven, 2007, p. 155), thus precipitating sudden bursts in the direction or pace of e-Government development (i.e., ‘turning points’).

## 4. METHODOLOGY: DEPLOYING SET-THEORETIC CONFIGURATIONALMETHODS IN A LONGITUDINAL FASHION

Qualitative Comparative Analysis (QCA) is both a research approach and a technique for data analysis (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012). As a research approach, QCA employs a multi-methods strategy in which returning to the cases at hand plays a fundamental role (Ibid). Conversely, as an analytical technique, QCA entails calibrating or scoring (causal) conditions and outcomes, constructing a truth table (i.e., a table of logical possibilities), and then populating it by determining the outcome values of the outcome of interest.

In this paper, we employ fsQCA to analyze panel data from 2010 to 2020. While Krishnan et al., (2017) conducted a cross-sectional analysis based on average scores of the 2010-2012 United Nations’ datasets, this study extends the analysis to panel data from 2010- 2020 to maximize the range of data available and subsequently identify distinct trajectories of development and change. Since the United Nations switched its focus from readiness to development in 2010 (United Nations, 2010, p. 3), we take that year as the starting point of our longitudinal analysis to monitor configurational patterns over time and portray the evolutionary trajectories of the EGDI scores within the most typical countries. In so doing, we complement our process theory of change with a configurational theory of e-Government maturity that captures the systemic effects of triadic configurations as opposed to additive two-way correlations (El Sawy et al., 2010).

Considering the choice of fsQCA, it is worth noting that QCA is a case-oriented methodology that combines cross-case comparative research with set-theoretic methods (Schneider & Wagemann, 2012); “To do so, QCA treats each case as a member of multiple sets and conceives both conditions and outcome as sets” (Aversa et al., 2015, p. 661). QCA is ideally suited to our research questions, which consider how governments strategically configure portfolios of investments while exploring the evolutionary trajectories of e-Government maturity in developed versus developing countries.

Previous applications of QCA with panel data have employed its crisp-set version where each case is coded as either “in” or “out” of a set (Aversa et al., 2015; Nishant & Ravishankar, 2020). We opted instead for fsQCA for two main reasons. First, the United Nations’ dataset includes variables that have been normalized along the 0-1 continuum. Second, we followed Ragin’s (2009, p. 141) suggestion that researchers should avoid crisp sets if they “can represent their causal conditions and outcomes as fuzzy sets.”

## 4.1 Data Collection and Analysis

Our data collection and analysis proceeded in three steps. First, we downloaded country data from the United Nations’ e-Government knowledge base for the period 2010-2020 (United Nations, 2010 – 2020) and removed nine country cases (i.e., Eritrea, Monaco, Nauru, North Korea, San Marino, Somalia, South Sudan, Syrian Arab Republic and Venezuela) to arrive at a balanced panel of 184 cases. In the second step, we conducted multiple fsQCAs for the same sample of cases for different time periods to track solution formulas (i.e., strings of solutions or configurations separated by logical OR) over time (Verweij & Vis, 2021). In the third step, we used formalized case selection techniques post-fsQCA (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016), to identify ideal types of developed and developing countries. This approach enabled us to trace the performance of the most typical instances of developed and developing countries over time, allowing us to track their EGDI evolutionary trajectories over time. Accordingly, our research strategy moved on two interrelated fronts. Initially, we tracked configurational patterns emerging over time to capture their inherent self-reinforcing dynamics (Sydow et al., 2009). Subsequently, we zoomed in on these configurational patterns to portray the process of EGDI development and change of the most typical cases.

## 4.2 QCA as a Data Analysis Technique

As a sequence of technical operations, QCA entails four steps. First, causal conditions and the outcome of interest must be calibrated (or scored) through the assignment of appropriate set-membership scores based on external ‘anchor points’ (Ragin, 2009). Second, the outcome value for each truth-table row is determined based on the minimum number of cases required for a solution and specific thresholds in terms of consistency and Proportional Reduction in Inconsistency (PRI). Third, the truth table is minimized using appropriate software (e.g., fsQCA 3.0 software program) to produce the intermediate solution (Ragin &

Davey, 2016). Fourth, QCA entails a constant return to the cases to grasp the configurations of causal conditions in a deeper way (Oana et al., 2021).

## 4.2.1 Calibration

QCA relies on a coding procedure known as ‘calibration’ to transform the causal and outcome conditions into fuzzy-set membership scores. The United Nations’ e-Government survey categorizes e-Government development into four levels: very high (EGDI score: 0.75- 1), high (EGDI score: 0.5 - 0.7499), middle (EGDI score: 0.25 - 0.499), and low (EGDI score 0.0 - 0.2499) levels of e-Government development. We used these thresholds as our external ‘anchor points’ for calibration (Ragin, 2009): 0.75 (cut-off value for full membership in the target set), 0.49 (cut-off value for the crossover point separating cases that are more in versus more out of the target set), and 0.25 (cut-off value for full non-membership in the target set).

For instance, a country with an EGDI score of 0.75 was assigned a membership score close to 1 in the set of countries with a high level of e-Government development (indicating full membership). Conversely, a country with an EGDI score of 0.25 was assigned a membership score close to 0 (indicating full non-membership). Countries with an EGDI score of 0.49 are at the point of maximum ambiguity between membership and non-membership in the set of countries with a high level of e-Government development, and therefore received a membership score of 0.501 (Fiss, 2011, p. 407, footnote 9). We applied the same thresholds as the ‘external anchor points’ for calibrating the three causal conditions: OSI, HCI, and TII.

## 4.2.2 Truth-Table and Standards of Good Practice

Starting from the calibrated data, the fsQCA program generates a ‘truth-table’ that lists all logically possible configurations of causal conditions with their respective frequency number and consistency score in its rows (Ragin & Davey, 2016). While consistency (sufficiency) measures the degree to which the configuration of causal conditions is a subset of the outcome of interest, the frequency number indicates the number of cases that belong to a truth table row. Following standards of good practice, we set a consistency threshold of 0.80 (“almost always sufficient”) and a frequency threshold of 3<sup>+</sup> observations (Greckhamer et al., 2013, p. 54).<sup>2</sup>

## 4.2.3 Minimization of the Truth Table

The truth table (i.e., the table of logical possibilities) should be minimized using appropriate software (e.g., fsQCA 3.0 software program) to produce the complex solution (where no or little simplification occurs), parsimonious solution (where there are many simplifications regardless of their plausibility), and intermediate solution (that strikes a balance between complexity and parsimony). However, it is worth stressing that, given our focus on a few explanatory conditions, our intermediate solution matches the complex solution regardless of theoretical expectations.<sup>3</sup>

## 4.2.4 Returning to the Cases at Hand

QCA entails a constant return to the cases to grasp the configurations of causal conditions in a deeper way (Oana et al., 2021). Following recently published guidelines on case selection post-fsQCA (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2013; Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016), we deployed the formula for determining the degree to which a typical case approaches the ideal type in an analysis of sufficiency (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016).<sup>4</sup> Since smaller values of this formula indicate more adequate cases, we set an arbitrary threshold of 0.20, which was sufficiently small to capture the best instances of developed and developing countries. Accordingly, we identified the most typical developed and developing countries within the boundary of $0 \leq \mathrm { S T } i \leq 0 . 2 0$ because “smaller values [of this formula] indicate more adequate cases, with 0 being the lower bound” (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016, p. 553).

Although Table 2 shows that there are two configurational recipes (or Solutions) leading to high levels of e-Government development over time (i.e., S1 and S2), investments in human capital stand out, pointing to a common yet dynamic ingredient across the two configurations throughout the 2010-2020 timespan. Accordingly, investments in human capital are both a dynamic ingredient in the recipe for e-Government maturity and a relevant necessary condition for high levels of e-Government development from 2016/18 onwards.<sup>5</sup> This point is made in the United Nations (2020) report that states:

E-Government mainly benefits people who are literate. ICT and e-Government applications are most effective when public sector entities and users have appropriate digital skills [sic] countries that have a larger segment of the population with higher education also tend to have higher digital skill levels [sic] Low levels of human capital development and public spending on education may effectively undermine e-Government development, as generic skills and education tend to be positively correlated with successful e-Government implementation. (Ibid, p. 67)

Far from working in isolation, investments in human capital produce a synergistic effect when combined with investments in telecommunication infrastructure and/or online services. This combination ensures that the right mix of capabilities and skills is in place, maximizing the return on investments in technological infrastructure and/or online services. These synergies highlight the complementary effects resulting from the interaction of these investments, which account for their self-reinforcing dynamics over time (Sydow et al., 2009).

Nevertheless, Solution 1 (S1) is more dynamic than Solution 2 (S2) because investments in human capital switch from a peripheral component to a core ingredient from 2016/18 onwards. This process mirrors the ‘patching’ process discussed in the literature (Siggelkow, 2002), that is, the emergence of a new core element in Solution 1 (S1), thus highlighting two sub-types of the same configuration, that is, $\mathbf { S } _ { \mathrm { 1 a } }$ and $\mathbf { S } _ { \mathrm { l b } } .$ Additionally, Solution 1 $\mathrm { ( S _ { 1 } ) }$ is empirically more important than Solution 2 (S2), as evidenced by its larger scores in terms of raw and unique coverage. Although S1 and S2 are partially overlapping paths from 2010/12 to 2016/18, S1 outperforms $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 }$ in terms of its raw and unique coverage. This, in turn, suggests that, although it is theoretically possible to achieve higher levels of e-Government maturity through investments in technological and human capabilities, it is much more likely that the journey toward e-Government maturity will follow a different route, namely $\mathbf { S } _ { 1 }$ , so much so that S1 is the only route to e-Government maturity at the end of the period under investigation.

“The explosive growth of broadband access in developed regions and mobile cellular subscriptions in developing countries” (United Nations, 2010, p. 3), along with significant advances in “online services”, were the key drivers of successful e-Government development back in 2010/12. In the following years, investments in online services became the dominant route to high levels of e-Government development, thanks to the rise of social media platforms enabling two-way interaction in the provision of online services (Pang et al., 2014). This argument “explains the contribution of each of the three sub-indexes indicating that the largest component of the rise in EGDI comes from improvements in OSI. This shows that investment in OSI is the fastest means of improving a country’s EGDI rankings” (United Nations, 2018, p. 128).

## 6. INVESTIGATING THE EVOLUTIONARY TRAJECTORIES OF DEVELOPED AND DEVELOPING COUNTRIES OVER TIME

While the analysis of positive cases reveals the dynamism of human capital investments and the patterns of dynamic stability thereof, our panel data analysis is silent about the evolutionary trajectory of the high EGDI scores over time. Furthermore, the configurational patterns observed over time encompass many countries that are either deviant consistency cases in degree and kind, or cases that are far off the main diagonal (see Appendix B for an illustration). To track the trajectories of the high EGDI scores of the most typical cases, we need to zoom in on the configurational patterns we observed over time by switching to formalized case selection strategies.

Accordingly, we draw on case selection guidelines post-fsQCA (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2013; 2016). In particular, we use formulas for the selection of the best cases approaching the ideal type. In other words, we select cases with strong membership in the configurations of causal conditions that are subsets of the outcome condition because they are the best empirical instances “given our calibration rules” (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016, p. 548). Setting a range spanning from 0 to 0.20 as a relatively-small range for determining the degree to which a typical case approaches the ideal type in an analysis of sufficiency, we obtain the EGDI trajectories depicted in Figure 2 for developed and developing countries, respectively.<sup>8</sup>

## 7. DISCUSSION

Motivated by conflicting findings on the influence of human capital as a contributor to e-Government maturity (Das et al., 2017; Krishnan et al., 2017), our research demonstrates that investments in human capital must work in combination with investments in online services and/or with investments in telecommunication infrastructure to effectively drive high levels of e-Government development. We have also shown that Solution 1 (S1) is the only configuration that exhibits a ‘patching’ process whereby investment in human capital moves from the periphery to the core from 2016/18 onwards (see Table 2). This corroborates the argument that e-Government users need more complex skillsets than general internet users to adopt online services (Zhao et al., 2014a/b). These findings have significant implications for IS research in general and e-Government research in particular.

First, even though most countries follow the same path to e-Government development by combining investments in online services, human capital, and telecommunication infrastructure (shown in the overlapping coverage between OSI AND HCI (S1) and HCI AND TII (S2) in Figure 4), our findings challenge the view that e-Government maturity is characterized by a single recipe for e-Government development (Das et al., 2017). Indeed, a few countries are uniquely covered by a single Solution, thus following either one path to high levels of e-Government development or the other path (e.g., Antigua and Barbuda, China, Dominican Republic, Egypt, India, etc.). Figure 4 depicts these Solutions using a Venn Diagram:

Hence, e-Government development and, indirectly, e-Government maturity, is a ‘configurational construct’: a complex, multifaceted construct emerging from heterogeneous configurations of causal conditions. Far from involving the same causal conditions in each instance of the outcome, each configuration instantiates a diverse combination of causal conditions that ‘fits’ or ‘coheres’ in the context under investigation. For example, while Solutions $\mathbf { S } _ { \mathrm { l a } }$ and $\mathbf { S } _ { 2 \mathbf { a } }$ fit with an environment where investments in online services or investments in telecommunication infrastructure are core, Solution $\mathbf { S } _ { \mathrm { 1 b } }$ fits within an environment where both investments in human capital and online services are key (see these Solutions’ core conditions in Table 2 and exemplars of uniquely-covered cases thereof).

While most countries exhibit either strong membership in both configurations $\mathrm { o r , }$ in the case of the least developed countries, weak membership in both configurations, there are a few cases that exhibit much more dynamism. Take Belarus, for example, as an exemplar of a typical developing country. Belarus switched from being a unique member of Solution 2 (S2) in 2012/14, 2014/16 and 2016/18 to becoming a unique member of Solution 1 $\mathrm { ( S _ { 1 } ) }$ in 2018/20, thanks to its renewed effort to digitalize services in healthcare, public procurement, and education (United Nations, 2018). On the contrary, Kuwait switched from being a unique member of Solution 1 (S1) in 2012/14 to becoming a member of both Solution 1 (S1) and Solution 2 (S2) in 2014/16 and 2016/18, thanks to its Kuwait 2035 Vision (Mahdi, 2018). Indeed, even developed countries like Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Finland, Iceland, Luxembourg, Slovenia and Switzerland have experienced dynamic shifts over time. Hence, far from being stable entities, a few countries are instances of dynamic configurations that switch their configurational attributes during the period of investigation (see Tables 2 and 3 and Appendix C). While extant QCA literature has primarily focused on cross-sectional studies of e-Government (e.g., Dawson et al., 2016; Denford et al., 2020; Federowicz et al., 2018), our study is a valuable addition that shifts the focus of configurational thinking from a static study of ‘final’ performance outcomes to performance outcomes conceptualized as “way-stations in ongoing processes” (Langley & Abdallah, 2011, p. 115). Our study therefore switches the e-Government discourse toward “longitudinal studies that develop process models of e-Government evolution” (Bélanger & Carter, 2012, p. 375).

Second, the configurational analysis over the 2010-2020 period masks an incredible amount of change that can only be captured by zooming in on the configurational patterns that emerge over time. The analysis of the evolutionary trajectory of the most typical countries’ EGDI scores reveals that both developed and developing countries adhere to a non-linear model of change, characterized by many radical shifts (i.e., peaks and troughs). Following in Pittaway and Montazemi’s (2020) footsteps, we label this model of change as a punctuated equilibrium model, due to the numerous radical shifts (or ‘turning points’) that interrupt (or ‘punctuate’) the process of e-Government development. Furthermore, turning points can be either ‘focal turning points’ that switch the trajectory of e-Government development toward a more stable path (see Figure 2), or ‘randomizing turning points’ that switch the trajectory of e-Government development toward random trajectories (see Figure 3). Though past studies describe e-Government maturity as an envisioned end state characterized by a sequential and irreversible process of change (Coursey & Norris, 2008; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011; Renteria et al., 2019), it turns out that – because of exogenous shocks – e-Government maturity is characterized by high levels of uncertainty particularly within less affluent developing countries. We submit that ‘turning points’ are the hallmark of e-Government development on the basis that they mark exogenous shocks that are radical, unanticipated, sudden, and abrupt. Indeed, ‘turning points’ call for a strategizing approach that revolves around the development of improvisational capabilities to cope with unexpected contingencies. Accordingly, ‘turning points’ switch the focus of maturity models from a prescriptive approach, where e-Government strategy is a means to an end, to an emergent approach where e-Government strategy is a more “dynamic, iterative, interactive and continuous” process (Peppard et al., 2014, p. 5).

Third, by deploying multiple fsQCAs for different time periods with formalized case selection in a sequential fashion (Schneider & Rohlfing, 2016; Verweij & Vis, 2021), we respond to recent calls “for further studies that shed light on longitudinal changes in configurations and underlying reasons for those” (Park & Mithas, 2020, p. 103). Compared with time-series analysis via QCA (Denford et al., 2022), our approach tracks configurational patterns and provides a more nuanced perspective of those conditions that switch from periphery to core (or vice versa) over time (see Appendix F). While Solution 2 (S2) disappears in 2018/20, Solution 1 (S1) reveals the growing importance of human capabilities and skills in the recipe for high levels of e-Government development, considering that investments in human capital switch from a peripheral ingredient to a central component of this configuration from 2016/18 onwards. These findings, in turn, lend partial support to the argument that investments in human and technological capabilities generate returns in the future. While investments in human and technological capabilities are clearly a path toward e-Government development, the journey toward e-Government development may follow a separate path that revolves around the combination of investments in human capital and online services. In addition, it is more likely that the journey toward e-Government development will be an “online services” play rather than an “infrastructure play” (cf. Das et al., 2017, p. 423). This occurs because the configuration of human capital and online services investments displays a larger raw and unique coverage than the configuration of investments in human capital and telecommunication infrastructure throughout the period under investigation, thus being more important empirically (see Table 2). As a result, it is more likely that policymakers will shift their focus from investments in telecommunication infrastructure toward investments in a portfolio of e-services revolving around emerging technologies such as machine learning, artificial intelligence, blockchain and the like. This is evident in the United Nations (2022) report that states: “14.5 percent of countries used AIenabled chatbot functionality in their national portals in 2018, compared to 30.1 percent in 2020 and 35.8 percent in 2022” (United Nations, 2022, p. 148).

Our findings speak to the policymaking process in less affluent developing countries too. For many developing countries, the EGDI is a vital data point—often one of the few available—for analyzing long-term trends and guiding decisions and investments in e-Government development. Our analysis helps governments guide investment decisions and may explain why investments in certain areas, such as telecommunications, have not produced the anticipated advancements in e-Government development. Although the policymaking process is quite unpredictable in both developed and developing countries, it can become an order of magnitude more unpredictable in less affluent developing countries if policymakers decide to focus on investments in telecommunication infrastructure to drive e-Government development considering that such investments are more resource intensive than online services investments.

Since our findings point to the growing importance of human capital investments in realizing sustained e-Government development, we urge policymakers in less affluent developing countries to channel their limited resources toward digital literacy investments, keeping in mind that human capital investments become a non-trivial necessary condition over time (see Appendix A). Furthermore, human capital investments produce synergistic effects when combined with online services that leverage the complementarities between digital skills and emerging technologies. These are bound to outweigh the synergistic effects stemming from investing in telecommunication infrastructure (see Table 2). Therefore, we urge policymakers in less affluent developing countries to focus on both human capital and online services investments to tap into prior investments in (mobile) telecommunication infrastructure and, hopefully, render the ‘catching-up’ process an order of magnitude faster.

Building telecommunication infrastructure is a way to avoid a low level of e-Government development (Dewan et al., 2010) as it removes what was found to be a common enabler of low EGDI throughout the period under investigation (see Table 3). Yet, achieving a high level of e-Government development is not the mirror image of avoiding a low level of e-Government development with reversed signs. For this reason, policymakers in less affluent developing countries should leverage the synergistic effects of online services in combination with human capital investments to achieve high levels of e-Government development and narrow the digital gap with developed countries. Compared with investments in telecommunication infrastructure, online services and human capital investments deliver quicker and more tangible benefits because they create the necessary skills for using these services amongst the citizenry, thus increasing citizen engagement and improving trust in government.

## 8. CONCLUSION

IS scholars have primarily used incremental process theories of change in the study of e-Government development, often ignoring the discontinuities that punctuate periods of stability and the evolutionary nature of e-Government development in response to technological advances. Drawing on the notion of theoretical multiplicity (e.g., pathdependence theory, punctuated equilibrium theory, trajectory-turning point theory, etc.), this study makes a threefold contribution to IS research in general and e-Government research in particular.

First, it demonstrates that e-Government maturity is a complex, multifaceted construct encompassing both temporal (i.e., longitudinal aspects) and causal dimensions (i.e., the drivers of e-Government development) that conjointly influence the outcome of interest (i.e., high levels of e-Government development). In particular, this study emphasizes the growing importance of human capital investments over time. While human capital investments are a trivial necessary condition in the early years of our 2010-2020 timeframe, they become a relevant necessary condition over time, switching from the periphery to the core of the online services configuration. This corroborates the argument that e-Government users need to possess more complex skillsets than general internet users to adopt online services (Zhao et al., 2014a/b). It also overcomes opposite views in the literature regarding the role of human capital (cf. Das et al., 2017; Krishnan et al., 2017).

Second, by zooming in on the performance trajectories of the most typical countries, we argue that there are so many shocks in the maturity process that they almost defy the very notion of phases and stages. We show that the evolutionary trajectories of high EGDI scores often display sudden bursts in the direction or pace of e-Government development. Extant literature argues for e-Government development models that feature linear trajectories based on an incremental process of change (Coursey & Norris, 2008; Poeppelbuss et al., 2011; Renteria et al., 2019). By contrast, our study shows that both developed and developing countries are constantly struggling to keep up with technological change, only to be disrupted by sudden and unexpected 'turning points' that can shift the process downward toward a regressive cycle, or at times, upward toward a progressive cycle of e-Government development.

Third, this study highlights the use of fsQCA with panel data in an abductive fashion to emphasize a diversity-oriented perspective in which cases can switch from one configuration to another over time (Pagliarin & Gerrits, 2020). Though extant research has advocated an inductive use of QCA with panel data (Nishant & Ravishankar, 2020), we deploy an abductive approach informed by the meta-theoretical perspective of socio-technical systems (Park et al., 2020; Sarker et al., 2019). Our study also interweaves multiple fsQCAs for different time periods with formalized case selection in a sequential fashion, thus responding to recent calls for the study of longitudinal configurational changes and their underlying reasons (Park & Mithas, 2020; Sadreddin & Chan, 2023). Far from neglecting the cases at hand, our approach is well-suited to capture those cases that are transitioning from one configuration to another. We submit that understanding configurational patterns over time is essential to understanding trajectories of e-Government development and, therefore, to refining the notion of 'turning point' recently advocated in the literature (Iannacci et al., 2019). Turning points mark non-linear peaks and troughs as a result of exogenous technological changes or geo-political events. They are the hallmark of e-Government development because they call for the development of improvisational capabilities to cope with exogenous shocks.

## 8.1 Limitations and Future Research Directions

This study is not without limitations and there are several opportunities to expand on our work. Future research, for example, could further develop the theoretical model presented in this study (see Figure 1) and build on the growing body of literature that uses QCA for

designing maturity models (Bley et al., 2024; Lasrado et al., 2016). This approach could help distill configurational patterns and identify distinct trajectories from institutional datasets and reports, such as the EGDI. While new maturity models can be developed in academic research, this work could also become a key aspect of engaged scholarship (Van de Ven, 2007), helping to close the theory-practice gap by first expanding and refining existing models available in practice before enveloping them into new theoretical frameworks. Besides, we acknowledge that our current dataset does not include the ‘public value dimension of the provision of online services (Desouza & Dawson, 2023). Future research could extend the dataset used in this paper by adding the ‘public value’ dimension (e.g., the OECD dataset).

In addition, the argument that e-Government development will become an “online services” play calls for longitudinal studies of digital transformation where policymakers “focus on the citizen and deliver more accessible and meaningful information and services to a diverse range of constituents” (Carter et al., 2024, p. 1). While the use of artificial intelligence by governments is likely to take center stage in the delivery of public-facing digital services, we caution policymakers that artificial intelligence may run the risk of introducing systematic unfairness into its outputs if, for instance, algorithms are trained with biased data or users and developers alike feed their unconscious biases into these algorithms (Desouza et al., 2020; Mikalef et al., 2022). Hence, future research should equally emphasize the promises and risks of artificial intelligence (Denford et al., 2023). Future research should also explore optimal areas for government investment in artificial intelligence, particularly considering recent findings indicating minimal economic impacts from such technologies (The Economist, 2024). This is critical as governments are under heightened scrutiny, face significant public pressure, and must uphold accountability, with severe consequences in cases of failure.

Another limitation worth pointing out pertains to the EGDI composition itself. Although the United Nations’ survey is broadly consistent from year to year (Ifinedo, 2012), the composition of the EGDI indicators has changed over time, adding further challenges to our comparative analyses. Furthermore, the EGDI does not capture investment levels directly, but only indirectly, as it requires long-term investment (cf. United Nations, 2014, p. 5; United Nations, 2018, p. 128). Further insights into the United Nations’ measures also serve as a direction for future research. The Human Capital Index is predicated on measures such as adult literacy and educational enrollments. However, in many developing countries and small island developing states, individuals may not encounter computers until they reach university level (MCIL, 2022). This highlights the necessity for alternative methodologies in evaluating the relationship between human capital (and digital skills in particular) and e-Government development. The new sub-component of e-Government literacy in the Human Capital Index may be interpreted as a step in this direction (cf. United Nations, 2024). Nevertheless, it may be a formidable challenge for the least developed countries to achieve adequate levels of digital literacy.

Additionally, the use of standardized cut-off thresholds for consistency (sufficiency), PRI scores, frequency thresholds, and STi (Solution Typical cases thresholds) does not account for gaps in these thresholds, nor does it account for contextual (or temporal) differences between developed and developing countries. Choosing 2010 as our starting point attenuated these limitations to some extent, as it helped us focus on issues of e-Government development rather than e-Government readiness. By narrowing the timespan of analysis to the ten years between 2010-2020, we have accounted for the most recent composition of various indicators, thus ensuring a reasonable level of robustness to our findings. Indeed, the use of more stringent external anchor points coupled with extensions of the model from 3 to 4 or even 6 conditions has added extra layers of robustness to our findings (see Appendix D, E,

and F).

Lastly, the two-year time lag between the EGDI at time t+2 and the EGDI at time t may be an artifact of the United Nations' methodology rather than a reflection of the real amount of time it takes for investments at time t to produce future returns. Indeed, the use of the word 'causality' throughout the paper does not imply the discovery of causal relations, as the configurational patterns we unravel over time are mere instantiations of set-theoretic relations. Undeniably, many geo-political factors during the 2010-2020 period 'pulled' the EGDI in one direction or another. Yet, we said little about these crucial factors, focusing instead on the endogenous drivers of e-Government development and the exogenous technological changes thereof. Future research should explore how global, regional, or local exogenous factors influence e-Government development (e.g., pandemics, conflicts etc.). Additionally, the role of underlying mobile money ecosystems in scaling up e-Government development warrants examination, because they may enable novel payment methods for government services, particularly in the developing world. Despite these limitations, this paper is a genuine attempt to switch the focus of configurational theorizing from static approaches toward evolutionary trajectories of development and change. We hope this paper will stimulate a desire in IS scholars to pursue a more dynamic use of configurational theorizing in general and fsQCA techniques in particular.

The EGDI is the weighted average of the normalized scores of three dimensions of e-Government: the scope and quality of online services (OSI); the status of the development of the telecommunication infrastructure (TII); and the development of human capital (HCI):

$$
E G D I = \frac {1}{3} (O S I _ {\text {normalized}} + T I I _ {\text {normalized}} + H C I _ {\text {normalized}})
$$

Nevertheless, the use of configurational methods in general and fsQCA methods in particular is warranted because it overcomes the net-effect thinking of conventional regression methods by shifting its focus from variables to cases in a situation of “multicollinearity” (Cf. Ragin, 2000; p. 289- 290). Not only are causal and outcome conditions strongly correlated, but also the presence of EGDI at time t is either necessary and/or sufficient for the presence of EGDI at time t+2, thus corroborating the argument for path dependence (cf. Bettinazzi et al., 2023). Hence, the configurational patterns emerging over time are driven by self-reinforcing processes, stemming from the fact that investments in human capital combined with investments in

online services and/or investments in telecommunication infrastructure mutually increase their benefits (Sydow et al., 2009; Sydow et al., 2020). Since the synergistic effects resulting from combining human capital investments with online services investments are stronger than the complementary effects associated with investments in telecommunication infrastructure and human capital, the former combination crowds out the latter, thus producing a lock-in effect over time.
