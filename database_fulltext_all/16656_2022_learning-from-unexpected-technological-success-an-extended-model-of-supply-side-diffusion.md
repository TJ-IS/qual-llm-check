# Learning from Unexpected Technological Success: An Extended Model of Supply-Side Diffusion

## Abstract

Researchers have studied the diffusion of information technology (IT) mainly as a process of adoption. However, the practices of supply-side agents of diffusion are just as critical to guaranteeing the adaptation of technology to the changing needs of the adopters. The execution of these practices over time and across multiple supply-side agents is both complex and understudied. Therefore, this study investigates IT diffusion practices from the perspective of supply-side agents. Drawing from a longitudinal case of development, sale, and institutionalization of a healthcare information system, this article proposes a model of the supply side of technology diffusion. The model shows that diffusion depends on supply-side agents engaging in practices that are both demanding and possibly misaligned with traditional agent practices. Accordingly, the study provides an additional explanation for the complexity of technological diffusion and extends the theoretical framework of technology diffusion by adding supply-side dynamics.

Keywords: IT diffusion, supply-side agents, long-term care, healthcare

## Introduction

Diffusion of technology is a classic area of focus for information system (IS) research. This process depends on the decisions and actions of two main categories of agents: those interested in diffusing technology and those interested in adopting it. The former group pursues strategies and tactics to push new information technology (IT) to diffuse faster (Benlian & Hess, 2011), whereas the latter group is interested in ensuring the technology fits its own strategic needs, practices, resources, and organisational designs (Goodhue & Thompson, 1995). Although these two groups share an interest in technology, and though the processes of diffusion and adoption are connected (Newell et al., 2000), the groups also are driven by very different dynamics (Jha & Bose, 2016; Lyytinen & Damsgaard, 2011; Mustonen-Ollila & Lyytinen, 2003; Sharma & Rai, 2015). Diffusion is a function of the adoption process: If technologies satisfy the needs of adopting innovators, early adopters, and the early majority, relatively smooth diffusion patterns emerge, with steady growth in the numbers of adopters (Rogers, 2003). But despite the availability of a well-grounded theory of the adoption side of diffusion, attention to the supply side is lacking. Supply-side agents clearly push the diffusion of technology (Moore & McKenna, 1999; Scarbrough et al., 2015), yet research on such efforts is recent and, to the best of our knowledge, limited (e.g., Nielsen et al., 2014; Öbrand et al., 2019). Related research on the diffusion of managerial innovations (Greenwood et al., 2002; Tolbert & Zucker, 1983) cites the importance of finely tuned sequences of practices and activities to promote the diffusion of innovations, regardless of the associated problems (Scarbrough et al., 2015). In a few cases, agents and practices can be orchestrated within the same organisations (e.g., Öbrand et al., 2019), but most cases involve multiple organisations, and they all demand sustained engagement over time. However, the execution of these practices across multiple

supply-side agents—whose goals and interests may differ—is a formidable challenge, especially considering the non-linear direction of innovation. To ensure the success of supply-side agents, it is fundamental to understand the nature and dynamics of their engagement practices, just as it is fundamental to understand adoption practices to ensure the success of technology-adopting organisations.

Therefore, this study seeks to contribute to understanding the theory of IT diffusion by investigating sustained practices and dynamics of diffusion in complex scenarios that involve multiple supply-side agents, driven by a central research question:

How does a technology diffuse through the sustained engagement of supply-side agents? To answer this question, we conduct a qualitative study of the development and diffusion of an IT system that supports clinical activities in long-term care (LTC) facilities. The timeline of our case spans more than a decade, and the data encompass the evolution of this system, from its development to its widespread commercial success. We use diffusion dynamics to identify critical, shifting practices of various supply-side agents, each of whom becomes involved in the collective process. We also specify a set of demanding activities that each supply-side agent must undertake, beyond normal operations, to push the diffusion of technology. The activities of the diffusion agents often extend beyond their missions and are not limited to the techno-economic considerations identified in prior literature. Rather, we show that even for adopting agents, diffusion dynamics depend on the key actions and decisions of supply-side agents.

In the next section, we examine current understanding of the role of supply-side agents of diffusion and argue for the need to understand how these agents co-create diffusion processes (in contrast to playing individual roles). We then present the research design, describe the case and methods, and analyse the roles of various supply-side agents in the diffusion of IS to manage nursing homes. Finally, we present a model that extends the role of supply-side agents in the

diffusion process and specifies their ancillary activities in trying to diffuse technologies.

## Theoretical Background

Research on the diffusion of IT has focused mostly on explaining the decisions and practices of those that adopt it. Substantial research investigates enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems (Rajagopal, 2002), electronic patient records (Greenhalgh et.al., 2008), customer relationship management (CRM) systems (Da Cunha, 2013), and e-commerce systems (Al-Jabri & Sohail, 2012). In contrast, the supply-side of technology innovation has received less attention, pertaining mainly to diffusion or institutionalization of practices (Ansari et al., 2010). Broader research on diffusion highlights the importance of agents in pushing the diffusion of technology and practices (Greenwood et al., 2002). Tolbert and Zucker (1983) show that though certain practices are likely to diffuse, pushes from legitimate authorities accelerate and justify the diffusion. Scarbrough et al. (2015) indicate a similar role for professional service organisations in pushing the diffusion of practices adopted by their members.

Various types of supply-side agents (Moore & McKenna, 1999) must adjust their practices in relation to the experiences of adopting organisations though (Scarbrough et al., 2015). Figure 1 summarizes the individual roles of each type of supply-side agent in the diffusion process. Some agents develop innovations for their own internal needs (Carugati et al., 2011), whereas others have commercial interests in promoting and diffusing innovations (Öbrand et al., 2019) or exert their power to force innovations on third parties (Greenwood et al., 2002). For technology diffusion to occur, each of these types of agents must engage in various practices to promote innovations, according to emerging and localized needs (Nielsen et al., 2014).

## Organisations that Develop Technology

Organisations that develop technology for internal use typically are motivated by a sense of unease and frustration with the status quo (Carugati et al., 2011). These motivations tend to be operational, and their innovations are designed to improve their internal processes (Badampudi et al., 2016) or meet market needs (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 2001). If other organisations, in the same or other fields, become aware of the benefits reaped by organisations that developed the innovation, they may attempt to adopt the innovations themselves to replicate those benefits. Thus, the extent to which technology diffuses is moderated by the positions of their original developers among their peers, because such positioning defines their informal power and visibility to others (Mezias & Glynn, 1993).

## Organisations that Sell Technology

In contrast with to organisations that develop technology for internal use, some organisations appropriate technology to commercialize it. Typically, they are motivated by financial objectives (Lepistö, 2015). They increase their performance by providing solutions to other organisations, often in an attempt to address the special needs of early adopters by codeveloping tailor-made solutions with them (Moore & McKenna, 1999). Compared with later adopters, early adopters are more interested in impactful innovations that give them strategic advantages, despite the expense of the extraordinary efforts needed to make them work. Technology sellers must work closely with this category of adopters to co-create. Selling organisations also can aim their efforts at the early majority of customers who are interested in incremental improvements and easy-to-use technologies (Moore & McKenna, 1999). Therefore, technology sellers must adjust their products and commercial practices to different types of adopters, who have distinct performance interests.

## Organisations that Set Standards for Technology Use

Agents have a stake in the technologies and administrative practices adopted by the organisations under their control. They also have sufficient power to enforce self-serving practices on these organisations (Smith et al., 2010). For example, government bodies, standardsetting organisations, regulators, industry associations, and unions possess formal power, leveraged through the rule of law or contracts, to impose certain practices (Mezias & Glynn, 1993). Typically, organisations that set technology standards are motivated by the advantages provided by the uniform adoption of certain practices and tools. For example, private organisations might seek to standardize practices across branches (Markus et al., 2000), or public organisations might try to standardize practices across offices (Moon, 2005). Although the organisations’ motivations may be rooted in performance improvements, they also can transcend such motivations to become “the legitimate way we do things here” (Di Maggio & Powell, 1983).

Supply-side agents also might broaden their engagements to include neighbouring diffusion processes. Standard-setting organisations scout the environment for emerging technologies that support their missions, then push those technologies to the organisations they influence (Scarbrough et al., 2015). Such behaviour indicates that, if the organisations that develop and sell technology can demonstrate the potential of their technology successfully to standard-setting organisations, they offload the burden of legitimisation to supply-side agents, which already have the formal power to enforce technology and practices on other organisations and monitor the extent to which they comply. However, these dynamics among supply-side agents remain opaque; even if the broader research stream on the adoption of innovations is rich, it cannot be applied directly to understanding the role of supply-side agents in the diffusion of IT, because it takes a monolithic view of suppliers, with the assumption that one type of supplier diffuses one type of innovation. Figure 1: Three Processes of Diffusion and their Corresponding Supply-Side Agents

Current research has not extended this model to diffusion processes that involve more than one type of supply-side agent. However, diffusion of technology differs from diffusion of managerial practices and other types of innovations, because to expand across a field of organisations, technology must be “handed over” from one type of supply-side agent to another (Nielsen et al., 2014). The handover is not a simple process; the next supply-side agents in line must remould the technologies to meet the needs and goals of the next groups of adopters (Moore & McKenna, 1999). Therefore, we lack specification of the differing roles of supply-side agents in the diffusion of same technology.

Research on digital innovations, such as management IS and other IT, argues that it is both necessary and valuable to explore the role of supply-side agents in the innovation process. Digital innovations offer the possibility of expanding and reconceptualizing the process of innovation, because they have "radically changed the nature and structure of new products and services, spawned novel value creation and value appropriation pathways, enabled innovation collectives that involve dynamic sets of actors with diverse goals and capabilities, produced a new breed of innovation processes, and, more broadly, transformed entire industries in its wake" (Nambisan et al., 2012, p. 223). Specifically, the possibilities for appropriation and reconfiguration offered by digital innovations support further specification of the diffusion process, as well as bridging the gap between linear models of innovation and models of innovation "at the interstices [between organisations and institutions], through the operation of networks, that distributed knowledge can be brought together and integrated into new products, processes and services" (Swan & Scarbrough, 2005, p. 913). Therefore, we use long-term qualitative data from the diffusion of a nursing home IS to explain the supplementary practices of supply-side agents when they are part of the diffusion of the same technology across time and place.

## Research Methods

To produce a conceptual description of how various supply-side agents pursue diffusion of a specific IT innovation, we conducted a longitudinal field-level study of the diffusion of an innovative IT system that supports clinical activities in LTC facilities in Italy. This sector notoriously lags in its technology use, such that, for example, key processes were completely paper-based when this study started in 1999. Therefore, this sector represents a suitable setting for our case study, from which we can theorize about initial diffusion phases (Orlikowski, 1993, 1996; Benbasat et al., 1987). To avoid the impulse of quick results and to persist with our observations, we collected data over multiple periods, without adopting any particular theoretical lens until the data were adequately clear (Glaser and Strauss, 1978, 2017).

In the following sections, we describe the IT system that we studied, because such information is key to understanding the diffusion process. We also describe the data collection and analysis procedure. Appendix A presents more detailed information about LTC setting.

The Research Context: Development and Adoption of the Health Care System PAI-SYS

The health care IS (pseudonym PAI-SYS) initially was developed internally in a nursing home (pseudonym Nursing Home Foundation [NHF]) but has been adopted by more than 100 other nursing homes since 2004. The NHF is a non-profit, government-accredited nursing home located in a village in northern Italy. Founded in 1982 by an initiative of the local priest, the NHF’s institutional aims are to assist elderly people, support cultural and social activities, promote the well-being of patients (whom it refers to as guests), and improve the living conditions of disadvantaged individuals, with respect for the surrounding nature and territory. The NHF uses PAI-SYS to support all its operational and informational needs. In turn, it has been able to reduce its operational costs while improving services to its guests. By investing the savings in human resources, it has improved the ratio of operators to guests to 1.5, higher than both the regional average and the legal ratio required by the local health authority.

In 2004, the NHF passed PAI-SYS to a software house, Sys-House, that commercialized the software among nursing homes in Italy. From 2004 to 2007, the sales and diffusion of PAI-SYS was slow but steady (see Figure 2). In 2007, the regional health authority, ASL, mandated that all nursing homes in the province should adopt PAI-SYS as standard software. Because of the authority’s mandate, the number of implementations of the software increased to over 70 installations by 2010, when all nursing homes in the province had implemented the software. After 2010, sales growth slowed, but the number of total installations exceeded 100 (Figure 2). Figure 3 depicts the timeline of the main events in the diffusion of PAI-SYS.

## Functions and Transformations Promised by PAI-SYS

The PAI-SYS technology innovation revolves around the life of the guest and produces descriptive statistics and behavioural indexes. As Figure 4 shows, the underlying data and information span six main categories: personal data, history of personal care, level of autonomy, updated medical records, well-being, and current individual plan of assistance. Because PAI-SYS largely has succeeded in the LTC sector, we consider the transformations that PAI-SYS promised to its adopters and describe the functionalities it provided. Today, PAI-SYS relies on a modular logic, such that its implementation in various contexts proceeds step-by-step, according to the needs of the organisation and the rapidity with which operators learn how it functions.

The system mainly collects data and information from the bottom up, fed by operators (nurses, nurse-aides, social animators, physiotherapists, doctors, and support staff) according to their daily contacts with guests. It also allows operators to interpret events and solve problems, select information efficiently, and systematize data for multiple purposes.

## Data Collection

For this longitudinal study, we collected data over six years, from October 2008 to June 2014. However, we also reconstructed a period dating to 1999, when the development of PAI-SYS began. Formal discussions with the NHF started in August 2008, when we asked for permission to investigate the creation and development of the health care system. The data collection followed the “story” of PAI-SYS (see Figure 3). As PAI-SYS moved from the NHF to the vendor, to the first clients, to the ASL’s decision, and finally to the mandated implementations, we interviewed the new actors as they appeared. Altogether, we conducted 116 interviews of informants from the organisation that developed the system, the software house that commercialized the systems, the authority that mandated the system, 14 of the nursing homes that implemented it, and two nursing homes that did not adopt PAI-SYS. Several environmental constraints limited the research, including an earthquake in northern Italy in 2011 that delayed or suspended some aspects of the diffusion process and the empirical investigation. ## Data Analysis

We analysed the data using a grounded theory method. Although we employed multiple data sources (internal documents, field observations, website content analysis, media documentation), our study centres on “the semi-structured interview—to obtain both retrospective and real-time accounts by those people experiencing the phenomenon of theoretical interest” (Gioia et al., 2013, p. 19).

We used the qualitative data analysis software ATLAS.ti to support the data-management and coding processes. However, during theory development, we did not use software automation (i.e. auto-coding) or a set of pre-established codes; rather, we relied on collegial discussions and collective analysis. Because exploratory research is never linear, none of the four phases, the activities of the supply-side agents, nor the appropriateness of diffusion theory emerged early in research. Rather, sensemaking was a team effort that involved false starts and backtracking before we found our way, such that “A key enabler of sensemaking for this study was a technique of focused theoretical discussion that we adopted and then applied throughout the study” (Carugati et al. 2018, p18).

We began the coding process with open coding of the interviews and other data sources, generating 127 first-order codes. We then reviewed the codes to aggregate them into 15 secondorder codes. This operation revealed that the main trigger of the development and diffusion of PAY-SYS was the need to cope with the emerging complexity of the environment while preserving the care of the guests. We conceptualized the second-order codes into aggregate dimensions, which resulted in four diffusion phases.

For the analysis, we also sought insight into both incidents that were occurring and how the incidents were resolved to become the engine for development and diffusion of the system. With input from NHF managers and employees, we started by coding the interviews over the case timeline. We began with the managerial setting, because the NHF’s management was responsible for initiating the development of PAI-SYS. We coded many statements about internal and external needs for a managerial approach, need for inclusiveness in developing the system, and lack of IT literacy in the nursing homes, along with pride in what was being achieved. Then a second round of coding focused on the how the system became known in the industry. This coding phase allowed us to corroborate the four diffusion phases that we found in the diffusion of PAY-SYS through the practices of the NHF, Sys-House, the local health authority, and the nursing homes (see Figure 5a). The third and final round of coding focused on the transitions

across the four phases. In particular, it helped us identify new practices in which supply-side agents engaged to facilitate the handover to the following agent of diffusion (see Figure 5b).

Case Analysis: Supply Side of Diffusion

Our case analysis reveals the different phases of the process of diffusion of PAI-SYS and the practices in which various supply-side agents engaged as the diffusion process proceeded. The Beginning: Finding an IT Solution

The development of PAI-SYS started with the need—identified by a newly appointed general manager (GM)—to collect usable data to manage the NHF nursing home. At the end of the 1990s, the NHF was suffering from its use of ad hoc processes; at the same time, the conditions of guests entering the facility were worsening, such that they required more professional and timely attention:

When I joined NHF, all the documentation was on paper…. It was just piled in a cardboard box and could not be used to manage anything. That, combined with the fact that we had multiple guests with very complex health conditions, made the situation very hard. (NHF, GM)

The GM, short of resources and faced with personnel who lacked computer skills, entered into an agreement with a retired computer engineer who volunteered to help with an organisational experiment to gather usable data for decision making:

The software PAI-SYS was born simply as an answer to the internal need for change and

foremost to monitor our processes so that we could evaluate the quality of our service.

Furthermore, we can link the processes to the administrative costs and monitor this

connection as well. PAI-SYS has therefore an economic function but also an

organisational function. (NHF GM)

Rather than acquiring software from the market, the GM decided to pursue a gradual development process. The developer agreed to create a system that could provide information and would be easy to use. Because NHF personnel were relatively computer illiterate, simplicity was

paramount.

The initial phases reflected the deep knowledge and physical proximity of the developer, general manager, nurses, operators, and doctors. In the initial phase, the development involved close collaboration between the developer and NHF personnel, who interacted often with the developer to add elements to PAI-SYS gradually, according to emerging needs. Thus, later transitions started from this first stage, with its underlying notions that the software should emerge from a specific problem and its development should be rooted in daily practice and the demands of the internal community. These elements remained important later in the process. Diffusion Process 1 (Potential Diffusion): Pride in Innovation

As the software became operational, the GM realized its value and began to promote it, as a special feature of the NHF. The organisation featured PAI-SYS on its website, touting it as an instrument of improved care, and presented it at professional LTC fairs, as part of its toolbox for improving services; it received wide appreciation and prizes:

The GM takes us around the buildings. He is talking about how proud they are of PAI-SYS. He shows us a brochure for the nursing home where PAI-SYS features prominently as part of their services.... He shows us the website of another nursing home user of PAI-SYS that also features the system as part of their services.… He says: "back then we would never have imagined that we would come this far. Now we are a professional reality at the top of our game.” (Observation Notes, 2008 visit)

As rumours about PAI-SYS spread, through the NHF website, fairs, and presentations, the GM began to receive inquiries from other nursing homes, asking whether they could install it. Evidence of this diffusion process comes from various case participants:

Considering the enthusiasm with which he [NHF GM] was describing the way PAY-SYS improved the quality of their processes, we asked if there was the chance for sharing the software. And we were not the only one…. (NH2 GM)

We were asked to show how PAI-SYS was working. Many nursing home coordinators came to NHF to see how the system worked. They also asked how to implement the system in their structures. Unfortunately, we couldn’t answer “yes, sure,” as PAI-SYS at that time was an in-house solution developed with MS Access. (NHF GM)

Transition 1: From Craft to Professional Agent

When the NHF realized that providing the software to other organisations was beyond its capacity, it decided to contact a software house that might license the software and help others carry out future development and sales, in exchange for free upgrades:

On the one hand, the role of the NHF is to provide services but, on the other hand, our

mission is also to promote the quality of such services. For this reason, we decided to

look for an organisation able to develop PAI-SYS in a more professional way. (NHF GM)

The partnership also made sense for the software house (Sys-House) that could begin to engineer, sell, and support NHF on the market. Sys-House linked its decision to the specific nature of the IT development:

This sector has been interesting for us for the social role that our solution can play....

This was a product born to support a real need, from the very people that use it. It is easy

to use. It is intuitive. This mix convinced us to invest in this project. (Owner, Sys-House)

Because the initial software was built for a single organisation, using technologies that were available to that user, one of the first steps required of Sys-House was to perform a technological overhaul and bring PAI-SYS to a professional level of performance:

We needed someone who could commercialize the system on our behalf. At the same time, we could not lose control over the development of future features (NHF GM).

From a more technical point of view,

There was the need for an improvement of the product and its innovativeness. The software was based on MS Access, and we changed that to a better-performing database technology. This was necessary to match the dimensions of other nursing homes in the market [some much larger than NHF] and the mindset of the people working there. (Owner, Sys-House)

Beyond technological improvements to the operational elements of the software, Sys-House also considered connectivity features for healthcare authorities. At this point, such connectivity was not required, but because health authorities were beginning to open their systems to external data flows, it was an appropriate feature to add to the PAI-SYS system:

We have created the necessary evaluation schemes [for the local health authority] and

the connection to the central information systems. The product has never given us

problems in terms of geographical diversity, and it was elastic enough to be used across

the territory. (Sys-House, PAI-SYS Product Manager)

Diffusion 2: Commercialization by Professional Agent

Sys-House also set up a marketing strategy and a commercial team to sell its new product. At the beginning, sales were slow, but then clients began to arrive:

Health care systems and nursing homes weren’t our traditional markets. So, we had to invent our sales strategy from scratch. We started contacting those nursing homes that

showed an interest in PAI-SYS with NHF. Then, we started a partnership with a reseller of diapers and other hygienic solutions for the elderly. (Sys-House Sales Manager) Some clients also attributed their purchases to changes in their own organisations:

We knew the PAI-SYS system since the time when NHF began to show it at the fairs. We were going through a modernisation and expansion phase, so we wanted technological support to help us in this transition. (Early Client Nursing Home NH1 GM)

However, sales in this initial period remained challenging:

Every time we showed PAI-SYS, the appreciation was high. Then budget, people’s IT literacy, and lack of time were constraints that often delayed the choice. (Sys-House Sales Manager)

The conditions of use that had prompted the PAI-SYS system in the first diffusion stage were no longer the same. At the NHF, development and use had gone together for years, but in the new structures, implementation happened in a “big bang” fashion:

We like the system. It is useful and helps a lot. Unfortunately, the little budget for the training and the little time allocated did not help us in becoming familiar with the system. Even if it is clear that PAI-SYS has been made by someone who knows the business … we must say that every nursing home has its own processes … and sometimes you need a bit of time to understand how PAI-SYS works. (Nurse, NH8)

To compensate for this lack of experience, nursing homes used various methods that took advantage of PAI-SYS’s flexibility. Accordingly, even less experienced organisations could attain good results by activating just a few functions.

Transition 2: From Diffusion by Commercialization to Diffusion by Imposition of Institutional Authority

As more nursing homes began to purchase PAI-SYS, the regional health authority also perceived the advantages of automatic data transfer:

In 2005, we realized that we had to make a quality jump and push on the informatization

of the nursing homes of the province. We started a little project analysing the market of

software specialized in nursing home processes (at that time very few). PAI-SYS seemed

the one that better fit our needs. (Health Care Service manager, Local Health Authority)

This recognition reflected an outcome of the effort, first by the NHF and later by Sys-House, to

For example, the local health authority has the need to receive data in a specific way....

This gave us the challenge to create some interface modules, but that in fact later became

a standard, and the product can be potentially interfaced with the authorities in the entire

Italian territory. (Sys-House Product Manager)

The local health authority noted two advantages of using IT for these data exchanges. First, it usually sent controllers to the various nursing home facilities, to manually collect and transcribe the paper-based data required to validate the procedures tied to financial support. With the introduction of the electronic data exchange, data could be exchanged regularly and with minimal human intervention or delocalization. Second, the health authority could collect data in a standard format from many structures and therefore perform analyses, comparisons, and performance reviews that previously had not been possible. In turn, noting the increasing but still-slow adoption of PAI-SYS, the local health authority decided to mandate its use by nursing homes throughout the territory:

Today, the relationship that the health authority has with the nursing homes is becoming more and more one of control. We remain in control of the medical part, the doctors and the pharmaceutical assistance. But all the rest is being verified by control systems of varying degree of complexity and sophistication (Health Care Service Manager, Local Health Authority).

## Diffusion 3: Diffusion by Imposition from Institutional Authority

In its effort to increase the efficiency of the nursing home sector, which was lagging in its technology use because of budget constraints, the local health authority established an agreement with a local bank that would finance the costs without any cost to the adopting organisations:

We started the project before the 2008 crisis. At that time, the local bank was still able to provide significant resources to projects in the social sector. We got €800,000, which was enough to cover the cost of licenses and a bit of training for the 60 nursing homes of the province. (Data Analyst, Local Health Authority).

However, the regional health authority recognized that imposing too much standardization might hinder widespread implementation, so it also allowed different structures to collect data in slightly different ways:

One of our main objectives is to compare the indicators. However, the main problem is

that the structures do not use the software in a standard way. They use it as it best fits

their activities. The result is that there is a mandatory part where controls are very

formalized, and they must comply. For other modules, we have given a lot of freedom for

how to customize the collected data. (Data Analyst, Local Health Authority)

For the nursing homes, the local health authority’s decision was game-changing; many of them had heard about the PAI-SYS or similar systems, but the investment had never reached a sufficiently high level of priority:

We had been presented this system already in 2001, and we saw the potential of such technology, but we are a public structure, and we were not ready; we had other priorities. (NH12 GM)

When the local heath authority mandated the use of PAI-SYS, the nursing homes changed their approach. As a result, when we asked informants in all organisations whether they would return to previous methods, they replied they depended on the system and would never go back:

PAI-SYS? It is like the air we breathe! At first, we were a bit afraid, but now we could

never go back. (Nurse, NH09)

Still, not everyone experienced PAI-SYS as easy, such that its implementation led to concerns:

Not all the employees agree on the fact that the system is useful; the nurses, for example,

still struggle to understand why they have to share the information with other operators.

The doctors refuse to use it as they think it is a waste of time, as they have to meet all the

guests in only a few hours. (Coordinator, NH10)

Transition 3: From Diffusion by Imposition to Diffusion by Commercialization

By 2011, the number of system installations began to taper off, because almost all nursing homes in the province had implemented it. Thus, Sys-House was able to start redirecting its commercial efforts to the free market in other geographic areas. This market was strongly influenced by evidence of the positive results of the PAI-SYS project within the province, such that other parties’ decisions were strongly influenced by the reputation PAI-SYS had gained in this sector. Even without an institutional mandate, selling PAI-SYS became less of a challenge:

In 2010, the board of directors approved the project to implement an IT-based system for managing data related to patient management. We knew some nursing homes that were using PAI-SYS, so we started contacting Sys-House to see if PAI-SYS fit our needs.

(Coordinator, NH13)

The experience that Sys-House accumulated from the mandated implementations had two direct consequences. First, it allowed the software house to generate a critical mass of references: NHF push towards innovation and the experience acquired through customers, both these two factors helped to bring fundamental elements of improvement for the implementation of the software (Sys-House Owner/Chairman).

Second, it provided the time and resources that Sys-House needed to develop a marketing strategy to appeal to other clients. The first effect was shared among managers and medical personnel:

We had remained in a waiting position to see what would come out of the PAI-SYS experiment. What we saw was that the general feedback was very positive and therefore we, very recently, decided to invest in it. (NH14 GM)

Other features of this later transition stage stemmed from the reputation gained through the positive results of the project. That is, PAI-SYS began to diffuse in other regions. Although health authorities in those provinces had not mandated any system, PAI-SYS’s flexibility in interfacing with central systems encouraged its adoption:

The product itself has never given problems so we never caught territorial differences that made it clear that the product was created only for a context because it is an extremely elastic product. (Sys-House Owner/Chairman).

Diffusion 4: Diffusion by Commercialization Reinforced by Mimetic Isomorphism

The experience gained from the mandate of the local health authority helped Sys-House standardize some software features and develop additional functionalities (e.g., tablet-based interfaces, visualization of guest data) that reflected the needs and requests that emerged during the implementation projects. At this point, the performance of PAI-SYS ultimately became less relevant, because it had achieved legitimisation through its many installations and positive reputation:

With PAY-SYS, we are now in a “normal situation.” I mean that now the process is a

classic selling process. We scan the potential clients, we contact them, and we negotiate.

Sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn’t. However, they know us before we contact them

… thanks to the project we run with the local health authority. (Sales Manager, Sys-

House)

A side effect of the diffusion of PAI-SYS was its contribution to increasing the general level of attention to IT in the LTC sector. Other vendors entered the market, and many more nursing homes decided voluntarily to invest in IT. One nursing home that did not buy PAI-SYS indicated:

We had heard so much about PAI-SYS that we decided to look into different solutions. We

decided for another vendor that we thought would better match our needs, but we would

never had gone down this road without the PAI-SYS example. (NH15 GM)

## Extending the Role of Supply-Side Agents in the Diffusion of Technology

How does a technology diffuse through the sustained engagement of supply-side agents? Our research points to a two-tiered model of the role of supply-side agents in the diffusion of technology, which can be represented by two concentric circles (see Figure 7). The innermost circle contains the core process of diffusion, which consists of exhibiting, selling, or legitimising a technology. In the previous theory section, we explained each of these processes by summarising literature that examines how each diffusion process works when it is led by only one type of supply-side agent. When different types of supply-side agents participate in the diffusion of a technology, these three processes need ancillary practices to ensure the technology actually diffuses. The practices improve the diffusion process and allow various types of supplyside agents not only to participate in the same diffusion process but also to hand off leading roles in diffusion to one another. practices; the arrows represent the passing of the leadership of the diffusion process from the organisation that develops the technology to the organisation that sells the technology (for readability, we do not map other possible transitions, but they follow a similar path).

## Adapting Technology

The process of adapting technology consists of adding or altering the technology’s functionalities. Authors have studied each of the three processes of diffusion separately (Parmentola et al., 2018) but have not examined the possibility of, and need for, changes in technology in cases in which the process of diffusion changes. Our research shows the same technology can be diffused by different types of supply-side agents at different points of its life cycle. When a technology moves from being diffused by one type of supply-side agent to being diffused by another type, it must incorporate new properties that allow it to be diffused through a different process. Accordingly, innovations and changes to technologies are not the exclusive domain of the organisations that develop those technologies; instead, they are practices intrinsic to diffusion that may be carried out by any type of supply-side agent in the diffusion process. Sometimes, rather than being linear, the innovation process is complex and iterative. In the current study, each of the organisations and institutions involved in the diffusion of PAI-SYS changed it, adding some new features and removing others. The adoption of PAI-SYS was an iterative cycle of innovation and diffusion, in which changes occurred even after it was broadly adopted and the organisation that created it was no longer its active developer.

This analysis shows that organisations that sell technology may need to transform it from product developed to support a specific organisation into a product that can be used in organizations which may have different configurations of routines, procedures, strategic requirements and leadership processes. Therefore, organisations that first develop the technology can alleviate some of the work necessary to transform it into a marketable product. However, organisations that sell that technology provide most of the resources to carry out this work; they must assess the field, reduce costs, minimize implementation efforts, and ensure the technology supports a compelling frame that will help selling it.

Standard-setting institutions also may need to develop a technology to achieve institutional goals. In some cases, they simply want to standardize practices across fields, such that technology requires little or no change. However, standard-setting institutions also may enforce technologies for purposes that require new functionalities and affordances. Our analysis shows that standard-setting institutions can regard operations-improving technologies as resources for gathering data about their operations and their fields as a whole.

Overall, the PAI-SYS story highlights the dynamics specific to digital innovation (see Nambisan et al., 2017). Management IS and other digital innovations can be transformed throughout the diffusion process, and it is this potential for transformation that ensures that multiple types of actors can enact different diffusion processes using the same IT.

The process of empowering adoption consists of taking an active role in increasing the drive to adopt a technology beyond that provided by each of the diffusion processes in the innermost circle of the model; it is the assurance that others see the merit or necessity of the technology (Rajagopal, 2002). This research shows that this process may entail more than having companies that sell technologies develop a rhetoric (and eventually, a sales and marketing strategy for accelerating and broadening adoption of the technology) and more than having standard-setting institutions enforce specific technologies. Organisations that develop technologies for their own use can boost the adoption of the technologies by exhibiting the technologies to peers in their industries. These organisations can use the technologies they develop to improve visibility in their fields and thereby access more resources via increased recognition. The manager of the NHF attributed the initial demand for PAI-SYS to its presentation in industry events and its showcasing on the nursing home’s website. This explanation suggests the possibility that organisations take an active role in the diffusion of technologies they develop for their own use. By doing so, the organisations attract the attention of other organisations that are seeking resources to address similar challenges. Although organisations may be motivated by the need to accrue additional resources, they also may be attempting to diffuse their technology to advance their fields as a whole; in the case analysed, such motivation was acknowledged by the leadership of the nursing home.

Our research also shows that standard-setting institutions can boost diffusion by improving their power over organisations in their fields. Standard-setting institutions earn their power from government bodies or from joint agreement among organisations in their fields. However, this power is contingent on how each of the organisations in a field appropriates its relationship with standard-setting institutions. Some organisations may be willing to comply with such institutions, but others resist them. Moreover, standard-setting institutions, such as the regional health authority in our study, do not need to accept these variations of compliance tacitly. Instead, they can work on their relationships with organisations in the field to improve their power to enforce technologies that suit their own goals. This iterative view of the innovation process can and should be extended to the diffusion process (Swan & Scarbrough, 2005). In the present study, the diffusion of PAI-SYS was not the result of various actors sequentially enacting the diffusion process; rather, it was an iteration of diffusion processes.

Facilitating Adoption of Technology

The process of facilitating adoption consists of implementing the ancillary but necessary conditions for organisations to buy and implement new technologies. Research on diffusion of technology has focused on the motivations of organisations to adopt technologies (Greenhalgh et al., 2004). Although this study also emphasises the key role of motivation, it adds the practices that supply-side agents can draw on to improve their organisations’ willingness to adopt new technologies; supply-side agents can improve diffusion by ensuring that organisations are able to adopt new technologies.

Organisations that develop technologies for their own use can boost the adoption of the technologies by supporting other adopters. We highlight the difficulties of transposing technologies to other organisations’ technologies that were developed to address challenges in one organisation; even in the same industry, considerable adaptation may be required due to

differences in internal processes and routines. Such adaptation may be difficult, because the development process acquaints organisations with technology functions and affordances. Organisations that simply adopt technologies that were developed elsewhere will not have had the opportunities to develop such knowledge and therefore may require the help of the original technology-developing organisations when they seek to implement the technologies. Informants from the NHF reported spending a lot of time and resources providing such support. The GM even explained that it was the amount of effort they had to put into supporting other users that motivated them to find a partner to sell PAI-SYS. The flexibility afforded by IT, as highlighted in prior research on digital innovation, is not only an occasion to improve technology but also a requirement for diffusing technology. Accordingly, digital innovations bring new opportunities to the diffusion process and pose new challenges and burdens that organisations must address and bear.

Our research also shows that standard-setting institutions can boost diffusion by offering resources to organisations to help them adopt mandated technology. Whereas research on diffusion has focused on organisations’ willingness to adopt, we study show organisations may lack the resources needed to do so. Standard-setting institutions can improve diffusion not only by creating motivation but also by creating the conditions organisations require to adopt mandated technologies. The regional health authority negotiated loans with a bank to ensure that nursing homes had the money to adopt PAI-SYS.

Handing Over the Diffusion Process

By studying technology diffusion processes together, we can improve understanding of the transition of one diffusion process to another. We show that one type of supply-side agent may actively hand off the process of diffusion to another type of supply-side agent to improve technology adoption, in and beyond fields. Organisations that develop technologies for their own use can boost their adoption by transferring them to organisations that will take over their diffusion. In the present case, the technology was transferred to an organisation that sold the technology for profit. Although technologies also can be transferred to standard-setting institutions (Greenwood et al., 2002), such as a regional authority, research on institutional change and institutional entrepreneurship highlights the difficulties of persuading standard setting organisations to impose technologies that have not yet reached critical mass (Brunsson et al. 2012). This role is not limited to selling technologies to third parties; it also implies working to find third parties that will advance organisations’ original goals for technologies at various stages of development. The manager of the NHF emphasised his attempts to find a company that would match his own values when PAI-SYS was sold. An additional finding is that transferring technologies to third parties is a burdensome achievement, because originating organisations must make (or participate in making) the technologies configurable and developable such that they can be implemented easily by other organisations in the field.

Organisations that sell technologies can transfer them to standard-setting institutions to accelerate their diffusion. They can achieve the critical mass necessary for standard-setting institutions to recognise the benefits of enforcing the adoption of specific technologies—not only to improve the performance of organisations in a field, but also to accrue benefits for themselves. In the case of PAY-SYS, the regional health authority sought not only to improve elderly care in the province, but also—for its own benefit—to expedite and make more efficient the process of collecting data about this type of care. This research emphasises the role of technology-selling organisations in getting enough other organisations adopt their technologies that the benefits of enforcing the technologies become clear to standard-setting institutions. Without this clarity, the broad diffusion of PAI-SYS and its benefits to the regional health authority would have been

harder to foresee; even if the authority had wanted to mandate adoption, it would have faced greater difficulties in developing a justification to do so.

## Conclusion

For many technologies, a single type of supply-side agent leads the process of diffusion, However, for some, diffusion is a mix of processes, such as selling and standard setting. The diffusion of such technologies involves multiple types of supply-side agents working simultaneously or in sequence. The core processes of diffusion require a set of ancillary practices. These ancillary practices not only improve the diffusion of the technology at any point in time but also ensure continuation of the diffusion process, by moving it from one type of supply-side agent to another. With this model of diffusion, future researchers could explore how diffusion processes change over time and, particularly, how they move from one type of supply-side agent to another.

## APPENDIX A

Research setting: Long Term Care System (LTC) in Italy The demography of the Italian population is consistent with the ageing trend of western countries. According to the Italian Institute of Statistics (ISTAT, 2010), in fact, the segment of 64 and older has more than doubled in the last fifty years passing from 8.2% of 1951 to 20,8% of the population in 2010. Along this trend, ISTAT forecasts that in 2050 the group age of 64 and older will be about one third of the population. The fact of living longer, however, does not mean necessarily living better in the later stage of life, due to the increase of chronic illnesses, comorbidities, and physical and mental degenerations. Somehow the ageing of population in Italy, and in many western countries, has also changed the way health care is organized and provided by the different health operators. Today, in fact, while hospitals take care of acute cares, the longterm care and continuous assistance are left to nursing and resident homes (Croce, 2010; Sebastiano and Porazzi, 2010). As a consequence of the transformation of the health care sector, the Italian nursing homes have received in the last fifteen years an increasing demand of long term care services both at the residential and home based levels. At the beginning of the nineties, only 2% of the over 64 received LTC home based services, and only 2.9% LTC residential services. In 2010, according to the data of the Ministry of Welfare, the percentage of over 64 receiving home based LTC was of 5.5% (Barbabella et al., 2013). In such a context, the Italian nursing homes have become multi-functional centers where services include the treatment of comas, hospice, Alzheimer, dementia, care of physical handicaps, postsurgery care, etc. This means that today the nursing homes are complex organizations in which the principles and tools of management, with a certain differentiation from one organization to the other, have replaced the administrative approach typical of the eighties. Indeed, the need of ensuring certain satisfaction levels, the limited public funding, and the growing population of users have been driving the management of nursing homes towards the priorities of being efficient and effective at the same time

The regionalized health system in Italy has produced a very fragmented system of nursing homes, very different from region to region in terms of average size (number of beds), institutional forms (company, foundation, no-profit organization, public body, cooperative, moral and religious body, etc.) and types of structure (nursing home, rest home, family center, daily center, etc.). It is in this context of varied and changing care practices, that the software PAI-SYS was first developed and then adopted. This inhomogeneity is the reason why we focused our study on a group of nursing homes operating in the same territory within the same framework of rules.

## Diary of data collection and panel of interviewees

With our first field trip (October 2008), we sought to understand the early PAI-SYS history by interviewing five people (see Table B1): the general manager, software developer, administrative assistant, and two nurses. We used semi-structured interviews to glean how the software has been developed and implemented, from the original idea to its current form.

After analyzing the initial data, we updated the interview guide for a second round of interviews (September–October 2009). We investigated individual uses and organizational consequences of PAI-SYS and how changes were fed back to the programmer to be included into the software. We interviewed 14 people in this round: 10 employees, 2 guests, the general manager, and the developer. Moreover, we conducted a nonparticipant observation, following several users during their daily activities to see how they used the software and observe the impact of PAI-SYS on the ways people worked and interacted.

With a third field trip (October 2012), we aimed to uncover the structure of the larger context in which PAI-SYS was used. We interviewed the director of the local health authority, responsible for the control and financing of the nursing homes of the region (ASL) and two managers in charge of designing and executing the project to digitalize the data exchange between this local health authority and nursing homes. We also interviewed the owner/chair of the software house and a sales manager. Finally, we re-interviewed the general manager of NHF. All interviews investigated the modality of adoption and diffusion of PAI-SYS: from a policy point of view for ASL and from the commercial perspective for the software house. We also gathered contacts for other nursing homes using the PAI-SYS system.

Thus the fourth data collection phase (November 2012 and February 2013) focused on PAI-SYS in use in other nursing homes that were comparable in size and service to NHF but were

mandated to use the PAI-SYS system, as part of the AUSL’s plan to standardize monitoring and quality assurance. We interviewed directors of the nursing homes that had contacts with the ASL and software house, as well as personnel (nurses and health technicians), regarding their use of PAI-SYS. Finally, the fifth data collection phase (July and October 2013 and June 2014) aimed to specify the difference between adopters that were mandated by the local health care authority to use the PAI-SYS and those that selected PAI-SYS on the basis of a unilateral, free software selection. Moreover, we interviewed the managers of two nursing homes that decided not to adopt the system. Then we went back to the software house and NHF, to confirm some of the evidence emerging from our analyses of the interviews. On this occasion, we collected the contracts for all the PAI-SYS installations, which provided contract signing and go live dates.
