# Impact of strategic capabilities on digital transformation success and firm performance: theory and empirical evidence

Pauline Weritz <sup>a</sup>, Jessica Braojos<sup>b</sup>, Jorge Matute<sup>c</sup> and Jose Benitez <sup>d</sup>

<sup>a</sup>Department of Industrial Engineering and Business Information Systems, Faculty of Behavioural, Management and Social Sciences, University of Twente, Enschede, The Netherlands; <sup>b</sup>Department of Management, School of Business and Economics, University of Granada, Granada, Spain; <sup>c</sup>IQS School of Management, Ramon Llull University, Barcelona, Spain; <sup>d</sup>Department of Information Systems and Business Analytics, Ambassador Crawford College of Business and Entrepreneurship, Kent State University, Kent, OH, USA

## ABSTRACT

What is needed for organisations to succeed in digital transformation, and what is its advantage? Motivated by the ongoing high amount of failing digital transformations, this study identifies necessary predictors and outcomes of this prominent matter on the macro level. Drawing on strategic capabilities as enablers for the redefinition of business value and organisational identity in the digital era, we propose that the complementary synergies of strategic capabilities empower digital transformation success, improving firm performance. The results support the proposed model using structural equations modelling with multi-data from 154 organisations and complementary objective data. This study identifies synergistic facilitators for digital transformation success by providing an integrated portfolio of complementary capabilities (i.e., outside-in, spanning, and inside-out orientation). The study also contributes to IS research by ofering a measurable conceptualisation of digital transformation success. In addition, findings empirically show the association between digital transformation success and firm performance to enable further investigations in the IS field. Finally, from the study’s findings, practical implications can guide organisations towards digital transformation success and firm performance.

## 1. Introduction

Organisations worldwide face technology-enabled shifts, and although it initially felt like an acute disruption, digital transformation (DT) became a persistent challenge for theory and practice (Baiyere et al., 2023; Kane et al., 2021). Societies and businesses are constantly confronted with new opportunities and threats that must be addressed with digital solutions to stay competitive (Kraus et al., 2022). However, firms often only make short-term changes (Subramaniam, 2021), although DT success is vital for strategic planning and compelling long-term advantages (Nishant et al., 2020). Given the complexity of DT as a phenomenon in IS research with only limited theoretical boundaries (Baiyere et al., 2023; Wessel et al., 2021), the contextual assumptions on DT relate to the involvement of technologies to (re)define the value proposition and change the identity of an organisation. Organisations could utilise digital opportunities and leverage the complexity of DT to change once executives know the resources enabling such and the outcomes of DT success. Although DT notably stands out as a critical matter and recent IS research discussed the meaning, complexity, and nature, we identify three remaining key challenges based on the scientific debate and the lack of organisational understanding.

First, although previous Information Systems (IS) literature identified the importance of organisational capabilities in the digital context (e.g., Li & Chan, 2019; Mikalef et al., 2021; Steininger et al., 2022), studies mainly focused on IT-related resources (e.g., big data analytics capability, digital platform capabilities, IT-enabled dynamic capabilities) or contextualised these for product development (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006), innovation (Joshi et al., 2010), or simply competitive advantage (Karimi & Walter, 2015; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005). However, given that many companies continuously fail DT, it might be the case that capabilities are not just a list of abilities for organisations to follow but different dimensions that supplement each other and draw on the respective resources in a complementary way. Hence, understanding the synergies and joint effects behind the capabilities with their enabling functions and mechanisms might be one missing piece for advancing literature or organisational capabilities in the context of DT.

Secondly, from a theoretical perspective, DT success is difficult to assess or measure (Baiyere et al., 2023). Although it is critical for firms to govern the implementation of different activities towards (re)

defining business value and the organisational identity, specific components to derive strategic actions or evaluate such are not far-reaching nor mirrored from real-world scenarios. For instance, previous work operationalising DT in IS predominantly understands DT with a primary emphasis on technology implementation, although it affects all organisational foundations, processes, data, platforms, and people (Baiyere et al., 2023; Vial, 2019). Moreover, only some seminal papers commence exploring ways to assess different maturity levels (e.g., Kane et al., 2021). So far, a concrete overview of what dimensions DT success entails is not tangible, which limits the advancement of the field. The challenge of measuring DT success to derive implications for organisations to assess their level and, moreover, advance future research with this methodological foundation remains.

Third, the importance of empirical evidence regarding the value of DT success for financial and market performance continues not to receive sufficient attention across the literature. While previous papers were often conceptual (e.g., Vial, 2019) or based on a literature review (Hanelt et al., 2021), additional work must theorise and empirically test the associations between DT success and firm performance. The reason to address this challenge is related to the need for firms to understand how to approach digital opportunities and build DT success to enhance performance indicators. For example, companies use data only for operational efficiency, not revenue generation (Subramaniam, 2021), due to the unclarity of the responses or resources required. Following that, providing empirical evidence on the association between DT success and performance might offer a foundation for future research in the context.

This study is motivated following the previously elaborated three challenges, which condense in the phenomenon-driven stimulus about the relevance of organisational capabilities for achieving DT success considering its complexity on the synergies, the theory-driven motivation of conceptualising DT success and developing a measurable operationalisation, and the empirical assessment of performance outcomes as a consequence of DT success. Matching this with the complexity of DT in the IS domain, the following questions offer the potential for clarification for the identified challenges through empirical assessment:

(1) How can the complementarity of strategic capabilities enhance DT success?

(2) How can DT success impact firm performance?

Drawing on the DT perspective and organisational capabilities-based theory, this study proposes a research model that analyses the association between the complementary portfolio of six strategic capabilities, DT success, and firm performance. This study aims to demonstrate the synergy between the strategic capabilities in the context of DT, which are critical for firms to achieve successful DT. We empirically contribute to organisational capabilities-based theory by leveraging DT as a context (Giustiziero et al., 2023; Gregor, 2006) and offer a bundle of strategic capabilities in which the synergistic effects facilitate an interconnection with mutual reinforcement. Moreover, we offer a conceptualisation and measurement for DT success as a multi-dimensional construct, advancing the existing body of knowledge and providing a solid foundation for further IS research and theory development around DT. Finally, our study provides empirical evidence for the often-forgotten importance of DT success for firm performance.

Next to these three theoretical contributions to IS literature, the study also offers practical implications. The lessons learned might support organisations in developing the synergy between critical strategic capabilities as a foundational block for creating business value and avoiding investing time and resources. The findings can also guide organisations with implications on how to increase firm performance initiated through DT success and pinpoint DT on a granular level.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. Digital transformation perspective and organisational capabilities-based theory

This study’s central lens is the DT perspective, which emerges as a relevant concept across IS literature (Benbya et al., 2020; Hanelt et al., 2021). Due to the increasingly strategic role of digital technologies in value creation, academia and practitioners aim to understand what DT entails (Wessel et al., 2021). DT is a complex process where firms (re)define business value and organisational identity (Vial, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021). Notably, this organisational change is predicated on the occurrence of digital technologies and is reshaping business models and structures (Benbya et al., 2020; Hess et al., 2016). Enabled by a changing business environment (i.e., companies, markets, industries), DT is required to reconsider companies’ strategic vision, people and culture, knowledge, and capabilities (Gurbaxani & Dunkle, 2019).

DT can be supported by complementary organisational capabilities that facilitate performance in the digital era (e.g., Steininger et al., 2022). Consequently, the research draws on organisational capabilities-based theory as a valuable framework for conceptualising strategic capabilities. Capabilities in management and IS research are a long-existing phenomenon (Brinch et al., 2021; Jha et al., 2020;

Steininger et al., 2022) since this approach is critical to addressing challenges in the business environment and creating a competitive advantage (Warner & Wäger, 2019; Yeow et al., 2018). Organisational capabilities are a group of assets that offer opportunities for firms and on which a company can build its strategy (Collis, 1994; Grant, 1991). From previous literature, the notion of capabilities is linked to the resource-based theory (Barney, 1991). This view considers a firm’s resource valuable, rare, inimitable, nonsubstitutable, and an enabler for performance (Barney, 1991; Wade & Hulland, 2004). However, the organisational capability-based theory challenges this concept by distinguishing tangible and intangible assets. Specifically, this means differentiating between capabilities and resources (e.g., patents or capital). Thus, capabilities are seen as an extension of the resourcebased theory because capabilities can subsist detached from companies’ existing resources. In particular, capabilities are a stable concept in the changing business environment regardless of the firm’s resources (Amiri et al., 2015). Such capabilities are abilities that support a firm’s development and strategic execution to create a competitive advantage (Hao & Song, 2016; Kane et al., 2021).

## 2.2. Conceptualisation of the key constructs

## 2.2.1. Digital transformation success

The buzzword DT finds attention across research streams, revealing a blurry definition, still, with several attempts for sustainable distinctions of related terminologies (e.g., Baiyere et al., 2023; Wessel et al., 2021). Nevertheless, the critical role of DT in the current economy insists across IS literature (e.g., Kauffman & Weber, 2018; Markus & Rowe, 2021) and demands responses from organisations and academia. DT is a complex procedure with a broad scope, created by a technology-enabled shift of value creation across organisations and requires operational efficiency (i.e., business model and strategy, operations and processes, people and culture, and tools and data) (Chanias et al., 2019; Subramaniam, 2021). To accomplish this redefinition, the “emergence of a new digital identity” (Wessel et al., 2021, p. 102) demands actions on different organisational levels (Vial, 2019).

For instance, businesses that achieve DT success possess a digital-first mindset, follow an open approach towards opportunities regarding digital products and services, and prioritise digital solutions compared to traditional ones (Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Gurbaxani & Dunkle, 2019). Those companies might have realised a digital business model and strategy and went through a process of organisational learning (Chanias et al., 2019) that again led to digitised practices and operations (e.g., automated processes and data-driven decision-making) (Soluk & Kammerlander, 2021;

Subramaniam, 2021). Besides the strategic and operational levels that experience a redefinition, non-technical factors are considered critical elements of successful DT (Kane et al., 2021). This includes the thriving development of digital competencies across the firm and the empowerment of the employees (Soluk & Kammerlander, 2021; Vial, 2019). Finally, success during DT might be recognised by organisations’ innovative work with customer operations data, such as using tools that retrieve generated data through social media (Kane et al., 2021; Subramaniam, 2021; Vial, 2019).

The previously described perspectives reflect a comprehensive approach to redefining business value creation and organisational identity in the digital context (Bonnet et al., 2015). To conclude, DT success is defined as the completed redefinition of business value and organisational identity concerning the business model and digital strategy, operations and processes, people and culture, and using digital tools and data to perform tasks effectively or create products and services.

## 2.2.2. Strategic capabilities

Eventually, organisational capabilities have been studied in the context of the business value of IT (e.g., Pang et al., 2014; Soluk & Kammerlander, 2021; Warner & Wäger, 2019). Hence, different organisational capabilities exist and guide a firm in building its business activities (foundational, core, and strategic) (Helfat, 2000; Steininger et al., 2022). Foundational capabilities (e.g., onboarding new employees) are required to do business and are the base for core and strategic capabilities (Kane et al., 2021). Core capabilities (e.g., compliance) are critical for the firm’s functionality but are not significantly crucial for competitive advantage (Kane et al., 2021). Strategic capabilities are a set of abilities that collectively generate value for the organisation (Kane et al., 2021). Within the frame of this study, one suitable typology of IS resources offers a systematic classification for the orientation of such capabilities: outside-in, spanning, and inside-out (Wade & Hulland, 2004). First, outside-in capabilities are the abilities to oversee the market and look beyond the company walls to manage relationships with the external environment (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Second, spanning capabilities refer to the company’s resources offering alignment and integration of knowledge (Wade & Hulland, 2004). Third, inside-out capabilities are future-oriented, deployed internally, and include developing and experimenting towards becoming a source of business value (Wade & Hulland, 2004).

A distinct portfolio of capabilities and the synergy between these might be relevant for DT success. An exhaustive literature review and nine exploratory interviews with IT and business executives were performed to obtain the most representative strategic capabilities and understand the synergies between the different abilities influencing DT success. The insights from multinational corporations from different countries (e.g., Germany, Japan, the U.S., and Spain) helped identify abilities that enhance DT success. At the same time, according to prior conceptual and executive papers in the Management and IS fields, organisational capabilities like cross-functional collaboration or organisational agility might be key in the DT process (e.g., Vial, 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2019). According to the insights from both data sources, we identified a list of six strategic capabilities (i.e., outside-in, spanning, and inside-out) that might be related to DT success (see Table B1 in the Appendix for an overview of the interview excerpts and literature).

First, for outside-in capabilities, we derive networking capability and customer-centricity capability. Networking capability happens to be the ability to connect and collaborate with external organisations (e.g., academia or start-ups) (Kane et al., 2021; Sisodiya et al., 2013) and thereby offers unique value to connect the outside-in knowledge with the insideout activities. For instance, institutions can share knowledge and resources (e.g., customer data or specific production tools) to build on. Customer-centricity capability is the ability to scan the market environment and understand the customer experience, including perceptions and expectations (Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006). For example, by focusing on the customers, market, and competitors, firms are aware of reaching the target group through different sales channels (Matarazzo et al., 2021; Tortora et al., 2021).

Second, we find cross-functional collaboration capability and absorptive capacity for the spanning capabilities. Cross-functional collaboration capability refers to the ability to align the work of all departments across the company by understanding different perspectives and tasks, transparency, and developing solutions by using new ways of working (Denison et al., 1996; Kane et al., 2021). For instance, sales and IT units in the workplace link up in team-based structures and operate towards common goals (Warner & Wäger, 2019). Absorptive capacity is the ability to identify valuable external knowledge and subsequently explore, transform, and adapt it within the organisation (Siachou et al., 2021; Zahra & George, 2002). It can include screening and scouting digital trends and evaluating new information (Roberts et al., 2012; Warner & Wäger, 2019).

Third, we identify organisational agility and innovation capability for the inside-out capabilities. Organisational agility draws on responding quickly to changes (e.g., customer preferences, market trends, and business opportunities) and consequently redefining organisational processes (Benitez et al., 2018; Lu & Ramamurthy, 2011). It might be critical when rapidly developing prototypes (Warner & Wäger, 2019) and continuously tracking operational data to include customer feedback (Subramaniam, 2021). Additionally, innovation capability detects and develops new ideas (Westerman & Curley, 2008). It includes building innovation infrastructure and empowering people in the company to experiment (e.g., with next-generation research) (Matarazzo et al., 2021).

Eventually, these six capabilities and the synergetic relations between outside-in, spanning, and inside-out capabilities jointly cover the abilities to manage DT success (Matarazzo et al., 2021; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2006; Siachou et al., 2021; Sisodiya et al., 2013; Warner & Wäger, 2019).

## 2.2.3. Firm performance

Firm performance is a highly used measure of success in the organisational context (Mikalef et al., 2021; Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005; Verhoef et al., 2021). It is designed as a construct composed of elements reflecting key performance indicators and the core of profitability (Mithas et al., 2011). Performance can be measured with objective and subjective indicators, and different outcomes in the business environment can be evaluated (Khin & Ho, 2018; Liu et al., 2013). In the context of this study, we decided on objective measurements as these factors cannot be altered by subjectivity and perceived biases (Heredia et al., 2022). The objective conceptualisation is the aptitude for financial ratios (e.g., EBITDA, liquidity ratio, gross income operating income, ROA, sales, and stock turnover) (Khin & Ho, 2018; Sousa-Zomer et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2012). The relevant key performance indicators show the company’s efficiency with measurable goals and objectives. The firm performance is one of the most relevant evaluating factors and a precise way to assess the success of businesses compared to other companies on the market (Mikalef et al., 2021; Mithas et al., 2011). ## 3. Hypotheses development

## 3.1. Relationship between complementary strategic capabilities and digital transformation success

As firms’ strategic capabilities are the foundation for generating business value (Benitez et al., 2018; Kane et al., 2021), they may have a critical function for DT success. While organisations redefine their business value as a DT activity (Wessel et al., 2021), essential abilities for these transformational processes must be reconsidered. Organisations require implications on exploiting new knowledge and ensuring leveraging synergies of driving factors to manage the transformation. The complexity of DT with the emergence of a new organisational identity highlights that it is not easy to achieve and that only building some capabilities is insufficient. For instance, only understanding the changing business environment but not reacting to it might be a reason not to achieve DT success. Hence, various capabilities with different functions (i.e., outside-in, spanning, and inside-out) might be critical to achieving DT success and must be built for a complementary function. Only organisations that are able to analyse the changing business environment, react by planning the redefinition of the business value and finally respond quickly to opportunities and threats can create a new organisational identity.

We possess that the synergistic relationship between the six capabilities as positive emergent capabilities leads to joint value (i.e., DT success) (Nevo & Wade, 2010; Someh et al., 2019). These forming synergistic relationships “work together, span their boundaries and complement each other” (Someh et al., 2019, p. 371). Following this motivation and building on previous studies discussed in IS literature relating to the systems perspective (e.g., Chen, 2012; Nevo & Wade, 2010, 2011; Someh et al., 2019), we presume different enablers and mechanisms. The additional function of the synergistic capabilities (i.e., outsidein, spanning, and inside-out) is only combined and merely as a complementarity of the six dimensions positively associated with DT success. Hence, implementing single capabilities without the others may not lead to the intended outcomes as only parts of the process towards the (re)definition of business value and organisational identity are covered (Tanriverdi, 2005). In the following, we argue how and why this synergy relates to DT success and explain the complementarity of all associated capabilities.

For the outside-in capabilities, possessing networking capability might create opportunities to achieve the identified objectives from the outside-in capabilities and share resources (e.g., access to customer data) in a joined ecosystem (Ghosh et al., 2022; Kane et al., 2021; Verhoef et al., 2021). Establishing strategic partnerships might broaden the organisation’s perspective and facilitate the adoption of digital solutions (i.e., digitised practises and operations) (Cenamor et al., 2019; Wade & Hulland, 2004). For instance, shared databases might increase DT success as firms access real-time operations data (Kane et al., 2021). Further, building relationships provides opportunities to leverage and accelerate the network between employees to connect, share knowledge, and offer opportunities for growth or development (i.e., empowered talent). Finally, possessing networking abilities might offer opportunities to foster the advantages of data-driven decision-making, which could ensure a digital-first mindset (Matarazzo et al., 2021). As a complementary outside-in capability, customercentricity capability might increase DT success. Not only might it be sufficient to explore the opportunities, but complementary, the needs and demands can be better assessed and addressed by integrating market dynamics (Abbu & Gopalakrishna, 2021). Putting the customer in the centre enables organisations to prioritise digital preferences and leverage technologies to deliver an extraordinary experience (i.e., a digital-first mindset) (Warner & Wäger, 2019). This might further drive organisations to digitise their practices and operations to meet these demands and to provide seamless digital interactions (Park & Mithas, 2020). By possessing the organisational ability to work in a customer-centric way, employees might also be empowered to take ownership of customer satisfaction during DT. They might proactively implement digital solutions to respond to customer needs.

Even if organisations possess outside-in capabilities, difficulties in internally making sense of these can lead to a complexity where further capabilities are needed to connect to the organisational processes required for DT success. Spanning capabilities (i.e., cross-functional collaboration capability and networking capability) might address these drawbacks and support. Cross-functional collaboration capability might foster a shared understanding of digital opportunities and challenges and enhance the digital-first mindset. It might further enable digitised practices and operations by facilitating the integration and implementation of digital technologies (Kraus et al., 2021). The integration of the business units might also attract empowered talent as it improves communication, transparency, participation, and creativity (Vial, 2019; Warner & Wäger, 2019). While breaking silos in the workplace, this ability impacts the redefinition of organisational identity as part of DT (Dremel et al., 2017). Finally, cross-functional collaboration capability might enhance data access and collaboration tools by facilitating efficient data sharing and analysis across different functions (Tabrizi et al., 2019). Also, absorptive capacity might enhance DT success by enabling organisations to handle uncertainty and manage new digital opportunities (i.e., a digital-first mindset). It might allow organisations to stay updated on technological infrastructure and its implications by exploring and exploiting new knowledge (Siachou et al., 2021). By actively monitoring external and internal information and trends, this ability can contribute to using digital opportunities and digital solutions (i.e., digitised practices and operations). Furthermore, having strong absorptive capacity might enable organisations to leverage data-driven knowledge (i.e., empower talent) and enhance data access and continuous usage of collaboration tools (e.g., IoT enables connectivity) (Arcidiacono et al., 2022; Subramaniam, 2021).

Still, building outside-in and spanning capabilities might not be sufficient to develop the strategic potential to achieve DT success (Nevo & Wade, 2011), and organisations might face challenges in understanding the knowledge gained and transferring it into business value. Possessing inside-out capabilities could offer the missing piece in this synergy as the unique mechanism of organisational agility and innovation capability combined deliver more value for DT success. Organisational agility might support DT success by enabling pivoting and scaling current aptitudes and increasing digitised practices and operations (e.g., Verhoef et al., 2021). When organisations embrace change in an agile way, this might further enhance the digital-first mindset by prioritising digital solutions based on efficiency principles (e.g., Warner & Wäger, 2019). Furthermore, organisations might quickly integrate collaboration tools to ensure fast coordination (AlNuaimi et al., 2022). Relating to the synergistic relationship between the outside-in and spanning capabilities, organisations with high agility might rapidly respond to changes and threats from the business environment, which can increase the exploration of digital opportunities for employees (i.e., empowered talent). Additionally, building on the outside-in and spanning capabilities, possessing innovation capability might allow firms to think differently and experiment with new ideas for products or services, driving the digital-first mindset of organisations and enabling the transformation of the firms’ mechanisms towards DT success (Braojos et al., 2015; Warner & Wäger, 2019). This ability might offer the infrastructure to create digital opportunities (i.e., digitised practises and operations) (Westerman & Curley, 2008). Innovation capabilities might empower talent by fostering an environment that encourages risk-taking and problem-solving, enabling employees to contribute to DT actively. Moreover, this ability might enhance data access and collaboration tools by exploring technologies such as analytics or automation to gather data and insights that facilitate decision-making (Bansal et al., 2023).

Hence, previous arguments show that implementing single capabilities without considering the others may not produce the intended DT success. By leveraging the synergies between these strategic capabilities, organisations might be able to analyse the changing business environment (i.e., outside-in), react by planning the redefinition of the business value and organisational identity (i.e., spanning), and finally respond quickly to opportunities and threats (inside-out). We propose that only the powerful synergistic relationship between the six capabilities might facilitate the journey towards DT success. Consistently, we hypothesise:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): The complementarity of the six dimensions of strategic capabilities is positively associated with digital transformation success.

## 3.2. Relationship between digital transformation success and firm performance

Previous research linked digital technologies with performance improvements and competitive advantage (Ravichandran & Lertwongsatien, 2005; Van der Meulen et al., 2020). Organisations that redesign and adapt to DT can perform better than their competitors (Bonnet et al., 2015). While several studies support a link between capabilities and firm performance (e.g., Laguir et al., 2022; Mikalef et al., 2020), it was often defined as an IT-enabled competitive advantage (Bharadwaj, 2000; Pavlou & El Sawy, 2010; Tang et al., 2022). We suppose that organisations can differentiate themselves based on their DT success. While each of the dimensions (i.e., digital-first mindset, digitised practices and operations, empowered talent, and data access and collaboration tools) are complex to achieve, firms that undergo a successful DT, in turn, enjoy a superior financial and market performance by exploring digital opportunities, prioritising digital solutions, automating processes and data-driven decision making, developing digital skills, and accessing real-time customer and operations data (e.g., Mikalef et al., 2021). With a digital-first mindset, companies can leverage technology to identify new market opportunities, streamline processes, and enhance customer experiences. This can lead to improved market performance (Khin & Ho, 2018). Moreover, DT success might lead to reduced costs and capitalised data-driven insights (Zhai et al., 2022). Digitising practices and operations might enhance market performance by enabling organisations to respond quickly to changing conditions (Sousa-Zomer et al., 2020). Empowering talent refers to providing employees with the necessary skills, tools, and autonomy to leverage digital technologies effectively and might enhance operating efficiency (Zhai et al., 2022). When empowered, employees can adapt to technological advancements, innovate, and contribute more to the organisation’s market success (e.g., sales). In addition, access to data and collaboration tools as part of DT success might enable organisations to make informed decisions, optimise processes, and drive innovation, which can increase financial indicators (Khin & Ho, 2018).

Based on the previous argumentation, strategic capabilities might serve as a foundation and enabler for DT success. By leveraging existing strengths and capabilities, organisations can design and implement digital strategies that align with their core competencies, enhancing their likelihood of success. Especially under current disruptions, firm performance might be explained by different levels of DT success. Accordingly, we see DT success as a key driver for firm performance and propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): Digital transformation success is positively associated with firm performance.

## 4. Research context, design, and methodology

## 4.1. Data collection procedure

We tested the hypothesis with a combination of crosssectional survey data and objective secondary data and thereby followed the recommendations of Maier et al. (2023) to ensure a high level of quality in this multimethod research. First, for the sampling strategy, we ensured a purposeful and strongly motivated opportunity to collect the data. More precisely, we chose the Spanish market as the digital competitiveness ranking is above average, indicating a solid digital performance (International Institute for Management Development, 2022). Thereby, the findings are comparable and generalisable for countries worldwide that are similar in terms of ranking, such as New Zealand, Japan, and Luxembourg (International Institute for Management Development, 2022). Intending to collect high-quality data, we searched for medium to large businesses with more than 50 employees and a minimum of 10 million EUR annual revenue in Spain. We selected industries where DT is advanced (e.g., manufacturing, technology, and communication).

As Maier et al. (2023) suggested, there must be sufficient power to detect the effects of the proposed research model, and the estimated sample size must be calculated. Therefore, we performed a statistical power analysis using G\*Power 3.1. software (http://www.gpower.hhu. de/) to check the minimum sample size required (Faul et al., 2009). Assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.150), a statistical power level of 0.800, fourteen predictors (i.e., the largest number of structural paths directed to firm performance at the first-order level), and an alpha level of 0.05, the minimum sample size that ensures robustness and generalisability are 135 (Cohen, 1988). The final sample suggests an acceptable size to test the statistical significance of the proposed research model.

## 4.2. Sample description

Following the sampling strategy, 2,578 firms were contacted through a well-established consulting and market research firm from October 2020 to April 2021. They finally gathered 154 valid questionnaires, which means an adequate response rate of 5.97%. Considering a potential focus on crisis management during the pandemic and the general difficulty of contacting Chief Digital Officers due to time constraints and an increased workload, this can be considered a satisfactory result due to the circumstances (Tiwana & Konsynski, 2010).

The sample of this study is composed of the following industries: manufacturing (42.21%), technology and communication (22.73%), retail (8.44%), and an additional 41 firms (26.62%) from other industries (e.g., finance, insurance, energy, and electricity). The firm size of the companies was between 50 to 249 (57 %), 250 to 1000 (31%), 1001–10,000 (10%) and above 10,000 (2%) employees with revenues between 10 to 20 (33%), 20 to 50 (30%) and above 50 (36%) million EUR per year. We selected one key informant per firm to answer the questions and addressed IT executives (36 % female). This position is most knowledgeable about the strategic redefinition and related resources during DT, and it can be said that they are aware of the social and technological factors, including abilities critical for DT. We assessed non-response bias by comparing respondents’ and non-respondents’ characteristics and responses from early (responded in the first month) and late respondents to see if they differed. T-test comparisons between the means of the groups showed non-significant differences.

## 4.3. Measures

## 4.3.1. Type of constructs

Second, following the suggestions by Maier et al. (2023), we report the instruments transparently and in detail and further display the analyses for both model steps in the Tables. Furthermore, previous scales informed the design of the study. Constructs can be modelled as behavioural (latent variables) or design constructs (artefacts) (Henseler, 2020). Behavioural constructs thereby exist in nature and describe attitudes or attributes. Particularly, these kinds of constructs are modelled as either reflective constructs (cause the observed indicators) or causalformative constructs (are caused by the observed indicators) (Castillo et al., 2021). However, design constructs are the product of theoretical thinking that refers to human-made creations (Benitez et al., 2022; Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). These artefacts can be considered a mix of elements and are typically modelled as composites as they are a combination of elements that make up the construct (e.g., as ingredients of a receipt). Eventually, all constructs in the proposed model are conceptualised as artefacts and modelled as composites.

## 4.3.2. Complementary strategic capabilities

Complementary strategic capabilities were measured as a second-order composite construct composed of six first-order constructs with 28 items in total. Operationalising this composite construct through absorptive capacity, organisational agility, cross-functional collaboration capability, customer-centricity capability, innovation capability, and networking capability shows the synergy of the six capabilities. Following the idea in the development of the hypotheses that the significance of complementary strategic capabilities is reinforced by the presence of the other capabilities (Tanriverdi, 2005), this is an approach to measure the complementarity inherent in the six identified capabilities. Only the measurement of a secondorder composite construct offers an operationalisation for the dependencies of the six strategic capabilities and makes the synergistic effects apparent with the emergent variables (Benitez et al., 2020). The items for the first-order constructs are measured as follows. First, absorptive capacity was assessed with four items from Kotabe et al. (2017). Second, organisational agility consisted of three items from Felipe et al. (2020) and Lu and Ramamurthy (2011). Third, we measured cross-functional collaboration capability with six items from Tsai and Hsu (2014). Fourth, customercentricity capability was assessed with five items based on Frambach et al. (2016) and Im and Workman (2004). Fifth, innovation capability was assessed with the help of five items based on the construct of firm innovativeness from Ravichandran (2017). Lastly, networking capability was operationalised with five items based on Sisodiya et al. (2013).

## 4.3.3. Digital transformation success

DT success is a multi-dimensional construct following Bonnet et al. (2015). We followed the steps suggested by Netemeyer et al. (2003) and MacKenzie et al. (2011) to develop a reliable and valid scale. We began by thoroughly reviewing the existing literature to understand the subject deeply. Using this knowledge, we developed a conceptual definition for DT success and created a pool of items based on Bonnet et al. (2015). These items were then tested using a q-sorting test, and to ensure the validity of the scale and its coverage of all relevant aspects, we conducted a pre-test with nine IT and business executives. This step allowed us to assess content validity and make necessary adjustments.

Consequently, we measured DT success using a second-order composite construct comprising four first-order dimensions. In the next step, we performed a confirmatory composite analysis to validate the structure of the measures. This analysis helped us confirm that the assignment of indicators to their respective dimensions was accurate. Finally, we evaluated weights, loadings, and multicollinearity to validate the measurement model, following the standard practices for composite measures. Based on this process, DT success is composed of four first-order dimensions: digital-first mindset (4 items), digitised practices and operations (4 items), empowered talent (3 items), and data access and collaboration tools (4 items).

## 4.3.4. Firm performance

To ensure the quality of cross-sectional data, we enriched the self-reported survey data with objective data (Maier et al., 2023). Hence, firm performance was measured with secondary data from the SABI (Iberian Balance Sheet Analysis System) database, which offers financial information for companies in Spain and Portugal. Firm performance was constructed as a second-order construct comprising six critical indicators from the fiscal year 2021. Although the dynamic nature of these indicators and their susceptibility to external factors are considered, these metrics provide valuable insights into the financial outcomes of DT success, especially when considered in conjunction with the two other non-financial indicators (Benitez et al., 2018). To establish causality in the model (Maier et al., 2023), the decision on the included indicators stems from reviewing previous literature exploring firm performance in digital firms (e.g., Khin & Ho, 2018) and follows the motivation to assess the profitability through different financial resources (EBITDA, liquidity ratio, operating income, ROA) but also include non-financial performance indicators which show the overall position of the company in the market (sales and stock turnover) (i.e., market performance). More precisely, the values include the earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortisation (EBITDA), the company’s ability to pay obligations (liquidity ratio), gross income (operating income), the profitability of the company concerning the total assets (ROA), the value of products or services sold (sales), and the rate at which goods are sold and replaced (stock turnover). By considering the market performance, we aim to capture the comprehensive outcomes of DT beyond profitability alone.

## 4.3.5. Control variables

The survey also included three control variables (i.e., firm size, industry, and IT investment), as they might directly affect DT success or firm performance. First, it is reasonable to assume that larger companies have higher resources to invest in DT and have higher revenue and market share (Lee & Xia, 2006). Firm size was measured with the number of employees as a composite construct (Braojos et al., 2020; Mikalef & Gupta, 2021). Second, different industries may have other levels of advancements in DT and financial performance results. The industry was assessed as a first-order composite with four indicators, while retail was chosen as a dummy variable. Lastly, investments in IT might influence DT success and firm performance as they directly support a successful DT and lead to higher business value (Lee & Xia, 2006). This variable was assessed with two items stating the amount of annual investment in IT and innovation in relation to the annual revenue on a scale from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high). ## 5. Empirical analysis and results

## 5.1. Motivation of the method of estimation

We employed partial least squares (PLS) path modelling to test the suggested model. PLS is a structural equation modelling approach widely utilised across IS research (Benitez et al., 2022; Ringle et al., 2012). Particularly, PLS is a helpful strategy for estimating composite models as it is an appropriate technique for complex models with multi-dimensional constructs (Hair et al., 2020), such as strategic capabilities, DT success, and firm performance in this study. Bootstrapping with 5,000 subsamples was used to determine significant levels of weights, loadings, and path coefficients.

## 5.2. Prevention of common method variance (CMV)

It is unlikely that composite measures will experience common method variance (CMV) (e.g., Podsakoff et al., 2003). Still, we made every effort to avoid CMV and test for it. First, respondents’ anonymity was guaranteed in administering the survey. Variables and items were not given in order, there were no labels, and respondents could not move back and forth through the survey (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, the consulting vendor in charge of administering the survey had a well-designed protocol. The telephone survey was answered by the IT executive or a similarly knowledgeable person because when the respondent was not comfortable or qualified to answer the questions, they were asked to identify the appropriate executive to answer them. Additionally, we tested CMV in several ways. First, the highest correlation between variables is 0.796, which does not show high correlations (r > 0.9) (Bagozzi et al., 1991). Second, a full collinearity test shows VIF values at a construct level ranging from 1 to 2.824, under the threshold of 5 set by Kock and Lynn (2012). Third, we used the marker variable approach to check for common method variance in our data (Chin et al., 2013; Rönkkö & Ylitalo, 2011). We added a marker variable composed of four items following a guideline<sup>1</sup> from Chin et al. (2013) to our proposed model. We first examined the correlation between the marker and study items to check whether the items measuring the marker variable were suitable markers. There is no strong association between marker and study items, as correlations vary between −0.047 and 0.319. Additionally, as the mean correlation between study items and marker items is larger than $0 . 0 5 \ ( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ 0.128), we can compare our proposed model with the marker variable model. We then estimated the marker variable model by adding the measured latent marker variable as a predictor of the endogenous variables (i.e., DT success and firm performance) (Rönkkö & Ylitalo, 2011). The results of the marker variable model are similar to the proposed model, which indicates that the research model is unlikely to experience CMV. Finally, the subjective self-reported data is complemented with objective measures.

## 5.3. Measurement model evaluation

The external and internal validation of the proposed model is of utmost importance to increase the quality of the data analysis (Maier et al., 2023). Thereby, the data analysis can ensure generalisability beyond the sample (i.e., external) and display the accuracy of the measures (i.e., internal). First, we checked the external validity of our composite constructs by performing a confirmatory composite analysis to evaluate the model fit of the saturated model before testing the actual model (Henseler, 2020). A confirmatory composite analysis compares the empirical and the modelimplied correlation matrixes to analyse whether the data support the structure of the composite measures (Benitez et al., 2020). The discrepancy between the empirical and the model-implied correlation matrixes at first and second-order levels was done by calculating SRMR, unweighted least squares $( \mathrm { { d } _ { U L S } ) }$ , and geodesic $\left( \mathrm { { d } _ { G } } \right)$ discrepancies. In our model, the values of the discrepancies at first and second-order levels are below the 99% quantile of the bootstrap discrepancies, which suggests that the structure of the measurements is supported with a 1% probability. We validate our model based on an alpha level of 0.01, which states that the structure of the measures is correct and should not be rejected. Table 2 shows the results of the confirmatory composite analysis of the first-order and second-order constructs.

Moreover, we evaluated the internal validity (i.e., content and discriminant), multicollinearity, and significance of weights and loadings for items and dimensions (Hair et al., 2020). First, content validity was ensured by using previously validated scales. Second, the HTMT ratios of the first-order latent constructs are below the suggested critical threshold of 0.90, so discriminant validity can be ensured. Further, multicollinearity was evaluated at firstand second-order levels. The values of the variance inflation factor (VIF) are below the threshold of 10 (Benitez et al., 2020; Hair et al., 2020), and thus multicollinearity is not a problem in the data. Lastly, we evaluated the level of significance of weights and loadings at first- and second-order levels, which are all significant. In sum, the measurement model results are appropriate, and we can proceed with testing the hypotheses of the proposed research model. The evaluation is available in Table A1 in the Appendix.

## 5.4. Structural model evaluation

## 5.4.1. Test of hypotheses

To evaluate the association between the constructs, we examined path coefficients for the direct effects of H1 and H2. Further, we assessed the level of significance, $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ values, and effect sizes. The results support both hypotheses, indicating that strategic capabilities enable DT success (H1) $( \beta = 0 . 6 9 4 , \mathrm { ~ p _ { o n e - t a i l e d } < 0 . 0 0 1 ) }$ , which in turn improves firm performance (H2) $( \beta = 0 . 3 0 0 , \ p _ { \mathrm { o n e - t a i l e d } } <$ 0.01). The $\mathrm { R } ^ { 2 }$ is 0.744 for DT success and 0.341 for firm performance, proving the variables’ good explanatory power. The effect size analysis shows the relative size of including the associations in the model, leading to $\mathrm { \Delta } \mathrm { f } ^ { 2 }$ values of 1.216 (H1) and 0.035 (H2). To analyse the potential mediating role of DT success, we evaluated the indirect<sup>2</sup> effects of strategic capabilities on firm performance Benitez et al. (2018). ## 5.4.2. Post hoc analysis: robustness of the structural results

To check the robustness of the proposed model, we conducted a post hoc analysis by estimating an alternative model. The alternative model entailed strategic capabilities as first-order constructs and DT success as a second-order construct. The estimation revealed that from the six capabilities, only customer-centricity capability does not significantly affect DT success $( \beta = 0 . 0 1 2 , \ \mathrm { p _ { o n e - t a i l e d } } = 0 . 4 3 4 )$ or firm performance $( \beta = - 0 . 0 1 3 , \mathrm { p } _ { \mathrm { o n e - t a i l e d } } = 0 . 4 4 1 )$ The non-significant effect of customer-centricity capability on DT success might show that the combination of the portfolio of strategic capabilities better predicts DT success. This would suggest that strategic capabilities may exert synergistic effects on DT success and its combination, rather than its individual impacts, better predict DT. Nevertheless, this alternative model’s goodness of fit was substantially lower than the original model, so these results should be taken cautiously.

## 6.2. Managerial implications

With this research, we advance not only academic but also practical knowledge about the portfolio of strategic capabilities which enable DT success. Following that, firms can develop robust action plans to address potential challenges, allocate recourses, and prioritise efforts to ensure a smoother journey and reduce the chance of DT failure and risks. Having the proper organisational resources and capabilities in this digital environment can meet expectations for competitive advantage and execute actions needed to align IT and business for this transformation (Wessel et al., 2021). Further, DT processes can consume resources, and possible failures can influence the firm’s performance, society, and the economy (Kane et al., 2021). Missed DT opportunities might harm the business value and prevent responses to the changing digital environment. From there, we recommend businesses focus on developing all six dimensions of the required strategic capabilities, as only these synergy and complementary functions can lead to DT success. Classified by the orientation, firstly for outside-in, we recommend collaborating with the ecosystem (i.e., networking capability) and understanding the market (i.e., customer-centricity capability). Secondly, for spanning capabilities, we propose to align the workforce functions (i.e., cross-functional collaboration) and explore new external knowledge (i.e., absorptive capacity). Thirdly, for the inside-out capabilities, firms must respond quickly to changes and threats (i.e., organisational agility) and develop revolutionary ideas (innovation capability).

Specifically, the empirical analysis suggests that organisations must constantly build relationships with stakeholders, and networking in a business ecosystem is crucial, and it is advisable to start collaborations with research institutions, start-ups, and competitors to exchange knowledge. Moreover, the customers and lis tening to the shifting needs are critical for DT success. For instance, it is recommended to continuously moni tor customers’ needs (e.g., through surveys or Apps) to adjust the business model accordingly. Besides that, our lessons highlight the importance of collaboration between different departments across the organisation. Thus, organisations and leaders must support new way of working and encourage high transparency within the workforce (Van der Meulen et al., 2020). This can also be done by empowering them to follow the common goals and values and include the employees with an entrepreneurial mind. In that regard, firms must also acquire new knowledge and integrate this into the firms’ processes. Because DT is naturally embedded in processes and routines that require organisations to build new knowledge, it is beneficial to consider this implication in recruiting processes. For example, new employees could be characterised by possessing and demonstrating abilities to detect and absorb market trends, knowledge, technologies, and innovations. These employees must use new channels (e.g., social media) to gain information and try new technologie (e.g., metaverse). They also might follow conferences and workshops on the newest advancements in the industry. Another lesson learned for managers from this paper is the relevance of digital innovation throughout all business processes (Nambisan et al., 2017), as opportunities and threats permanently affect all levels within the firm. This is why organisations have to build innovation capabilities, for example, as the technology firm Intel did by building innovation cen tres for their employees, both in a virtual and physical environment where they can experiment and creatively develop the future of value creation (Westerman & Curley, 2008). With that, organisations must respond quickly and flexibly adapt to the changing business environment. This also includes monitoring and pre paring for digital threats (Dery et al., 2017).

To conclude, this study theorised that the synergy of strategic capabilities enables DT success, further relating to firm performance. We can explain this association following a capability-led approach, and the empirical analysis of a sample of Spanish firms supports the proposed model. Even though DT has been a relevant topic across IS literature, outcomes are still rare, and with this research, we showed a direct effect on firm performance. Based on the findings, we can provide lessons learned for organisations and leadership roles in designing organisational structures.

## 6.3. Limitations and avenues for future IS research

This research is not without limitations, which offers further research opportunities. We discuss constraints and potentials with the sample composition, the sample size concerning model modifications, and finally, the associations between the variables and additional factors. The results explain the Spanish market and can only be generalised to countries with comparable digital competitiveness (International Institute for Management Development, 2022). Future IS research can explore whether the model is supported in other countries with a different ranking, for example, leading the ranking, such as Denmark or Sweden, or also countries with less digital competence, such as Mongolia or Venezuela. The robustness of the model is consistent if the association is also supported in different industries, comprising challenges, risks (e.g., in the public sector and relation to society and sustainability), or resources (Ngwenyama et al., 2021; Nishant et al., 2020). Moreover, we use one single key informant per firm in the study, and results could be extended with different views by using a minimum of two key informants. However, firm performance can also be measured differently, which might be another avenue for further investigation (Mithas et al., 2011).

Although the sample size of 154 has sufficient statistical power to test the proposed model (Cohen, 1988), we recognise that it is relatively small to make comparisons between different tiers of DT success (Subramaniam, 2021). Also, considering the effect size for the association between DT success and firm performance and the variance explained as small, there are limitations in generalising these findings to a broader population. Future research might consider whether the size of the sample limits the precision of the parameter estimates by using larger sample sizes in other contexts or countries. Given these potential sample limitations, we acknowledge that while DT success is associated with firm performance, the data and model are not comprehensive enough to explain a large proportion of the explained variance. In line with that, we encourage further research to analyse which additional advantages and outcomes can be obtained from DT success.

Furthermore, a potential reciprocal association between DT success and strategic capabilities could be explored. In times of digital disruptions, information overload could also influence how employees can fulfil the demands of organisations towards DT. For example, it would be interesting to explore if there are further predictors on the individual level that support DT success. Investigating the individual and dark side (e.g., technostress) is an exciting idea to follow (Srivastava et al., 2015). This might be the case as employees influence the execution and building of organisational capabilities (Kane et al., 2021). Overall, we provide a sufficient framework for future advancements in this field, and the results provide practical lessons for executives to consider during DT.
