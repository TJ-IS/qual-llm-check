## RECONSIDERING THE IMPLICATIONS OF FORMATIVE VS. REFLECTIVE

## MEASUREMENT MODEL MISSPECIFICATION

Running head: Reconsidering Model Misspecification

## Abstract

The literature on formative modeling (“formative measurement”) in the information systems discipline claims that measurement model misspecification, where a reflective model is used instead of a more appropriate formative model, is widespread. In this research, we argue that this cannot be true because models misspecified in this way would fail the measurement validation procedures used with reflective models and thus would not be publishable. To support this argument, we present two extensive simulation studies. The simulation results show that in most cases where data originates from a formative model, estimating a reflective model would not produce results that satisfy the commonly used measurement validation guidelines. Based on these results, we conclude that widespread publication of models where the direction of measurement is misspecified is unlikely in IS and other disciplines that use similar measurement validation guidelines. Moreover, building on recent discussions on modeling endogenous formatively-specified latent variables, we demonstrate that the effects of misspecification are minor in models that do pass the model quality check. Our results address important issues in the literature on the consequences of measurement model misspecification and provide a starting point for new advances in this area.

Keywords: formative, reflective, measurement model specification, fit indexes, measurement validation rules

## INTRODUCTION

In the behavioral and social sciences, many concepts of interest cannot be observed directly but are measured through other variables that are observed and assumed to relate to the concept of interest. As a result, measurement theory and related measurement models have received much attention in the methodological literature. The simplest and most widely used way to present the relationship between a concept and a measure is a linear structural relation where the theoretical concept (construct) is presented as a cause of the observed variables (indicators). This formulation adopts a realist perspective, where the construct is assumed to exist independent of measurement and is believed to cause variation in the indicators (Borsboom et al., 2003). The typical statistical model for this kind of indicator presents the construct as a common factor on which the indicators load (“reflective measurement”). In recent years, we have seen several articles applying an alternative approach where the indicators are not modeled as effects but as causes of the construct of interest. This alternative approach to the specification of latent variables has been called “causal” or “formative” in this literature (Bollen & Bauldry, 2011; Bollen & Diamantopoulos, 2017; Bollen & Lennox, 1991). In this case, a structural equation model (SEM) represents the construct with a latent variable regressed on the indicators. If presented as a path diagram, the latter model takes the arrows that point from the latent variable to the indicators and reverses them to point to the latent variable. From now on, we refer to a latent variable with incoming regression paths from observed indicators as a formative latent variable and the associated indicators as formative indicators, and the overall approach of specifying models with formative latent variables as formative modeling.

The proponents of formative modeling argue that model misspecification, where a reflective model is used instead of a more appropriate formative one, is a severe problem in the literature. This argument rests on two propositions. First, simulations have been used as evidence that this kind of misspecification would lead to substantial bias in path estimates between latent variables (e.g., MacKenzie et al., 2005), which are of prime theoretical interest. Second, posthoc reviews of survey instruments used in different business fields have concluded that a large portion of the constructs that were modeled as reflective should have been modeled as formative, according to the conceptual criteria used in the formative modeling literature (Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007; Podsakoff et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2015). These two propositions, if valid, imply that many of the findings in these fields, including the IS discipline, are likely invalid.

Both propositions have been challenged in the literature. First, Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012) noted that the apparent bias in estimates when formative latent variables are misspecified as reflective is mostly an artifact of not carefully considering the unit of measurement in which results are expressed. Subsequent research has pointed out some limitations in the models used in this line of work (e.g., that the model for the endogenous case does not accurately reflect a formatively-specified latent variable) (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2014) or suggested alternative explanations for the observed findings (Petter et al., 2012). Moreover, this criticism is further challenged by the recent realization that both the seminal work by Jarvis et al. (2003) and MacKenzie et al. (2005) and subsequent extensions by Petter et al. (2007) and Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012) used inappropriate population models for endogenous formative latent variable (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2014; Cadogan & Lee, 2013; Rigdon et al., 2014). That is, in these population models, the effects of antecedent latent variables on the formatively-specified one were not channeled through the formative indicators, as should be the case in a formative specification, but rather the formative indicators were modeled as covariates to the antecedent latent variables; see also the distinction between causal and covariate indicators by Bollen and Bauldry (2011).

Second, researchers have pointed out that it is implausible that indicators would cause a construct, and thus, formative measurement is itself a fallacy (Edwards, 2011; Markus, 2018; Markus & Borsboom, 2013, Chapter 8). Nevertheless, counter-arguments for this position exist (Bollen & Diamantopoulos, 2017), and the debate is unlikely to be resolved soon. Moreover, even if formative models were sometimes appropriate, Hardin and Marcoulides (2011) have argued that the conceptual definitions of the constructs determine the appropriate modeling approaches and the modeling decisions should not be questioned retrospectively by researchers unfamiliar with these definitions or the measurement development procedures in the original studies. A counter-argument to this criticism is that many researchers may have applied reflective procedures out of habit without considering conceptual definitions and measurement theory.

We contribute to the debate on the consequences of formative latent variable misspecification in two novel ways. First, past research has claimed that the misspecification of formative indicators as reflective is common in several business fields, such as marketing, leadership, and information systems. However, this claim rests on an unstated assumption that studies with such misspecified models would have been able to pass the set of validation rules used in the discipline and required for passing a peer review process. In short, if a model included a latent variable that should have been modeled formatively but was erroneously modeled as reflective, the resulting misspecified model would need to satisfy the commonly used validation rules for reflectively specified models. We contribute to the debate by presenting simulation evidence that models misspecified in this way are unlikely to pass these evaluation criteria and, therefore, unlikely to pass peer review. Consequently, it is doubtful that the publication record is contaminated with such studies to a meaningful degree.

Second, past studies addressing the effects of measurement model misspecification have applied inappropriate population models for endogenous formative latent variables. Therefore, estimates of bias due to misspecification as reflective, obtained from these models, are invalid. We contribute to this issue by correctly specifying the relevant population model, which allows us to accurately assess the degree of estimation bias that results from the misspecification of a formative latent variable as a reflective one.

In the remainder of this paper, we first review commonly employed fit indices and validation rules applied to reflective models. Next, we report on the results of a simulation study where the misspecified latent variable is in an exogenous position. After that, we do the same for the endogenous case. In both scenarios, our results show it would be unlikely that a misspecified model would have been able to satisfy the measurement validation rules commonly employed in the discipline and be thus unpublishable, leading to our conclusion that misspecification is unlikely to be widespread.

## A NOTE ON TERMINOLOGY AND THE NATURE OF LATENT VARIABLES

A part of the disagreement and confusion in the formative modeling literature is due to the loose use of terminology. Therefore, we start by defining the terms that we use. We use the term construct when referring to the conceptual variables in theories. There is widespread agreement in this literature that constructs are neither formative nor reflective; instead, formative and reflective refer to the relationship between a latent variable representing a construct in a statistical model and the observed indicators directly attached to the latent variable. As such, terms like reflective and formative constructs are nonsensical and are not used in the article. Following Bentler (1982), we define a latent variable as “A variable in a linear structural

equation system is a latent variable if the equations cannot be manipulated so as to express the variable as a function of manifest variables only.” (p. 106) This is in contrast to an index, which is a function of observed variables, possibly with some weights which determine the influence of each observed variable in the index. While constructs can be considered latent because they cannot be expressed as functions of their indicators (Borsboom et al., 2003), we use the term latent variable only in the context of statistical models where latent variables can represent constructs. We use the term composite to refer to an index variable constructed as a (weighted) sum of the indicators. A composite variable is thus a formative latent variable without the latent error term. We do not use the term formative measurement because it seems implausible that indicators could cause constructs (Edwards, 2011), and we have not seen any evidence that they do. As such, while formative models can be useful (Markus & Borsboom, 2013, Chapter 8), they are not measurement models, and we do not refer to them as such. For these reasons, we do not use the term formative measurement to refer to the practice of specifying formative models but apply a more neutral term, formative modeling, instead.

Whereas constructs are neither reflective nor formative, regardless of how the relationships between a latent variable and its indicators are specified, all latent variables are, to some extent, reflective. Because a latent variable cannot be expressed as a function of observed variables, it receives its identity from the observed variables that depend on it in the statistical model. These observed variables can be either direct reflective indicators, added for identification purposes (Jarvis et al., 2003), or reflective latent variables, which makes the formative latent variable a second-order factor (Treiblmaier et al., 2011). More specifically, the weights of the formative indicators are defined so that the formative latent variable maximally explains the downstream variables (Franke et al., 2008), and the latent error term is defined as the remaining shared variance of the downstream variables that the formative indicators do not explain (Aguirre-Urreta et al., 2016a, 2016b). Whereas the identity of formative latent variables has important yet unexplored consequences for the theoretical interpretation of those, our analysis does not require any particular interpretation. Instead, we ask whether reflective models would have biased results if the data truly originated from a formative model and whether such results would be publishable, thus potentially contaminating the literature with misleading results.

## MODEL FIT INDICES AND MEASUREMENT VALIDATION RULES

Two classes of model quality statistics influence whether a study applying SEMs is publishable in the IS discipline. First, the overall fit of a structural equation model (SEM) is evaluated. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic tests if the model fits exactly and should ideally be non-significant. Because models rarely fit precisely, several approximate fit indices have been introduced to quantify the degree of overall model misspecification (Kline, 2011, Chapter 8). Of these, the most common indices and thresholds (in parentheses) that are considered acceptable in the IS discipline are the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA < 0.08), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI > 0.90), and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR < 0.08).

Second, after the model fit assessment, a second set of rules and cutoffs is used to assess whether the measures demonstrate sufficient reliability and validity. In the IS discipline, the widely used guidelines (e.g., Fornell & Larcker, 1981; Straub et al., 2004) recommend the composite reliability (CR), the average variance extracted (AVE), and the standardized factor loadings be evaluated. These rules establish that the indicators are significantly related to the latent variable and that the latent variable explains at least half of the variance in its attached indicators. Convergent validity and reliability are considered sufficient when standardized loadings are statistically significantly different from zero and greater than or equal to 0.70; all AVEs are at least 0.50; and all CRs are at least 0.70. For discriminant validity, we add the requirement that the AVEs for any two correlated latent variables are greater than their squared correlation. Even if the last rule has been heavily criticized in the methodological literature (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022), we use it because it is widely used in the IS discipline.

While the validity or usefulness of each specific rule, a particular cutoff for a statistic, or the use of cutoffs can and has been debated in the literature (see, e.g., Kline, 2011, Chapter 8), we believe that these two sets of rules (except for a non-significant $\chi ^ { 2 }$ statistic) nevertheless represent the state of the practice that an article including reflectively specified latent variables must reach to be publishable in the IS discipline. First, researchers would not seek to interpret a model that shows marked ill-fit (as denoted by our first set of rules). Second, once this first requirement has been satisfied, any latent variable that fails the validation process and does not show sufficient evidence of convergent and discriminant validity will likely be called into question and possibly eliminated from the model. As a result, any published research that

incorrectly modeled a genuine formative latent variable as a reflective one must have successfully complied with these requirements.

Our main argument, therefore, consists of two parts. First, we argue that if a model with a formative latent variable (formative model) were the correct model for the data but a researcher erroneously specified it as a reflective model, the results would not pass the measurement validation criteria listed above. Second, because models misspecified this way do not pass these validation rules, they are unlikely to be published. Consequently, the publication record is unlikely to be severely contaminated with such misspecified models. As the current literature provides little evidence of how well these misspecified models satisfy the model quality criteria presented above<sup>1</sup>, we conducted two simulation studies addressing the scenarios where the formative latent variable was in exogenous and endogenous positions in the latent variable model.

## First Simulation Design and Analysis

The population model for the first simulation is shown in Figure 1. The model includes three reflective latent variables (<sub>1</sub>-<sub>3</sub>), with paths from the latent variables to their indicators and one formative latent variable () where the paths go from the observed (formative) indicators to the latent variable. Similar models have been employed in relevant past research by Jarvis et al. (2003), Petter et al. (2007), Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012), Howell et al. (2007), Edwards (2011), and MacCallum and Browne (1993), with the only difference that our model in Figure 1 has three latent variables as outcomes of the formative latent variable, while the cited models had only two outcomes (the minimum necessary for identification). Our population model is, in all other respects, the same as used in the past literature in this stream of work<sup>2</sup>. The main focus of our research is on the formative part of the model consisting of the x<sub>i</sub> observed

variables, the formative latent variable $\xi$ to which the observed indicators send regression paths, and an error term, which makes it a formative latent variable (specifically, a causal-formative latent variable, cf. Bollen and Diamantopoulos, 2017).

We vary the specification of the formative latent variable through several design factors. The number of formative indicators was 3, 5, or 7, and the correlation between the indicators varied between conditions using two design factors, one which specified the base value of the correlations at .1, .3, or .5, and another which defined whether the correlations were homogeneous. In conditions where correlations were homogeneous, the same value was used for every correlation; in the heterogeneous conditions, the correlations were varied +/- .10 around the base value. For example, for five indicators and a base correlation value of .5, the vector of correlations would be [.400, .425, .450, .475, .500, .500, .525, .550, .575, .600], used sequentially to build the lower-diagonal of the correlation matrix row-by-row. The relationship between the formative indicators and the latent variable was parameterized with two design factors. The share of variance explained by the formative indicators was .75, .85, or .95. The regression paths $( \gamma _ { \mathrm { { s } } } )$ were homogenous or heterogenous, again using either one value or the base value +/- .10 used sequentially. For example, for the case of 3 indicators, homogenous correlations at .3, variance explained at .85, and heterogenous paths, the vector of gamma coefficients would be [0.317, 0.417, 0.517]. Finally, the sample size was 100, 300, or 500. The paths from the formative latent variable $\xi$ to the reflective latent variables $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { \ n } } _ { 1 } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { - } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \ n } } _ { 3 }$ and the measurement models for $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \Pi } } ^ { - \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \Pi } } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { 3 } }$ were the same for all simulation conditions. The population followed a multivariate normal distribution.

## First Simulation Results

## Misspecification and Convergence Rates

We begin the discussion of our results with an analysis of convergence and admissibility rates for the misspecified models, which have been neglected in prior research on formative

modeling. While the overall convergence and admissibility rate<sup>4</sup> was relatively high (93.67%), a few specific conditions had much lower rates. In particular, conditions with only three formative indicators, weak indicator correlations, and heterogenous formative paths exhibited convergence and admissibility rates of around 50% for all conditions. Also, conditions with three indicators and .1 base correlations (excluding those in the preceding discussion) had poor convergence and admissibility rates in the 60-70% range. The remaining combinations of simulation conditions had acceptable convergence and admissibility rates in the 80-90% and above range. This finding is explained chiefly by inadmissibility: the correlations between the x indicators are implied by the factor loadings estimates. The factor loadings are estimated at low values when these correlations are low. However, these same loadings, together with the correlation between $\xi$ and $\boldsymbol { \mathrm { \ n } } _ { 1 } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { - } } \boldsymbol { \mathrm { \ n } } _ { 3 }$ and loadings of the y indicators, imply the correlations between the x and y indicators. To compensate for the small loadings of the x indicators, the estimated factor correlation increases and ultimately exceeds one, thus becoming inadmissible. Following the rationale that IS researchers would not attempt to interpret the results of a non-converged or inadmissible model, these results suggest that data with weakly correlated formative indicators would rarely produce publishable results if used to estimate a reflective model.

## Detection of Misspecification – Fit Indices

The next concern in model evaluation involves overall model fit. For approximate fit indices, the results are not encouraging (from the perspective of detecting the misspecification of interest). The overall misfit detection rates were 0.7% for RMSEA (where RMSEA > .08), 1.3% for CFI (where CFI < .90), and 9.6% for SRMR (where SRMR > .08). For the case of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ rule, the overall rate of misspecification detection was much better, but still unacceptably low at 41.9%. In all, averaged over all experimental conditions, the power of these indices to detect if a formative latent variable was misspecified as reflective is low. Table 1 shows how frequently each rule detected the misspecification in the analysis model out of the 324 conditions included in the simulation. For example, RMSEA was able to detect the misspecification less than 20% of the time in 318 out of the 324 simulation conditions (or 98% of the total) and was able to detect the misspecification between 21 and 40% of the time in only 6 of the simulation conditions (or 2% of the total). In no simulation conditions was the RMSEA able to detect the misspecification more than 40% of the time.

However, looking at the average rejection rates overall conditions provides an incomplete picture of the results because the rejection rates varied substantially by condition. Therefore, we examined the conditions where at least 80% (the commonly used acceptable power level) of the replications were detected as problematic. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ rule achieved 80% power in a few different scenarios: (1) 5 or 7 indicators and homogenous indicator correlations of 0.5 and across all other levels of all other factors, (2) 5 or 7 indicators and homogenous indicator correlations of 0.3 or 0.5 when the sample size was 300 or 500, and (3) 7 indicators with homogeneous indicator correlations of 0.1 and sample size of 500. The effect of sample size on the power of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ is well-known (Kline, 2011, Chapter 8), and the effect of the number of indicators on power is explained by the fact that when the number of these indicators increases, the share of covariances that are affected by misspecification increases, thus increasing power. The $\chi ^ { 2 }$ rule detected the misspecification 80% of the time or more in only 66 of the 324 conditions included in the simulation design (or 20% of the total). The power levels of the approximate fit indices were much worse. Only the SRMR rule achieved 80% power in just three simulation conditions (or 1% of the time).

In sum, approximate fit indices were generally underpowered. While the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ rule performed better, it, too, had 80% power in just a subset of the experimental conditions. Nevertheless, given that the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ is often ignored, these model fit indices, as currently applied in the IS discipline, do not perform well in detecting formative models misspecified as reflective. If the overall model fit were the only criterion on which analysis results are judged, it would thus be possible that models where formative indicators are misspecified as reflective, would indeed be publishable. In conclusion, model fit indices cannot be relied upon to detect this type of model misspecification reliably.

The requirement that each standardized factor loading must reach a minimum cutoff (.7 in standardized metric) is the best-performing criterion, flagging as problematic 98.7% of the replications across all simulation conditions. For this rule, the lowest performing condition was that with a sample size of 500, 3 formative indicators, base correlations at .5, variance explained of .95, and homogenous formative regression paths and correlations. Even in this worst case, the rule almost reached 80% power, flagging 75.6% of replications as problematic. This result is explained by the observation that in the formative modeling literature, formative indicators are typically argued to be weakly correlated – a feature that guided the choice of our simulation design – and that standardized factor loading estimates depend strongly on these correlations.

The other reliability statistics did not perform as well. The somewhat less common requirement that all loadings be significantly different from zero had an overall effectiveness of only 32.2%. This result varied substantially between simulation conditions. Whereas the rule works very well with 0.1 correlations and 5 or 7 indicators, .3 or .5 base correlations, and homogenous indicator correlations (well above 80% effectiveness and above 90% in many of these cases), the same requirement is very ineffective in this last set of conditions, where the formative indicator correlations are heterogeneous. In most of these, few replications are flagged based on this rule. The requirement that the latent variable exhibit an AVE of at least .5 had a base overall effectiveness rate of 86.1%, only slightly below the rate of inspecting the loadings. This finding is not surprising since the requirement that standardized loading exceeds 0.7 is equivalent to requiring that the squared loading is greater than 0.49, and the AVE is simply the mean of squared loadings. Due to the similarity of these two criteria, the pattern of results for different conditions was very similar to the loading rule and thus not reported in detail. The main difference was that this rule was less affected by the number of indicators.

The overall rejection rate of the CR > .7 rule was 68.7%. While the composite reliability of a set of indicators is based on their loadings, this rule is less sensitive than inspecting the loadings themselves. Further, it becomes even less sensitive with increasing indicators because the index depends on the number of indicators and can reach acceptable levels even with poorly performing indicators as long as the number of indicators is sufficiently large. Indeed, the CR rule performed very poorly in some scenarios: 3 indicators and correlations of .5 (effectiveness less than 10%), 5 indicators, correlations of .5 and heterogeneous formative regression paths (effectiveness 0%), and 7 indicators, correlations of .3 or .5 and heterogeneous formative regression paths (effectiveness less than 10%).

Finally, the overall effectiveness of the rule that the square root of the AVE for each of two correlated latent variables is greater than the correlation between them — generally interpreted as evidence of discriminant validity in the IS discipline — was 46.9%. As before, the effectiveness of the rule varied between simulation conditions, performing very poorly at detecting misspecification when the correlations among the formative indicators were .5

(essentially 0% effectiveness) but also when they were of medium strength (e.g., .3, where the effectiveness of the rule was only in the 40% range). However, when the correlations among the formative indicators were very low, in the .1 condition, this rule could flag almost every replication as problematic (98.4% effectiveness). Taken together, one or more of the rules examined in this first simulation flagged 98.9% of all replications as problematic. We conclude, therefore, that measurement validation rules (unlike model fit indices discussed previously) can be relied upon to detect the misspecification of interest. This has important implications for our contention that widespread publication of misspecified models in the IS literature is unlikely, as we discuss next in more detail.

## Summary of the First Simulation

The first simulation focused on the case of a formative latent variable which is in an exogenous position and has been misspecified as reflective in the estimated model<sup>5</sup>. The main question was how frequently the misspecified model would show acceptable fit based on commonly used fit indices and would have been able to satisfy the commonly used measurement validation rules. If the misspecification would not be detected with this collection of indices and rules, then the claims that such measurement model misspecification is widespread in published research would be tenable. However, results from this first simulation (summarized in Table 3) show this is not the case. While model fit indices generally cannot detect this misspecification, measurement validation rules performed much better. In particular, two of the rules – the requirement that standardized loadings be at least .7 and the AVE of the latent variable be at least .5 – would have detected that most of the replications had a severe problem with the measurement model and would have made it unlikely that these models pass the peer review and publication process.

Moreover, the overall effectiveness rate of the rules as a collection – that any single rule would have flagged a replication as problematic – was 98.9% across all conditions. That is, in this first simulation, only 1.1% of the 324,000 replications, where a set of formative indicators were misspecified as reflective, would have been able to comply with the rules. In empirical applications, the percentage would be even lower because we only considered the converged

replications that resulted in admissible estimates, a subset of all data generated for this first simulation. These results, therefore, strongly contradict the claims that there has been extensive model misspecification in the past (Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007; Podsakoff et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2015). To summarize, as long as the measurement validation rules discussed here are consistently applied in a discipline, as is the case in IS, this type of model misspecification is highly unlikely to appear in the published literature.

## ENDOGENOUS POSITION

## Second Simulation Design and Analysis

The second simulation’s factorial design and estimation procedures were identical to the first study. However, the population model differed, including a formative latent variable () receiving regression paths from three exogenous reflective latent variables $( \xi _ { 1 } \mathfrak { - } \xi _ { 3 } )$ . As previously discussed, studies in the debate thus far (e.g., Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2012; Jarvis et al., 2003; MacKenzie et al., 2005; Petter et al., 2007) have incorrectly modeled the formative indicators as covariates in this scenario; specifically, the latent variables modeled as antecedents to the endogenous formative latent variable had direct paths to the formative latent variable, which is inconsistent with the idea that it is the formative indicators and the error term that determine the formative latent variable; see also Cadogan and Lee (2013) and Bollen and Bauldry (2011) for more extensive discussions on this issue. Due to this incorrect specification of the past population models, little is known about the consequences of misspecifying a formative latent variable, which is in an endogenous position in a research model, as reflective. With this research, we seek to redress that omission.

Specifying effects on a formative latent variable requires a mediation model. Because a formative latent variable is, by definition, a sum of the formative indicators and an error term (e.g., Bollen & Lennox, 1991; Diamantopoulos, 2006), all effects to a formative latent variable must be mediated by these components that it consists of. That is, specifying the effects of the exogenous latent variables $\xi _ { 1 } - \xi _ { 3 }$ on the endogenous latent variable  as fully mediated by the formative indicators $\mathbf { X } 1 { - } \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k }$ (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2014; Cadogan & Lee, 2013; Rigdon et al., 2014).

Our specification of the endogenous formative latent variable is based on the seminal work of Cadogan & Lee (2013, fig. 5) on the proper and improper use, modeling, and specification of endogenous formative latent variables. Specifically, we first specified  as a formative latent variable modeled as a weighted sum of the formative indicators $\mathbf { X } 1 { - } \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k }$ and a formative error term $\zeta ,$ representing omitted causes (Diamantopoulos, 2006). The error term is crucial because, without it, the variable ceases to be a latent variable but becomes a mere composite of the formative indicators (Cadogan & Lee, 2013). Thus, to model the effects of $\xi _ { 1 } - \xi _ { 3 }$ on , we specified another latent variable, $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { \ast } .$ , which was regressed on the exogenous variables $\xi _ { 1 } - \xi _ { 3 }$ with regression path values shown in Figure 3. The model parameters were set so that $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } }$ and $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { * }$ had unit variances in the population model. To model the mediation effects through the formative indicators (and the formative error term, which for data generation purposes is just another indicator), we specified each as depending on the $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { * }$ by regression paths. These paths were parameterized so that the correlation between $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } }$ and $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { * }$ was 1, thus making the two latent variables identical. The error terms of regressions of $\mathbf { X } 1 \ldots \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k } .$ , and $\zeta \mathrm { o n } \eta ^ { * }$ were specified as correlated with a structure that, after adding the correlation due to the common cause $\boldsymbol { \eta } ^ { \ast } .$ produced a structure where the correlations between $\mathbf { X } 1 \ldots \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k }$ were always the same as in the corresponding experimental condition in the first simulation and $\zeta$ was uncorrelated with all $\mathbf { X } 1 \ldots \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k } .$ <sub>.</sub> This variable and path configuration produced a model where the total effects of $\xi _ { 1 } - \xi _ { 3 }$ on $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } }$ were identical to the direct effects of $\xi _ { 1 } - \xi _ { 3 } \mathrm { o n } \ \eta ^ { * }$ and the relationships between $\mathbf { X } 1 \ldots \mathbf { X } \mathbf { k }$ and $\boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { \Pi } }$ were identical to the first simulation.

## Misspecification and Convergence Rates

The overall convergence rate for this second simulation, calculated as the total number of converged replications in each condition over the total number of replications that had to be generated to obtain the desired number of converged replications (i.e., 1,000, as noted above), was 76.8%. This result indicates that when formative indicators for a latent variable in an endogenous position are misspecified as reflective, researchers can expect that their estimated model will not successfully converge to a proper solution about one-quarter of the time. This low convergence rate already limits the likelihood that a misspecified model will enter published research.

The overall convergence rate, however, masks the presence of several conditions where convergence was very problematic. First, there were five conditions in the study where 1,000 converged replications could not be obtained after more than 200,000 attempted replications, leading to the conclusion that researchers operating under these conditions are extremely unlikely to publish anything. Conditions with a sample size of 500, 3 indicators, formative indicator base correlations of 0.1, 95% of the variance explained in the latent variable, and all combinations of homogenous and heterogeneous indicator correlations and paths, as well as the condition with a sample size of 500, 3 indicators, formative indicator base correlations of 0.1, 85% of the variance explained in the latent variable, and heterogeneous indicator correlations and paths. Because these highly problematic conditions failed to generate the 1,000 successful replications we required, we omitted them from further analyses.

Second, convergence rates in the remaining 319 conditions varied across simulation designs, as shown in Table 4. The reasons are the same as in the first simulation. In particular, convergence to an acceptable solution is less likely with few indicators and when their base correlations are weak. Taking these together, the overall convergence rate for conditions with base correlations of 0.1 and 3 indicators was 6.3%, with 5 indicators 26.7%, and with 7 indicators was 51.6%.

## Misspecification and Estimate Bias

Before assessing model fit and measurement quality statistics for the second simulation, we revisit the debate on the extent to which misspecification for a formative set of indicators as reflective leads to bias in the estimates of the parameters of interest (e.g., Aguirre-Urreta &

Marakas, 2012; Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007). Early work argued that bias in the estimates was considerable, thus supporting the contention that measurement model misspecification was a dire problem in the behavioral and social sciences (Jarvis et al., 2003; Petter et al., 2007). Subsequent work, however, showed that such apparent bias was more the result of the scales in which various estimates were expressed. When those estimates were rescaled to a common metric, much of the apparent bias disappeared (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2012). Nevertheless, even after expressing estimates in a common metric, some degree of bias remained in the case of a misspecified latent variable in an endogenous position. However, the recent exchange between Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2014) and Rigdon and colleagues (Rigdon et al., 2014), as well as recent work in this area (e.g., Cadogan & Lee, 2013), raised the concern that these results, demonstrating bias for paths pointing to an endogenous formative latent variable, may not be valid because the simulation models were not consistent with how the formative indicators were conceptually presented. As a result, little is known about the degree to which misspecification as reflective in the endogenous case, does and to what degree it leads to bias in the estimates of the structural parameters relating the misspecified latent variable to others in the research model. Given that our population model used a more logical specification, consistent with the recent debates (Aguirre-Urreta & Marakas, 2014; Cadogan & Lee, 2013), our simulation results provide an opportunity to contribute to this discussion.

## Detection of Misspecification – Fit Indices

We now return to our main topic on whether the commonly used model fit indices and measurement validation rules can detect when formative latent variables are erroneously modeled as reflective. As before, we begin with fit indices and consider measurement validation guidelines in the next section. Whereas the performance of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test was relatively weak, with pockets of adequate performance, that of the approximate fit indexes (e.g., RMSEA, CFI, and SRMR) was abysmal. While the overall ability of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test to detect the misspecification of a formative latent variable as reflective in an endogenous position was 35.6%, that of the RMSEA, CFI, and SRMR was 0.2%, 0.2%, and 1.4%, respectively.

Table 7 shows the relative efficacy of the different fit indices in detecting the misspecification of interest. As these results show, two common fit indices (RMSEA and CFI) could not detect the misspecification in any given simulation condition more than 20% of the time. The performance of the SRMR was slightly better (but still very poor) with six simulation conditions (out of a total of 324, or 2%) where the detection of the misspecification was possible in the 21 to 40% range of all replications in those few conditions; otherwise, the performance of the SRMR is as dismal as that of the CFI and RMSEA indexes. These results are discussed in more detail next.

Next, we consider the requirement that loadings be significantly different from zero. The overall effectiveness of this rule in detecting the misspecification of interest was very low (5%), ranging from being completely ineffective (i.e., 0% detection rate) in the majority of the conditions (210 out of a total of 324 conditions, or 65% of those) to an effectiveness rate in the 60% range in a subset of conditions (with maximum effectiveness of 67% for a sample size of 100, 7 formative indicators, base indicator correlations of 0.1, 85% of the variance explained in the formative latent variable, heterogeneous formative correlations, and homogeneous formative paths). Aside from this pocket of effectiveness, it seems reasonable that the rule cannot be reliably depended upon to detect the misspecification.

Continuing, we consider the rule that the AVE of a set of indicators must be at least 0.5 (or 50%), such that, on average, each indicator has more variance in common with the latent variable it presumably measures than it does with random measurement error. The overall effectiveness of this rule was 43%, but its performance varied widely across subsets of simulation conditions. The rule performed very well, in the 90% or higher range, for all conditions where the base formative correlations were 0.1 and across levels of all other simulation factors. The rule also performed very well, in the 80-90% range, for conditions with a sample size of 100, 7 formative indicators, base formative indicator correlations at 0.3, and homogenous formative indicator correlations, as well as for the same conditions with a sample size of 300 or 500. In the 60-70% range, performance was adequate for conditions with 5 formative indicators, base formative correlations at 0.3, and homogenous formative correlations (and across all levels of the other design factors). This indicates that it would be improbable for formative indicators with weak or medium correlations to pass off as reflective indicators. However, the AVE rule cannot reliably detect misspecification when the formative indicators are strongly correlated.

Next, we turn to the requirement that the CR of the set of reflective indicators of a latent variable is at least 0.7, deemed the minimum acceptable threshold for interpreting the results obtained from any relationships involving the focal latent variable. The overall effectiveness of this measurement validation rule is 59%, but, as before, in the case of the AVE rule, its

performance depends on the particular set of conditions under examination. First, the rule has a high effectiveness of 90% or more when the base formative correlation was 0.1 or when the base correlation was 0.3, and there were 3 or 5 formative indicators regardless of any other design factors. Second, the rule also performed very well, at similar effectiveness rates, when formative indicator correlations were homogenous at 0.3 and across other simulation design factors. Finally, the rule had a somewhat adequate performance for detecting the misspecification, in the 60% range, for those conditions with heterogeneous formative indicator correlations, base correlations at 0.3, and 5 indicators. Overall, it seems unlikely that formative indicators that are not at least strongly correlated would be able to be validated as reflective when misspecified as such.

Finally, we consider the effectiveness of the requirement that the square root of the AVE for each of the two correlated latent variables be greater than the correlation between them (e.g., a discriminant validity test). The overall effectiveness of this rule in detecting the misspecification was 28% and, as was the case with the previous two rules, entirely dependent on the particular simulation conditions under consideration. First, the rule was very effective, in the 90% and upwards range, for those conditions with base formative indicator correlations at 0.1, and sample size of 300 and 5 or 7 indicators or sample size of 500 for any number of formative indicators (and, for all these, across levels of all other simulation factors). Second, the rule had effectiveness in the 70-80% range in conditions with a base formative indicator correlation of 0.1 and 5 or 7 indicators and a sample size of 100, or 3 indicators and a sample size of 300. Finally, in the 50-60% range, the rule was only somewhat effective for those conditions with a sample size of 100, 3 formative indicators, and a base formative correlation of 0.1. As before, it seems highly unlikely that formative indicators that are only weakly correlated would be able to comply with the measurement validation rules commonly applied to reflective indicators.

## DISCUSSION AND IMPLICATIONS

The measurement model misspecification debate has reached an impasse. Both proponents of dire consequences and those of limited effects, if any, of misspecification have expressed their positions in published research but with no apparent resolution of the underlying disagreement. Whereas much of the original work in this area dealt with this issue in conceptual terms, based on researcher experience, judgment, and intuition, this study, and the work on which it builds, have addressed the problem from a modeling standpoint. This approach has provided evidence that many conceptual discussions are not wholly consistent when translated into the statistical models that represent the conceptual models. This disconnect has fueled some exciting debates in the literature.

We make two main contributions to this debate. Most importantly, we consider whether widespread misspecification of formative indicators as reflective is likely in published IS research. Whereas Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012) showed that the consequences of this misspecification, in terms of bias in structural parameter estimates of interest, may not be as dire as previously thought, Jarvis et al. (2012) correctly noted that these new results did not speak to the issue of whether misspecification is widespread, only that its consequences may be less problematic. This research addresses this concern by considering whether a set of formative indicators misspecified as reflective would have satisfied the commonly used reflective model validation rules required to pass the peer review and publication process. In particular, we focused on both fit indices and measurement validation rules and had the misspecified variable in both exogenous and endogenous positions in the population model.

The results from both our simulations demonstrate that one or more of these rules (in particular, the measurement validation practices) would have flagged the misspecified latent variable as problematic in the vast majority of the conditions we studied: for the exogenous case, in 98.9% of the replications, across all levels of the various simulation design factors examined here; for the endogenous case, in 98.8% of the cases, across the same. Moreover, convergence was a significant problem in several conditions in the endogenous scenario, providing yet another hurdle that misspecified models may be unable to overcome. Therefore, it is unlikely that a formative measure misspecified as reflective would have successfully survived the peer review and publication process. This is strong evidence against the position that the misspecification of formative indicators as reflective is widespread in our literature, which many seminal articles on formative modeling in the IS discipline have advocated.

Second, we address the issue that prior research has used an invalid population model to simulate data for an endogenous formative latent variable when assessing the bias that the kind of misspecification may cause. Our results indicate that the bias, while not as severe as previously thought, can still be substantial at times, ranging from -16% to +39%, depending on the particular simulation condition (e.g., the combination of levels from the various manipulated factors in our simulation design). Interestingly, our results show that estimate bias can also be negative; therefore, a misspecified model may provide more conservative estimates in some conditions. This occurs when the formative indicators are strongly correlated (base correlation of 0.5 in our design) and when the variance explained by the formative indicators in their latent variables is lower (our research examined the cases of 75%, 85%, and 95% variance explained by the formative indicators). Moreover, heterogenous formative indicator correlations make this negative bias more pronounced.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, our research relies on various rules that we assume are applied in the research and peer review process. Nevertheless, misspecified models might be accepted for publication if these rules are not applied consistently in the IS discipline’s peer review process. However, we believe researchers would be hard-pressed to publish their work in the discipline’s premier journals if they could not satisfy the rules we studied. The inclusion of these measurement validation rules was motivated by research practice in the discipline and the standards set by Gefen et al. (2000; see, for example, their Table 12) and Straub et al. (2004; see, for example, their Table 3) and our own observations on what rules are applied in SEM articles published in leading IS journals. When extending our findings to other disciplines, we should note that our results rely on the regular application of the measurement validation rules we discussed. If this is not the case in a discipline and models are evaluated solely based on model fit indices, these misspecifications would not be reliably detected. As a result, in disciplines where these measurement validation rules are either not common or consistently enforced, measurement model misspecification (of this particular type, formative as reflective) may be common. However, as discussed below, disciplines where a similar measurement validation system is not followed seem rare.

Second, all our results here are based on two population models, one for each misspecification scenario we examined. Other structural and measurement models (with more or fewer latent variables, relationships, etc.) could be used to examine these issues. While we believe our underlying logic has universal applicability, in that the fit and measurement

validation rules do not depend on the structure of the focal model – that is, they are intended to be universal rules – it is nonetheless the case that other base models could be used to replicate our findings.

Third, though we examined multiple combinations of several design factors within each of the two main misspecification scenarios of interest, other features of the research model or different levels for those included here could be considered to extend further and validate our results. Many of these limitations also present meaningful opportunities for future research.

Before we address the implications of our findings, we want to raise two broader issues on generalizability. While we discussed various model fit statistics and measurement validation heuristics, our focus was strictly on the specific misspecification of a formative latent variable as reflective. While we demonstrated that model validation rules are very powerful in detecting the misspecification we focused on, these results do not mean that other types of misspecifications would also be detected by the same rules and guidelines. Researchers interested in other types of misspecifications, such as, for example, the correct number of factors underlying a set of reflective indicators (e.g., whether a one-factor model or a two-factor model may better represent the actual underlying structure of the data), should consult the methodological literature that studies this question specifically (Rönkkö & Cho, 2022) or up-to-date textbooks resources (e.g., Kline, 2023) that provide reviews of this more technical work.

Whereas we focused on the model and measurement validation criteria used in the IS discipline, we believe the results are applicable more broadly. Unlike the model fit indices (e.g., CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR) that performed poorly, the measurement validation rules we studied essentially prevent misspecified models of this type from being publishable. This means that our results should directly apply to other disciplines, such as marketing, which, like IS, follow the “Fornell-Larcker” tradition of model evaluation (e.g., Fornell & Larcker, 1981). Whereas other disciplines may use different rules, it is encouraging that the factor loadings, which are — as far as we know — used universally, were highly effective in detecting the misspecification we focused on. Moreover, given that the CR statistic is often nearly identical to the ubiquitous alpha reliability coefficient (Peterson & Kim, 2013), our results should also apply to disciplines relying on this statistic. However, we would be remiss not to note that the regular and widespread application of the measurement validation guidelines discussed here is necessary for generalizing our results to disciplines other than those, such as IS, where those guidelines are consistently followed and enforced.

Our research contributes to understanding the extent and consequences of the misspecification of formative indicators as reflective. The seminal work in this area (e.g., Jarvis et al., 2003) has made two central claims about the misspecification of interest. First, the misspecification of formative indicators as reflective was widespread in the business disciplines, particularly in marketing, leadership, and IS (see Petter et al., 2007 for the latter). Second, this misspecification has led to extensive bias in structural parameter estimates of prime theoretical interest. Subsequent research by Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012) showed that much of this bias resulted from the metric in which results were expressed and largely disappeared (for the exogenous case) when restated in a comparable metric. However, given that the population model for the endogenous case was later shown to be misspecified, little is known about estimate bias due to misspecification in that scenario. Here, we sought to quantify that bias and show that while not necessarily minor, it is also not always upwards. In this case, misspecification can also lead to a downward bias in the estimates.

However, the more accurate assessment of estimate bias did not address the first of the two major claims discussed above: that misspecification is widespread in the discipline. The basis for this claim, which has been criticized by others as well (Hardin & Marcoulides, 2011), is the interpretation of the authors of past research as to what should be, conceptually, the directionality of the relationship between a construct and its associated measures. In this research, however, we do not rely on armchair theorizing but rather argue that any set of measures that are specified as reflective (including those that are genuinely formative but which have been inadvertently misspecified, which is the main scenario of interest here) should be able to comply with a set of fit indices and measurement validation rules commonly recommended and employed in the discipline for reflective models. Our results show that formative indicators that are misspecified as reflective would, almost exclusively, not pass these checks; in particular, they would be flagged by the measurement validation guidelines (but not by the model fit indices, which are unreliable methods for detecting this misspecification). Therefore, we argue that widespread misspecification of formative indicators as reflective is not likely in the discipline.

However, our results and the work on which they build should not be taken to mean that the issue of the appropriate and accurate latent variable specification is moot. Even if the consequences of misspecifying latent variables do not seem to be too problematic (e.g., Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas, 2012, and our results about bias) and the misspecifications we studied are likely to be detected during the measurement validation process, our results do not mean that measurement model specification does not matter or that the choice of measurement model specification can be outsourced to measurement model quality heuristics. For example, the fact that a reflective model fails model quality checks does not mean that a formative model is appropriate because reflective models can also fail in other ways (e.g., cross-loadings). In contrast, the appropriate measurement and operationalization of the constructs in our theories is as fundamental an issue as the relationships between the constructs themselves. Poor or inaccurate measurement makes it impossible to appropriately formulate and test theories, as one can never be sure whether equivocal results result from the poor measurement of the constructs, inaccurate development of the relationships in the theory, or a combination of both. For the specific scenario of causal-formative indicators and the corresponding formative latent variables, there is still a need for measurement theory that outlines when and how those should be employed (e.g., Hardin, 2017; Markus, 2018).

Statistical issues aside, there is great value in understanding the proper direction of the relationship between items and latent variables or the constructs they represent in a research model. A largely ignored issue in these discussions has to do with formative items representing presumed causes of the latent variable to which they are attached, whether observed items can be causes in themselves, an issue that has been contended in the literature (e.g., Edwards, 2011), or whether they should be taken as representing a latent variable which is itself the posited cause of the formative latent one (cf., MacCallum & Browne, 1993). What is clear is that these formative indicators (themselves or as representations of an intervening latent variable) provide mechanisms by which antecedent constructs impact the focal ones. That is, because the set of formative indicators either represents antecedents to a construct or mediates the effect of those on the focal construct (a position consistent with the design of our second simulation study), they offer avenues for exploring how a construct of interest can be affected, either by experimental manipulation or as a consequence of affecting change into an antecedent. This is something for which reflective indicators are not well suited, as they can only be used to measure the level of a latent variable but provide no insight into the (theoretical) antecedents and reasons why that latent variable would have the level they measure. Although still underdeveloped, this is an interesting perspective on using formative indicators (whether independently or as representative of an unmeasured latent variable) for theory development. However, accurate and properly specified measurement is paramount for this to be the case.

Taken together, our work and Aguirre-Urreta and Marakas (2012) paint a very different picture of the current status of measurement model misspecification (formative as reflective) in our literature. That is not to say that appropriate model specification is not a major goal in and of itself; in that much, we largely agree with Jarvis et al. (2012). We also agree with the renewed call by Hardin (2017) on the need to develop measurement theory that supports the use of formative (e.g., causal-formative) indicators in research while at the same time recognizing that it may be challenging to do so (Markus, 2018). However, we contend, and support with this research, that widespread misspecification – which would invalidate large swaths of information systems research – in the discipline is unlikely, and urge researchers to continue ensuring that proper measurement development and validation rules are enforced for the case of reflective specifications, and further developed for the case of formative ones (which are still in flux; see Hardin, 2017), as an important safeguard against specification errors in future work.
