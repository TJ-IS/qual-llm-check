## Abstract

Renewing digital platforms is increasingly vital to ensure organisational performance and competitiveness. Managing such renewal is challenging, however, because it requires organisations to remove their exiting digital platform while at the same time building on the practises that depend on it in order to implement a new platform. Unfortunately, the literature offers little guidance on how to launch and manage this inherently complex process. Against this backdrop, we conducted a three-year case study of how a local government organisation organised and managed the renewal of its digital platform, which 4000 health professionals in eldercare use on a daily basis. We use two concepts— framing and overflowing—to reflect the complexity of the process and to describe how it unfolded. Initially, managers used persuasive language to carefully frame a vision of the change and prepare platform users for the renewal process. Despite these framing efforts, however, unexpected events led to overflow situations in which events rendered the framing untenable and threatened successful renewal. This, in turn, led to a decision to postpone the new platform's golive date. While the postponement did defuse the situation, new overflows emerged, and management was forced to initiate further framing activities to move the platform renewal forward. Based on our insights into these events and extant literature, we present a conceptual model of framing and overflowing in organising and managing digital platform renewal that unpacks how framing–overflowing dynamics play out over time in response to the complexity of the process.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Renewal of digital platforms has become increasingly vital for organisations to sustain and advance their competitiv positions (Agarwal & Helfat, 2009; Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Bhatt & Grover, 2005; Piccoli & Ives, 2005). To survive and grow, organisations must periodically replace legacy technology and implement new digital platforms that advance their portfolio of applications; doing so allows them to adapt to changing environments, continuously transform business models, and realise strategic goals (Barrett et al., 2006; Kohli & Melville, 2019; Wessel et al., 2021) Defined as ‘the activity through which an organization seeks to refresh or replace its digital platforms and infrastruc tures to realise strategic goals’ (Wimelius et al., 2021, p. 199), digital platform renewal is a crucial topic for researc and practise (Baiyere et al., 2023). Implementing organisation-wide digital platforms—such as commercial offthe-shelf systems or electronic patient record (EPR) systems—is an attractive option for private and public organisa tions (Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023), but these platforms eventually must be replaced

Renewing digital platforms is challenging because of its complexity. Many platforms involve numerous actors and technologies, which makes it difficult to understand, let alone communicate, their various tasks and how thos tasks interact. Another reason for the difficulty is that the information needed for tasks is often insufficiently avail able and reliable, and task outcomes are often uncertain (Daft & Lengel, 1986; Sandberg et al., 2014). Further because the technologies being replaced contain the scaffold for building the new technologies' use practise (Gregory et al., 2015; Mehrizi et al., 2022; Wimelius et al., 2021), managers must simultaneously remove their tech nological foundation and build on the practises that depend on it to implement a new technological foundation (Wimelius et al., 2021. p. 200). Managers are, as Beane (2023) articulates it, torn between new and familiar technol ogies’ (p. 2) and social and technical inertia make legacy technology hard to abandon (Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023). A these examples show, digital platform renewal is associated with considerable challenges, and it is difficult to orga nise and manage (Beane, 2023; Gregory et al., 2015; Mehrizi et al., 2019; Wimelius et al., 2021).

How managers use framing (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014; Goffman, 1974) to create a collective understandin of this platform replacement is therefore crucial to launching the renewal process and setting the direction of the project's path (Wimelius et al., 2021). However, while extant framing studies in information systems (IS) research emphasise the importance of cognitive (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) and strategic framing (Barrett et al., 2013), the largely ignore the technology—and thus the relation between existing and future technologies (Essén & Värlander, 2019; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). Specifically, previous studies stress the incongruences and inconsistencies in how different actor groups frame sociotechnical phenomena (Lin & Silva, 2005; Young et al., 2016), and they show how managers use rhetorical framing strategies to shape employee interpretations of technology-enable change (Azad & Faraj, 2011; Davidson, 2002). Such perspectives tend to downplay how framing is established and plays out over time (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014), however, and they fail to foreground how managers handle the complexity in sociotechnical change processes such as digital platform renewal (Gregory et al., 2015; Sarke et al., 2019: Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023). This is unfortunate since the relationship between existing and future technologies in platform renewal is anything but unproblematic (Wimelius et al., 2021; Mehrizi et al., 2022; Rinta Kahila et al., 2023; Beane, 2023).

Viewing digital platform renewal as an inherently challenging sociotechnical change, we therefore connect fram ing to the notion of overflowing (Callon, 1998a)—that is, situations in which events render the framing untenable and threaten successful renewal. From this perspective, framing of platform renewal inevitably creates overflowing and managers must address and accommodate those overflows by attending to the related issues through reframing While framing–reframing is intentional and purposeful, overflowing is the unplanned excess, surplus, and overspil that omits the framing to such a degree that it causes a new framing. Adding overflowing offers a complementar perspective that emphasises the importance of considering the nature and character of the overflowing issues impli cated in framing–reframing. Consequently, by combining framing and overflowing, we disclose limits in how IS research perceives framing and expand this perception by providing a nuanced account of the process of managing the complexity in digital platform renewal. Thus, we ask: How do framing and overflowing shape the organisation an management of digital platform renewal processes?

We address this question through a three-year qualitative field study in which we investigated digital platform renewal in a Danish local government organisation that was replacing its existing EPR system with a new digita platform—a process that affected the work of 4000 health professionals in eldercare. We observed how managers expended considerable resources to frame the renewal in a positive way—using persuasive language, online games, visual posters, and more—to establish a vision for change. While these activities served to frame the digital platform renewal to the health professionals in a particular way, the framing also caused problems and produced overflows expressed as setbacks, changes of direction, and tensions and controversies over the existing and new platforms Consistent with extant framing research (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014), these overflowing events broke the initia framing and led to changes in the renewal's path, including major reorganising of the project and postponement of the new technology's original go-live date, which then led to even more overflowing. However, while these over flowing issues broke the initial framing, the reactions were in fact productive for the renewal because of the wa managers handled the framing–overflowing dynamics and used them to define and constitute the renewal path

Our findings provide key insights into the emerging literature on digital platform renewal by revealing in detail how the renewal process was organised and managed through framing–overflowing dynamics, defined by managers framing efforts and their responses to overflowing. Specifically, we provide a processual account of how framing– overflowing dynamics played out over time in response to the inherently complex nature of the renewal process. A such, we also address scholarly calls for advancing knowledge on framing processes in IS research (Azad & Faraj, 2011; Davidson, 2006; Spieth et al., 2021).

In the following, we review the literature on digital platform renewal and framing, and add overflowing to our conceptualization. We then provide a description of our research design and method choices before presenting a detailed case analysis. We close by discussing our empirical findings and theoretical advances on the role of framing and overflowing in organising and managing digital platform renewal.

## 2 | THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

## 2.1 | Digital platform renewal

While digital platform renewal is expected to transform how organisations operate in terms of the scale, scope speed and sources of value creation (Agarwal & Helfat, 2009; Baiyere et al., 2023; Bharadwaj et al., 2013; Bhatt & Grover, 2005), there is no guarantee that renewals will succeed—or do so in a timely fashion (Barker & Frolick, 2003 Davenport, 1998). The switching costs of replacing existing platforms are extensive, and implementing new ones is a complex undertaking (Gregory et al.. 2015: Montealegre et al.. 2019: Wimelius et al., 2021) that consumes budgets and places significant demands on organisations' scarce resources (Beane, 2023). The multilevel and evolutionar nature of resistance to implementing new systems (Lapointe & Rivard, 2005) likewise poses severe impediments (Furneaux & Wade, 2017). Therefore, replacing supply chain management (SCM) or enterprise resource planning (ERP) platforms is not simply a matter of implementing new technical features. It is a highly comprehensive organisational change process (Leonardi, 2009) that impacts professional boundaries and requires careful balancing of interdependences between existing and future technologies (Beane, 2023; Mehrizi et al., 2019; Wimelius et al., 2021). Hence, while digital platform renewal shares similarities with the broader technology implementation phenomenon, it differs because of the important role that legacy platform dependencies play (Mehrizi et al., 2022 Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023).

Recently, IS research has examined the complex nature of technology renewal (Montealegre et al., 2019; Danneels & Viaene, 2022; Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023), describing how replacing an organisation's technology necessarily builds on practises that depend on the existing technology to shape use of the new technology (Gregory et al., 2015; Wimelius et al., 2021). These tensions pose considerable challenges; specifically, status quo bias and leg acy technology habits often produce technical and organisational inertia (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Polites & Karahanna, 2012; Lee & Joshi, 2017; Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023) and lead to post-implementation workarounds (Azad & King, 2008; Tucker et al., 2020). Even when the new technology's uptake proceeds as intended and aligns with its purpose, it may simultaneously catalyse unintended negative consequences that counteract and defeat that original purpose (Mazmanian et al., 2013).

Another factor that has important consequences for organising and managing digital platform renewal is that some users retain their legacy technology habits even as the new technology takes over (Gregory et al., 2015; Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Polites & Karahanna, 2013). While such habits can inhibit change, they can also act as a bridge letting users rely on certain legacy habits when adopting the new platform, as well as serving as a legacy deterrent by enhancing dissatisfaction with the old technology (Mehrizi et al., 2022). To achieve such positive effects, organisa tions must be able to manage, coordinate, and prioritise work on many fronts (Montealegre et al., 2019, p. 649) Despite the clear stakes outlined here, the emerging research on digital platform renewal has yet to elaborate on management strategies (Gregory et al., 2015) as framing plays out over time in response to the complex nature of platform renewal. As such, while previous studies tend to cast user resistance as central (Kim & Kankanhalli, 2009; Lapointe & Rivard, 2005; Rivard & Lapointe, 2012; Shirish & Batuekueno, 2021), our focus on framing–overflowing links the implementation challenges to the intrinsic relationships between the existing and new digital platforms

## 2.2 | Framing in digital platform renewal

Framing is a ubiquitous concept that spans multiple disciplines, and while the various usages of frames and framing have muddied the concept (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014), it remains vibrant and relevant for studying meaning making and sense-giving in organisations (Azad & Faraj, 2011; Giorgi, 2017; Klein & Amis, 2021; Reinecke & Ansari, 2021). With roots in sociology and popularised by Goffman (1974), frames were originally considered a ‘schemata for interpretation’ with framing representing the orchestration of various events, symbols, and ideas to manage interactions among organisational actors. In terms of IS research, two main literature streams have gained ground, focusing on cognitive and strategic perspectives.

The cognitive perspective examines how individuals or groups activate cognitive frames to make sense of technology (Kaplan & Tripsas, 2008; Olesen, 2014; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994). In digital platform renewal and other change processes, actors use cognitive imageries to make sense of the change, applying varied frames of reference that may inhibit shared understandings (Boland, 1978; Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) and lead to specific frames domi nating among specific actor groups (Olesen, 2014). Because platform renewal involves diverse actors with varying commitments, groups often pull in opposite directions due to experiential and interpretive variations (Wimeliu et al., 2021). Thus, it becomes important to address frame incongruence among actor groups (Hsu, 2009) and frame inconsistencies within actor groups (Young et al., 2016) to avoid misalignments. Lin and Silva (2005) show how th nature of technology frames is dynamic rather than static, as language, communication, and symbolic power continually modify such frames. Given this susceptibility, frames are naturally highly political, infused with multiple meanings and ever-evolving (Azad & Faraj, 2011).

The strategic perspective focuses on controlling discourse through rhetorical framing strategies (Barrett et al., 2013) that allow frames to shift into and out of salience (Davidson, 2002). Barrett et al. (2013) define framing as employing certain rhetorical strategies with intent to represent the issue in a certain light and invite others to see it in this light; for example, in positive or negative terms or with certain associations and connotations’ (p. 207). Digi tal platform renewals may be framed as opportunities or threats (Kennedy & Fiss, 2009) or as a way to strategically justify technology investments (Polykarpou et al., 2018). Leonardi (2011) makes a case for how organisations strategically mobilise and activate cultural resources to frame the ways in which employees construct problems with new technologies. Kaplan (2008) advocates for framing as an ex-ante process through which organisations strategicall produce decisions and mobilise action in their favour—or, as Azad and Faraj (2011) formulate it, ‘framing is effec tively an invitation by the framing actor to the audience to read and interpret the targeted discourse about IT con figuration’ (p. 37). Managers may, for example, use framing devices—such as metaphorical phrases, slogans, and stories—to influence how actors make sense of technology and make new technologies workable (Davidson, 2006) and to shape ‘how people notice and interpret what is going on’ (Fiss & Zajac, 2006, p. 1174). Azad and Faraj (2008) argue for focusing on frame evolution and negotiating ‘truce frames’; similarly, Sandeep and Ravishankar (2016) cal for reshaping and altering viewpoints to align competing frames.

In IS framing research, these two perspectives offer a sociotechnical foundation for highlighting cognitive and strategic issues (Majchrzak & Markus, 2013; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001; Sarker et al., 2019). However, while this research provides rich insights into the key role of cognitive and strategic framing perspectives, it tends to ignore the actual technology (Essén & Värlander, 2019; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001) and to downplay the relationship between existing and future technologies in renewal proiects (Beane. 2023: Gregory et al., 2015: Mehrizi et al., 2019; Montealegre et al., 2019; Wimelius et al., 2021). As such, while current research problematises frames it typically focuses on how organisations fix broken frames by mobilising reframing to buttress a frame and secure the desired outcome (Azad & Faraj, 2011; Cornelissen & Werner, 2014). This is problematic because elements outside the frame do not cease to exist and will easily find ways to challenge the framing's performance (Callon, 1998a; Mouritsen et al., 2022). To address this, we include the relationship between existing and future platforms, and explain how and why omitting that relationship may cause an overflow; this leads to a new, mor effective framing. That is, to afford a thorough, fine-grained analysis of the constitutive elements of organising and managing digital platform renewal, we combine framing with the sociology of technology's notion of over flowing (Callon, 1998a; Callon, 1998b).

## 2.3 | Overflowing in digital platform renewal

As discussed, framing identifies and selects key aspects to consider to make platform renewal meaningful and valu able for the organisation at large. Mouritsen et al. (2022) describe how framing creates clarity, reduces ambiguity defines priorities, and produces focus that are mobilised by its tools and makes it perform’ (p. 4). By doing this, how ever, framing also draws up a boundary that separates factors that are inside and outside of platform renewal; i thereby excludes particular aspects from scrutiny and consideration. Hence, while framings appear to draw fixed an sealed boundaries, they are in fact permeable and influenced by these externalities (Callon, 1998a). In contrast to most IS framing research, Callon therefore suggests that framing is always incomplete because there are always rela tions between people, practises, and technologies that defy framing (Callon, 1998b, p.17). The performing of a framing is typically challenged by problematization of what is inside and outside that framing, causing overflowing— that is, the ‘excess or surplus that cannot be accommodated in the space available’ (Czarniawska & Löfgren, 2019, p. 4).

The overflowing notion has been used as an analytical lens in various research contexts, including innovating accountability reporting (Christensen & Skærbæk, 2007), coping with excess people and activities in open workspace (Dudek, 2019), explaining the near-bankruptcy of a newspaper (Raviola, 2019), and understanding management control practises in supply chain (Mouritsen et al., 2022). Czarniawska and Löfgren (2019) emphasise the interrelated ness between framing and overflowing by identifying how ‘there can be no overflow until some flow has bee framed’ (p. 5). Therefore, while framing has an ordering effect (Latour, 2005), overflowing messes it up and produces a need for reframing, which in turn affords opportunities for new overflowing to arise (Callon, 1998b). What make the framing–overflowing dynamics challenging to manage is how ‘overflows have many sources and can flow in many directions’ (Callon, 1998a, p. 253). For instance, overflowing does not occur solely due to disfunction and destructive tensions; it also happens when things move forward successfully

There are two approaches to managing framing–overflowing dynamics (Callon, 1998a). One is to view framing as the norm and overflows as leaks. In this view, framing must be upheld at all costs to continue forward movement and to maintain legitimacy. Here, overflowing represents a frame breakdown—that is, a situation in which expected calculations have failed and more technological, organisational, or symbolic investments are needed to contain and channel the overflow. Such cases include a priori efforts that seek to identify possible overflows and thereby design more effective framings that do not fail or break. The second approach views overflowing as the norm and framin as expensive and always imperfect. In this view, framing is always permeable and incomplete; it is therefore fragil and requires costly investments to be established and maintained. For that reason, iron-clad framing is not the norm but a rarity, while overflowing is not a failure but rather is always happening as a constitutive part of framing

In both approaches, if actors involved in framing cannot reconcile conflicting perspectives, it may be difficult to cool down the situation (Callon, 1998a, pp. 260–261) and achieve a workable consensus. It is therefore important to identify and address both the sources and impacts of overflows so that they can be addressed and accommodate in the ongoing framing and reframing process. The two approaches provide different responses to such accommoda tion, however, and hence forge different paths. When overflows are expected, managers recognise the multiple per spectives from outside of the framing and the ‘heat they generate’ (Callon, 1998a, p. 264). When overflows are see as anomalous, managers seek closure by producing stronger framings to avoid these outside connections and thei accompanying ‘heat.

In summary, existing IS research offers detailed insights into strategic framing efforts (Barrett et al., 2013 Davidson, 2006) and how actors perceive and make sense of them (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994; Young et al., 2016). However, this framing research is relatively static (Azad & Faraj, 2011, pp. 37–38) and lacks a detailed accounting of both the relationship between existing and future technologies and how framing evolves over time (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014). To advance our understanding of framing in digital platform renewal, a more nuanced view is required. Here, we offer such a view by considering how framing and overflowing interrelate, as well as by investi gating the situations, incidents, and activities that create a framing overflow and thereby trigger the need for reframing efforts.

## 3 | RESEARCH METHODS

## 3.1 | Research site

Our research is based on a case study of digital platform renewal in a local government provider of elderly care ser vices in Denmark. Services for the elderly are a cornerstone of Denmark's publicly financed welfare system, wit nursing homes—owned and operated by the local governments—offering extensive homecare services includin assistance with personal care, training, and housekeeping (Nielsen & Andersen, 2006). Although local government have considerable autonomy over the substance and extent of their eldercare services, pressures for them to digitalise and document practises have increased in recent years (Nielsen et al., 2022). As a case in point, Loca

Government Denmark—the association for all 98 Danish local governments—has mandated a third generation of their standard work procedure (‘Shared Language’), which requires changes in health professionals' documentation practises. The new procedures aim to enable uniform documentation and improve data use and sharing acros eldercare departments.

Our case organisation had to follow this new standard and method for data exchange across eldercare and general healthcare areas. In addition to being outdated, the organisation's ERP system—which had been in use for 25 years—did not support the new Shared Language mandate. The supplier of the platform therefore decided to phase it out, and the organisation had to abandon and replace it with a new platform to future-proof the quality and safety of operations and services. The new platform, CURA, included built-in mobility, but many health professional had yet to integrate smartphones and tablets into their work practises. In addition, the new platform was highly com plex, as it had to integrate a vast number of applications to process client information and information from hospital and general practitioners.

## 3.2 | Research design

We used a qualitative research approach (Denzin & Lincoln, 1994), which fits well with our goal of providing rich an contextualised descriptions that drill deep into platform renewal organisation and management. Our single cas study approach (Flyvbjerg, 2006) facilitated an intensive empirical account of how managers in the organisation prepared for and organised digital platform renewal. While our commitment to ‘being there’ in the field (Langley & Klag, 2019) afforded opportunity to provide empirical illustrations that account for the specificity of the particular (Tsoukas, 2009), it also forced us to consider how to balance engagement and detachment (Klag & Langley, 2013) To address this tension, we organised data collection and data analysis following a distributed involvement strateg (Langley & Klag, 2019). Accordingly, the first author took part in all data collection and was highly immersed in the case organisation. The second author engaged in initial data collection with the first author to familiarise with the case organisation; otherwise, he was detached from data collection to facilitate critical observations, while also engaging in informed discussions with the first author on data collection issues. The third author did not participate in data collection and served as a valuable ‘outsider’ during both data collection and data analysis. By moving recip rocally along this continuum of engagement and detachment, we strived to balance the insider–outsider roles to strengthen the foundation of our argument (Klag & Langley, 2013).

## 3.3 | Data sources

We concentrated data collection within a specific timespan to allow for a detailed and context-specific view on organising and managing platform renewal. Our primary data collection took place over an 18-month period (September 2018–March 2020). We focused on how managers prepared for a ‘big bang’ implementation of the new digital platform, and how they managed the renewal in the year after. We had ongoing meetings and discussion with the management team that included going further back in time to understand the circumstances that led to launching the platform renewal. These meetings continued during preparation and on through the platform switch a we followed up after the big bang. Figure 1 shows a timeline, with key events indicated below the arrow and dat collection points indicated above the arrow.

To unravel the organisation and management of the digital platform renewal, we relied on three main dat sources: transcriptions of semi-structured interviews, field notes from observations, and archival documents. Table 1 offers a summary of each source.

As described in Table 2, we conducted 51 semi-structured interviews with the management team (directors, IT managers, and project managers); middle managers; and frontline health professionals (nurses, homecare workers and physiotherapists). The interviews took place in the working environments of the interviewees, which gave u opportunities for in situ inquiries and observations before and after the interview. We used an open, explorative approach to interviewing, guided by our focus on managing platform renewal as experienced from different levels of the organisational hierarchy. Our questions for top management interviewees focused on the reasons for the renewal initiative as well as their visions and aspirations for it. We did two rounds of interviews with thes managers—one before and one after the go-live date—to ensure a nuanced understanding of their perspectives. Ou questions were structured around issues related to preparing for and managing the renewal. For instance, we aske how they initially communicated the replacement of the platform, what types of resources they used to support replacement, and how they dealt with potential resistance from health professionals. For the middle managers, our interview questions probed their perspectives on the initiative as intermediaries; we also asked them how th renewal in their experience unfolded. These questions related to how top management organised and manage the initiative and how they, as middle managers, saw their role in managing the renewal. For the frontline health professionals, we also did two rounds of interviews before and after the go-live date. Our initial interview focused on the existing platform—which they were still using at that time—as well as on how they responded to the way man agement had prepared for and organised the platform renewal. In the second round of interviews, we asked about their use of the new platform. These questions focused on the moment of replacement and how they experienced the aftermath. We paid special attention to the clash and overlap between the existing and new platform, as well as to how the process was managed. For instance, we asked about their reactions and any resistances to the change controversies or other matters of concern that may have had an impact on the renewal process.

For our shadowing, we chose six of the 12 health professionals from the interviewing round, and th first author followed them over the course of a full day (Czarniawska, 2007). As with the interviews, his observation during these full-day sessions was open and explorative. He directed attention toward practises related to the use of the digital platform and how the health professionals interacted with and used the platform during a workday. He also paid attention to how the renewal was referred to by health professionals during their daily work

Finally, the organisation shared written documents with us, and we collected others during observation and through our own research. Our data source consisted of more than 200 files, including emails, minutes and slide from meetings, strategy papers, reports, business cases, and educational material. We also collected visual materials such as photographs, drawings, and YouTube videos (Margolis & Pauwels, 2011; Rose, 2007). For instance, during the observation period, the first author made multiple visits to geographically dispersed units within the local government organisation. During these visits, he collected and compiled visual materials and took pictures to document working environments and platform use. The visuals offered another account of how managers prepared for and organised the platform renewal, and how health professionals responded (Meyer et al., 2013, p. 15). Overall, the doc uments were important sources for understanding how managers framed the renewal for the health professionals.

## 3.4 | Data analysis

We analysed our data using a categorization strategy, focusing on the ‘puzzles that challenge the current under standing of the phenomenon at hand’ (Grodal et al., 2021, p. 606). We developed explanations through multiple rounds of data analysis, in which we compared themes and categories adhering to our roles as ‘insiders and outsiders’ (Klag & Langley, 2013). Thus, in each round, the first and second authors conducted the initial analysis as insiders, with the third author serving as an outsider who joined for questioning and clarification.

In our initial approach to the data, we focused on understanding how the organisation could successfully manage platform renewal using a benefit-realisation perspective, with an emphasis on value creation and value capturing. A we immersed ourselves in the data, however, interesting patterns emerged around how managers articulated the renewal in certain ways with certain aspirations, and our research began to mature out on a different pat (Crosina & Pratt, 2019). We then went from being inductive ‘collectors’ to abductive ‘tool users’ (Brinkmann, 2014 p. 722), looking for existing theories as sensitizing notions that inform research but do not determine the scope o perceivable findings’ (Timmermann & Tavory, 2012, p. 173).

During a second round of data analysis, we identified framing (Cornelissen & Werner, 2014; Davidson, 2006) as a suitable concept for understanding platform renewal management. Our empirical story showed how framing revolved around various counter responses and controversies that led to reframing. Through this, we noticed th importance of the voice and presence of the existing platform in how the involved actors made sense of the renewal process, as well as in how tensions emerged between the existing and new platforms, and the ways managers handled and accommodated these tensions. Given the existing categorizations in the IS framing literature we found it puzzling (Grodal et al., 2021) that extant literature scarcely accounted for the existing platform's pres ence and the tensions it engenders. Hence, we engaged in problematization (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) aimed at presenting ‘alternate viewpoints to the existing literature and … how these alternate viewpoints are more appropri ate than the previous ones (Chatterjee & Davidson, 2021, p. 228). To achieve this, we again consulted prior litera ture to find theoretical concepts that could inform our revision of the initial conceptual framing theme. Through this we selected overflowing because it explicitly allows us to trace how framing dynamics play out over time, thu accounting for the complex nature of digital platform renewal. We thus combined framing and overflowing to ‘con vey the theoretical significance of events in an empirical narrative (Berends & Deken, 2021, p. 140). Thi problematization led us to revise our research question to focus on how framing and overflowing shape digital plat form renewal. From this point onward, we shifted from an exploratory approach to a conceptual composition struc ture (Berends & Deken, 2021) in which we took advantage of the notions of framing and overflowing to code and develop our qualitative research narrative

In a third round of data analysis, we coded for organising and managing digital platform renewal using our fram ing and overflowing concepts to ‘label actions, occurrences, or moments to signpost the relationship betwee empirics and theoretical concepts’ (Berends & Deken, 2021, p. 140). We coded the empirical data for framing (i.e., use of pervasive language and articulations, and material and symbolic artefacts) and overflowing (i.e., organisational and technological setbacks, controversies between existing and future platforms, breakdowns and changes of direction) during the platform renewal process. Tables 3–5 show empirical framing and overflowing insights based on our coding and analysis. This process revealed how issues and characteristics related to framing the renewal produced overflows, which, in turn, triggered new framing efforts.

During coding, we identified three formative stages of platform renewal—Launching, Negotiating, and Engaging—which progressed through framing–overflowing dynamics within and across the stages. Launching refers to establishing the platform renewal program, where managers strived to ensure the necessary resources and mobi lise support for the initiative by communicating a vision for change (Spring 2017–Spring 2018). Negotiating refers to mitigating technical platform ruptures, where managers strived to reconcile tensions between the existing and new platform to restore trust in the platform renewal program (Spring 2018–Spring 2019). Engaging refers to facilitatin organisation-wide education for platform renewal, where managers strived to enable use of the new platform (Spring 2019–Spring 2020). Hence, we combined temporal bracketing (the three stages) with thematic bracketin (framing and overflowing) to develop a theorised storyline (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2007) about organising and man aging platform renewal. With this foundation, we then leveraged analytical generalisation (Halkier, 2011; Lee & Baskerville, 2003) to advance conceptual model of framing–overflowing in organising and managing digital platform renewal.

## 4.1 | Launching platform renewal

In the Launching stage, managers focused on committing resources and building expectations for the platform renewal. The management team argued that the renewal had to take place because the existing platform was being phased out and new national legislation for documentation in eldercare created new compliance requirements. For many frontline health professionals, however, the existing platform did not cause problems—it was a platform that facilitated their daily work, and they had developed habits and functional workarounds in relation to it. Early on, th management team underestimated the complexity of replacing the platform, viewing it primarily as a technical pro ject. This framing was quickly problematised by external experts, forcing management to rearrange and mobilise fo large-scale organisational change, framing the renewal in a positive manner to establish a clear vision for this change Hence, framing at this stage was future- and goal-oriented around expected outcomes as managers anticipated th renewal by articulating potential benefit of the new platform. While some health professionals bought into this nar rative, others were not keen on the renewal, and they expressed concerns about replacing the existing platform. ## 4.1.1 | Committing resources

According to the director of administration, 2017 was the ‘right timing’ for engaging in dialogue with suppliers and exploiting possibilities for a new digital platform. Given that the existing EPR platform was phased out, the renewal was quickly approved by top management, and the delivery of a new platform was put out for bids. In June 2017 the management team announced that the digital platform CURA—which was provided as a software package by Danish technology supplier—had won the bidding round. Initially, the renewal was presented as a technical update A project manager was appointed to the project and a kick-off event was scheduled for 6 months later, in November 2017.

This framing was soon problematised to such a degree that the initial plans had to be modified. As the project manager recalls: ‘It became quite clear that it was not the most robust solution for such a big project.’ The project manager's feelings were confirmed when the CURA project was formally presented to the executive steering committee and top management. This meeting only confirmed the misgivings about the setup, including the lack of resources to support the proposed kick-off. At the meeting, representatives from different departments and profes sional domains, as well as external consultants, voiced their concerns. Because the initial framing of the renewa could not address the concerns appropriately, the reactions led to frame breaking and overflowing. The director of administration explained the situation as follows:

Our own project organization was simply not good enough, and we asked for a second opinion from the outside to get input on how to organise the implementation. They came up with a completely different approach than what we had even thought of.

Due to external input challenging the initial framing, the management team recognised how the complexity and mag nitude of the digital platform renewal went far beyond a technical update, requiring a significant increase in resources for project management and a new organisation and management approach. The initial framing had focused mostly on addressing technical features and issues such as system integration and interfaces, and down played organisational issues. The frame problematization, however, surfaced organisational issues as being a critica matter of concern’ for the launch, according to the director

Having realised the need for a new direction, the management team championed a reframing of the platform renewal. In the following months, to secure responsiveness through a robust and flexible setup, they established a larger project organisation within the eldercare department, which was next to the IT department and executive management. Led by the same project manager, the new organisation was supported by a team of 15–20 consul tants with various responsibilities, including configuration, testing, education, operation, and support. As an overflow this major organisational change postponed the original kick-off; it also made the platform renewal far more expen sive than expected and forecasted in the business case. Although this process of bolstering the renewal also turne out to overflow further into other organisational domains due to increased project staffing, it was important becaus it helped frame the renewal as being in professional hands.

## 4.1.2 | Building expectations

The commitment of additional resources to the renewal program created new opportunities for questioning the existing platform and building expectations for the new platform. By ceremonially announcing the commencement of the platform renewal at a big kick-off event in January 2018, managers intensified the building of expectations fo the new technology. Managers from different levels in the organisational hierarchy—along with select healt professionals—were physically present at the kick-off event; everyone else joined via online streaming or watched video shared on the local government's YouTube channel and intranet. The kick-off event was a symbolic representation of prior decisions and massive investments in creating anticipation and expectations for the platform renewal. At the event, the management team emphasised that the renewal was the largest, most complicated implementation the organisation had ever faced and, according to the head of IT, was inevitable because the existing platform ‘did not support the transition to the third generation of Shared Language.’ By highlighting key material features of th existing platform as defective and describing how they could not ‘function as an organization if we do not have thi new digital platform,’ the head of Eldercare and Health problematised the existing platform to make room not only for accepting the renewal, but also for anticipating it with great expectations. Hence, the management team posi tioned key material features of the existing platform as outdated, user unfriendly, and technically unable to meet th increasing demands for delivering high-quality healthcare services.

The management team used this framing to construct the problem and anticipate what could be expected from the new platform. In the wake of the kick-off event, the project management shared video interviews on the organi sation's YouTube channel and on CURA News—a website created to support CURA implementation as a communica tion and information platform for health professionals and management. On this platform, managers articulated and emphasised how key material features of CURA would deliver a high degree of operational reliability and perfor mance, and enable health professionals to work smarter, be more innovative, and improve cross-sector collaboration According to the head of IT, this vision evolved to be the motor that drove the organisation through the platform renewal.

Although the vision was strong within the management team, framing the renewal in this way was not without problems. Conflicting attitudes about the framing of the existing and the new platform found their way into the orga nisation, as illustrated by the following statements (the first from a physiotherapist who took ownership of the over all vision, and the second from a caregiver who did not):

When we have problems with the current platform, then we say with a smile that, ‘We will fix this with the new one. This is something that must be done.’ – Physiotherapis

I'm used to the way by which we document [in the existing platform]—it is so deeply rooted in me. So, in relation to me doing my job, I think the existing platform runs pretty well. There is nothing in this respect that I find hopeless. – Caregiver

This overflowing of organisational issues exemplifies divisions among health professionals in relation to the existing platform, which resulted in some factions pulling the renewal process in opposite directions

## 4.2 | Negotiating platform renewal

In the Negotiating stage, managers focused on mitigating platform ruptures and restoring trust. While dealing with th overflowing of organisational issues (cf. Launching stage), severe technical issues emerged during configuration of th new platform that further accelerated the need for stabilisation. Despite efforts to handle and settle the escalating situ ation, the management team was compelled to postpone the go-live to stabilise the platform renewal. While fearing this postponement would escalate organisational issues anew, the management team carefully framed the postpone ment as necessary to arrive at an acceptable platform solution in agreement with expected outcomes articulated during the launch. Hence, to address the technical overflowing issues, framing changed from revolving around anticipation to focusing on stabilisation. ## 4.2.1 | Mitigating platform ruptures

Despite high expectations and a robust project organisation, critical technical issues forced the management team to handle a challenging situation. Early in summer 2018, the management team ‘felt a tremor,’ as one project manager recalls. Realising that the platform configuration did not go as planned, the management team quickly speeded things up to control the renewal path by fixing the issues and stabilising the situation. However, during this mitigation, th management team became aware of other critical issues—including that the platform did not support key features and functionalities that ‘had been promised by the supplier,’ according to the head of IT. At regular status meetings, the management team assessed the current and associated risks in replacing the existing platform. As further mitigation the team used visualisation to map and categorise risks and problems, with colours indicating the risk levels. During regular follow-ups, approximately 100 employees from various professional domains rated the impact of potential risk as well as their perceived likelihood. Orange represented ‘high consequence,’ which may involve compromises in qual ity, resource allocation, and functionality, according to a risk management consult from the CURA project organisation while red illustrated ‘a very high consequence that impacts the go-live date and implementation.’ These risk diagrams were continually updated and, coupled with minutes from meetings, it was clear that problems began to escalate during spring and summer 2018. At earlier meetings, few risks were discussed and rarely any posed serious problems; all risk and problems were depicted as orange. But in an August 2018 meeting, the number of risks and problems went from five orange risks and one orange problem (as recorded in the May 2018 meeting) to five risks (three of which were red and seven problems (one of which was red). As the director recollects, the problems were serious:

We started to see that the platform did not live up to our vision and our intentions. There were too many problems, too many obstacles. We had to solve this.

To counteract the overflowing of technical issues, managers began to stabilise by aligning CURA's functionalities with organisational expectations. They mobilised and invested significant resources to that end—and replaced th project manager to emphasise the change in direction of the renewal. This new framing was described as a second chance to make it. While it was the biggest implementation the organisation had ever faced, the dedication to suc ceed with the vision was equally pronounced given the stakes. The management team refused to compromise and settle for a solution that did not live up to the agreed vision articulated at the kick-off. As the head of Eldercar and Health recollected, the entire organisation had already ‘approved’ the vision and taken ownership of it. While pressing for a solution, the management team located the source of the ruptures as not only technical but also con tractual; issues between the technology supplier and the Eldercare and Health department were further escalating the situation, as disagreements and different interpretations of contractual obligations began to surface. Employee began hearing about conflictual encounters with the supplier, and they reacted by lowering their expectations and trust in the renewal. As one nurse put it, ‘I'm scared that such a comprehensive renewal of platforms will produc more technical problems than we are used to.’ The initial high expectations that were formed during the Launching stage turned into a source of overflowing when health professionals realised that they were not going to be able to perform the tasks as promised because the functional interfaces were improperly designed. As the nature of th technical issues could not be solved, the organisation and supplier eventually agreed to postpone the go-live date to stabilise the renewal organisation.

## 4.2.2 | Restoring trust

The management team, the technology supplier and the steering committee decided on 14 August 2018 to postpone the go-live date for CURA. Our vision is that all employees get a platform that is easy to use and helps them in everyday work and above all a platform that everyone can trust. If we maintain the originally planned dates for when we launch the platform into use, we will not be able to fulfill that vision. – Organization newsletter, 14 August 2018

Once the decision to postpone the go-live date was made, the management team began framing the postponement as unfortunate, but necessary for the greater good. As the newsletter above notes, it all came down to staying true to th original vision and trusting that the new platform would deliver on that vision. The management team wanted to avoid new controversies and unrest among the health professionals, as well as avoid a lack of trust and motivation. Therefore to further stabilise the situation and renewal organisation, the management team paid particular attention to reassuring health professionals of the good intentions behind the decision to delay. For instance, they communicated that long term costs would have been higher if they had proceeded and that the new platform's technical features would have not delivered as promised, resulting in low usability. During the Launching stage, the management team had strategicall framed the existing platform as inferior to the new platform to support a shared understanding of the situation. Given the ruptures, this framing had to be re-established to restore trust. One manager described this as follows

I stepped up in front of all my 250 employees to explain why we made the [postponement] decision, and we had full support. They would of course have liked to have the new platform now, but they did realise why it was best to postpone.

In addition, the CURA News website kept the organisation updated on key decisions and provided insights into behind-the-scenes renewal activities. As such, the postponement had a reordering effect: the tensions that led to postponement were strategically re-used as a resource for rebuilding expectations and anticipation for the future renewal. By articulating how the technical issues were detrimental to use of the new platform, the situation graduall cooled down and permitted a restoration of trustful relationships. The quality consultant from the CURA project organisation characterised the situation as follows:

Actually, I experienced that the trust in the project organization has risen as a result of the postponement. They (health professionals) realise how we respect their work and professionalism—and that we would rather wait to ensure high quality than rushing for implementation. It is as if they realise ‘it is actually for us that they are doing this.

This view was supported by a department manager:

The choice to postpone was great. The process has become so much better. I'm calmed down because I know that they now keep a check on how to integrate processes and work procedures.

Another manager described the difference between before and after the postponement:

It was with a rapid pulse back then. I know project managers are very busy, but we do not have the drama now that we had before.

The ‘drama’ refers to how the organisation tried to counteract and deal with the overflowing of technical issues in the period leading up to the postponement decision—a time when the situation easily could have changed to one of mistrust and resistance. Instead, stabilising the renewal organisation provided space for negotiation, which ultimately led to restored trust in both the renewal process and in how the management team handled it relative to before th postponement. Hence, the postponement of the go-live date influenced how the organisation perceived the platform renewal and allowed for a reconciliation between the existing and new platform

## 4.3 | Engaging platform renewal participants

In the Engaging stage, managers focused on educating for platform renewal and enabling platform use. While dealing with the overflowing of technical issues in the Negotiating stage, framing shifted toward aiding renewal change by engag ing health professionals in platform use, as well as handling emerging overflowing of performance issues related to their resistance to change. Hence, to prepare for go-live and increase trust among health professionals, management intensi fied education for platform renewal. While this onboarding was a huge success in terms of preparing health professional for the new platform, education and involvement also made the health professionals knowledgeable, critical, and demanding in terms of evaluating how the platform delivered on promises. Although the new platform was reframed by managers as a ‘new colleague’ to humanise the platform, use problems and performance issues quickly surfaced. Hence user acceptance was challenged and old habits came back, resulting in the resurfacing of tensions between the forme and the new platform that had been negotiated in the previous stage. ## 4.3.2 | Enabling platform use

Dear employees—When you show up on Monday, you'll have a new colleague: CURA. To make everything ready for CURA, many of you have been preparing for more than a year. You can bring CURA with you when visiting citizens, which is why we call CURA a good colleague. Have fun! – Th director, in video shared with the entire organization

On 13 May 2019, the big-bang implementation took place. On that day, approximately 4000 health professionals arrived at work, where the new platform was waiting for them. To make it appear more human-like, the management team had reframed CURA as a good colleague that everyone should be excited to meet and greet. However, in the 2 weeks prior to the big-bang implementation, the organisation was frozen while legacy data was transferred to the new platform. During those 2 weeks, the health professionals had to work using pen and paper. Once the plat form was live, they then had to bring it up-to-date with all the manually gathered documentation. In combination with learning how to use the new platform while unlearning the former one, this information transfer required enor mous efforts at all levels of the organisation, which influenced CURA's reception. Despite the strong belief in th new platform, the managers were not starry-eyed optimists. The director continued his welcoming speech by articu lating how ‘there will always be speed bumps in such large-scale initiatives.’ At the same time, he reassured every one that ‘we are prepared to help and support you.’ On this point, the management team walked the talk, allocating considerable resources for aiding the renewal change. As one caregiver summarised:

If you had a measure my frustrations, it would be like 100 the first three weeks, while now it is around 10.

A physiotherapist from a rehabilitation centre agreed:

I would say that it has turned out surprisingly well. Just think about how we have turned to a completely new platform.

Despite the management team and health professionals overall deeming the renewal ‘unproblematic,’ controversies incidents, and reactions began creating a stir in the organisation as the former platform turned out to be ‘sticky.’ Leg acy habits—coupled with the former platform's presence in the memory of users—created overflowing as user began comparing the two platforms on various promises made during the Launching and Negotiating stages. Provid ing a better, simpler client-overview feature was a key promise that the new platform had a hard time delivering on as one nurse noted:

We do not have the same overview as in [existing technology]. I do not have my finger on the pulse in relation to my patients. Before I could get an overview with lightning speed if I, for instance, had been away for a couple of weeks. Now, I do not get any updates or notifications.

This lack of overview made many health professionals feel ill-prepared in their work, and they viewed the new platform as offering little help. If they needed specific client information, users had to make additional clicks and go through each patient record one by one, and some information was unavailable if the client moved to othe domains or institutions, such as a hospital. This lack of system integration and inferior user interface (increase in clicks) brought back legacy habits. Hence, despite framing CURA as a new colleague, the functionality of the for mer platform continued to lurk in both the memory and use practises of the health professionals. As a result, fram ing and overflowing continued to shape the renewal as the new platform was further assimilated into the organisation.

## 5 | DISCUSSION

Our study extends emerging scholarly work on organising and managing digital platform renewal by advancing and empirically substantiating a framing–overflowing perspective. Rooted in a case study of how a Danish local government organisation replaced its digital platform, our analysis illustrates how framing and overflowing played out ove time in response to the complex nature of platform renewal, where managers must navigate between existing and future platforms (Wimelius et al., 2021; Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023). In the following, we draw on these empirica insights and extant literature to present a conceptual model of framing–overflowing dynamics during the formativ stages of digital platform renewal. We then discuss our study's implications for theory and practise.

## 5.1 | Framing and overflowing in digital platform renewal

Our analysis reveals how the management team organised and managed platform renewal and framing–overflowin dynamics across the three stages of launching, negotiating and engaging (Tables 3–5). As Figure 3 shows, we gener alised these findings into a conceptual model that depicts how the three stages progress iteratively during digita platform renewal. Further, each stage involves interaction between framing and overflowing activities, and the foc of these activities changes as the renewal progresses across the stages: In the Launching stage, framing focuses on anticipating the renewal future, while overflowing reveals organisational issues; during Negotiating, framing focuses on stabilising the renewal organisation, while overflowing reveals technical issues; and, during Engaging, framing focuses on aiding renewal change, while overflowing reveals performance issues.

In our case study organisation, managers took on the pivotal role of committing resources and establishing a renewal vision through framing efforts that appealed to the future platform's potential benefits while problematizin the existing technology as outdated and user unfriendly. But overflowing tensions, controversies, and discussions created setbacks, making the renewal process deviate from its initial trajectory. As such, our study highlights how actors involved in digital platform renewal make interpretations and choices that connect to their old worlds and ways of doing things with the former technology (Mehrizi et al., 2022; Polites & Karahanna, 2012). As illustrated in Figure 3, the focus in the organisationa framing efforts therefore changed over time—from initially anticipating the renewal future, to focusing on stabilising the renewal organisation and aiding renewal change. Moreover, in this process managers did not fight overflowing as an anomaly but rather strived to embrace it by using the framing–overflowing frictions to constructively yield pro gress (Callon, 1998b).

Accordingly, we found that overflowing does not always occur due to disfunction or failure; it also arises whe things move forward successfully. While the existing platform was framed as outdated to construct a ‘collective defi nition of the situation’ (Azad & Faraj, 2011, p. 38), this framing also produced high expectations for the future plat form among health professionals that were impossible to honour. Hence, when the new platform faced technical an performance issues, users reacted by reinstating their belief in the former platform. Likewise, the high success leve regarding user attendance at the renewal onboarding workshops turned into overflowing when users began criticall questioning the future platform based on insights they gained from the workshops. Conversely, while the postpone ment of go-live was unintended—and initially caused disruption and unease—it ultimately had a positive impact on the renewal path, serving as a welcome respite and time-out that left health professionals feeling more at ease with the renewal. Thus, while existing literature typically depicts frame breakdowns as a result of disfunction or failure our study finds that overflows, depending on how managers respond, may work to move the platform renewal pro cess forward.

## 5.2 | Implications for theory

Our study has theoretical implications for two important IS research areas: digital platform renewal and framing First, while existing literature has stressed that platform renewal is highly challenging because it requires organisa tions to remove their existing technology while at the same time building on practises that depend on it to implement a new technological foundation (Wimelius et al., 2021), we know little about how to organise and manage thi complex process (Beane, 2023; Gregory et al., 2015; Mehrizi et al., 2019). Against this backdrop, our framing– overflowing perspective sheds new light on platform renewal management as an act of balancing opposites and understanding the ‘irrepressible and productive overflows’ of framing (Callon, 1998a, p. 250). Specifically, as highlighted above, we revealed how managers, rather than fight overflows, turned frame breaks and the ensuing overflow into opportunities to move platform renewal forward while avoiding destructive frame disputes (Azad & Faraj, 2011). Hence, our study details how organising and managing platform renewal requires addressing emergin overflows and engaging in reframing to accommodate the intrinsic relationships between existing and future plat forms (cf. Figure 3). At the same time, however, our study differs from extant research in that it uncovers the produc tive potentials of overflowing in organising and managing platform renewal. As such, we add managerial perspectives to previous studies that have recognised the complex nature of platform renewal (Wimelius et al., 2021) and how such processes are loaded with sociotechnical inertia that makes the existing platform demanding to replac (Beane, 2023; Rinta-Kahila et al., 2023).

Second, we contribute to ongoing debates in the IS framing literature (Davidson, 2002; Davidson, 2006). Whil current literature emphasises the importance of cognitive (Orlikowski & Gash, 1994) and strategic framing (Barrett et al., 2013), with preference given to the incongruences and inconsistencies in how different actor groups frame sociotechnical phenomena (Lin & Silva, 2005; Young et al., 2016), the literature tends to downplay the technology itself (Essén & Värlander, 2019; Orlikowski & Iacono, 2001). As a result, extant research lacks clarity on how to manage the complex relationship between existing and future technologies in platform renewal (Beane, 2023; Gregor et al., 2015; Wimelius et al., 2021); instead, it stresses and centralises user resistance. In response, we have combined framing and overflowing into a theoretical scaffold that affords close examination of the tensions between existing and future technologies. In this perspective, framing is always imperfect, causing overflowing to happen (Callon, 1998a; Callon, 1998b) and making framing an ongoing and ever-incomplete effort (Azad & Faraj, 2011). By highlighting the ongoing tensions between existing and future platforms, our study points to crucial—yet ofte overlooked—aspects of framing in IS research. Prior research has stressed the importance of responding to tensions and frame breakdowns by means of reframing (Davidson, 2006; Azad & Faraj, 2011; Cornelissen & Werner, 2014) but attention has focused primarily on how managers aim to repair frame breaks by reframing to restore strategic narratives and shared cognitive frames. Our study points to the importance of attending not only to framing– reframing activities, but also to the active role of aspects, incidents, and situations that cause frames to overflow, and thus result in the need for reframing. Hence, by combining framing with overflowing and focusing on the dynam ics between the two, our study addresses recent calls to advance knowledge about how framing plays out over tim (Davidson, 2006; Azad & Faraj, 2011; Cornelissen and Werner (2014); Polykarpou et al., 2018; Spieth et al., 2021).

## 5.3 | Implications for practise

Our study is relevant for the growing interest in digital platform renewal, and our findings encourage managers to pay close attention to framing–overflowing dynamics. Our work reveals the inescapable role of overflowing in fram ing, as well as why managers should treat overflows as an opportunity to expand framing rather than as a constraint that constricts framing. In this way, our study foregrounds the challenging managerial task of negotiating and reconciling the tensions that drive platform renewal (Wimelius et al., 2021). It also raises questions: Should managers high light problems with the existing platform to move employees toward accepting the new platform—that is, use the existing platform's ‘voice’ to frame the new platform? Or is it better to maintain silence on the existing platform to avoid activating memories of legacy habits? Our study suggests that managers should be open to the productive fric tion that overflowing may produce, rather than actively fight against overflows. Indeed, managers who hastily repai frame breakdowns may not see how overflows open new avenues to productively move digital platform renewa forward.

## 5.4 | Limitations and future research

Our study relies on a qualitative single case study design. Qualitative findings are often complex and messy, and do not easily lend themselves to generalisation (Barley, 2006). Such findings can, however, provide vivid example (Flyvbjerg, 2006) that—in a rich, concrete way—illustrate new conceptualizations (Siggelkow, 2007). With this in mind, our study still has some limitations. First, we focus primarily on the formative stages of platform renewal in which managers launch the renewal, negotiate renewal, and engage renewal participants. As such, it would be rele vant to look further into value creation and value capture beyond platform implementation to add to research streams on exploitation in digital platform renewal (Kohli & Melville, 2019). Second, using a public sector organisa tion as a site for studying platform renewal imposes context-specific characteristics and application conditions Future studies could validate and develop our theorising beyond the public-sector context. Third, to explain platform renewal, we use framing–overflowing dynamics, through which organisations refresh and renew their digita platform to reach strategic goals (Wimelius et al., 2021). Hence, it would be interesting to investigate whether our framing–overflowing perspective could be applied to explore how and why legacy technologies and platforms i some cases re-emerge with new value instead of being replaced by new ones (Raffaelli, 2019).

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Given that current research provides little guidance on how to organise and manage the inherently complex process of digital platform renewal, we have contributed knowledge grounded in a case study of how a local government organisation organised and managed renewal of its existing platform with a new digital platform. As a result, we hav advanced a framing–overflowing perspective to manage the involved complexities and unpack how framing– overflowing dynamics play out over time in organising platform renewal. We hope other researchers find our insight useful to further advance platform renewal knowledge and that practitioners will be inspired to approach these ven tures by embracing their inherent complexities
