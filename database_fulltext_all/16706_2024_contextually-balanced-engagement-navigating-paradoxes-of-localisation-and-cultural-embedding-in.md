# Contextually balanced engagement: Navigating paradoxes of localisation and cultural embedding in rural health information systems implementation

## Abstract

Although health information systems (HIS) play an important role in elevating health standards, a comprehensive understanding of how to effectively implement HIS in rural areas is lacking. This issue becomes more significant when considering that globally a majority of the approximately 1.5 million deaths of children under the age of five in 2019 that were attributable to vaccine-preventable diseases occurred in rural areas. Accordingly, we ask two questions. How does rurality influence the implementation of information and communication technology for development (ICT4D) projects? How do organisations navigate challenges associated with rurality during the implementation of these projects? Our findings, derived from an in-depth case study of a socia enterprise in rural India, reveal two paradoxes that pose challenges to the effective implementation of HIS in rural settings: the localisation paradox and the cultural embedding paradox. We found that contextually balanced engagement was comprised of four organisational responses—prioritising; localising; cultural adjustment; and engaging stakeholders—that help navigate the challenges posed by the localisation and cultural embedding paradoxes. Synthesising these findings, we develop a process model that shows how the implementation of HIS in rural areas is shaped by the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality. Further, organisations require a dynamic approach, engaging in multiple responses over time to navigate the paradoxes inherent in HIS implementation. We suggest that organisational responses to paradoxical tensions stemming from the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality lead to the development of HIS enabled for rurality. Our findings contribute the understanding of ICT4D projects implementation.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Information and communication technology for development (ICT4D) projects have significant potential fo addressing many development challenges (Bonina et al., 2021; Lagna & Ravishankar, 2022), with healthcare standing out as a critical focus (Haenssgen & Ariana, 2017; Venkatesh et al., 2020). Therefore, the implementation<sup>1</sup> of health information systems (HIS) has gained traction in many developing countries, including India. In India, many nongovernmental organisations, social enterprises and government agencies have recognised the potential benefits of HIS, integrating them into broader initiatives for rural healthcare (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Nevertheless, the adoptio and effectiveness of HIS in rural areas remain questionable, demanding critical examination to understand how th implementation process interacts with rural cultures and values (Haenssgen & Ariana, 2017).

Rural contexts exhibit distinctive descriptive and sociocultural characteristics. The former include challenges such as low literacy, inadequate infrastructure, limited technology penetration and absence of comprehensive information channels (Goh et al., 2016; Parthiban et al., 2020, 2024; Sandeep & Ravishankar, 2018; Tim et al., 2018; Venkatesh et al., 2016). The latter encompass challenges such as pronounced gender discrimination, entrenched cultural practices and a greater inclination to adhere to traditional norms (Bhatt et al., 2022; Karanasios & Slavova, 2019; Qureshi et al., 2018; Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023; Sutter et al., 2023). The exploration of HIS implementation challenges in rural contexts set the stage for delving into the tensions arising from the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality. Understanding these tensions is crucial as organisations navigate the com plexities of implementing ICT4D projects in rural areas, prompting further investigation into how they navigate these challenges. While studies have acknowledged the need for special consideration when implementing ICT4D projects in rural contexts (Cui et al., 2019; Heeks & Ospina, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2020), a comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to successful rural ICT4D project implementation remains elusive. This gap persists because many ICT4D projects are initiated in urban areas, designed for standardised systems and prioritise universality, effi ciency and scale through expert knowledge (Díaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012; Reed, 1996), which inevitably create tension in implementing ICT4D projects in rural contexts.

Drawing on the paradox literature (Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011), we posit that these descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality can give rise to paradoxical tensions encountered by organisations implementing ICT4D in the rural context. The paradox literature suggests that organisations must navigate these ten sions to successfully implement projects (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Schad et al., 2016). Based on these pre mises, we pose the following research questions: (1) How does rurality influence the implementation of ICT4D projects? and (2) How do organisations navigate challenges associated with rurality during the implementation of these projects?

We delved into these questions through an in-depth case study of ChildHealth, a social enterprise in India dedi cated to reducing infant and child mortality by enhancing the monitoring of antenatal and neonatal healthcare ChildHealth offers a uniquely insightful context that allows us to gain understanding into HIS implementation in a challenging rural context. Further, because ChildHealth has become increasingly aware of the cultural heterogeneity and intricate social dynamics in the communities it serves, its responses provided us with an opportunity to observe the evolution of rural HIS. Our data collection involved a diverse array of sources including unstructured interviews, semi-structured interviews, field observations, informal interactions, organisational documents and published mate rials. Our findings show that ChildHealth's HIS implementation was an iterative process, evolving as it encountered and navigated the paradoxical tensions arising from the characteristics of rurality. Specifically, ChildHealth navigated localisation and cultural embedding paradoxes during HIS implementation for two user groups—intermediaries and beneficiaries—by employing contextually balanced engagement comprised of four organisational responses localising; prioritising; cultural adjustment; and stakeholder engagement. This allows ChildHealth to effectively navi gate these persistent paradoxical tensions over time.

We make three significant contributions to the ICT4D literature by (1) examining how descriptive and sociocul tural characteristics of rurality shape implementation and evolution of rural HIS; (2) exploring how descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality trigger paradoxes of localisation and cultural embedding, respectively and (3) highlighting that a contextually balanced engagement that is comprised of four organisational responses—pri oritising; localising; cultural adjustment; and engaging stakeholders—is needed to navigate the challenges posed b the localisation and cultural embedding paradoxes

## 2.1 | ICT4D and rurality

ICT4D research aims to explore the role of information and communication technology (ICT) intervention in bringing about social change through the promotion of economic growth (Zheng et al., 2018), marginalised voices (Riaz & Qureshi, 2017), political rights (Díaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012), health benefits (Holeman & Barrett, 2017), knowl edge commoning (Qureshi et al., 2022), cultural empowerment (Pandey & Zheng, 2019), inclusive markets (Bhatt et al., 2022) and education (Lin et al., 2015). The implementation of ICT4D projects related to healthcare in rura areas has increasingly attracted scholarly attention (e.g., Miscione, 2007; Venkatesh et al., 2020). However, there are many critiques and controversies regarding ICT4D project practices that question why they often fail to produce the expected development outcomes and, in some cases, even have negative consequences Some critics point out that a lack of awareness regarding cultural differences and local conditions may exacerbat power inequality (Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023; Schelenz & Pawelec, 2022; Sutter et al., 2023), and argue that a more profound consideration of the local context can result in improved practice and policy impact (Bhatt et al., 2022). In summary, the peculiarities of the rural context should be considered when implementing ICT4D projects to ensure positive developmental outcomes.

The literature conceptualises rurality from three perspectives: symbolic, descriptive and sociocultural (Hardy et al., 2019). The symbolic perspec tive describes how different symbols are used by urban elites and the middle class to define rural areas while com municating among themselves (Bell, 2007). In the case of HIS implementation in rural areas, symbolic characteristics are not applicable because they focus an external perspective of rurality, whereas our focus is on the indigenou characteristics of rurality. Moreover, a high prevalence of illiteracy (including digital illiteracy) (Riaz & Qureshi, 2017; Tim et al., 2018), absence of access to comprehensive healthcare information and services (Chamakiotis et al., 2021; Goh et al., 2016) and pervasive poverty (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2018) impact the engagement of rural stakeholders in ICT4D interventions (Sutter et al., 2023).

The sociocultural characteristics of rural areas relate to specific values, attitudes and behaviours that shape com munity dynamics profoundly. A defining aspect of rural communities is the hierarchical structure manifested in power dynamics, with ‘upper caste’ elites dominating all aspects of rural activities (Bhatt et al., 2022; Qureshi et al., 2018 Ray, 2014). Similarly, the prevalent patriarchy marginalises women (Bhatt et al., 2024; Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023), constraining their engagement in social activities (Bhatt et al., 2022). The hierarchy of social status also manifests in higher trust in traditional authorities, which is evident in the elevated levels of trust bestowed upon religious leaders, spiritists and traditional herbalists in rural settings (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Further, the provision of medical service in rural regions often relies on untrained individuals, leading to elevated health risks such as maternal and newborn mortality (Venkatesh et al., 2016). Consequently, consistent with the role of other intermediaries (Hota et al., 2019; Qureshi et al., 2018; Ravishankar, 2015), there is a pervasive sense of distrust among rural populations towards external entities engaged in healthcare interventions stemming from negative experiences and outcome (Ray, 2014).

Given that rural areas have unique descriptive and sociocultural characteristics, scholars have highlighted the important role of social institutions and indigenous knowledge in the implementation of ICT4D projects (Haines et al., 2023; Miscione, 2007; Qureshi et al., 2022). They advocate for a balanced approach in ICT4D projects, incorporating urban advancements while customising solutions for rural contexts (Foster & Heeks, 2013; Nakata & Weidner, 2012) and involving rural communities in project co-creation (London, 2016; Parth et al., 2021; Parthiban et al., 2021; Pillai et al., 2021). Effective deployment necessitates a comprehensive understanding of local challenges and robust engagement with rural users (Goh et al., 2016: Oureshi et al., 2022). However. the design of ICT4D appli cations for rural areas is often driven by urban experts, neglecting the nuanced realities of rural contexts; that is, applications are often designed for rural users rather than with them (Hardy et al., 2019), under the assumption that what is beneficial for urban areas is also suitable for rural areas, as the urban-based design tends to normalise and advance values such as standardisation, universalisation, rationality and efficiency (Díaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012 Reed, 1996). There has been a lack of emphasis on the need to situate ICT projects in rural contexts and to carefully consider rural practices and values (Hardy et al., 2019)

Implementing ICT4D projects without considering rural contexts can have unintended consequences including heightened inequality, diminished trust and excessive dependence on external providers (Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023; Qureshi, Pandey, et al., 2023; Sutter et al., 2023; Wyche et al., 2016). While promising for rural development, outcomes of ICT4D initiatives may vary because of the unique descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rural con texts. Therefore, organisations must navigate specific tensions arising from rural characteristics (Bhatt et al., 2022 Goh et al., 2016; Karanasios & Slavova, 2019; Parthiban et al., 2020, 2024). Understanding these tensions is crucia as organisations navigate complexities in rural ICT4D implementation (Cui et al., 2019; Heeks & Ospina, 2019; Venkatesh et al., 2020). The gap in understanding of successful rural ICT4D projects persists because of an underem phasis on the tensions arising from urban-centric designs and standardised systems, which neglect rural nuance (Díaz Andrade & Urquhart, 2012; Reed, 1996). Thus, exploring tensions in rural ICT4D projects is essential to appre ciate the complexities faced by organisations in ICT4D project implementation

## 2.3 | Tensions in implementing ICT4D projects in rural areas

Informed by the paradox literature (Keller & Sadler-Smith, 2019; Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011), we frame the challenges encountered by organisations in rural ICT4D proiects as tensions. encompassing stability and evolvability; advanced technology and technoficing; and cohesiveness and diversity (Jarvenpaa & Wernick, 2011 Qureshi et al., 2022; Qureshi, Pandey, et al., 2023; Wimelius et al., 2021). Smith and Lewis (2011) classify tensions RI G H TS LIN K) H T S LIN K0

as three types: paradoxes, dilemmas and dialectics. Paradox involves contradictory yet interrelated elements persisting over time, offering a ‘both-and’ perspective (Smith & Lewis, 2011, p. 386). In contrast, dilemmas present clear alternatives, while dialectics resolve tensions through integration. In rural ICT4D projects, the tension betwee standardised and customised solutions underscores paradoxical choices (Sabherwal & Newman, 2003). Despite this the literature on standardisation–customisation tension lacks insights into rural context triggers. Thus, embracing a paradox perspective is essential for comprehending the complexities of rural ICT4D implementation.

Foreshadowing our findings, we unveil two forms of standardisation–customisation tension during the imple mentation of ICT4D projects—in our case HIS—which we conceptualise as the localisation paradox and the cultura embedding paradox. Next, we draw from the literature to provide conceptual groundings for these two paradoxe triggered respectively by the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality in the context of ICT4D project implementation.

## 2.3.1 | Paradoxical tension triggered by descriptive rurality

The descriptive approach highlights the general lack of literacy—especially information technology literacy—in rura areas, along with insufficient infrastructure and substantial poverty (Chamakiotis et al., 2021; Parthiban et al., 2021; Sandeep & Ravishankar, 2018). Therefore, ICT projects that can be helpful in urban areas may not be beneficial in rural areas because of challenges related to lack of familiarity, usage and access (Pearson et al., 2016; Quresh et al., 2022; Wyche et al., 2016). The descriptive characteristics of rurality necessitate the implementation of ICT projects tailored for local situations, taking into account the specific constraints in these areas. For example, to address poor connectivity in rural areas, one organisation developed interactive audio photos for low-end camera phones, offering health information to rural users (Pearson et al., 2016). Another organisation used portable batteryoperated pocket projectors to overcome the lack of connectivity, electricity and motorable roads (Qureshi, Pan, & Zheng, 2021; Qureshi, Pandey, et al., 2023). While such customisations are important for the success of ICT4D implementation, several aspects of projects also need to be standardised to increase their reach and effectiveness. For example, to overcome connectivity issues, Digital Green devised Connect Online, Connect Offline (COCO), which enables data to be collected in a standardised format even when there is no connectivity (Gandhi et al., 2016). Simi larly, to ensure information absorption, one organisation reduced and standardised the length of videos disseminat ing indigenous knowledge (Qureshi et al., 2022). In summary, organisations implementing ICT4D projects may encounter heightened tensions when attempting to accommodate standardised urban-designed ICT projects to reach a wide range of beneficiaries, and developing customised projects to provide maximum benefits to a specifi set of beneficiaries.

## 2.3.2 | Paradoxical tension triggered by sociocultural rurality

Sociocultural rurality emphasises the cultural intricacies and societal norms prevalent in rural areas that significantly influence daily practices and attitudes (Bhatt et al., 2022, 2023b, 2024; Karanasios & Slavova, 2019). Cultural consid erations are pivotal in ICT implementation outcomes (Veiga et al., 2001; Zakaria et al., 2003). For example, in a comparative study of website implementation, Marcus and Gould (2000) find that cultural elements are embedded in user interfaces as a set of contextual and social cues for effective use. Consequently, an organisation needs to appreciate the culture of community members in rural areas to ensure ICT4D project success (Chipidza & Leidner, 2019) The literature supports the idea that successful implementation of ICT in a cross-cultural setting requires careful con sideration of prevailing cultural norms and values (Alexander et al., 2017; Martinsons & Westwood, 1997). However, many rural communities exhibit cultural heterogeneity, characterised by conflicting social norms and practices (Bhatt et al., 2023c; Mahajan & Qureshi, 2023). Therefore, an organisation implementing an ICT4D project might encounte the paradox of aligning the project with the dominant culture, excluding marginalised social groups or structuring th project without alignment with any social group, risking its uptake by rural communities.

The literature identifies two broad types of response practices used to tackle paradoxes. The first type, ‘eitheror responses, is defensive, providing short-term relief and temporarily enabling actors to address paradoxes but leading to a vicious cycle of increasing underpinning tension and poor performance over the long run (Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011). These responses include splitting, regression, repression, projection, reaction for mation and ambivalence (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Jarzabkowski et al., 2013). The second type, ‘both-and responses, are proactive strategies that help an organisation navigate paradoxes on a long-term basis (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Schad et al., 2016); they include acceptance, confrontation, transcendence, synthesis and purposeful iterations of temporal or spatial separation (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Lewis, 2000; Smith & Lewis, 2011).

## 3 | RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

We conducted an in-depth case study of a social enterprise called ChildHealth operating in India. Established in 2014 by a young technology developer, ChildHealth is a not-for-profit organisation dedicated to developing and implementing digital technologies to provide community healthcare services, particularly reproductive and chil health, for marginalised populations in rural India. Following established guidelines to answer the ‘how’ question (Klein & Myers, 1999), we conducted the case study. Recognising the nascent stage of the literature on HIS imple mentation in rural areas, we adopted an interpretive approach. This approach highlights the background of the study and the researchers' role therein; it involves multiple interpretations of data, necessitating several iterations betwee data and theory (Klein & Myers, 1999). In interpretive research, phenomena such as HIS implementation are viewe as socially constructed and embedded within specific social contexts, rather than as pure technology implementation projects with objective characteristics and universal applications. Interpretive case studies aim to gain a subjectiv understanding of social norms, structures, practices and interactions expressed by participants (Klein & Myers, 1999). Next, we provide details on the research context, a brief overview of the organisation, data collectio methods and analysis approaches

## 3.1 | Research context

The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal #3 (SDG3) aims to eradicate all preventable deaths of newborns and children under the age of five.<sup>2</sup> Despite a declining trend in child mortality rates (CMRs) over the past decade,<sup>3</sup> an estimated 1.5 million children worldwide still succumb to vaccine-preventable diseases annually.<sup>4</sup> As reported b UNICEF (2018), geographical disparities within countries persist, with the risk of child mortality in rural areas remaining 1.5 times higher than that in urban areas. For example, India, which has the highest $\mathsf { C M R } ^ { 5 }$ globally, recorded over 880 000 child deaths in 2018, with over 500 000 occurring in rural areas.<sup>6</sup> Despite timely vaccination being a cost-effective means to prevent infection-related child mortality,<sup>7</sup> less than 44% of children under the age of five years in India had in 2018 received a full vaccination schedule, a figure that increased to 65% in the most recent estimation by UNICEF.<sup>8</sup> The persistently high CMR in India and other developing countries presents a significant challenge to achieving SDG3 by 2030. Consequently, there has been increasing interest in understanding the causes and solutions of low vaccination rates in these regions.

This study was conducted in the state of Rajasthan, India, which is recognised by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare as one of nine high-priority states for immunisation. In 2016, the state's CMR stood at 41 per 1000 births, surpassing the national average of 34 per 1000 births.<sup>9</sup> Moreover, both India's and Rajasthan's CMRs are significantly higher than those of developed countries (e.g., 3.0 in Europe, 3.0 in Australia and 5.9 in the United States) and peer developing countries (e.g., 7.0 in China and 13.0 in Brazil).<sup>10</sup>

ChildHealth was established in Rajasthan in 2014 with the mission of harnessing digital technologies to track maternal and child health records, aiming to address rural healthcare challenges through the implementation of an ICT4D project. Headquartered in a major city in Rajasthan, the organisation endeavours to improve vaccination rate in rural Rajasthan using a multi-tier approach. This approach includes the deployment of near field communication (NFC)-enabled digital necklaces for pregnant women and young mothers, accompanied by mobile apps for auxiliary nurse midwives (ANMs) to streamline the vaccination process at the point of care.<sup>11</sup> The NFC-enabled necklace allows mothers and children to carry with them their entire vaccination record. All data are stored on an NFC chip accessible without an Internet connection. The necklace, which is durable, battery-free and costs less than one USD to produce for ChildHealth, is provided to users at no charge. Healthcare workers use the ChildHealth mobile app to track each child's vaccination history and access information about pregnant women and new mothers. Real-time data facilitate ChildHealth in maintaining adequate vaccine and medication stocks and offering timely assistance to mothers and children. Additionally, the system delivers automatic voice calls to mothers in local dialects. These voic messages emphasise the importance of vaccinations and notify mothers of vaccination locations and schedules

## 3.2 | Data collection

We used an inductive, single-revelatory case research design to uncover the intricate processes involved in implementing an HIS in a rural context (Sarker et al., 2012). Data spanning seven years of ChildHealth's activi ties were gathered from various sources to examine its endeavours in implementing HIS in rural Rajasthan We employed two types of triangulation: data and researcher (or investigator) triangulation. We triangulated data from semi-structured and unstructured interviews, field observations, informal interactions, organisationa documents and published material. The researcher (or investigator) triangulation approach allowed diverse researchers to investigate the same question, thereby contributing varied perspectives, considerations and ana lyses (Archibald, 2016). The composition of the team facilitated researcher triangulation. Being comprised of five researchers—two Indian citizens, two researchers of Indian origin without citizenship and one from non-Indian origin—the team struck a balance between emic and etic perspectives. Four researchers were fluent in the local language and all five had previous experience working in rural contexts, with four possessing extensive research experience in rural India. The team included one female and four male researchers, aided in the field by one male and one female research assistant, both proficient in speaking and understanding th local dialects.

To trace the unfolding of HIS implementation, we employed a forward and backward tracing approac (Langley & Tsoukas, 2010). Using multiple-stage interviews and field observations enabled us to track events as the transpired (Chughtai & Myers, 2017). Initially, we reviewed online information on ChildHealth spanning from

February 2019 to April 2019.<sup>12</sup> This initial step aimed to acquire background information about the organisation and its operations, thus facilitating efficient and effective data collection. With a foundational understanding of ChildHealth established, we identified three social enterprises that had developed and implemented HIS for materna and childcare in other regions of India.<sup>13</sup> Subsequently, between May and August 2019, we conducted eight inter views with the founders, chief executive officers (CEOs), chief operating officers (COO) and field managers of these organisations. These interviews provided valuable insights into the challenges encountered by ChildHealth in implementing HIS.

This topic was the subject of our email exchanges and two initial unstructured Skype interviews with the COO and system development executive of ChildHealth between September and October 2019. This was followed by two months of field visits to ChildHealth's operations between December 2019 and January 2020. We conducted follow up interviews from February to April 2020 and again from September to October 2021. During the field visits and follow-up interview stages, we engaged with 12 accredited social health activists (ASHAs), 14 ANMs, 4 field coordi nators, 2 field executives (FEs) and 2 field staff. These interviews were conducted across 17 villages, each averaging approximately 45 minutes.

Additionally, we conducted two group interviews: one with ASHAs and the other with ANMs, each lasting approximately 1.5 h (see Appendix II). We visited the ChildHealth headquarters twice and held two group interviews with the top executive team. Each of these interviews lasted around two hours. We conducted two additional inter views with system development executives, lasting approximately two hours each. Moreover, we had informa interactions with approximately 21 mothers enrolled in the ChildHealth system. Throughout the interviews, w focused on understanding the implementation process, challenges encountered, responses to those challenges and the intended and realised benefits for intermediaries (ASHAs and ANMs) and beneficiaries (rural women). We sought to capture the phenomenon by triangulating multiple perspectives (Silverman, 1998).

## 3.3 | Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted iteratively, with on-going analysis conducted as new data became avail able. A flowchart outlining the data collection and analysis process is provided in Appendix III. Initially, we reviewe the data collected during the first stage. Insights from the data included that the organisation has a focus on cultur ally responsive implementation to engage community members. Therefore, during the second-stage interviews, ou focus shifted to exploring the benefits and challenges of culturally responsive implementation for improving vaccina tion coverage. Interviews with three social enterprises engaged in similar activities revealed contextual factors affecting their implementation processes. As our understanding of the contextual challenges in HIS implementation deepened, we organised our interviews with ChildHealth executives around these concepts. Our overall analysis adhered to the established process of inductive theory-building research (Gioia et al., 2013; Miles & Huberman, 1994), involving a continuous interplay between data and emerging theoretical categories. While itera tive, our analysis followed three major steps, as outlined below.

## 3.3.1 | Step 1: Creating categories and first-order codes

We employed first-order coding techniques to establish a set of first-order codes and provisional categories for fur ther analysis (Gioia et al., 2013). We systematically read and compared sentences during this process, assigning the first-order code accordingly. For example, when encountering a sentence discussing the development of a solution offering various options for entering data entry, we categorised it as ‘Adding flexibility for data entry’. This initia coding cycle was recursive, involving multiple iterations of coding and recoding our data

## 3.3.2 | Step 2: Aggregating first-order codes and creating second-order themes

In this step, we used axial coding to transition from first-order codes and categories to more abstract-level theoretica categories (Miles & Huberman, 1994). We identified similarities and differences among the first-order codes, cate gorising them accordingly to derive second-order themes. For example, the first-order codes Incorporating flexibilit into data entry processes’, ‘Easily understandable language adapted for rural users’ and ‘Intuitive and simple user inter face tailored for rural users’ were combined to form the second-order theme ‘Developing a localised HIS’.

## 3.3.3 | Step 3: Theoretical coding, overarching dimensions, and empirically grounded framework

Once the second-order themes were generated, we focused on understanding how they could be integrated to buil a coherent framework (Gioia et al., 2013). We elevated the level of abstraction and focused on identifying overarch ing theoretical dimensions from the second-order codes and exploring the relationships between these dimensions Throughout this stage, a constant comparison was used as we continuously iterated between the data, emergin dimensions and relevant literature to assess their alignment (Gioia et al., 2013). For example, the second-orde themes ‘Developing a generalised HIS’, ‘Developing a localised HIS’ and ‘Challenges in implementing a localised or generalised HIS were synthesised to yield the aggregate dimension Localisation paradox

Once we had identified the first-order codes, second-order themes and aggregate theoretical dimensions, we built the data structure (Gioia et al., 2013). ## 4.1 | Descriptive and sociocultural challenges in HIS implementation

In our study, we explored how the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rural communities have shaped a healthcare system by the implementation of ChildHealth. In Rajasthan, where our study was conducted, a significant portion of the population lived in rural areas, with most individuals relying on agricultural work for livelihood. Thes villages were characterised by poor infrastructure, limited access to healthcare facilities and low literacy, particularly among women. ChildHealth was operating in impoverished villages where healthcare awareness, especially regardin vaccination, was lacking because of these challenges. Apart from these descriptive characteristics, the sociocultura characteristics of rurality presented significant hurdles for healthcare system implementation. Rajasthan's societ was entrenched in patriarchal norms, caste-based divisions and gender disparities, impeding social development Gender disparities were evident in low female literacy rates, limited property ownership by women and restricted mobile phone usage among women. Gender roles were strictly defined, with women primarily confined to househol duties. Harmful practices such as child marriage and son preference had adverse effects on maternal and chil wellbeing. Caste divisions and superstitious beliefs further complicated social interactions (refer to Appendix I fo more details).

## 4.2 | Paradoxical tension triggered by descriptive rurality: The localisation paradox

ChildHealth provides its services in remote rural areas through intermediaries and frontline health workers (i.e., ANMs and ASHAs), who use the mobile interface developed by ChildHealth to deliver services. The interfac implemented for intermediaries primarily creates the localisation paradox, defined as the enduring tension betwee generalisation (uniformity across the system) and localisation (tailoring the system to meet local needs). Descriptiv characteristics of rurality trigger the need for localisation. Through a comprehensive examination of the case, we traced the evolution of HER over seven years. By dissecting the implementation process, we show how ChildHealt navigated descriptive rurality challenges and responded to persistent paradoxical tensions.

While implementing ICT4D projects for intermediaries, the organisation faced challenges reconciling local needs arising from rurality with the standardised options available in off-the-shelf modules. These off-the-shelf module were designed to work more efficiently with standardised data collection and storage processes, offering advantages such as accelerating the implementation process and facilitating rapid scalability. However, the descriptive character istics of rurality posed significant challenges to the practicality of using off-the-shelf modules. Moreover, most loca females lacked basic English language proficiency and possessed low digital literacy skills. Consequently, a standardised mobile application with technical language and a non-intuitive user interface was ineffective for them.

## 4.2.1 | Experiencing the localisation paradox

ChildHealth's founders had a profound connection to the local context and recognised that off-the-shelf module would not effectively address the rurality challenges. They believed that some form of localisation would be neces sary to tackle these challenges. Initially, they opted to develop an application with the assistance of interns and vol unteers. However, they faced capability constraints in developing a mobile application from scratch. Consequently they integrated some generic open-source modules. Despite this, the initial application leaned more toward localisation than generalisation. In this instance, their limited capabilities hindered the pace of the implementation process. Recognising the challenges of rapid deployment and scalability, the founders soon realised the need to outsource application development activities. They engaged DigiTech, a technology company, to handle these tasks Reflecting on the initial paradoxical tension between a generalised and localised HIS, the CEO and founder of ChildHealth shared the following:

We wanted to develop a custom application suitable for this context [rural] and engaged a few interns and volunteers. But these interns had little experience in custom application development. They relied on open-source modules. The first release took several months and was full of bugs. Then, we decided to take help from professionals. (CEO).

ChildHealth responded to this paradoxical tension by prioritising the rapid rollout of the application over achieving a fully localised HIS. They believed that the urgent priority was to expedite the implementation process. As the founder recalled:

Our initial efforts to develop a customised application with the help of interns and volunteers failed. This affected our implementation plan. Now, our immediate concern was to roll out the application as soon as possible. They [DigiTech] helped us. (Founder).

DigiTech then developed a generalised HIS. ChildHealth believed that the application developed by DigiTech could provide tangible benefits by accelerating the implementation and scaling up the process. ChildHealth's founder recalled how sourcing a generalised HIS from DigiTech helped them quicken the implementation:

Initially, we started developing the application with the help of volunteers, but we soon realised that we needed support from a professional organisation with experience and expertise for quick implementation. Then, our association with DigiTech started and the CEO was very helpful. (Founder)

Moreover, it was thought the generalised HIS could address the major concerns associated with current paperbased tracking systems, enabling faster capture and synchronisation of health data. Both the COO and a FE, who also oversaw system development, shared their perspectives on the potential benefits of the generalised HIS devel oped by DigiTech:

You can go for things [technology] that reduce the processing time and that are commonly used items then it makes the process more likely to scale faster. (COO).

Earlier, the data [paper-based] was provided by the health worker to the other government authori ties after one month … but now with our newly launched application whenever she [ANM] comes to C I I N

the network area, in the evening or in one or two days, she will sync the data and the data will come to the dashboard. (FE 1).

Initially, ChildHealth developed an app with interns to reduce intermediaries' dependency on the paper log sys tem for data collection. The paper log system posed issues such as erroneous entries, the need to carry a large register and the risk of data loss because of damaged or misplaced registers, among other issues. The mobile phon device provided to intermediaries sought to address these problems by maintaining digital records. However, inter mediaries faced challenges using the digital device because of their limited skills and experience. Most intermediarie had only studied up to the tenth grade, spoke Hindi or the local dialect and had minimal digital literacy. We observe that most intermediaries had access to phones, and only half had access to a mobile network and experience wit smartphone apps.

Moreover, intermediaries—accustomed to paper-based tracking—found data entry on mobile devices challeng ing, as they lacked experience with smartphone apps. This created difficulty for ChildHealth founders and interns as they were mostly dependent on off-the-shelf modules, which assumed a certain familiarity of the users with apps Outsourcing HIS development to DigiTech helped ChildHealth to ensure that the tablets and application interfac matched users' skill levels. This outsourcing was partly based on the assumption that a software company experi enced in developing user interfaces for several applications for different clients could implement a quick and scalabl project suitable for rural contexts as well. DigiTech was located 1000 km from rural Rajasthan. ChildHealth communi cated the local requirements to DigiTech to localise the new health system in a way that was suitable for ANMs Building on the initial HIS developed by the ChildHealth team, DigiTech delivered a new version in which important modules included new-mother registration, registered mother, new-child registration, registered child, patient searc and delivery.

The multi-tier health platform relied on intermediaries to collect and report online data through NFC-enabled mobile devices. Each intermediary was given a mobile device with the ChildHealth app, which allowed them to scan the data directly from patients (who carried NFC tags). However, when ChildHealth released the ‘improved’ HIS to intermediaries in rural Rajasthan, they received complaints that it was not localised for intermediaries' requirements

Developing localised systems with geographically distant outsourcing partners posed a significant challenge for ChildHealth. Digitech's limited experience in developing applications for rural users, stemming from their developers lack of exposure to rural contexts, infrastructure and user needs, hindered the localisation of the HIS as they strug gled to grasp user requirements without prior rural exposure. ChildHealth faced difficulties in effectively communi cating local requirements to DigiTech even after realising that the digital system lacked sufficient localisation features. For example, the data entry fields were prepopulated with standardised options such as checkboxes, with out provision for free input. The app's format differed significantly from the paper-based register used by intermedi aries, and its display settings were challenging for them to navigate. The technical language used in the app posed an additional barrier to usability. Intermediaries preferred a relatable and simple user interface that they could intuitivel navigate, highlighting the need for improved localisation efforts by DigiTech. One of the ANMs and a FE highlighted the following:

I explained to them [internal developers] that I am used to a paper-based system and do not understand most of it [application developed by DigiTech]. I requested them to add images which I can understand and click. It took some time but they made changes in the application. (ANM 6)

Most of the intermediaries [ANMs] do not understand English and are unaware of technical terms. They could not articulate what kind of changes they needed in the existing application [developed by DigiTech]. Our internal developers spent several months with them to find out how to simplify the language. (FE 1).

Intermediaries also wanted flexibility in capturing and entering data. The generalised application used prepopulated options (such as dropdowns, checkboxes and radio buttons) for several data fields. Intermediarie could not make sense of many of these prepopulated fields and requested the option to enter the data freely:

The initial app [developed by DigiTech] used standard prepopulated options for entering data. Severa users [ANMs] complained about it. (COO).

Based on their experience of working with DigiTech, ChildHealth realised that while DigiTech was suitable for implementing a highly generalised HIS that assumes higher digital literacy and skills, it fell short on developing digita systems to address rurality concerns characterised by low literacy and shallow digital skills. One of the FEs men tioned how local rural users faced difficulties using a generalised HIS:

The application developed [by DigiTech] was not intuitive. It used technical terms which intermedi aries did not understand. During the training sessions, they said they understood how to use this app. But, later, we came to know that they were not using it. They were very hesitant in complaining about it initially. It took some time when they opened up with us and explained the issues in detail. (FE 1).

DigiTech developers developed technologically robust and reliable applications that required strong Internet connections and user sophistication; however, both were unavailable in the rural context. Moreover, whil DigiTech's existing clients were from the corporate sector and had a clear idea of the role of technology in their stan dard business processes—thus, they could capture and articulate their requirements well in use cases—ChildHealth's operations were evolving based on their experience and experimentation in the field, and it was difficult to capture the emerging user requirements in use cases or communicate them. Moreover, the physical distance betwee ChildHealth and DigiTech inhibited effective communication between them. ChildHealth's executives found it difficult to communicate the requirements of rural users to distant partners over the telephone:

The application development team [from DigiTech] was not in touch with the rural. So[,] even if you express [it] in the best possible way, the communication gap will always be there. Even when we try to bring engineers from there to stay here [at ChildHealth's headquarters] for two or three months, we will not get the desired result. We don't have a kind of control on the quality we want to achieve.

This underscores the importance of addressing connectivity issues, low literacy rates, limited digital skills and inadequate infrastructure for developing suitable systems for rural users (Hardy et al., 2019). As research indicates as distance—both physical and psychological—increases (O'Grady & Lane, 1996), integration into the local contex diminishes, hindering the development of applications tailored to rural needs (Lloyd Ashley et al., 2016). In essence our analysis during the initial implementation phase highlighted a paradoxical challenge encountered by ChildHealth; namely, the localisation paradox. This challenge primarily stemmed from the difficulties posed by rural condition and was exacerbated by the organisation's initial limitations in capabilities.

## 4.2.2 | Contextually balanced engagement as a response to the localisation paradox

ChildHealth experienced the localisation paradox during the implementation process. Its responses to this paradoxica tension set the trajectory of the evolution of HER, presented schematically in Figure 1. First, the organisation con ceived the need for a localised HIS, and decided to experiment and develop a localised application with a team of interns and volunteers. However, the organisation faced capability constraints and implementation slowed down highlighting the tension between accelerating the deployment and scaling up of the HIS, and its localisation to address the rurality challenges. The organisation responded by prioritising quick deployment and scaling up; subsequently, it outsourced the application development to a professional organisation. Their expectation was that reliabl and bug-free standard modules would reduce the deployment time and help the organisation implement its projects faster. However, as mentioned above, the lack of localisation while accounting for rurality concerns—which resulted from a lack of contextual understanding of the distant partner organisation—hampered the adoption of the HIS. Consequently, the localisation paradox re-emerged. ChildHealth responded to this paradoxical tension by localising its application development and engaging stakeholders. These responses are described in the following paragraphs

The organisation began seeking local rural talents, or individuals who had freshly graduated from the local area, wer interested in technology and lived within 20–80 km of the ChildHealth headquarters. These individuals would have expe rience and knowledge of the local context. ChildHealth also made it compulsory for all staff and developers to spend tim in the villages and interact with intermediaries. To further situate the developers in the local context, the organisation decided that all developers would be required to spend three to six months in the villages served by ChildHealth

I have been talking about such kind of things … a developer cannot give a smart solution without seeing these things at the village level. So it is our standard process, we not only take developer, the technician to the field but also ask them to sit with them (community), talk with them, stay with them and then come out with the solution. (CEO).

The expectation was that this direct field experience would help developers develop an application that was flexibl and rooted in local requirements. ChildHealth dedicated itself to develop in-house competence to build a localised digital sys tem. It decided to take ownership of the application development process to create an interactive system that allow refinement of the digital system based on feedback from ANMs, who were at the frontline of using these systems:

You need to take ownership over what is the core of the project. In our case, the technology and implementation process are the core and we need to develop in-house capacity in these core areas. (COO).

The decentralisation of the data storage and carrying of the NFC chip, combined with the features of the mobil application enabling access to and updating of NFC chip data, helped address the data portability needs of intermedi aries. With a decentralised HIS, the ANM did not face the burden of maintaining and carrying physical records to track cases where a pregnant woman or a new mother moved to the ANM's village from another village. Moreover, it allowed pregnant women and new mothers to seamlessly register with and avail themselves of healthcare services.

The decentralisation of data storage and carrying was crucial in customising the HIS to meet local needs. Inadequate infrastructure and low network connectivity characterise the rural context, making it difficult to have an app-based implementation that relies on the Internet or intranet to access and update data from a centralised server in real time Moreover, owing to inadequate healthcare facilities, intermediaries had to move from one place to another to provid primary care. For example, the government appointed one intermediary (ANM) for a population of 5000 people. The ANM divided their assigned target population into five groups of approximately 1000 each. Each ASHA who worked with an ANM was responsible for one such group. The ASHA visited the target members of each group once a month They helped the ANM organise the camp visited by pregnant women, new mothers and children to receive services from the ANM and ASHA. The ANM had to enter all data in the designated register at this camp or immediately thereafter. Before ChildHealth's HER was implemented, the ANM was expected to carry a large physical paper register with them however, with the HER, it became easier for them to update and access records without carrying physical registers

In fact, ChildHealth executives deliberated on several options before choosing NFC as a device that could facilitate decentralisation. The choice of NFC was appropriate, considering the poor Internet connectivity in rural areas According to the organisation's COO:

For other options such as cloud-based implementation, we need Internet connectivity. However, in rural areas, Internet connectivity is not available. Therefore, many Internet-based solutions [generally] fail. We are using NFC because the tag the beneficiary holds doesn't require any battery. For Bluetooth[,] both devices require battery and power back up. But this (NFC) chip doesn't require any battery, it doesn't require any connectivity, you just need to bring that tag [within] specified range of phone [or tablet]. (COO).

A ChildHealth executive described how the decentralised HER coped with local practices, such as the movement of pregnant women to maternal homes before delivery, and enabled the delivery of health care services:

If that particular beneficiary is moving from one place to another place what ANM has to do is she just put this card [or pendant] near [the] NFC antenna [reader] and all the record of the beneficiary will be shown there. There is no need to remember all the records not only to ANM but also to the beneficiary. There is no need to remember the past medical history. All the past medical history [is] recorded in this NFC chip. (FE 1).

Thus, ChildHealth responded to the localisation paradox by localising most of the front-end activities and standardising back-end features. This helped address several issues present in the initial fully standardised applica tion developed by the outsourcing partner. However, this localisation process also created challenges for the organi sation. Crucially, localisation was resource and time intensive. The customisation of the HIS to meet local need involved a relatively high cost and slowed the speed of implementation. The founder and one of the FEs mentioned:

Customisation takes time, effort and money. We have [an] internal team of application developers. They are helpful [in customising the application] but it is costly. (Founder).

We [ChildHealth] consider technology just a [small] part of the process. It's more about [structuring] operations right[,] so technology is just a small part. [The] big part is operations [around it]. It took several months of training and changes [in the application] before [the] ANMs could understand how to use it. (FE 1).

ChildHealth also employed an additional response strategy of engaging stakeholders to mitigate these challenges. ChildHealth partnered with the state government so that the HIS implementation plan could become part of the larger initiatives of the state government. This helped them secure funding and provided opportunities to scale up projects. As the founder mentioned:

We have been in contact with the government at the state level. We have regular interactions with [the] top bureaucrats of Rajasthan state and we have been able to include this project [HIS implementation] in the upcoming implementation plan of the government. [The] government has approved to scale this project currently from five blocks to the five districts of Rajasthan under [the] Nirogi Rajasthan Program [Healthy Rajasthan Program]. The government will fund us. So, under this [program] there will be an overarching umbrella where there will be application of NFC-based applications for ANMs. For ASHA, there will be another set of applications that will cover all the requirements of the ASHA at the village level. (Founder).

Thus, ChildHealth's response to engaging the state government as a key stakeholder helped mitigate the two ke challenges of customisation—the cost and speed of implementation—by providing access to funds and an opportu nity to rapidly scale up.

In summary, ChildHealth experienced a paradoxical tension when implementing a mobile application for interme diaries. This paradoxical tension surfaced and resurfaced over time. During the initial phase, the organisatio responded to this persistent tension by prioritising quick deployment and scaling up the customisation of the HIS However, as this tension heightened over time and limited the effectiveness of the HIS, the organisation responde by localising the HIS according to the rural context. However, the localisation paradox resurfaced, as customisation was time and cost intensive. At this stage, the organisation expanded its response strategy by engaging stakeholder who could provide access to funds and scale-up opportunities such that the challenges posed by the paradoxical ten sion could be mitigated.

## 4.3 | Cultural embedding paradox

From their field experiences. ChildHealth realised that the earlier approach in which the rural patient carried a paperbased card with the child's vaccine information had multiple issues. It took longer to compile the data, and th records were lost or damaged in many cases. Consequently, health workers had to painstakingly find which patien had received which vaccination; invariably, some were missed or duplicated. To track the vaccination process more effectively, ChildHealth developed an NFC-enabled chip that could hold a digital copy of the wearer's health histor and could be accessed without an Internet connection. However, the appropriate format for this NFC-enabled chip was the focus of much discussion. Archival documents show that when ChildHealth developed the prototype NFC chip, it deliberated over several options including stickers, health cards, bracelets, chips on bangles for mothers, chip embedded in silicon wristbands for children, anklets and wristlets.

## 4.3.1 | Experiencing the cultural embedding paradox

The effectiveness of the multi-tier HER depended on its receptivity among beneficiaries. We found that while implementing the NFC-enabled data digitisation for beneficiaries, the organisation faced a challenge in choosing between socioculturally aligned or socioculturally adaptable technological artefacts. A socioculturally aligned artefact is designed to appeal to cultural elements of the majority or dominant social group. While such an artefact can func tion well in a homogenous community, it can potentially exclude marginalised social groups in heterogenous commu nities (cf. Bhatt et al., 2023a; Bhatt et al., 2023b; Bhatt et al., 2023c; Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023; Qureshi, Pandey,

et al., 2023). Conversely, if the artefact is not aligned with the cultural elements of any social group, it risks lack of uptake by rural communities. A socioculturally adaptable artefact is flexible and can make a uniform appeal to all cul tures. Thus, it can mitigate implementation challenges due to cultural heterogeneity. However, such artefacts ma not be specific to the sociocultural characteristics of rural contexts. Rurality is characterised by local traditions tha have deep religious, spiritual and cultural connections with local social groups. Artefacts aligned with rural sociocul tural contexts can invoke more interest among rural people, leading to better adoption. The top executives of ChildHealth deliberated on the merits and challenges of implementing artefacts that had no cultural connotations for local communities. The COO recalled:

We wanted to use a technology which is advance[d] but does not require Internet connectivity. [The] NFC chip appeared the most suitable. This allowed capturing and storing data at the beneficiary level. But the big question was how to implement this modern technology, which is not specific to this [rural] culture. (COO).

A socioculturally adaptable artefact may have uniform appeal across all cultures as it is not specific to a particular culture, as the CEO of the ChildHealth suggested:

NFC-based cards or stickers have an advantage. Women across all regions and religions can keep it. (CEO).

However, lack of alignment with the local rural sociocultural context could fail to invoke interest in most in the rural areas, limiting its effectiveness. One FE mentioned:

We are using [an] NFC chip as the core technology. So, there were several options to embed it. For example, stickers, bracelets, anklets, pendant etc. Stickers were also [a] good option [cost wise]. But you know if something is related to the local culture, people talk about it. So, we thought [that] it [stickers] will not be very effective. They [rural women] will not realise what it is. (FE 1).

Further, a uniform design of the artefact runs the risk of being perceived as unaligned with local cultural practices. Rural women may not find such artefacts sufficiently intuitive to sense their significance and thus may not us them. The founder highlighted this limitation of socioculturally adaptable artefacts (e.g., an NFC sticker)

We thought that a pendant with black thread [culturally aligned] will serve as a symbol of protection. So, [rural] women will remember its significance. [An] NFC sticker [culturally unaligned] will not convey the same meaning. (Founder)

ChildHealth decided to develop an artefact that was socioculturally aligned. It evaluated multiple options includ ing stickers, bracelets, wristlets, anklets and pendants, based on their potential for quick adoption by rural women. Of all options considered, ChildHealth settled for the use of NFC-embedded pendants, which aligned with the culture associated with the majority. ChildHealth believed that a socioculturally aligned artefact such as a pendant could invoke interest and lead to better adoption. The COO reflected on the organisation's preference for a socioculturall aligned artefact as follows:

We debated whether to choose [an] NFC sticker that could be pasted on [the] Aadhaar card or any other card, or any other design such as bracelet, wristlet, anklet or pendant. We believed that the pendant [culturally aligned artefact] will have better adoption and retention. Indeed, [the] initial survey suggested [that the] NFC-enabled pendant had a 92% retention rate. (COO).

The choice of artefact was embedded in rural cultural practices of the majority social group. ChildHealth's field survey indicated that for rural communities in the region, the black thread (kaalo dhaago) had the cultural significance of protecting children from the evil eye (buri nazar). According to rural customs, the black thread on which the pendant is strung is commonly believed to keep evil spirits away from newborns (Jain & Agrawal, 2005), babies and thei mothers. The organisation found that the majority social group in the region was accustomed to wearing something similar to a black thread around their necks:

Actually, in the villages, it is a common practice to wear [a] black thread. You see a newborn wearing it on their neck, especially within the first year of their birth. Grownup women also wear it around their waist or neck. (ANM 8).

The organisation believed that by using black thread, the pendant could not only be used as a data storag device, but could serve as a cultural symbol. The organisation highlighted many unique properties of the pendants such as that they were waterproof, required no battery or charge, and each cost less than I USD to produce. Th pendants were embedded with the NFC chip, which stored all health information about the patient. It had a capacit of 886 bytes, which allowed the storage of all information related to the four prenatal visits required for a pregnant woman. Women or children could wear it around their necks, which limited the possibility of losing it or forgetting to bring to the clinic or health camp. Several ANMs and ASHAs highlighted the significance of black thread pendants:

If instead of black thread, there was any other colour or if it was made very colourful, they (people in the village) might not have used it … but because here people believe in jadu-tona [diablerie, witchcraft, superstition], they prefer black thread. (ANM 7).

According to ChildHealth, the strongest feature of their innovation was its alignment with the majority culture and tradition. Owing to its cultural relevance, the organisation assumed that the pendant would be widely adopted by beneficiaries and would encourage more mothers to bring their children to immunisation camps, thereby increas ing immunisation rates. FEs and intermediaries made several recollections of the use of pendants and their socia significance:

I told them [to] wear this around their neck to keep it safe. I said this is to protect you from bad omens. Remember to bring it back whenever you return to the centre. (ANM 5).

In our village, there is [a] different atmosphere. People believe in certain things. This pendant uses copper wires. We tell them [that] these copper wires are good for the babies. (ANM 7).

Often in our village, women are accustomed to wearing a black thread. So, they can wear this too. (ANM 7).

The HER also employed dialect-specific voice call reminders to remind mothers of their child's immunisation sta tus and to wear the NFC necklace. The constant reminder to wear the NFC necklace had two purposes: to avoid los ing the pendant and to display it prominently. The prominent display of the pendant could potentially increas discussions around the necklace. ChildHealth believed that the visibility of the pendant would lead to education and awareness of others about immunisation programs and vaccination services. ChildHealth's founder highlighted in a report:

By symbolically protecting the child from disease, the pendant can act as an educational tool for health workers as well as a social signal, generating a spillover effect within the community. (Founder).

However, less than 20% of women and children were wearing the pendants. Many women were carrying them in their bags. Our data analysis revealed three broad sources of unintended outcomes: (a) existing social hierarchy (b) diverse social norms and (c) changes in the rural social context. In our field observations, we noted the interpla between social hierarchy and technological artefacts. Even though the black thread was culturally aligned wit majority social group norms, women from ‘higher’ social castes found it unsuitable for their social standing. One FE narrated the following story:

I will tell you one thing. Here, actually the caste system is dominant. If a poor and ‘lower’ caste person is wearing the pendant, then the dominant caste and rich people do not want to wear it … The wife of [the] village head came to one of the health centres. Other beneficiaries were also attending the camp. So, all the beneficiaries were wearing these pendants. And that village chief's wife decided not to wear it, saying that everyone is wearing the same pendant, how would I wear it. (FE 2).

Further, in rural Rajasthan, women in general, and first-time pregnant women in particular, are not very comfort able sharing information about their pregnancy with others. ChildHealth quickly found that many women at the earl stage of pregnancy were not wearing a pendant, as this would have announced to the entire village that they wer pregnant. They carried these pendants in small polybags instead of wearing them around their necks:

Early-stage first-time pregnant women have hesitation to actually roam around [,] but after four [to] five months, people themselves are able to know that so that something really observed, many of them are not wearing actually. (FE 1).

Some women say, we will bring it to the centre without wearing it. What is the point of wearing it? There are some wise women who wear it. (ANM 5).

In addition, we observed that individual preferences, shaped by educational level and age, inhibited the uninform adoption of socioculturally aligned artefacts. Educated women associated the black thread with superstition and witchcraft, and did not want to look ignorant. They preferred more colourful threads and pendants of different shapes and sizes.

Now[,] we have some literate women. They do not like to wear it. Those who are not educated, they wear it. (ANM 5).

As an adult member, you don't want to wear the same things that many others are already wearing … there is one issue we face … they are already wearing sarees and [other] wearables and [now] they [have] one more wearable. (ANM 6).

We also observed that some cultural and religious groups in this region were less willing to wear the pendant on the neck; instead, they were more used to wearing a black thread on their upper arms. Thus, while a cultural symbo may be useful in giving the traditional appearance to a digital artefact, if the rural context is comprised of heteroge neous groups, the meaning of that cultural symbol would be interpreted differently. The region in which ChildHealt implemented this project was inhabited by various social groups that differed in religion, caste and socioeconomic status, with different cultural beliefs. The heterogeneity of rural social groups was a challenge to finding a sociocul turally aligned option that was interpreted similarly and adopted uniformly by all social groups

In summary, during implementation, the organisation faced a persistent tension in choosing between sociocul turally adaptable and socioculturally aligned artefacts. We label this tension as the cultural embedding paradox. Below, we describe contextually balanced engagement with this paradoxical tension over time.

Many times, the black thread breaks easily … the kids put it in their mouth, or sometimes women lose it. If it is a card, they can keep [it] in their purse … the card will have their name, so they will know whose card is it. The card will have the woman's name and the name of the child. (ANM 9).

Finally, we observed that using cards instead of pendants was advantageous for partnering with the government, a crucial stakeholder. The government was more familiar with producing and using cards than pendants. The founde explained:

From the government's perspective, they have experience with the card. while the government can manufacture pendants, but they are not familiar with using [the] pendant. The government is used to using cards. Their experience is with cards. (Founder)

Archival sources suggest that engaging the government as a stakeholder improved the scalability of the HIS from five blocks to five districts in the state. This partnership also provided an opportunity to utilise government resources such as public infrastructure (e.g., government school buildings) and personnel (e.g., healthcare workers and primar school teachers) to drive the adoption of the technological artefacts. During our field visits, we observed severa meetings organised by government personnel in government schools and panchayat bhawans (local administration offices).

The organisation navigated through the cultural embedding paradox by incorpo rating multiple responses: localising; cultural adjustment; prioritising; and engaging stakeholders. In the initial phase the organisation responded to this tension by localising. Later, to navigate the challenges posed by the social hierar chy and diverse subcultures, it responded by culturally adjusting the artefact; that is, by providing several options or constructing an artefact that was acceptable to all social groups. However, this response elevated the tension ove time as the organisation faced operational challenges in offering both socioculturally adaptable and socioculturall aligned artefacts. The organisation responded to this paradox by prioritising a socioculturally adaptable artefact (i.e., an NFC-based card) over a socioculturally aligned artefact (i.e., a pendant). This prioritisation was based on the familiarity and practical benefits of the culturally adaptable artefacts. Finally, the organisation expanded its response strategy by engaging the government as a key stakeholder. This helped drive the scaling and adoption of NFC-based cards, thus addressing the potential adoption concerns associated with ‘universal’ artefacts

## 4.4 | Implementation outcomes

By implementing the HER, ChildHealth improved the quality and coverage of healthcare services delivered to preg nant women, new mothers and their infants in rural areas over the seven years under observation. This also benefited intermediaries because the mobile application and NFC-enabled chip eased their workload by improving data access and storage. Our data analysis revealed that ChildHealth created impact through two types of outcome process and health outcomes. Process outcomes included health tracking for rural mothers, vaccination tracking for chil dren and benefits for intermediaries.

We found that HER improved the health and vaccination tracking processes, benefiting all stakeholders. As th HER increased the speed of data capturing and syncing, government health officials could track healthcare coverag in near real-time and apply the necessary interventions. ChildHealth's COO and founder highlighted how this multi tier HER improved healthcare tracking in around 600 villages in Rajasthan:

Our solution is helping health workers [ASHA and ANM] to monitor more than 30 000 mothers … in around 600 villages. Health officials can see the updated data on [the] dashboard (COO).

The vaccination history of children is recorded in the NFC chip. Now, it is easy to track which vaccination is due and which are given. Our solution [HER] can help achieve the full immunisation goal of the state government. They [the government] are now supporting us to expand from five blocks to five districts. (Founder).

The use of socioculturally aligned HER could invoke the interest of beneficiaries, thereby increasing their awareness of the importance of vaccination and healthcare initiatives by the government. Intermediaries particularly benefited from rural mothers' enhanced awareness and interest. One intermedi ary described her experience as follows:

[The] situation has improved in my village. Earlier, people (with infants) did not come for vaccination but now they are coming. Our work has also become easier because of the tablet (mobile app). (ANM 14).

In addition, these process outcomes helped the organisation achieve tangible health benefits for beneficiaries including an increase in immunisation rate and reduction in malnutrition. In collaboration with a reputed impact evalua tion agency, ChildHealth conducted a study during 2016–18 to assess the impact of the HER. The results of the ran domised control trial suggested that the HER helped increase the immunisation rate by around 12% and reduce th malnutrition rate by around 4%. These findings were also highlighted by the founder and one of the FEs who wa actively engaged in the study:

We identified 81 subcentres as control group and made interventions in 81 where we gave our app to them. After two years of study, we got results that in the intervention areas, the immunisation rate increased by 12% and malnutrition decreased by 4%. (FE1)

Within two years [2016–17], we expanded the scope of our application [HER] from immunisation to maternal healthcare. Our application [HER] helps both mothers and children [covering] from antenata care to postnatal care to immunisation. Its [HER] impact is beyond full immunisation. Our impact assessment study shows [that] malnutrition has also gone down [by 4%]. (Founder)

Thus, the preliminary evidence shows positive outcomes of the HER implementation. Our analysis revealed how contextually balanced engagement with the localisation and cultura embedding paradoxes shaped the evolution of the HER. ## 5 | DISCUSSION

We traced the process of the implementation of an HIS by ChildHealth to explore how rurality influences the implementation of HIS and how organisations navigate the challenges arising during implementation because of characteristics of rurality. We specifically identified two paradoxes faced by ChildHealth during the implementation of an HI rooted in the characteristics of rurality. ChildHealth responded to these tensions by localising, engaging stakeholders cultural adjustment and prioritising, ultimately leading to the evolution of HIS into HER. Overall, we contribute to th ICT4D literature by (1) improving understanding of the implementation and evolution of HER, (2) identifying the paradoxes experienced by organisations during implementation and (3) exploring the responses involved in navigating these paradoxes. We discuss each of the contributions below.

## 5.1 | Rurality and ICT4D: Evolution of HER

In rural areas, descriptive and sociocultural characteristics demand that ICT4D projects undergo significant localisation and cultural embedding through contextually balanced engagement to result in positive implementation outcomes. Ignoring intricate rural dynamics such as resource constraints, patriarchy, caste hierarchy, illiteracy, pov erty and lack of infrastructure (Bhatt et al., 2022; Goh et al., 2016; Qureshi et al., 2018; Qureshi, Bhatt, et al., 2023 Riaz & Qureshi, 2017; Tim et al., 2018; Venkatesh et al., 2020), could have unintended consequences. Such consequences may exacerbate social inequalities (Wyche et al., 2016), breed distrust (Sutter et al., 2023), foster over reliance on external entities (Wade, 2002) and disproportionately empower dominant social groups (Quresh et al., 2018).

Numerous studies have explored ICT4D projects aimed at delivering various services in rural settings, spanning health information capture (Pearson et al., 2016), temperature-sensitive vaccine tracking (Holeman & Barrett, 2017) and maternal healthcare monitoring (Venkatesh et al., 2020). However, it is crucial to recognise that most of the rura populace lacks the basic economic and technological capabilities to fully utilise ICT4D services. To ensure successfu implementation, ICT4D initiatives must adapt urban advances to suit rural needs (Foster & Heeks, 2013; Nakata & Weidner, 2012) and involve rural communities in the co-creation process to address challenges faced by Indigenous communities (London, 2016; Parthiban et al., 2021; Qureshi, Pan, & Zheng, 2021). Projects that fail to tailor their offerings to local needs risk low adoption rates and limited impact (Hardy et al., 2019). Rurality, far from being static (Hardy et al., 2019; Lichter & Brown, 2011; Zheng et al., 2018), undergoes constant evolution, demanding careful monitoring and consideration during project imple mentation. We emphasise that the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rural contexts serve as catalysts giving rise to paradoxes for organisations implementing ICT4D projects. The evolution of HER by ChildHealth wa also influenced by broader changes sweeping through rural communities, driven largely by governmental directives local banking institutions and other authorised agencies. Notably, we found that a state government initiative aime to streamline all state government services through a unified identity card closely following the central government' allocation of identity cards for various sectors. such as public distribution. healthcare and financial schemes. This resulted in the familiarity of rural communities with NFC cards, paving the way for their implementation b ChildHealth. Our findings reveal that organisations need to be cognisant of the overall rural context, instead of onl paying attention to community characteristics or adopting a static understanding of rurality.

## 5.2 | Paradoxes in implementing ICT4D projects

We leverage the literature on paradox (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017) and view tensions as meta-level concepts that organisational actors may experience (Smith & Lewis, 2011; Wareham et al., 2014) while implementing an ICT4D project. When tensions arising from descriptive and sociocultural characteristics manifest in practice, they present organisational actors with conflicting choices (Wimelius et al., 2021). Recognising such tensions is important because this contributes to ICT4D performance and innovation (Hahn et al., 2014; 2016; Ingram, Lewis, Barton, & Gartner, 2016). In our analysis of ChildHealth, we leverage the paradox literature to reveal empirically observable tensions during the implementation of an ICT4D project. We also demonstrate how different characteristics of rurality trigger these tensions.

While the tension between standardisation and customisation is well discussed in the literature (Woodard et al., 2013), we move beyond it and explore two forms of underpinning tensions experienced by the organisation The localisation paradox highlights the dynamics between the localisation of the implementation and a generalised implementation approach. The cultural embedding paradox highlights the dynamics between developing sociocultur ally aligned and socioculturally adaptable implementation, Further, we show that these paradoxes surface and resur face in the organisation, indicating the persistence of underlying tensions. Competing demands cannot be resolved but rather continually resurface, and organisations need to manage them (Smith & Lewis, 2011). In addition, our find ings highlight the role of the rural context in making tensions salient and persistent over time (Hargrave & Van d Ven, 2017; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Despite the burgeoning literature on paradox, the role of context has been understudied. Recent studies have called for more research to understand how context plays an important role in shaping tension dynamics (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Pradies et al., 2021; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Our finding emphasise that the descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality largely trigger localisation and cultura embedding paradoxes, respectively.

## 5.3 | Responses to the paradoxical tensions

Paradox scholars have identified different types of ‘either-or’ (defensive) and ‘both-and’ (active) responses by organisations that encounter tensions (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Schad et al., 2016; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Th either-or response includes splitting, regression, repression, projection, reaction formation and ambivalence (Andriopoulos & Lewis, 2009; Jarzabkowski et al., 2013). Both-and responses include acceptance, confrontation, transcendence, synthesis and purposeful iteration of temporal or spatial separation between them (Hargrave & Van de Ven, 2017; Lewis, 2000; Smith & Lewis, 2011). Although these responses are valuable for understanding how organisations address paradoxes, their primary focus is internal to the organisation. However, the implementation of ICT4D projects involves collaboration with external actors. For example, ChildHealth needed to work with externa parties, including health workers and community members, to effectively deliver its services. While we acknowledg the usefulness of both-and responses by ChildHealth in navigating paradoxes in an external context, we contend that the actions through which the organisation adopts such a response are less well understood. Our findings suggest that contextually balanced engagement in navigating paradoxes triggered by an external context go beyond established both-and responses, such as splitting and purposeful iteration between temporal or spatial separation which aid in managing paradoxes arising from the internal context. In this regard, our findings emphasise ‘stake holder engagement as a response intricately linked to the external context.

Additionally, cultural adjustment as a response to the cultural embedding paradox extends the understanding of established both-and responses by suggesting how an organisation can navigate the paradoxical tensions posed b the varied interpretations of the technology in a diverse sociocultural context. The concept of cultural adjustment aligns with the theme of interpretive flexibility (Doherty et al., 2006; Orlikowski, 1992). Interpretive flexibility, as introduced by Orlikowski (1992), suggests that technology's meaning is subject to interpretation by individuals and groups. In the case of ChildHealth, cultural adjustments reflect the organisation's response to diverse and evolving interpretations of technology in the rural context. Thus, while interpretive flexibility as a concept is focused on users interpretations of an artefact, the concept of cultural adjustments is the response by implementing organisation to accommodate or address those varied interpretations by users.

Further, we believe that cultural adjustment also resonates well with the congruent and incongruent technologi cal frames proposed by Orlikowski and Gash (1994). Cultural adjustment can be seen as aligning with congruent frames, facilitating smoother integration; while incongruent frames might necessitate adjustments to accommodat diverse sociocultural perspectives. Thus, if all community members have a congruent frame about a particular cultura artefact, an implementing organisation can resort to use of a socioculturally aligned cultural artefact; however, if vari ous social groups have incongruent technological frames the implementing organisation can resort to a sociocultur ally adaptable artefact. Again, the difference between congruent and incongruent technological frames as proposed in the literature, and our proposed construct of cultural adjustment, is that the former represents the perspectives of users about the artefact, whereas the latter is the implementation strategy employed by the organisation. In sum, th concept of cultural adjustment is similar to established concepts such as interpretive flexibility and congruent incongruent technological frames in terms of emphasising that technology is not neutral; its meaning and impact ar shaped by diverse and dynamic perspectives. However, cultural adjustment goes beyond emphasising the social con struction of technology and underscores how implementing organisations need to respond to the diverse and evolv ing interpretations of technology.

Encapsulating the need to appreciate the external context and the diverse and evolving interpretations of tech nology, the concept of contextually balanced engagement captures how an organisation's responses to paradoxe evolve over time and are context specific. In this way, we address the growing concern in the literature with one best way to deal with paradoxical tension and the taming paradox (Berti & Cunha, 2023; Cunha & Putnam, 2019), and instead, emphasise that responses should be dynamic in nature. In the case of ChildHealth, we observed that th organisation initially pursued a strategy of prioritising requirements, focusing on immediate needs. Although effec tive for a brief period, this approach led to the recurrence of the paradox (Smith & Lewis, 2011). To address thi issue, ChildHealth followed a strategy of localising or cultural adjustment depending on the triggers that made th paradoxes salient. For example, the localising approach, involving customised implementation of a mobile app proved effective for addressing the localisation paradox, which was triggered by the descriptive characteristics of rurality. However, in dealing with the cultural embedding paradox, which was triggered by the sociocultural characteristics of rurality, a socioculturally aligned artefact was less successful, often resulting in non-participation by use groups that perceived the artefact as a symbol specific to a particular cultural group. To navigate the diverse inter pretation of the technological artefact, the organisation followed a strategy of cultural adjustment, introducing a socioculturally adaptable artefact that offered interpretive flexibility. Further, when the paradoxes resurfaced in an evolving external context, the organisation expanded its response strategy by engaging external stakeholder (Ramus et al., 2021).

In this way, the organisation's comprehension of the evolving context and diverse interpretations of the technol ogy guided all four responses—prioritising; localising; cultural adjustment; and engaging stakeholders—to navigate the localisation and cultural embedding paradoxes

## 5.4 | Future research

While our study has some limitations, we see them as opportunities for future research. First, contextual factors in other cases may differ, adding nuance to our findings. Future research should consider using multiple cases of socia enterprises implementing the HER. Second, this research was conducted in a single state in India. We provided thick descriptions to assist transferability to different social and economic contexts. Subsequent research can investigat the HER in various sociocultural contexts to understand the role of culture in the implementation of ICT4D projects more broadly. Third. our findings highlight the cultural embedding paradox. suggesting that cultural alignment can enhance ICT implementation in rural areas but is also associated with challenges in highly diverse contexts. Our find ings reveal organisational responses over time to navigate this paradox. Future research may explore alternative approaches whereby an artefact remains responsive to the local cultural context without aligning too closely with any specific culture in a multicultural setting. For example, a standardised artefact with artificial intelligence that can adapt locally may follow a distinct implementation trajectory. Such artefacts can be designed to achieve the appropriate cultural balance based on the context, preserving their appeal for implementation without compromising cul tural sensitivity. Future studies should consider these alternatives to provide further insights into implementation strategies for ICT4D projects. Further, our findings underscore that the descriptive and sociocultural attributes of rurality predominantly result in localisation and cultural embedding paradoxes, respectively. However, we also observed that practical issues related to descriptive rurality, such as unreliable Internet connectivity, can shape the need for the artefact (i.e., NFC-enabled chip). The NFC chip inherently offers flexibility, accommodating divers design formats such as pendants, cards and wristbands. However, ChildHealth's understanding of the sociocultura context played a pivotal role in the initial selection of the kind of artefact. Hence, our findings reveal that sociocul tural characteristics significantly shape the choice between a socioculturally aligned or socioculturally adaptable artefact. In essence, in our specific context, the interplay between the paradoxes was not salient and manifested distinctly in triggers related to descriptive and sociocultural characteristics of rurality. However, in alternative con texts, this interplay of paradoxes may be salient and may offer intriguing insights, warranting further exploration

## 6 | CONCLUSION

Our investigation into the journey of ChildHealth, a social enterprise operating in rural India to combat child mortalit through a digitally enabled vaccine tracking system, underscores the profound impact of rurality on HIS implementa tion. Through our comprehensive field study, we reveal how rural characteristics instigate localisation and cultura embedding paradoxes, prompting adaptive responses from ChildHealth. Our contribution to the ICT4D literature lie in spotlighting the transformation of HIS into HER as a strategic response to the intricate paradoxes inherent in rura settings. Rural contexts, with their distinctive descriptive and sociocultural characteristics, necessitate a nuance approach to ICT4D projects. Our findings underscore the imperative for significant localisation and cultural embed ding, achieved through contextually balanced engagement, to result in positive implementation outcomes. In navigating the complex interplay of rural dynamics, organisations like ChildHealth must adopt tailored strategies tha recognise the diverse needs and cultural nuances of rural communities.

Drawing from our insights, we propose several recommendations for organisations embarking on ICT4D initia tives in rural areas. First, they should prioritise comprehensive understanding of local contexts, including resourc constraints, gender dynamics and traditional norms, to inform project design and implementation. Second, the should embrace a dynamic approach that acknowledges the evolving nature of rural settings, ensuring flexibility and adaptability in project strategies. Third, effective stakeholder engagement, including collaboration with local commu nities and government agencies, is essential for project success. By fostering meaningful partnerships and solicitin community input, organisations can cultivate ownership and sustainability within rural contexts. Fourth, we advocate for continuous evaluation and iterative improvement throughout project lifecycles. Embracing a culture of learning and adaptation enables organisations to navigate emerging challenges and capitalise on opportunities for innovation. Thus, our study underscores the transformative potential of ICT4D initiatives in rural contexts, while emphasisin the critical role of localisation, cultural embedding and contextually balanced engagement in achieving sustainabl impact. Using these recommendations, organisations can navigate the complexities of rural implementation and con tribute meaningfully to the advancement of rural health outcomes.
