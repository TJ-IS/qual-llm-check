## Abstract

Time is central to many debates about hybrid work- the impact on speed and productivity, commute times, and synchronisation of work. However, we argue that time is often over-simplified in extant hybrid work literature and tends to ignore many temporal concepts that capture the inherently complex, multi-faceted, subtle, and socially embedded nature of time. To address this issue, we conducted studies of five hybrid work teams across two large organisations. The paper contributes to current research on hybrid work by (i) illustrating the various temporal concepts that may be considered in the design of hybrid work environments, as well as the impact of considering or not considering them; (ii) illustrating how these temporal concepts in a physica space can be augmented in the digital space, rather than assuming the digital space should simply represent the physical; and (iii) providing a framework for the consideration of time–aware hybrid work. We hope to spark scientific interest in studying the temporal nuances of hybrid work but also the temporal aspects of design in other areas.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

While hybrid work is not a new concept, its prevalence in society has increased dramatically in the wake of th COVID-19 pandemic (Abdullah et al., 2020; Chamakiotis et al., 2021; Hilberath et al., 2020). It encompasses practices ranging from working from home, remote working, telecommuting, to digital nomadism (Wang et al., 2020). To achieve this relies heavily on digital technologies as a mediating interface or infrastructure (e.g., Dorow & Jean, 2022; Fang et al., 2022; Schoch et al., 2022). In the context of this study, we therefore define hybrid work as a sociotechnical work arrangement, mediated by digital technology to enable collaboration among employees workin from different times (e.g., synchronously and asynchronously) and different spaces (e.g., in the office and at home)’. As wil be discussed later, we interpret the term digital technologies broadly, referring to ‘any digital technology that is used to enable hybrid work to mediate their communication (Klein & Watson-Manheim, 2021; O'Leary & Cummings, 2007; Sarker, Ahuja, et al., 2019; Schoch et al., 2022; Watson Fritz et al., 1997), coordination (Watson Fritz et al., 1997) knowledge sharing management (Fang et al., 2022; Griffith et al., 2003), or collaboration (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009 Sarker & Sahay, 2004; Waizenegger et al., 2020). Nevertheless, these technologies which ‘support goal attainment are instances of Information Systems (IS) (Watson, 2014). As such, in our definition, acknowledging the role of IS which includes both technical and social factors (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Sarker, Chatterjee, et al., 2019; Struijk et al., 2022), we view hybrid work as a sociotechnical arrangement.

While some have looked at the spatial aspects, existing hybrid work research tends to over-simplify time's inher ently complex, multi-faceted, subtle, and socially embedded nature (Zamani & Spanaki, 2023). Indeed, most IS and management research ignore such complexity (Ancona et al., 2001; de Vaujany et al., 2021; Venkatesh et al., 2021) often simply focusing on concepts such as speed and simple clock time measured in hours and days for example. Hybri work requires us to think about strategies for organising work between employees located in different spatial and temporal settings (Asatiani et al., 2021; Halford, 2005; Messenger & Gschwind, 2016; Sewell & Taskin, 2015; Smit<sup>ˇ</sup> et al., 2023).

In general management research, Ancona et al. (2001) created an overarching and exhaustive set of tempora concepts and structured them under three categories. These are (i) conceptions of time–concepts which refer to how we think about and structure time (e.g., around the traditional clock and calendar, around events, around social constructions of time); (ii) mapping activities to time–concepts which refer to how we schedule activities such as their duration, their frequency, their synchronisation, interruptions to activities or the rhythm of activities; and (iii) actor relating to time–concepts which refer to how the people involved in those activities have varying temporal personali ties and perceptions as to how they view time. These temporal categories and associated concepts will be discussed further in Section 3.

Examining the hybrid work literature it is clear that most of the concepts in the Ancona framework have not been considered sufficiently. We argue it is very important to address this gap. First, any one of these temporal con cepts can have very negative implications if not considered in a hybrid setting, or in contrast can make a hybrid setting more enjoyable and productive. For example, Shin & Pérez-Nordtvedt, (2018) have examined the importance of rhythm in organisational life and the detrimental effects of ignoring it. Likewise, others have studied deadline behaviour (Waller et al., 2001), but again not in a hybrid setting. Over-simplification or omitting temporal considerations may mean that specific claims regarding increased productivity and time savings may be wrong or at least inflated Even if these temporal benefits are possible in a given hybrid work context, the various complexities and ambiguities regarding time may stop or reduce the realisation of these benefits. Second, one can argue that a full examination of temporal concepts is particularly important when designing hybrid environments as time binds together the different physical and virtual spaces of hybrid work. Once a team splits into on-site and remote workers, the synchronisation of their time provides the primary means of interaction, and temporal coordination often determines whether a particular hybrid work arrangement is viable and effective (O'Leary & Cummings, 2007). Digital technologies are particularl pivotal as they often provide the only interface between the various workers. Third, it is clear that there are differ ences in temporal concepts between the physical and virtual spaces of hybrid work. For example, studies have coined the term “zoom fatigue” where the duration of meetings needs to be adjusted in hybrid settings as a worker's capac ity to pay attention and be productive is lower in a virtual setting than in a physical one<sup>1</sup> (e.g., Chamakiotis et al., 2021; Christian, 2021). A study that examines all temporal concepts can identify these differences and allow those implanting digital technologies in hybrid settings to do so in a more considered manner.

To address these issues, this paper seeks to answer the following research questions:

RQ1. How do temporal categories and associated concepts affect the design of hybrid work?

RQ2. How can digital technologies address the temporal categories and concepts of hybrid work?

The paper contributes to the existing literature by providing researchers and practitioners in hybrid work with a framework to identify the temporal concepts and categories that might be relevant in a particular hybrid work envi ronment. By identifying the relevant and non-relevant concepts they can design or adapt hybrid work processes and the associated digital technologies to address these temporal concepts. The case studies illustrate the importance of considering these concepts, the negative consequences when they are not carefully considered, and identify some exemplar uses of digital technologies to address these temporal concepts. In doing so, we respond to the recent call for increased focus on non-economic interests of different stakeholders while highlighting the unique role of the IS that includes both the technical and the social factor, along with their interrelations (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Sarker Chatterjee, et al., 2019; Struijk et al., 2022).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. The next section presents the relevant literature on core ideas on hybrid work, used to inform the study. This is followed by a review of the temporal concepts that underpin this study, with a description of the temporality framework. The research method, findings, and analysis based on cases of hybrid work teams at two large organisations are then presented. We then propose some recommendation for designing time-aware hybrid work design. The paper ends with conclusions and limitations of the study.

## 2 | PRIOR STUDIES ON HYBRID WORK

## 2.1 | Core ideas in hybrid work research

Scientific discourse on hybrid work has a considerable history, tracing its roots to the streams of virtual teams, geo graphically dispersed teams, technology-mediated teams, remote work, virtual work, and flexible work. These different research streams mainly discuss three core ideas (Cheng et al., 2016; Cousins et al., 2007; Gilstrap et al., 2022 Mrass et al., 2021; Sewell & Taskin, 2015): digital technology mediation/facilitation, space, and time. Whe referencing to time authors mainly focus on a few time concepts (e.g., clock time, time zone differences) (Asatian et al., 2021: Espinosa et al., 2003: Gratton, 2021: Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010). However, despite this agreement of existing literature on the core ideas of hybrid work, there are a few definitions of hybrid work or teams (see Table A1 in Appendix), and even these do not necessarily incorporate explicitly the core ideas identified

Given our definition of hybrid work stated in the introduction, and the core ideas identified in existing literature Figure 1 depicts the elements required to define hybrid work. In a nutshell, hybrid work requires the definition of space, the selection of the appropriate digital technologies to ensure a seamless connection of the spaces, and the definition of time and temporal mapping. Below we briefly discuss space and digital technologies. While useful in understanding prior hybrid work practices, these core ideas need refinement for the recent changes in the “third way of working” (Zamani et al., 2021). To address the emerging calls for a more comprehensive approach to hybrid work, this paper emphasises the importance of incorporating time for the design of hybrid work, as advocated in Section 3.

## 2.2 | Hybrid workspaces and digital technology

Hybrid workers can choose the space to perform their work, working from home, in their dedicated office rooms, or from other locations (Zamani & Spanaki, 2023) (e.g., while travelling) (Bednorz, 2024). As such there are cases that workers can design their work arrangement in space—or choose from predesigned work arrangement patterns (Gratton, 2021; Smite, Tkalich, et al., 2022). Common concepts when discussing space, are space flexibility whic means that employees can work from any space (i.e., digital or physical) and blended spaces which means designing a seamless connection between the physical workspace (i.e., office rooms or a meeting room) and digital workspace (i.e., video conferencing system) (Benyon & Mival, 2015; Dubé & Robey, 2009; Wallace et al., 2017). This blendin allows employees to gain the best of both worlds (Mitchell & Brewer, 2022). It is clear that in all these cases, th notion of space remains fluid, as it can also refer to working while travelling, on the move, from several spaces (e.g., cafeterias, co-working spaces etc.) or as digital nomads (Bassyiouny & Wilkesmann, 2023; Bednorz, 2024). The fluidity of digital technologies has led to a widespread diffusion in their use (Aleem et al., 2023). Digital technologies encompass various functions and offer seamless information access across multiple channels, enabling users to inter act smoothly. Users can now access applications and platforms seamlessly across various devices—such as tablets smartphones, or laptops—while dynamically switching among them based on individual needs. This seamless interaction has facilitated the development of Ubiquitous Media Systems, as reported in IS research (Carillo et al., 2017).

Space is one determinant of the level of hybridity. IS research has focused on the degrees of geographic dispersion (O'Leary & Cummings, 2007), or on the degrees of virtuality (e.g., Asatiani et al., 2021; Asatiani & Penttinen, 2019) to define the space spectrum. There are also several studies on how to measure these; for instance calculating the percentage of people working remotely (Scott & Tlmmerman, 1999), examining the proportion of members who work virtually and their distance (Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010), or examining more factors including team distribution, mobility, and other practices such as changing team members (Chudoba et al., 2005).

Focusing on digital technologies, a common discussion in existing literature is predominantly about how the can be used for the digitalization of work that is, to transform the traditional on-site work activities into digital remote-compatible activities (Gilson et al., 2015; Mitchell & Brewer, 2022; Wang & Haggerty, 2009). However, in practice this is only one element of hybridity, since digital technologies can not only assist workers digitise thei activities as-is but also enhance them and create new opportunities for different forms of activities (Gilstrap et al., 2022). In essence, digital technology plays a key mediating role in hybrid work. Hybrid workers use digital tech nologies to enable seamless communication and collaboration among them, across different physical and digita spaces (Gilson et al., 2015; Watson Fritz et al., 1997; Watson-Manheim et al., 2002). Digital technologies help hybrid workers to mediate their communication (Klein & Watson-Manheim, 2021; O'Leary & Cummings, 2007; Sarker Ahuja, et al., 2019; Schoch et al., 2022; Watson Fritz et al., 1997), coordination (Watson Fritz et al., 1997), manage ment (Fang et al., 2022; Griffith et al., 2003), or collaboration (Bjørn & Ngwenyama, 2009; Sarker & Sahay, 2004 Waizenegger et al., 2020), or even social interactions (Deschênes, 2024) which can lead in providing more opportuni ties than face-to-face/on-site (Smith et al., 2018).

Overall, the role of these digital technologies is not only to improve performance (Pauleen & Yoong, 2001) effectiveness (Furst et al., 1999; Griffith et al., 2003; Raghuram et al., 2019), and support decision making (Majchrzak et al., 2000); but also to improve workers' experience (Dery & Sebastian, 2017), avoid isolation (Kirkman et al., 2005) and create a sense of togetherness (Hacker et al., 2020). These outcomes point to what IS scholars call humanisti (related to achieving organisational goals, such as revenue generation, customer acquisition, increased productivity, improved performance, higher customer satisfaction, improved quality); and instrumental (related to various aspects of work and personal life including job satisfaction, social relationships, family time, flexibility, work-life balance wellbeing) outcomes from the sociotechnical perspective (Sarker, Chatterjee, et al., 2019).

## 2.3 | The role of digital technology and space in the design of hybrid work

The above discussion implies an important assumption about virtual teams, geographically dispersed teams, technology-mediated teams, remote work, virtual work, and flexible work (see Table A1 in Appendix). The assump tion is that work activities that used to be conducted on-site can be redesigned to be doable remotely, thanks to dig ital technology. This is the called digitalization of work and includes a faithful representation, replicating the wor activities that usually take place in the physical space in a digital space faithfully—without changing any aspects of the work (the left part of Figure 2 visualises the mechanism behind this assumption). Following the assumption, th more faithful the representation is, the better the work outcomes. As elaborated in our findings (see Section 5), this paper challenges the above assumption.

To challenge this assumption, we view digital technologies as an IS which has a set of entities, shared patterns and information processing capabilities that support goal attainment” (Watson, 2014). In essence, in our context th digital technologies are used to process information and serve the goals of the workers (entities). These goals are the humanistic and instrumental outcomes (as described in Section 2.2). The shared patterns involve the way they coordinate, cooperate, communicate, synchronise etc. in hybrid. These interactions apart from the digital technologies also involve humans, giving apart from the technology component, a social component (Chatterjee et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2015; Leonardi, 2012). We further elaborate on this sociotechnical component in hybrid design of work in the discussion section (Leonardi, 2012; Sarker, Chatterjee, et al., 2019)

Moreover, we rely on representation theory to explain our idea of digitalizing work from the physical space (on site working) to the digital space (remoted or digitalized working) (see Figure 2 left). Representation theory proposes that an IS should faithfully represent the meaning of real-word phenomena to be useful (Recker et al., 2019). This representational fidelity is a widely accepted criterion for assessing an IS (Burton-Jones et al., 2017; Recker et al., 2019). However, a faithful representation of work activities (or else digitalization of work) is not always feasible or effective. The design of hybrid work indeed starts with digitalization of work, but it can also mean combining remot or digitalized work with on-site work (see Figure 2 right). Different workspace combination also affects the ways work is done and, in turn, the outcomes of hybrid work (Gratton, 2021; Smite, Tkalich, et al., 2022). Digital technology again plays important mediating roles in coordinating and synchronising works and team members across different physical and digital workspaces. Digital technologies can be used for video conference, text communication, file sharing, remote access, project and task management, and knowledge management (Killoran et al., 2023; Meluso et al., 2022; Wang & Haggerty, 2009). But collaboration in hybrid work requires more than the commonly used systems for synchronous text editing. Instead, such collaboration can be supported by systems dedicated to specific types of work. For instance, digital pair programming can help to improve productivity and higher code quality, sinc these tasks are difficult to execute in hybrid environments due to the lack of digital technology support (Smite et al., 2021; Smite et al.,<sup>ˇ</sup> 2023). Similarly, whiteboarding platforms can act as catalysts for engagement facilitating visual problem-solving, drawing, and sharing ideas in real-time (Smit et al., 2022).

## 3 | TIME FOR THE DESIGN OF HYBRID WORK

In existing literature when referring to time, we mainly mean that workers can work at any time, having the freedom to decide when (Gratton, 2021; Smite, Christensen, et al., 2022). Some researchers admit that the level or hybridity of a company apart from the space, is also affected by time and temporal arrangements (e.g., Chudoba et al., 2005 O'Leary & Cummings, 2007; Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010; Staples & Webster, 2008). However, even in this case they focus on time differences among the members of a team, the difference in time zone, the working hours, etc Also, more recently the majority of IS studies are focused on time but in terms of time flexibility. For instance pinpointing that workers can allocate the workdays/hours to spend not only in which work location (Smite Christensen, et al., 2022) but also within the day (Asatiani et al., 2021; Gratton, 2021). Apart from this simplistic view of time, IS research has also studied concepts such as asynchronicity (Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010) and tempora boundaries (Espinosa et al., 2003). Still, authors are focused on how these are caused due to differences in time zones, shifts, and working rhythms. In another stream of studies, temporal concepts such as continuities and discon tinuities are also discussed, mainly to pinpoint how to achieve a seamless experience in the blended workspaces (Watson-Manheim et al., 2002; Watson-Manheim et al., 2012).

Despite the importance of time in hybrid work design, there is a lack of studies in existing IS literature. How ever, temporal concepts are crucial in hybrid settings as speeding up of activities, temporal rhythms, and personali ties affect employees' collaboration and productivity (Holt & Johnsen, 2019; Sarker & Sahay, 2004). As such, th next step in this study is to understand the temporal concepts one must consider when thinking about a hybrid work design. This is not straightforward as time is an inherently fluid, complex, multi-faceted, and subtle concept (Conboy et al., 2020; Mousavi Baygi et al., 2021), and in IS research is often omitted (e.g., Asatiani & Penttinen, 2019; Dubé & Robey, 2009), ignored or over-simplified (de Vaujany et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2021).

To address this gap in IS, this study adopts the Ancona et al. (2001) framework for two key reasons. First, it offers an extensive examination of the temporal concepts, bringing together a broad range of temporal ideas from various fields of temporal research and establishing a unified structure for organising these concepts (Shen et al., 2015). While some IS studies examine one or a few concepts of the framework in a hybrid (e.g., Zamani & Spanaki, 2023) or virtual setting (e.g., Dubé & Robey, 2009), none have looked at temporality in such a broad and holistic manner. Second, it places considerable focus on the social, human, and hermeneutic concepts of temporalit (Conboy et al., 2020), which is critically important for the study of hybrid work and is also explicitly called for in this Special Issue.

Since this framework will be used later as a core to present our findings, below we provide some examples from existing literature for each category of Table 1. These examples serve as applied studies in work environments but are not necessarily related to hybrid work. Regarding the first category, individuals can set their conceptions o time’. In a work setting for example employees can set a specific time for a meeting (clock time) or meet when a specific event takes place (event time). For instance, as noted above, clock time has been examined in several studies (e.g., Anderson et al., 2018; Asatiani et al., 2021; Gratton, 2021) mainly to see how the different time zones affect teams. Regarding, the second, actors relating to time explains how individuals, teams, and/or organisations perceiv and respond to the passage of time along the continuum when involved in the two previous categories (Ancona et al., 2001; O'Connor et al., 2022). For instance, Chinchanachokchai et al. (2015) focus on the temporal perception of workers and examine the effect on time passing when multitasking. Closing, ‘mapping activities to time’, it is related to how the different events and activities taking place can be mapped to time (Ancona et al., 2001). Duratio for example is a single activity, whereas a cycle is a repeated activity. For instance, Baskerville and Pries-Heje (2004 focus on short time cycles in systems development. Deadline on the other hand can cause a transformation in how an activity is approached and executed. As the deadline approaches, behaviours might change since there may b changes in the prioritisation of tasks to ensure that the activity is completed within the given time frame, etc. Ther are some studies (e.g., Gevers et al., 2006; Waller et al., 2001) that examined the deadline behaviour in teams.

Each of the sample concepts listed in Table 1 is used as starting prompts to aid a more nuanced and multi dimensional perspective of time in hybrid work. Each may apply to hybrid work or, indeed, digital or physical space that make up that hybrid environment. Let's take rhythm, for example. In a physical space, we may see a rhythm of meetings or activities occurring simultaneously each day, week, or month. However, the rhythm of meetings may dif fer among teams working in a digital-only space. In spaces with a hybrid mix of digital and physical participants, we may see a third different rhythm in action. Similarly, with an event- or clock-based organisation, the use of subjectiv or objective time, and all the other example concepts listed in Table 1, may vary between different settings in a give team context.

## 4 | RESEARCH METHOD

We conducted a multiple case study (Yin, 2003) to address our research questions. The study context comprises two multinational companies, one of which had already relied on combining remote work with office presence before th COVID-19 pandemic. Still, this company needed to rethink its hybrid work design when the confinement period began. Both companies also needed to adjust their strategies after the confinement policy was lifted in most coun tries. These strategies are implemented differently across teams worldwide.

## 4.1 | Study context

Company A is a multinational engineering company and a leading specialist in factory automation, industrial hydrau lics, drive and control technologies, and mobile applications. The company develops innovative solutions for machine and plant manufacturing worldwide. They deliver high-quality digital system solutions and machine equipment with critical technologies for B2B and B2C markets. One of their challenges is to create good customer relationships which lead to co-creation between suppliers and customers. They operate on five continents and in 20 countries with more than 3200 employees

In Sweden, the company has one manufacturing plant in the north, the head office in Stockholm, two additiona head offices, and four sales offices. They are operating in nine different regions, with a total of 650 employees. For this company, we collected data from the Swedish strategic (C-level) business and sales management team, and th mobile applications and industrial hydraulics team, hereafter labelled mobile applications. The employees are often working remotely from wherever they like, at home, onsite, or on customers' premises. As technological develop ment expert leaders and technical developers, their “working tools” include a variety of engineering software and digital tools (see Table 2). According to the company's culture, they try to use a variety of tools to sustain a human centric culture, in a workplace of technocratic values.

Since 2019, before COVID-19, a strategic workplace policy was implemented in which office-based employee can work remotely. Managers and employees have complete freedom to agree on how much of the working week i spent at the office, remotely, or with the customers. However, if required, an employee must always be able to report to an office or alternative location (customer, supplier, etc.) within 24 h. Every team manager decides in agree ment with the employees about the working conditions. The employee ensures that the work environment is appro priate (e.g., without disturbance, having great connectivity, etc.). The company supports home office furniture and technical equipment. Both the C-level and the mobile application team are travelling or working with customers approximately 10%–20% of their time

Company B is a multinational company and a leading provider of Information and Communication Technology The company uses 5G and the Internet of Things (IoT) to manage and develop telecommunication networks and pro vide solutions to telecommunications service providers and enterprises. Also, they support customers using cloud native software and automated operations. They operate in approximately 190 countries, with approximatel 105 000 employees.

For this company, we collected data from team managers, software engineers, and scrum masters at an engi neering research development group in Ireland. The employees' expertise ranges from software development, data communication, IoT applications, etc., to customer-related communications. As agile and scrum development i implemented with sharp sprints to follow, they mostly rely on the scrum masters to communicate with customers and other stakeholders, and employees to manage the project deadlines. Before the pandemic, the employees usually worked at the office, however, after the pandemic, 99% of the employees work in a hybrid way. To facilitate hybrid the company has renovated its offices with new equipment. They work at the office on special occasions, often once per month or at scheduled events. Each team has the freedom to decide where to work. For example, some teams i Ireland have decided that all team members should be available online at 09:30

## 4.2 | Data collection

We studied two cases in company A and three cases in company B. We interviewed managers, team leaders, and team members for each case between September 2022 and January 2023. We went back to some of these informants between June and August 2023 for a second round of interviews to clarify our findings. ## 4.3 | Data analysis

We analysed the data in three phases (see Figure 3). In Phase 1, we conducted a thematic analysis (Robert et al., 2012) and coded the data against the Ancona et al. (2001) framework. Our goal was first to identify the temporal concepts that emerged through our interviews and observations and find concrete examples describing thes concepts. The data were coded independently, and then the authors discussed and agreed on the identified con cepts. Following Ancona's taxonomy, we identified the temporal concepts and organised this analysis around three temporal categories: conception of time, mapping activities to time, and actors relating to time. The temporal con cepts that emerged are presented in Appendix A (Figure A1)

In Phase 2, the goal was to perform a second round of thematic analysis, with a specific focus on the strategie used to represent the temporality of work activities that are implemented using digital technologies. As such, to understand temporality we used each of Ancona's temporal categories and concepts identified above. First, we examined how each team mapped their work activities to time and space. Second, we examined how team member conceived of time and related to time in their work activities (i.e., their time awareness). It was in this phase that th idea of representation and augmentation emerged. We realised that some aspects of hybrid work activities wer the exact replicas (i.e., representation) of how they were done on-site in the office, while others were their improve (i.e., augmented) versions. Findings of Phase 2 can be found in Sections 5.1–5.3.

Then, in Phase 3 (Section 5.4), we moved higher in the level of abstraction and conducted a meta-analysis of findings from previous phases. We went back to the literature and found concepts to describe emerging themes from the data. It was in this phase that we started to incorporate the concepts of humanistic and instrumental objec tives and outcomes (Sarker, Chatterjee, et al., 2019; Wallace et al., 2004) into our theorising, resulting in a socio technical understanding of hybrid work.

To ensure the validity of our analysis, instead of concluding our data collection and analysis only when no more themes (code saturation) could be identified; we adopted a methodological stance aimed at reaching for theoretical sufficiency (Andrade, 2009) or meaning saturation as Hennink et al., (2017) suggest. Given tha our study incorporated Ancona's temporal framework, which includes several temporal concepts (see Table 1), our objective was not to cease data collection once we had evidence for every single concept. Rather, our goal was to be able to describe in depth each of the three overarching temporal categories. The following section details our findings.

## 5 | TIME, SPACE, DIGITAL TECHNOLOGY, AND OUTCOMES OFHYBRID WORK

According to interviewees, hybrid work means orchestrating between different physical and digital workspaces The collaboration is mediated by digital technology, without which hybrid work would be unthinkable—making it a sociotechnical phenomenon. Hybrid teams rely on seamless coordination of team members working on-sit and those working remotely. This requirement is not trivial to fulfil, because workers may naturally form sub groups based on their physical proximity which, in turn, leads to the “us versus them” thinking. “It's sometime awkward to speak up in this meeting which does not necessarily mean that they in the room are making better points, than us online” (IB7). Moreover, employees working on-site can have different time awareness from those working remotely.

## 5.2.4 | Rhythms change due to entrainment

In several cases, employees reported changes in rhythm during hybrid meetings due to issues related to digital tech nologies. ## 5.3 | Time-awareness in hybrid work

## 5.3.1 | The vividness of subjective time in hybrid work

The integration of digital technologies in hybrid work brings forth intriguing shifts in how workers perceive and con ceptualise time. According to some respondents, “digital meetings seem to last longer” (IB3), (IA7). Others state th opposite: “everything is quicker in the digital space (IB1). Even when asked about the same specific activit (e.g., meeting, coding tasks) replies varied (e.g., “formal meetings last more” (IA5)” vs. “are quicker” (IA4)). This differ ence highlights their subjective conception of time.

Many reported that digital technologies helped them become more time aware, monitor clock time, and elimi nate subjective views in time perception. For instance, “the ability to see the duration of the call helps us have a shared understanding, and track how much time we spend at each topic” (IA4); “as the scrum manager I download the atten dance report after each meeting, I track who joined and when, and monitor the time allocated per meeting and the activit involved, in cases of disagreement I have hard evidence (IB8). Although by default the team used these digital technol ogies before the COVID-19 outbreak, now the tasks are more meticulously monitored and the correct time is essen tial (e.g., Figure 8).

## 5.3.2 | The monitoring of socially constructed time in hybrid work

In hybrid, digital technologies are used to monitor and enhance socially constructed time. In hybrid, team member constructed their shared meanings of time and established time-oriented behavioural norms: “people do not e-mai late, and I do the same” (IA4), “HR tell us not to reply to anything after 17:00, so I am quite happy about this. Also, people tend to follow this” (IB7); “We try to avoid meetings after lunch” (IB1). Moreover, digital technologies are used to remind or declare socially constructed time: “Some colleagues put ‘lunch’ in their open calendars so that the rest won't bother them” (IB2); “We respect lunchtime and not contacting after working hours, but sometimes this might create delays. See our chat last week (Figure 9), we had an issue on Thursday it was at lunchtime and we didn't meet after lunch then we had to discuss the day after and inform the team on Monday's stand-up (IB3). However, the majority of th team does not perceive these time-oriented behavioural norms as constraints. Instead, they define the minimum requirements and provide teams with the freedom to manage their course of action: I know that usually after lunch time I will receive no call from almost anyone, I might go shopping and then I will work late, I might set myself as busy o Teams or as out-of-office in case someone is searching for me” (IA5).

Closing, although socially constructed time could be viewed as a limitation to event-based scheduling, events are used more than before as reference points to schedule preceding and succeeding activities. Our informants observe a shift from fixed scheduling to the more agile, event-based scheduling: instead of linear clock time: “If there is a hot topic, we can meet up with the team” (IA3); “in my team, we meet based on certain events, when I invite someone I decide whether the meeting is hybrid, digital or on-site” (IA2).

## 5.3.3 | Hybrid workers' awareness of their temporal personalities

With the representation and augmentation comes a new relationship between hybrid workers and the temporal map of their work activities—a new way of relating to time. Some new ways of relating to time in the hybrid may be simi lar to how people relate to time in the physical space. But our informants also report some new ways that are mor vivid and pluralistic from how they relate to time in a non-hybrid environment. Many admitted they become more time-aware in the hybrid: “my perception of time is stronger due to working online” (IB8), “on-site our building wa quite large, so we were usually five minutes late for the next meeting, this was ok, but online when people are a couple of minutes late; I might be wondering if they will join (IB7); if you are late and you join the call, sneak in. Do not interrup us to say hi. In physical space this could be done more quietly” (IA4)

The new ways of relating to time may be shaped by changes in the temporal map of an activity (i.e., augmentation of activity) or by the hybrid space's characteristics (i.e., digital technologies). The functionalities of digital space assist hybrid workers in being punctual: “I tend not to forget about digital meetings, as there is a notifica tion 15 min before” (IB7). These functionalities are also used to distinguish private versus collaborative work time: “I am more organized now. I use colors in my calendar, yellow notes are for me, the orange is customer meetings, and blu are internal meetings, this way I feel more relaxed (IA6), I book my time so that I know which my tasks are and for other to know that they can't interrupt me” (IA6), “I book my own slot to work; otherwise, I can't be focused” (IA1). ## 5.4 | The humanistic and instrumental outcomes of hybrid work

All teams noted a heightened sense of time awareness in the hybrid environment, not only in terms of efficiently performing work activities but also in understanding the impact and outcomes. Outcomes are categorised as humanistic and instrumental (Sarker, Chatterjee, et al., 2019; Wallace et al., 2004). In our cases, team members actively embraced digital technologies to represent or augment an activity to improve outcomes.

In the cases studied, outcomes may be more easily attainable with less effort, through organising virtual meet ings: “We decrease costs and allocate them to other activities, saving money with hybrid meetings with customers” (IA3) Similarly, the efficiency gains resulting from digital technologies play a vital role in instrumental outcomes. Team members can now collaborate more effectively: “The efficiency of the team is higher, we can chat urgent issues easily i a digital room, instead of searching for a room to book” (IA4); “We use digital whiteboards for planning purposes and brainstorming, we are becoming more efficient this way” (IB3); Moreover, hybrid work arrangements can lead to improved time management and greater productivity: “Deadlines are shorter and we work more efficiently” (IB8); “In sprints now we are more productive, we work more the first two weeks and then we have a one week slack in case of bugs (IB7). Team members also actively exploit the capabilities of digital tools to enhance their efficiency: “I am more effi cient since the tools we use send me notifications for tasks and meetings” (IA6). Also, they leverage analytical feature to track time usage and interaction patterns, leading to increased personal efficiency e.g., “often I monitor how I spend my time using the analytics feature provided in the platform to be more efficient. I track how I spend my time and wit whom I interact the most (IB3).

## 6 | DISCUSSION

In this study, we define hybrid work as ‘a sociotechnical work arrangement, mediated by digital technology to enable col laboration among employees working from different times (e.g., synchronously and asynchronously) and different spaces (e.g., in the office and at home)’. This definition goes beyond the traditional view of hybrid work, that typically com bines online, working away from the office, and in-person working on-site, as it allows us to consider temporalit when creating strategies for the “third way of working”. The idea of time for the design of hybrid work inspires our study as a tool to encourage user engagement (Rapp, 2022), while seeking a better understanding of how worker engage more fundamentally with technology in different forms of engagement (Sarker, Ahuja, et al., 2019). Some aspects of work can easily be replicated in the digital space while others need to be enhanced for a better fit (Burton-Jones et al., 2017; Chandra Kruse & Drechsler, 2022; Recker et al., 2019) and our understanding of how the use of digital technologies reshape new ways of working requires further examination (Zamani et al., 2021). Such an understanding begins with the acknowledgement of hybrid work as a sociotechnical phenomenon (see Figure 11)

## 6.2 | Digital technology as a temporal boundary object for time awareness

Past IS research on remote work has identified five types of boundaries, including geographical and temporal bound aries. This suggests that relying solely on static measures of geographic distribution cannot fully reflect the intricacie of teams spread across different locations and time zones (Espinosa et al., 2003). Two decades later, our finding show that some issues remain (e.g., the distribution of people being in the same time zone affects how the other feel) showing that more novel inquiry is required into this matter. We go one step further, by considering multipl temporal concepts (Ancona et al., 2001), to offer a better understanding of the dynamic relation between spatial and temporal boundaries. As new types of IS continue to emerge, they push IS research in new directions, blurring th boundaries between the social and the technical components in unexpected ways (Lyytinen et al., 2021). Our informants were using several digital tools such as project management tools (e.g., JIRA), wikis (e.g., Confluence), group chats, open calendars, etc., to coordinate and map their work activities to time. In literature, such tools are called temporal boundary objects (Ancona et al., 2001). The “third way of working” goes beyond traditional remote work This happens since temporal boundaries exist within teams that not only work in different time zones or shifts (Ancona & Chong, 1996; Espinosa et al., 2003), but also because considering time in hybrid work design enables more dynamic changes in the physical-digital space continuum, showing that hybrid work is characterised not onl by flexibility but also by fluidity.

Olson's (1983) definition of remote work, that is “organizational work performed outside of the normal organiza tional confines of space and time, remains relevant. However, our perceptions and experience in both the physica and the digital space are constantly evolving. Our findings correspond with arguments for seeing IS phenomena not just in spatial, terms but also considering their dynamic view (Elbanna, 2010) and their temporal flow of actio (Mousavi Baygi et al., 2021). Recent studies on hybrid work suggest clear distinctions regarding space between office-first and remote-first work, focusing on the flexibility offered to workers to design the way they work (Smite Christensen, et al., 2022). However, we argue that temporal coordination, mediated by technology, can allow location an extended view of location that is not only limited to digital and physical, but is expanded in time. Blendin the workspace allows workers to dynamically switch between spaces and time depending on the activities. While time has been considered in IS research, it is usually associated either with the time differences among, the amount of work hours etc. (Chudoba et al., 2005; O'Leary & Cummings, 2007; Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010; Staples & Webster, 2008), or with the outcomes of using digital technologies as a measure of user engagement (cf. Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000; Lee, 1999; Shen et al., 2015). Our findings highlight the importance of hybrid workers' time aware ness and how it influences their strategies for designing hybrid work that includes both synchronous and asynchro nous flows of action (Mousavi Baygi et al., 2021). We view time awareness as an interplay between how peopl conceive of time and how they relate to time when performing their activities in space. This view is consistent wit the psychology of time awareness (Brown, 1990) and the sociology of time (Perlow, 1999).

Digital artefacts or objects have been demonstrated as boundary objects (Fang et al., 2022; Prasopoulou et al., 2006). Our findings show how some functionalities of digital technologies help teams to synchronise their space–time gap. Even though our findings include some simplistic temporal boundaries (e.g., digital whiteboards, open calendars), IS trends can be exploited to offer more vivid examples to augment activities and effectively bridge temporal and arrhythmic voids in hybrid work. In other domains, the meta-verse has permeated, providing examples of avatars adjusting their properties to fit the environment (Giang Barrera & Shah, 2023). This could potentially con tribute to the augmentation of activities to achieve humanistic outcomes. This paper also contributes to the management literature on temporality (e.g., Ancona et al., 2001). Our analysis shows how comparison and meshing of temporal patterns do not occur only between two activities but between the physical and the digital time–space Considering both time and space allows us to examine how the temporality in one space affects the other. We iden tified that the distribution of the people (how many are in the same physical and digital space), is an important facto influencing the team's collaboration. Previous IS research explains how IT can have a temporal logic, and its user may become entrained in it (Shen et al., 2015). During synchronous collaborations, team members are likely to b entrained to the rhythm of the other. Entrainment affects how users interact with each other as well as with IT itself both critical aspects of hybrid work. Keeping a balance between the number of people in each space may help to b better synchronised, as our findings show that the space in which the larger group of people is present will entrai the other. Nonetheless, specific people may influence an activity's rhythm (e.g., a top-level manager) since attentio is shifted to that person. The above examples strengthen our arguments on the dynamic notion of hybrid work.

## 6.3 | Key contributions to IS research and hybrid work design

This study makes a number of contributions to the literature. The first and most fundamental, is a temporal perspec tive. While time is central to discussions about hybrid work, it is often over-simplified in the literature on hybrid work (de Vaujany et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2015; Venkatesh et al., 2021) usually focusing on time differences, flexibility of time, speed or synchronisation (e.g., Asatiani et al., 2021; Gratton, 2021; O'Leary & Cummings, 2007; Schweitzer & Duxbury, 2010; Smite, Christensen, et al., 2022; Smite, Tkalich, et al., 2022). This paper contributes to the contemporary discourse on hybrid work by highlighting the often subtle yet significant temporal concepts and categories at play in hybrid work environments. It also showed how hybrid work design could consider the role of digital technology as temporal boundary objects and can also seek to augment the temporal mapping of activities in the digita space rather than simply aiming to represent and mimic those in the physical space.

Second, our study challenges and refines the following assumptions. We show that representing work activities and their temporal mapping is not sufficient for an effective design of hybrid work. This is in line with existing litera ture which pinpoints that representation alone is insufficient (Recker et al., 2021), particularly in the case of disrup tion (Davison, 2020), even when the properties of the digital technologies remain unchanged (i.e., the redesign of hybrid work). Instead, hybrid workers can benefit from adjustments in how their work activities are structured and conducted in the digital space—a mechanism we call augmentation. Some work activities in the digital space are augmented and sometimes better versions of their in-person counterparts in the physical space. For example, when sev eral team members work on an online document simultaneously during a meeting, they can do more compared to when working individually in the same physical space.

Third, we unpack the understanding of hybrid work into two levels. The initial phase involves the digitalization of work, which can either be a faithful representation or, as our findings suggest, an augmentation. Additionally, it involves configuring the interplay between remote or digitised work and on-site work, often in real time. Existing lit erature predominantly discusses one side of the digitalization of work that is, to transform the traditional on-sit work activities into digital/remote-compatible activities (Gilson et al., 2015; Mitchell & Brewer, 2022; Wang & Haggerty, 2009). However, we argue that practice this is only one element of hybridity, since digital technologie can not only assist workers digitise their activities as-is but also enhance them and create new opportunities for dif ferent forms of activities (Gilson et al., 2015; Gilstrap et al., 2022; Watson Fritz et al., 1997; Watson-Manheim et al., 2002).

Fourth, we consider both instrumental and humanistic outcomes as indicators of effective hybrid work design Focusing on the humanistic outcomes, our findings show that they are reflected in satisfaction and well-being, which is in accordance with recent studies (Praslova, 2023; Tarafdar & Saunders, 2022). Being mindful of both instrumenta and humanistic outcomes of hybrid work can help leaders and hybrid workers to avoid performance and wellbeing issues (Hacker et al., 2020; Magni et al., 2023), such as zoom fatigue (Chamakiotis et al., 2021; Christian, 2021) Finally, we highlight the importance of considering time in designing hybrid work. While the common assumption about hybridity tends to focus on its spatiality, we add temporality to the equation—considering hybrid work as a sociotechnical, digital-physical, and time-aware phenomenon

## 7 | CONCLUSIONS AND IMPLICATIONS

The four contributions outlined above have various implications for research and practice. From a practical perspec tive, first our study shows the importance of viewing hybrid work as a sociotechnical phenomenon rather than a fixed concept. We urge practitioners to move away from the fixed notion of working some days per week remotel or that each employee is either present or remote. Instead, hybrid design should embrace that physically present versus remote, will change, sometimes drastically, and others even fluctuate within times of the same day. This can allow flexibility to employees (e.g., work on their peak productivity times). For managers, whose objectives are often mainly instrumental, the hybrid work strategies presented in this paper offer a suitable toolbox for better under standing and designing the third way of working. As the landscape of hybrid work evolves, managers should revisit both humanistic (employee wellbeing, satisfaction) and instrumental (productivity, efficiency) objectives periodicall to ensure alignment with the current reality.

Second, this also has implications for policymakers who, post-pandemic, often implement legislation around suc fixed concepts (e.g., the right to work one day per week from home). It is important to develop policies that will hel empower and train team leaders to adjust their management style based on the shifting landscape of team member locations, being adept at managing both in-person and remote teams simultaneously. Further, we emphasise how well-being and adaptability initiatives can be improved by considering spatial and temporal concepts at the same time, while, for example, organising workshops on adaptability, resilience, and wellbeing to help employees cope wit the constant shifts in their work environments. This study highlights the importance of technology as the tempora boundary object that connects the different spaces of a work environment. Managers should consider more than just speed and synchronisation of work. This study shows that they should consider rhythm, event-driven organisation temporal personalities, and many other temporal concepts in the design of their hybrid work. Further, we extend the discussions on developing tools (Seeber & Erhardt, 2023; Smit et al., 2022) that mediate synchronous and asynchro nous collaboration. Such tool, should incorporate performance metrics that capture both the qualitative and quanti tative aspects of work, ensuring that the fluid nature of hybrid work doesn't lead to biases in evaluations. Embracing a holistic perspective on outcomes, companies can ensure they thrive in this new era of work

There are also limitations to be considered. First, while this study demonstrates the importance of considering time when designing hybrid work, one should not automatically assume that doing so always leads to better tempo ral performance (i.e., speed or productivity). Nor should it be assumed that increased temporal performance is neces sarily a good thing. Before assessing temporal concepts in a hybrid work environment, it's crucial to understand thei role. Second, time should not be considered in isolation. The temporal concepts identified in this study will need to be researched or applied with other trade-offs and complementarities in mind. Third, the temporal concepts dis cussed are often very subtle and complex, as is hybrid work generally. Therefore, while the selected cases provided rich setting to identify and explain certain temporal categories and concepts in hybrid work, other researchers and practitioners should avoid overgeneralizing our findings and instead strive to identify the subtle and complex concepts at play in their own context. In conclusion, acknowledging that hybrid work is one among various contemporary work forms, future research can examine whether our findings may extend to other recent alternative setting such as fluid, liquid, or flexible work or working arrangements<sup>3</sup> (Izak et al., 2023). By situating our findings within broader frameworks of contemporary work arrangements, which vary in definition and focus, we can collectively advance discussions on fluid work configurations, informing policies, customising training, and identifying perfor mance metrics tailored to these evolving work environments.
