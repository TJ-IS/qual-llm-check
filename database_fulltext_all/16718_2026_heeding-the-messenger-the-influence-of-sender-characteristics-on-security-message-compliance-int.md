# Heeding the Messenger: The Influence of Sender Characteristics on Security Message Compliance Intentions

## ABSTRACT

How security messages can be used to motivate information technology (IT) users’ security behaviour has been of keen interest to IS research. To that end, studies have focused on the content of security messages; however, few studies have examined the influence of message senders. In this paper, we build on social influence theory and integrate it with the concept of inferences of manipulative intent (IMI) to develop a model that examines how perceptions of sender characteristics—cybersecurity expertise, coercive power, and similarity—can yield positive and negative influence on message outcomes, captured in recipients message compliance intentions. We test our model in four different studies using field and scenario experiments across three target populations: the general public, students, and employees. Perceived expertise, power, and similarity had similar effects among the general public and students: Perceived expertise was positively associated with message outcomes, but perceived power and similarity were negatively associated. In contrast, employees reacted differently from the general public and students in that they responded positively to perceived power, with perceived expertise and similarity having negligible effects. Across these three target populations, we found that participants reacted to senders high in perceived power and similarity with IMI, which reduced their message compliance intentions. Our results suggest that senders must be chosen carefully, depending on the target population, because selecting the wrong sender can increase the likelihood of a message being rejected.

## KEYWORDS

Security message, compliance, spokesperson, social influence, social influence theory (SIT), inference of manipulative intent (IMI)

## PRACTITIONER POINTS

1. Choose message senders deliberately: Practitioners should select message senders with the same care they use to craft the content of security messages due to the significant impact of sender characteristics on message outcomes.

2. The effect of sender characteristics is population dependent: Among the general public or students, senders perceived as authorities (e.g. leaders or executives) are less likely to secure compliance than credible experts. Among employees, however, authority senders improve message acceptance more than experts. Practitioners must choose the right senders to maximise the effectiveness of their security messages.

3. Tactics such as choosing peers do not work and may backfire.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Security messages, such as cybersecurity-related email or video messages, are key to promoting secure user behaviour.<sup>1</sup> By highlighting imminent cyber risks and providing clear, actionable guidance on how to protect their data and systems, security messages seek to motivate users to take security precautions to reduce their vulnerability to cyber risks (Boss et al., 2015; Schuetz et al., 2020). However, security messages are often ineffective, prompting extensive research on how the content of security messages can be improved (e.g. Johnston et al., 2015; Mattson et al., 2023; Menard et al., 2017; Nehme & George, 2022; Schuetz et al., 2021; Schuetz et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2022). Due to the heavy focus on message content, little is known about how other factors, such as the sender of security messages, affect their effectiveness.

The sender of a security message can be expected to play a key role in determining whether a message is accepted or rejected. This is because the influence of who sends a message is well-known in noncybersecurity contexts. For instance, consider the public health communications during the COVID-19 pandemic, where preventive measures were accepted or rejected based on who made the recommendations (Boynton et al., 2021). Likewise, in cybersecurity, it can be expected that the acceptance or rejection of security messages depends on who sends them. Therefore, given the predominant focus on the content of security messages, we can further advance our understanding of what makes security messages effective by examining the characteristics of message senders.

In this paper, we pursue this opportunity by investigating how perceived senders’ characteristics influence the outcome of security messages within the context of three target populations that are frequently studied in cybersecurity research: the general public, students, and employees (e.g. Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Boss et al., 2015; Schuetz et al., 2020). To do so, we build on social influence theory (SIT) (Kelman, 1958) and examine three key characteristics of senders of security messages. SIT is particularly relevant to our work as it explains how others influence individuals through internalisation, compliance, and identification processes resulting from perceptions of others’ credibility, power, and attractiveness (Kelman, 1958). We contextualise SIT to cybersecurity to explain how senders’ characteristics (i.e. cybersecurity expertise, coercive power, and similarity) influence security message outcomes. The outcome we seek to predict is message compliance intentions, which refers to recipients’ intentions to comply with what is recommended in a security message (Boss et al., 2015).<sup>2</sup> Message compliance intentions have been established as predictors of behavioural responses—i.e., compliance with what is recommended in a message (Boss et al., 2015; Mattson et al., 2023; Nehme & George, 2022; Schuetz et al., 2020)—and are hence an appropriate measure to capture message acceptance.

Furthermore, users may respond negatively to security messages, rejecting the message altogether and resisting behavioural change (Chen et al., 2021; Lowry et al., 2023; Vance et al., 2022). Because SIT offers no theoretical mechanisms that would explain such negative responses (Turner, 1991)—but rather how perceptions of expertise, power, and similarity factor into persuasive outcomes—we extend SIT by integrating inferences of manipulative intent (IMI). IMI refers to the recipients’ inference that a message sender attempts to persuade them through manipulation (Campbell, 1995) and integrating IMI into SIT allows us to explain how perceived sender characteristics may also negatively affect message outcomes. In doing so, we theoretically integrate IMI into SIT by positioning it as a mechanism through which a sender’s social influence characteristics—which SIT theorises to stimulate compliance—can also lead to adverse message outcomes. This allows us to provide a more holistic understanding of the potentially positive and negative effects of senders with specific characteristics on message outcomes.

To validate our model, we conducted four experimental studies in which we manipulated the sender of security messages to elicit different perceptions about sender characteristics—their cybersecurity expertise, power, and similarity—and studied how these perceptions influence message outcomes among three different target populations (i.e. the general public, students, and employees). To balance our methods’ external validity with internal validity, we conducted two field experiments, which offer a highly realistic and natural setting but with less control (Diamond, 1983; Falk & Heckman, 2009; Harrison & List, 2004), and two scenario experiments, which offer more control (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Hyman & Steiner, 1996).

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We first review prior research on security messages and related literature in the Background section, then develop our hypotheses by integrating SIT and IMI in the Hypothesis Development section. The Method section outlines the procedures used across four studies and three different study populations, followed by the Analysis & Results section, which presents findings for each study. Finally, the Discussion section summarises key results, highlights differences across studies, and concludes with theoretical and practical implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

## 2 BACKGROUND

## 2.1 Research on Security Messages

The cybersecurity literature has focused on how the content of security messages elicits message compliance intentions and behaviours (see Table 1). As outlined in the table, this research question has been pursued in the context of various target populations, such as the general public, students, and employees. For example, Anderson and Agarwal (2010) manipulated the wording of security messages and tested their effects on the general public. Boss et al. (2015) examined the power of “low” and “high” security threats and tested them among students. Schuetz et al. (2020) contrasted the effects of abstract and concrete message content among employees. Given that the focus on message content is consistent with protection motivation theory (PMT) (Rogers, 1975, 1983), PMT has been a frequent foundation of work in this literature.

In contrast to the intense focus on the content of security messages, work on the message sender is scarce (see Table 1). From our literature review, we are unaware of any study that manipulated the message sender or message sender characteristics. The only study that is an exception, Johnston and Warkentin (2010b), examined the role of sender credibility but did not contrast it with other sender attributes. Thus, our understanding of (a) what are essential sender attributes in the context of security communications, (b) how these attributes affect message outcomes, and (c) whether these effects differ among different target populations (i.e., public, students, employees) remains limited.

This gap is problematic because prior research indicates that sender characteristics can influence message reception (e.g. Boynton et al., 2021; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), yet investigations omitting sender characteristics and focusing solely on message content risk drawing inaccurate conclusions. For example, a study might attribute persuasiveness to the content itself when, in reality, the effect stems from who the sender of a message was. Additionally, failing to consider how different target populations respond to messages can lead to misguided generalisations, such as assuming that what resonates with students will equally persuade employees. Consequently, examining how sender characteristics affect message outcomes across key populations is essential to ensure more accurate and generalisable insights.

## 2.2 Research on Phishing Messages

This gap in our knowledge cannot be filled by related literature on phishing. Although many studies have focused on training and recipient characteristics (e.g. Jensen et al., 2017; Jensen et al., 2022; Silic & Lowry, 2020; Wright & Marett, 2010), we found only two focused on sender characteristics. Moody et al. (2017) show that recipients are more susceptible to phishing attacks from senders they know. Jagatic et al. (2007) demonstrate that socially engineered phishing attacks (i.e. attacks that impersonate known individuals sourced from a target’s social network) are more successful than generic phishing attacks. However, it is challenging to translate this knowledge to our context because the senders of security messages are generally known. Thus, the question of how sender characteristics affect message outcomes remains unanswered.

Understanding the role of sender characteristics in shaping the efficacy of security messages presents two attractive opportunities for advancing security research, both of which we leverage. First, related theory and empirics suggest that messages are processed not just based on their content but also based on peripheral cues (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), one of which could be “from whom” a message is coming. Moreover, because many recipients are often not intrinsically motivated to engage in security behaviours (Pham et al. 2017; Puhakainen & Siponen, 2010), the right message source may create sufficient motivation for them to comply with security messages. Because senders are likely a key factor in the success of security messages, examining how sender characteristics can influence the effectiveness of security messages presents an opportunity to contribute new knowledge of high relevance (Siponen & Baskerville, 2018).

Second, considering sender characteristics offers an opportunity to explain recipients’ negative responses to security messages. As much research has focused on improving message content design (e.g., Johnston et al., 2015; Mattson et al., 2023; Menard et al., 2017; Nehme & George, 2022; Schuetz et al., 2021; Schuetz et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2022), explanations for why security messages fail have always been scoped to the content. For instance, Boss et al. (2015) suggest that messages that depict low threats are ineffective, and therefore, security messages should depict high threats; Schuetz et al. (2020) indicate that messages with abstract descriptions of threats are inadequate; and Chen et al. (2021) suggest that too strong depictions of threats, if not matched with high efficacy appraisals, can be problematic. However, the fact that messages might be rejected because they come from the wrong sources has not been considered. Thus, there is a second opportunity to evaluate and explain the negative influences that senders could play.

## 3 HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT

Despite extensive research on security message content, a critical gap remains in understanding how message senders influence compliance. Prior studies have primarily overlooked how sender characteristics shape recipients’ reactions. To address this, we draw on SIT, which explains how individuals are persuaded through internalisation, compliance, and identification processes (Allport, 1985; Kelman, 1958; Turner

1991). Following Kelman (1958), we map these processes to sender characteristics (i.e. cybersecurity expertise, power, similarity) to explain how they affect the outcomes of security messages. SIT’s processes, however, would only explain positive effects, that is, why perceived sender characteristics would enhance the influence of a message. To also explain why message recipients may react negatively to sender characteristics, we extend SIT by integrating IMI. To recap, IMI refers to recipients’ inference that a message sender attempts to manipulate them (Campbell, 1995). IMI has been developed in the marketing and public health literature to explain consumer resistance to advertisements (e.g. Campbell, 1995; Cotte et al., 2005; Wentzel et al., 2010) and has also been used to explain resistance to public service announcements (Boudewyns et al., 2013; Erceg-Hurn & Steed, 2011).

Examining how sender characteristics may stimulate IMI expands the literature on IMI by delineating new antecedents to IMI and expands SIT by including IMI as the mechanism that explains the negative influences of sender characteristics. ## 3.1 Social Influence Theory

SIT explains the processes through which others influence individuals. It is thus helpful for understanding how secure message senders influence recipients. According to Kelman’s (1958) original study, social influence could be established by the influence of either a person or a group. In his original study, Kelman focused on the social influence of the message sender as the influencing agent. Specifically, he argued that the probability of one accepting influence is a function of the relative influencing power of the influencing agent. Kelman posited three main influence processes in social relationships (see Table 2): internalisation, referring to the process of accepting influence because it aligns with a recipient’s intrinsic values and beliefs; compliance, referring to the process of accepting influence because “one hopes to achieve a favourable reaction from another person or group” (p. 53); and identification, referring to the process of accepting influence because of an individual’s desire to form a relationship. Based on these three influence processes, SIT explains how an agent’s characteristics influence the attitudes of message recipients. Specifically, Kelman’s original study tested how agents with varying levels of credibility, power, and attractiveness influenced the attitudes of first-year students. He hypothesised that the agents’ credibility exerts influence through internalisation, the agents’ power exerts influence through the process of compliance, and the agents’ attractiveness exerts influence through identification (see Kelman, 1958, p. 57). Of course, for these processes to work, the senders’ characteristics must be perceived by message recipients. Hence, recipients’ perceptions determine the senders’ power, credibility, or attractiveness.

We contextualise Kelman’s (1958) SIT by examining perceived cybersecurity expertise as recipients’ perceptions of the credibility of a sender of security messages; perceived coercive power as recipients’ perceptions of senders’ power, which captures the perceived authority of the sender to compel compliance through punishments (Raven & French, 1958), and is particularly relevant in corporate cybersecurity contexts (Burns et al., 2023; Yazdanmehr et al., 2020); and perceived similarity instead of attractiveness. Turner (1991) explained that similarity is an antecedent to attractiveness and thus also exerts influence through identification. The perceived similarity construct is more suitable for our context because the perceived similarity between senders and recipients may play a more critical role in compliance than the perceived attractiveness of a sender. For example, consider the difference between a new hire delivering a security message to new hires and a new hire delivering such a message to established professionals; in such circumstances, similarity—not physical attractiveness—should exert a powerful influence. Thus, we consider recipients’ perceptions about a sender’s cybersecurity expertise, coercive power, and similarity to explain recipients’ responses to security messages by contextualising these factors and formulating hypotheses based on the three social influence processes. Then, we integrate IMI into SIT by explaining why these characteristics may also negatively influence recipients’ responses.

## 3.1.1 Cybersecurity Expertise

According to SIT, internalisation is the first process through which credible senders exert influence. Formally, credibility refers to the degree to which a person is believed or trusted (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006). In the context of security messages that advocate precautions to avert cybersecurity threats, credibility materialises as the perceived cybersecurity expertise of a sender (hereafter: expertise), referring to the extent to which a sender is perceived as a knowledgeable, believable, trusted source of expertise on the topic of cybersecurity. According to SIT, people are more likely to find information convincing and subjectively valid, and, as a result, are more likely to be influenced when messages come from senders who are seen as credible (Turner, 1991). Recipients may be inclined to trust senders due to prior experience of their trustworthiness or because they appear credible, reducing perceived risks related to following their advice (Rousseau et al., 1998). Additionally, if assurances exist, such as the sender’s reputation for being a credible expert on a topic, recipients may be further inclined to trust the sender (McKnight et al., 1998). The trust in the sender may then be carried over to this person’s message (Hovland & Weiss, 1951). Applying SIT to our context, a sender’s expertise can be expected to regulate the extent to which the recommendation within a cybersecurity message will be seen as valid and believed. Along these lines, numerous studies have established the positive effects of sender credibility on message acceptance. For example, the seminal research Hovland and Weiss (1951) shows that sender credibility exerts a strong influence on recipients’ message acceptance. In IS, this effect has been further confirmed in the context of persuasion in IT adoption (Bhattacherjee & Sanford, 2006) and eliciting message compliance intentions from recipients (Johnston & Warkentin, 2010b). We thus expect:

H1a. Higher perceived cybersecurity expertise of the sender of a security message increases the message compliance intentions of the message recipients.

## 3.1.2 Coercive Power

Power refers to a person’s ability to influence others due to the authority implied by this person’s organisational position (Hinkin & Schriesheim, 1989). SIT ties power closely to compliance, which is a normative influence. Just as Kelman (1958) stated, when the influence of an agent is based on means of control, “conformity will tend to take the form of compliance” (p. 54). According to Kelman (1958), compliance occurs when another person’s power and status influence decisions. Compliance forms when an individual adopts a specific behaviour “not because he believes in its content but because he expects to gain specific rewards or approval and avoid specific punishments or disapproval by conforming” (Kelman, 1958, p. 53). The word “conforming” indicates that a person adopts a behaviour because of outside factors such as power or status. As this definition suggests, power can take multiple forms, such as rewards and punishments (Hinkin & Schriesheim, 1989; Raven & French, 1958).

In the context of compliance with security messages, we are interested in coercive power, that is, power related to the ability to force compliance through punishments (Raven & French, 1958). We focus on coercive power (hereafter: power) because people rarely receive rewards for compliance with security messages (Pahnila et al., 2007). Instead, deterrence of noncompliance through sanctions is the predominant approach (Willison & Lowry, 2018), partially because it is an effective way of controlling human behaviour (Greene & Podsakoff, 1981). Evidence supports the conclusion that people with formal authority are more likely to engender compliance (Bushman, 1984). Leaders who typically hold coercive power have also been shown to impact employees’ security decisions (e.g. Feng et al., 2019; Guhr et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2024).

Thus, we argue that recipients can infer that non-compliance with a security message may lead to negative consequences if a sender is perceived to hold power, such as that gained through formal authority, due to this person’s position in an organisation or society. This is due to the expectation that a sender has the authority to compel compliance and, to do so, can take actions that may negatively affect the message recipients. Thus, recipients may accept messages not necessarily because they believe in them but to conform to authority and avoid such negative consequences. Frequently, such power arises due to formal rules and regulations. Individuals and agencies can exercise power in public settings by shaping regulations and exercising prosecutorial discretion. Leaders can directly exercise power in institutional and organisational settings by influencing daily operations. Across these settings, we hence expect the sender’s power to positively influence the recipients’ willingness to comply with the request of a security message:

H1b. Higher perceived coercive power of the sender of a security message increases the message compliance intentions of the message recipients.

## 3.1.3 Similarity to the Recipient

Similarity refers to the degree to which one considers another individual to be close to oneself in terms of social characteristics (Chang, 2011). In our context, similarity to the recipient (hereafter: similarity) refers to the extent to which a recipient considers the sender of a security message similar to the self, such as being in the same role, which leads to identification. According to SIT, shared category identification increases mutual influence; however, although identification and compliance involve normative influence via relationship-driven conformity, they are fundamentally distinct. Identification is predicated on perceived similarity, in contrast to compliance, which is driven by power (Deutsch & Gerard, 1955).

Similarity is a crucial factor for persuasion through identification with a sender. Identification suggests that a person adopts a particular behaviour because of a “classical relationship” (Kelman, 1958, p. 53). In this usage, “classical” refers to placing oneself within a category. Identification-based influence occurs when individuals classify themselves as belonging to the same group or assume the role of another person (Kelman, 1958). This classification is driven by perceived similarities, such as shared demographic traits like age and gender, implicit attributes like cultural and educational background, or sometimes, attitudes. Even having similar names has been shown to influence persuasive outcomes (Garmer, 2005).

In the context of security messages, we argue that similar roles will lead to identification through perceived similarity. For example, when a person receives a security message sent by someone on the same rank or role (e.g. another student, another user, another admin), they may believe that the message sender is in the same class or has shared experiences, common concerns or priorities, which increases identification and therefore recipients’ receptiveness to the message. As a result, recipients may be more responsive to messages by this sender than by one they perceive as dissimilar, which may lead to better compliance. We thus hypothesise:

H1c. Higher perceived similarity to the sender of a security message increases the message compliance intentions of the message recipients.

## 3.2 The Role of Inferences of Manipulative Intent

Although SIT helps explain the effects of sender characteristics on recipients’ message compliance intentions, it does not account for why recipients resist influence. Here, the concept of IMI may provide insight into why some security messages fail. Developed in research on consumer behaviour to explain why the use of persuasion tactics in advertisements sometimes backfires (Campbell, 1995), IMI refers to consumers’ inferences that “the advertiser is attempting to manipulate […] consumers” (p. 226). When people suspect they are being manipulated, they may react negatively for two reasons. First, they may feel their autonomy and freedoms are being threatened, thereby prompting resistance to preserve their sense of self-determination (see Brehm, 1989; Erceg-Hurn & Steed, 2011). Second, they may also lose trust in the sender’s credibility, diminishing the message’s persuasiveness (see Campbell, 1995; Cotte et al., 2005). Although we do not attempt to disentangle these mechanisms, both factors suggest a negative effect of IMI on message persuasiveness. Whereas other constructs, such as reactance (resistance to persuasion) and scepticism (doubts about the truthfulness of a message), are relevant, these responses stem from recipients’ attribution of manipulative and self-serving intentions to the message source (see Brehm, 1989; Erceg-Hurn & Steed, 2011; Foreh & Grier, 2003). Therefore, we focus on IMI as it directly captures such perceptions directed at the sender, which aligns with the focus of SIT.

Accordingly, bringing IMI into our context, we expect that IMI will reduce message compliance intentions in the context of security messages because receivers may feel their autonomy being encroached upon and lose trust in the message sender. Along these lines, it has been shown that security-relevant restrictions on users’ freedom can backfire (e.g. Lowry & Moody, 2015; Lowry et al., 2015), suggesting the existence of this phenomenon in the context of cybersecurity. Therefore, we offer:

H2. Higher IMI of the sender of a security message decreases the message compliance intentions of the message recipients.

In the same way, sender characteristics influence message compliance intentions, perceptions of a sender’s expertise, power, and similarity may influence recipients’ IMI. Specifically, we argue that perceptions of high expertise reduce recipients’ IMI. Expertise functions as an informational influence that strengthens recipients’ beliefs that arguments contained in a cybersecurity message are genuine and sincere (Sussman & Siegal, 2003), thereby counterbalancing recipients’ concerns that a sender tries to persuade for self-serving purposes (LaTour & Manrai, 1989). Consequently, perceptions of cybersecurity expertise should reinforce recipients’ beliefs about the validity of a cybersecurity message, that its content is accurate, and that recommendations serve the audience’s interest rather than the senders’ personal goals.

Conversely, power and similarity are normative influences that motivate actions through appeals to authority and social expectations (Turner, 1991). When recipients perceive that a sender is high in power or similarity, they may question whether these attributes were deliberately emphasised to manipulate recipients into complying with a security message, rather than persuading them through reason. The suspicion of manipulative tactics leads to IMI (Campbell, 1995). Specifically, high power may signal an attempt to foster a sense of obligation, whereas high similarity may be perceived as an attempt to foster trust. Messages coming from senders high in power may thus be perceived as threatening personal autonomy and freedom. In contrast, senders high in similarity may signal an attempt to persuade by overemphasizing shared identity. Consequently, messages from senders high in power or similarity may backfire by increasing recipients’ IMI. Similarly, LaTour and Manrai (1989) show that appeals based solely on normative influences elicit lower motivations to make donations among consumers. Therefore, we offer:

H3a. Higher perceived expertise of the sender of a security message decreases the IMI of the message recipients.

H3b. Higher perceived power of the sender of a security message increases the IMI of the message recipients.

H3c. Higher perceived similarity of the sender of a security message increases the IMI of the message recipients.

## 4.1 Research Strategy

Research methods, ideally, should maximise internal and external validity (Campbell & Stanley, 1963). In practice, however, any single method often only maximises one of these criteria at the expense of the other, leading to dilemmatic research choices (McGrath, 1981). We conducted a series of studies with different research designs to maximise external and internal validity. Table 3 outlines these studies and their characteristics. First, we decided to test our model with varying target populations to enhance external validity. As summarised in Table 1, previous research has examined the effectiveness of security messages among the general public, students, and corporate employees. Therefore, we tested our model across these three populations to allow our findings to connect to prior work and generalise to these target populations of interest.

Second, to maximise internal validity, we conducted experiments, which are widely considered the gold standard for establishing causal relationships between variables (Cook et al., 2008). Although strong on internal validity, experiments are sometimes criticised for lower external validity (Falk & Heckman, 2009). To somewhat counteract this weakness, we conducted two different types of experiments. First, we conducted field experiments for Studies 1 and 2 because they offer the most naturalistic, realistic setting (Diamond, 1983; Falk & Heckman, 2009; Harrison & List, 2004), which was desirable to maximise the generalizability of our findings. We also chose to vary the media format of the stimuli between Studies 1 and 2 to improve the ability of our findings to generalise across different stimuli, thus enhancing external validity (Cook, 1979). However, field experiments come at the expense of reduced control. In a natural setting, our choices of authentic senders were limited, which reduced our ability to isolate our manipulations of the constructs of interest. This is because authentic senders vary in their characteristics (i.e. expertise, power, and similarity) simultaneously, making it impossible to manipulate one factor while keeping the others constant when featuring an authentic sender.<sup>3</sup> Therefore, our choice to maximise realism came with the trade-off of less control over sender characteristics. We dealt with this issue analytically (see Section 4.3 Analytical Approach) and complemented Studies 1 and 2 with further studies using a slightly different approach that allowed for more control.

Third, in Studies 3 and 4, we conducted scenario experiments to complement the field experiments of Studies 1 and 2. In this method, participants are presented with written descriptions of a hypothetical reallife decision-making situation to examine how participants would respond in such a situation (Hox et al.,

1991).<sup>4</sup> The scenario experiments allowed us more control over manipulating the characteristics of hypothetical senders while maintaining a high degree of realism (Aguinis & Bradley, 2014; Hyman & Steiner, 1996). As an added benefit, conducting scenario experiments also helped our investigation of the corporate setting (Study 3), where we lacked access to participants within a single organisation. Consequently, similar to other scenario studies that examine cybersecurity-related responses in corporate settings (e.g. D'Arcy et al., 2014; Siponen & Vance, 2010; Vance et al., 2013), we sampled across multiple organisations, which was only possible because we used scenarios, given that authentic senders were unknown and varied across organisations. Therefore, for higher internal control and the ability to obtain a cross-organisational sample, choosing a scenario-based method with hypothetical senders (i.e. a hypothetical scenario) was preferable for Study 3. In Study 4, we replicated Study 2 using the scenario method of Study 3 to assess whether the change in method had potentially influenced our findings, which provides essential insights to interpret differences in findings between the studies correctly.

## 4.2 Measurements

All our studies build on the same set of established measures. Specifically, we measured power based on Hinkin and Schriesheim (1989), similarity based on Chang (2011), and expertise based on Bhattacherjee and Sanford (2006). We measured IMI using the scale of Campbell (1995). The measure for message compliance intentions was based on Johnston and Warkentin (2010a). The Limitations section discusses our decision to measure message compliance intentions over actual behaviours. Following the phishing literature (e.g. Wang et al., 2017), we measured gender, age, education, email experience (history and extent to which emails are used in their daily lives), online transaction experience (the extent to which participants use the Internet to conduct financial transactions), prior phishing experience (whether a participant had previously been phished before), and the use of email for work as controls. Because previous research has indicated that threat and risk perceptions may influence message acceptance (e.g., Lowry et al., 2023), we further measured risk propensity (participants’ orientation to take risks) (Keil et al., 2000) and security threat relevancy (perceptions that the security threat depicted in the message may personally affect the participant) (Kim & Kim, 2018) as additional controls to capture individual dispositions and situational perceptions. Appendix A presents the complete measurement instrument used in the field experiments (Studies 1 and 2). Appendix B reports the full survey instrument for the scenario experiments (Studies 3 and 4).

We followed established approaches in our design and procedures to reduce the risk of common method bias (CMB). First, we used reverse-coded items and randomisation (Podsakoff et al., 2003). Second, we used a latent marker variable approach (Chin et al., 2012; Chin et al., 2013) with fantasising as a marker variable (Rönkkö & Ylitalo, 2011; Son & Kim, 2008) to account for any potential influence of CMB on our model estimates, as a precautionary step. Additionally, the scenario experiments necessitated controlling for social desirability bias, wherefore we followed the recommendations of Kwak et al. (2019) and incorporated the impression management scale of Hart et al. (2015) in Studies 3 and 4.

Across all studies, we addressed the threat of insufficient effort response bias in several ways. First, we warned participants about the attention checks so they would not perceive the checks as unfair (Breitsohl & Steidelmueller, 2018). Second, we included three instruction response measures (i.e. “Please select ‘strongly disagree’”) (Bansal et al., 2023; Breitsohl & Steidelmueller, 2018). These approaches can identif participants who do not pay sufficient attention to a study and act as a nudge to improve participants attention (Hauser & Schwarz, 2015; Huang et al., 2015).

## 4.3 Analytical Approach

Traditionally, analytical approaches in experimental designs compare the means of the outcomes among treatment conditions using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or regression. Instead, we used SEM to examine the simultaneous effects of our treatments for two reasons.

First, as Bagozzi (1977) noted, traditional approaches assume that manipulations lead to unidimensional effects [i.e. that a singular manipulation affects one intended independent variable (IV) and no other variables (Perdue & Summers, 1986)]. The underlying logic is that if a manipulation affects only one IV and leads to changes in the dependent variable (DV), a causal relationship between the IV and the DV can be inferred (Sawyer et al., 1995). However, our research design does not meet this assumption because using authentic senders led to manipulating multiple, sometimes overlapping, sender characteristics. For instance, a CEO differs from a security expert in terms of their power and knowledge. Therefore, a clean manipulation of just one characteristic (e.g. power, expertise, or similarity) while holding the other characteristics constant is infeasible (we will also demonstrate this empirically in the manipulation checks we report in the following sections). Consequently, comparing differences in outcomes between treatments—as done in the experimental tradition of using ANOVA analysis—cannot lead to inferences of causal relationships because it is unclear which manipulated IV leads to the observed change in the outcome (Sawyer et al., 1995). Hence, in such circumstances, rather than relying on traditional analytic approaches (e.g. ANOVA), methodologists recommend using SEM (Bagozzi, 1977), which has the advantage of statistically controlling for observed differences in the other factors, thereby holding these constant and isolating their collective effect on the focal variable. The SEM-based approach is consistent with many cybersecurity papers that use SEM to understand the impact of security messages that simultaneously manipulate multiple variables (Boss et al., 2015; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010a; Johnston et al., 2015; Warkentin et al., 2017).

Second, our theoretical model delineates competitive effects, which are effects in which a direct relationship points in one direction (i.e. H1a–c) but an indirect relationship points in the opposite direction (i.e. H2, H3a–c) (Zhao et al., 2010). When competing causal paths exist, a traditional treatment outcome analysis cannot identify the causal pathways, which can only be revealed in a mediation analysis that controls each causal path. An SEM approach allows us to tease these competing effects apart. Thus, we analyse our experiments using SEM.

## 4.4 Participants, Materials, and Procedures

## 4.4.1 Study 1 Participants, Materials, and Procedures

Study 1 was a field experiment to validate our model among the general public. We recruited 207 MTurk participants to obtain such a sample. Studies have shown that results based on data gathered from MTurk are comparable to those based on data acquired through other means (Horton et al., 2011; Mason & Suri, 2012; Paolacci et al., 2010; Steelman et al., 2014). To ensure high-quality responses, we followed established guidelines, such as restricting participants to workers from the U.S. with high approval ratings who passed our three attention checks (Lowry et al., 2016; Steelman et al., 2014). Responses that did not meet these criteria were removed. Removing responses that fail such checks is pivotal in improving statistical power (Oppenheimer et al., 2009) and reducing the threat of insufficient effort bias (Breitsohl & Steidelmueller, 2018). We tested whether removing these responses led to different results by running all analyses with the complete sample. The results led to conclusions that were identical to those associated with the reduced sample, leading us to conclude that removing these responses did not influence our findings.

The final sample comprised 174 participants, averaging 38.7 years; 56% were female. Participants reported their highest educational attainment as follows: bachelor’s degree (55%), some college (20%), master’s degree (16%), high school degree (5%), and doctoral degree (5%). Participants reported checking their email very often (49%), always (37%), sometimes (10%), and rarely (3%). Most participants reported they always use email at work (52%), followed by very often (35%), sometimes (9%), rarely (4%), and never (1%). Most participants reported no prior experience with falling victim to phishing (80%), 9% reported having some experience, and 11% reported maybe on this measure. Compared to the U.S. general public data according to the 2020 Census, the sample slightly overrepresents females but closely matches the age distribution (c.f., Blakaslee et al., 2023). It also slightly overrepresents higher educational attainment (c.f., Census, 2021).

Participants were randomly assigned to the different treatment conditions featuring the same message with varying manipulations of the sender. At the start of the experiment, participants received their randomised manipulation. They read the associated security message based on a phishing awareness message developed by Jensen et al. (2017) (see Appendix A). Next, they were asked to respond to the manipulation check questions about power, similarity, and credibility, followed by questions measuring IMI and message compliance intentions.

As a baseline security message, we used a message on phishing. This was considered an authentic choice because the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and Social Security Administration disseminated phishing warning messages to the general public (e.g. IRS, 2018). To maximise internal validity, we chose simple textual manipulations that changed only the sender’s name and kept the security message consistent. This approach aimed to isolate the effects of different message sender characteristics on recipients responses. As to different message senders, we identified plausible senders that were highest in one of the characteristics of interest. Accordingly, we chose (1) the head of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) as being the highest in power, (2) a security expert as being the highest in expertise, and (3) a generic Citizen as being the highest in similarity. In the DHS condition, the message was presented as coming from the head of the U.S. DHS. The sender was described as a cybersecurity professional in the security expert condition. Lastly, in the citizen condition, the message was attributed to coming from a U.S. citizen.

## 4.4.2 Study 2 Participants, Materials, and Procedures

Study 2 was another field experiment to validate our model among students. Accordingly, we recruited students from a large U.S. university as participants. To recruit participants, we sent a solicitation email to the population of about 8,000 enrolled students, generating 172 valid responses. The low participation rate was likely due to the study being timed during the summer break and its comparatively low incentives (opportunity to win university dining gift cards). As in Study 1, we removed participants’ responses who failed our attention checks and could not identify the speaker in the video. This reduced the sample to 147 valid responses. Again, we tested whether this reduction led to different results by running all analyses with the complete sample. The results led to identical conclusions using the reduced sample, leading us to conclude that the reduction in sample size did not influence the findings of our work.

In the final sample, 77% of participants were female, 3% chose not to disclose their gender, and the average age was 24. Most participants held an associate degree or were earning a college degree (63%), followed by those who had a four-year college degree (25%), a high school degree (11%), and a master’s degree (2%). Most of the participants indicated that they check their email sometimes (47%), very often (33%), rarely (18%), and always (2%). A sizable portion of our sample also indicated that they always use email at work (33%), followed by very often (22%), sometimes (22%), rarely (9%), and never (14%). Most of our participants reported no prior experience falling victim to phishing attacks (84%), 10% reported some experience, and 6% indicated maybe on this measure. Compared with current enrolment trends, and even though female enrolment has outpaced male enrolment, the sample overrepresented female students (Hanson, 2024). The sample also slightly underrepresented associate degree students and master’s degree students (Hanson, 2024).

In Study 2, we used video-based treatments that manipulated the spokesperson of a university-branded video featuring the same anti-phishing security message from Study 1. Videos increased the ecological validity of our research design because they offered authentic footage of the research site and real characters in the field, thereby further increasing the authenticity of the stimulus. To parallel Study 1, we recruited real individuals in the university setting who varied in expertise, power, and similarity compared to the target audience. We asked the university chancellor, a professor, a university IT security officer, and a student to recite the security message and video recorded it. Although the spokespeople differed in their characteristics (e.g. the Chancellor was older than the student), the video messages were identical across all conditions. Therefore, the videos featured the same background, and we used the same text overlay position and layout to name the video speakers and identify their positions (e.g. Chancellor, Professor, Security Officer, Student) to inform the viewer about who was speaking. Thus, the study featured four experiential conditions. In the chancellor's condition, the sender was the university’s Chancellor, as represented by himself and identified by a text overlay. The professor’s condition featured a university professor, whereas the security officer presented the university’s IT security officer. Lastly, in the student condition, the video featured a university student. The remaining procedures employed in Study 2 were similar to those of Study 1. Thus, participants viewed only the video message after they agreed to participate in the study. Again, participants were randomly assigned to the treatment conditions. The measurements from Study 1 were adapted to the university context but otherwise identical, as reported in Appendix A.

## 4.4.3 Study 3 Participants, Materials, and Procedures

Study 3 was a scenario experiment to validate our model among employees. To obtain a sample of employees, we recruited 200 participants from Prolific. We screened for participants who were employed full-time, U.S.-based, worked with computers daily, sometimes or always worked remotely (to have sufficient knowledge to understand the remote-work scenario we used), and had at least two years of work experience. Given Prolific’s extensive pool of participants and the option to select this targeted sampling frame, using Prolific to recruit participants allowed us to randomly sample our target population and thus generalise our findings to this population. After eliminating participants for failing the attention checks and rushing through the survey, 189 valid responses remained in our sample. 57% of the participants in our sample were men, 40% women, and 3% nonbinary. Age was distributed as: 4% (18–24 years), 39% (25– 34 years), 34% (35–44 years), 9% (45–54 years), 10% (55–64 years), and 3% older than 65. 3% held a high school degree as their highest obtained degree, 10% had a college degree, 7% had a 2-year degree, 47% had a 4-year degree, 27% had a professional degree, and 6% had a doctoral degree. The average participant worked remotely most days of the week and thus used remote access technology between 3 to 5 times a week (because this study was conducted during the COVID pandemic, remote work was prevalent among IT workers). Compared to the latest U.S. workforce demographic data, the sample skews younger, slightly underrepresents females, and slightly overrepresents workers with higher education (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2024).

Our scenario asked participants to evaluate how they would respond to receiving a security message from a coworker, a security expert, or a leader. Therefore, the experimental design had three different conditions. The sender of a particular security message was randomly chosen for each participant. The scenario was developed to set up a situation in which a hypothetical character decides to either accept a security message and change this person’s behaviour or reject a message and continue in this person’s noncompliant, ISP-violating ways (see Appendix B). To create a more manipulative message and elicit higher levels of IMI, we included a deceptive statement in the security message that allowed participants to interpret the message as manipulative.

Overall, the realism of the scenario was rated at 6.20 (SD = 0.75) on a 7-point Likert-type scale and thus high. Throughout the study, we used attention checks that probed participants’ knowledge of details of the scenario (e.g. who sent the message) and ensured their careful reading of each question. Participants who failed these checks were eliminated from the study. Participants who sped through the survey were also eliminated (i.e. spent less than 5 minutes completing the entire survey). On average, participants spent 10.56 minutes (SD = 6.79) reading the scenario and answering the questions.

## 4.4.4 Study 4 Participants, Materials, and Procedures

Study 4 was intended to replicate the results of Study 2 using the scenario method used in Study 3. The idea behind this approach was to examine to what extent the change in method (i.e., field experiment to scenario experiment) affected our results, which provides essential insights to correctly interpret differences in findings between the studies. Therefore, akin to Study 2, we recruited a student sample of 200 students. Akin to Study 3, we used a scenario design and recruited participants from Prolific. Our screening procedures required participants to be students and U.S.-based. We recruited 250 participants and again eliminated participants for failing the attention checks and rushing through the survey, after which 194 valid responses remained. 45% of the sample were men, 53% women, 2% nonbinary, and 1% preferred not to say. Age was distributed as: 27% (18–24 years), 40% (25–34 years), 18% (35–44 years), 11% (45–54 years), 3% (55–64 years), and 2% older than 65. 6% held a high school degree as their highest obtained degree, 26% had a college degree, 19% had a 2-year degree, 36% had a 4-year degree, 12% had a professional degree, and 1% had a doctoral degree. Compared with current enrolment trends, the sample is roughly representative of students’ gender distribution but skews older and more educated (Hanson, 2024).

## 5.5 Mediation and Total Effects

We completed a mediation analysis to understand how sender characteristics influence compliance (i.e., directly or indirectly by raising IMI). Our mediation analysis followed the guidelines of Zhao et al. (2010), which expanded on the recommendations proposed by Baron and Kenny (1986) to understand the different types of mediation. Direct effects refer to the estimated relationship between two variables. Indirect effects occur when the relationship is mediated by another variable and are calculated as the product of the coefficients along the mediation path. In contrast, total effects are the sum of the direct and indirect effects (Preacher & Hayes, 2008). Zhao et al. (2010) differentiate between complementary mediation effects, where indirect and direct effects point in the same direction; competitive mediation effects, where the indirect and direct effects point in opposite directions; and mediation-only effects, which occur when there is only an indirect effect without any direct effect.

Table 16 reports the results of our mediation analysis. For expertise, we found complementary mediation effects in Studies 1 and 4, because both direct and indirect effects were positive. In Study 2, the effect was only direct. In Study 3, the direct effect was insignificant, leaving a mediation-only indirect impact. Across Studies 1, 2, and 4, the direct effect was considerably larger than the indirect effect, suggesting that in these studies, perceptions of expertise affected message compliance intentions mainly directly rather than through IMI.

## 6.2 Differences between Studies

The findings from Studies 1, 2, and 4—which examined the role of perceived sender characteristics among the general public and student populations—were broadly consistent. However, some differences emerged depending on whether the effects of perceived cybersecurity expertise, power, and similarity on compliance were direct (i.e. H1a, H1b, H1c) or indirect through IMI (i.e. H2, H3a, H3b, H4c). The varying salience of factors across populations may explain these differences.

In Study 1, participants from the general public appeared more sensitive to the sender’s power. Given that the most potent sender in this context was a leader of a prominent public institution, this sensitivity might stem from the public’s general scepticism toward institutions. In contrast, Study 2, which focused on students, revealed that participants were more responsive to perceived similarity with the sender. This suggests that using a sender perceived as “too similar” (e.g. “They are just like me; what do they know?”) may be ineffective. Power did not significantly influence compliance in Study 2, but it did in Study 4, which also involved students but employed a scenario-based design rather than a field experiment. The discrepancy between Studies 2 and 4 regarding power could have two sources. First, the Chancellor’s perceived authority may have differed across studies, so students in the field experiment (Study 2) may not have viewed the real Chancellor as authoritative. In contrast, in the scenario experiment (Study 4), the hypothetical Chancellor was explicitly framed as such. Second, the video manipulation in Study 2 may have confounded the authority of the Chancellor, because the actual Chancellor may not have been recognised as such, or the impression conveyed did not meet students’ expectations or lacked a sufficient sense of authority.

The most pronounced difference emerged when comparing Studies 1, 2, and 4 to Study 3, which surveyed employees. In Study 3, power had a substantial and positive direct effect on message compliance intentions, whereas in the other studies, the effect was negative. Differences in methodology (i.e. Study 3 being a scenario experiment versus Studies 1 and 2 being field experiments) are an unlikely explanation, because Study 4—also a scenario experiment—produced results similar to the field experiments. Therefore, the discrepancy is more plausibly attributed to differences in the populations studied.

One plausible explanation relates to the context in which employee behaviours occur. Employee behaviour is typically governed by organisational information security policies, unlike the general public’s behaviour (Menard et al., 2017), and to a lesser extent, student behaviour. Because organisational leaders hold direct authority over employees and can enforce compliance with sanctions, power becomes an essential, if not even the most crucial factor, as individuals seek to avoid sanctions. This might especially be the case when the recommendations in a security message touch upon policy-related issues, as in our operationalisation in Study 3. Therefore, the perception that senders can enforce compliance dominates recipients’ responses. In contrast, expertise had minimal direct influence but slightly reduced IMI, resulting in a small indirect effect on message compliance intentions. This starkly contrasts the settings of Studies 1, 2, and 4, where leaders had limited actual authority to enforce compliance through sanctions, so that power just ended up having a negative effect by eliciting IMI, and perceptions of expertise mainly drove positive effects.

## 6.3 Theoretical Contributions

Our work contributes to security message compliance research. First, our results show that many recipients’ intentions to comply with a security message can be explained by factors that pertain to the message senders’ characteristics and IMI. Specifically, our model explained 56%, 42%, 26%, and 66% of the variance in message compliance intentions in Studies 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. This variance is consistent with research that uses message content to explain message compliance intentions.<sup>6</sup> This suggests that the senders’ social influence characteristics are an equally crucial factor contributing to the persuasiveness of security messages. Omitting sender characteristics, as it has previously been done, may thus be a grave oversight in our endeavour to understand how to create persuasive security messages.

Second, by integrating IMI into SIT, our work offers a novel theoretical model that can explain why recipients respond negatively to security messages. Previous explanations, often rooted in PMT (Rogers,

1983), have centred on shortcomings in content design (e.g. Johnston et al., 2015; Mattson et al., 2023; Menard et al., 2017; Nehme & George, 2022; Schuetz et al., 2021; Schuetz et al., 2020; Vance et al., 2022). However, our integrated model shows that sender characteristics, either directly or by raising IMI, can affect message outcomes negatively. Therefore, our extension of SIT through IMI offers the new insight that messages may not just be rejected because of weak arguments or poor design, as previous work implies, but because senders’ characteristics give cues of manipulative intentions, which enhances the literature’s ability to understand and explain why messages succeed or fail. Integrating IMI into SIT seems particularly important as many of the effects theorised based on SIT (i.e. positive, direct effects) did not materialise; instead, negative effects were observed. Thus, our work reveals settings in which predictions of positive influences of SIT’s sender characteristics are valid and in which IMI adds additional insights by explaining negative effects. Furthermore, integrating IMI into SIT provides new insights into the antecedents of IMI— precisely, sender characteristics. As previous work on IMI has not yet considered sender characteristics, the integration contributes novel insights about antecedents to IMI to the emerging IMI literature (e.g. Boudewyns et al., 2013; Campbell, 1995; Cotte et al., 2005; Erceg-Hurn & Steed, 2011; Wentzel et al., 2010).

Third, our tests across three distinct study populations significantly contribute to the ongoing debate on the comparability of findings across various contexts (e.g. Compeau et al., 2012; Johnston et al., 2015; Schuetz et al., 2020). It is common practice to recruit student subjects to generalise to the general public (i.e. home users) or employee populations (e.g. Anderson & Agarwal, 2010; Boss et al., 2015; Johnston & Warkentin, 2010a; Mattson et al., 2023; Warkentin et al., 2016). However, research has questioned whether these populations perceive security messages similarly. For example, Johnston et al. (2015) questioned whether employees perceive security threats described in security messages as personally relevant. Schuetz et al. (2020) discovered that organisational users evaluate security messages differently from users in private settings. Our work reveals a clear divergence in responses across these different populations. Although the general public and students may respond similarly to sender characteristics (i.e. power has a total negative effect, whereas expertise has a total positive effect), employees exhibit a distinct response pattern in that they react positively to power. These findings underscore the need for caution when generalising from one study population to another and demonstrate the need for context-sensitive theorising for each target population. Here, our findings contribute the first insights into appraisal differences for each population, with further research being needed to explore these differences in greater depth to develop context-sensitive theory on effective security communication strategies.

Fourth, our findings offer new insights relevant to established theories within the persuasion literature. Several of our constructs, grounded in SIT, overlap with seminal persuasion frameworks, such as the elaboration likelihood model (ELM; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) and Cialdini’s principles of persuasion (e.g.

Cialdini, 2001). Our construct of expertise parallels the source credibility cues in ELM, whereas power aligns with Cialdini’s concept of authority. Our study advances these theories by examining these constructs within the cybersecurity communication context, highlighting conditions under which they influence message compliance positively or negatively. For instance, although authority typically enhances persuasion, our findings show that excessive power cues may backfire when perceived as manipulative, a nuance less explored in traditional persuasion research and for which our integration of IMI offers a new explanation. Thus, our work extends SIT and prominent persuasion models by demonstrating how senderrelated factors and perceived manipulative intent jointly shape message compliance, particularly in highstakes domains like cybersecurity.

## 6.4 Practical Implications

Our work addresses the problem of choosing a “good” message sender. Given the large variance in outcomes explained by sender characteristics, our work suggests that practitioners carefully consider their choice of message senders, possibly with the same deliberateness with which they craft the content of security messages. Our findings imply that senders (e.g. leaders or executives) who have authority over recipients are less likely to secure compliance and that senders perceived as security experts are more able to ensure compliance. Our results further suggest that to maximise the efficacy of a security message, a credible expert sender needs to be chosen.

These findings have direct implications for how cybersecurity functions propagate security messages. In the public domain, security messages are frequently sent by government agencies or public institutions (Cole, 2016; Wright et al., 2014). Our results show that public security messages sent by high-ranking officials perceived as high in power but not high in cybersecurity expertise do not elicit compliance. Our findings suggest that the efficacy of these messages could be improved by choosing a sender who is high in expertise but low in power, such as a field expert or long-serving public servant with established credibility, compared to relying on senior officials with less technical knowledge or those perceived as politically motivated.

Likewise, our research in an academic setting with students as message recipients suggests that leaders (i.e. top-level administrators) may not be the right people to send a security message. Instead, our study indicates that security messages would be more efficacious if sent by someone high in expertise but low in power, such as a security expert (e.g. an external security consultant). This implies that identifying credible, nonthreatening representatives to disseminate messages effectively improves message outcomes in public and academic settings. For example, asking a respected, long-time security expert to disseminate security messages instead of a less credible leader can help avoid the potential negative effects of power.

However, our results suggest that power can positively affect compliance intentions in a corporate setting. This finding thus provides academic backing to the advice of leading security providers, which recommends that firms adopt a “top-down” approach with messages from executives to employees (cf. Wirth, 2018).

## 6.5 Limitations and Future Research

Our research has several limitations that provide opportunities for future research. We measured message compliance intentions instead of actual behaviours. In our work, as with many security contexts, actually observing compliance behaviours in response to security messages constituted in many ways “unaffordable experiments” that are “practically implausible or prohibitively costly” to execute (Tiwana & Kim, 2019, p. 1050). Specifically, whereas message appraisal outcomes are immediate, behavioural outcomes are often temporally distal (e.g. a participant becomes determined to learn more about phishing after reading a message but gets around to doing so only a week later) and may be influenced by confounding factors such as abilities, possibilities, and other factors (Schwarzer, 2008). Recognising the difficulty of studying behaviours, Rogers (1983) suggested that individual responses to persuasive messages are best measured in changes in behavioural intentions. Thus, measuring behavioural consequences of social influence was beyond the scope of our research and should be considered in future research. Such a model should acknowledge the many intermediate steps that precede security-relevant behaviours (e.g. Abbasi et al., 2021). Accordingly, the immediate focus of our research is not the effect of messages on behaviours but the effect of sender characteristics on recipients’ psychological responses. For this reason, it was meaningful for our research to focus on what is not yet known—namely, the influence of sender characteristics on message compliance intentions—and not on what we already know—that message compliance intentions lead to behaviours, as has been shown by a host of studies, such as Silic and Lowry (2020). Tiwana and Kim (2019) noted that doing so allows researchers to focus on making a unique contribution by adding to the already cumulative body of knowledge.

Additional research on sender characteristics is needed. We motivated our choices of sender characteristics based on SIT; however, other factors could illuminate sender characteristics, such as attractiveness (Peng et al., 2020) and personality traits (Zhao et al., 2021). Future research may extend and refine our model by either incorporating other sender-related factors or expanding the current sender-related factors. For example, one could examine the effects of different forms of power (cf. Hinkin & Schriesheim, 1989). An interaction effect might also exist between the message sender and content (e.g., sender-content fit), which is beyond the scope of our research. Future research could thus explore how sender–content fit influences recipients’ credibility perceptions, IMI, and message outcomes.

Two additional limitations of our research in the corporate setting, where we sampled across organisations, are worth noting. (a) Our sample, recruited via Prolific, is not a random sample in the statistical sense but a convenience sample. As such, participants may not fully represent the broader population, with the possibility that specific characteristics related to their organizational contexts may have influenced our results. Therefore, we encourage future research to explicitly account for organisational differences and examine their potential influence on our findings. (b) In our research design, the requested behaviour in the security message overlapped with a behaviour regulated by an ISP, which we considered more realistic. However, this overlap may have introduced ISP-related factors influencing employees responses. Future studies may explore the effect of sender characteristics on policy-unrelated recommendation scenarios.

Another limitation pertains to the frame of reference in which the general public was asked to respond to the security messages in Study 1. Whereas we provided no cues that the frame of reference was of an organisational or academic nature and provided a brief context in that the study was conducted to examine participants’ general computer behaviours, we did not ascertain whether participants used an organisational or academic frame of reference. Future research should address this by clearly specifying the intended context before message delivery to ensure consistent participant interpretation.

Another potential limitation concerns our operationalisation of the IMI construct. Although our conceptual definition captures perceptions of whether a sender is seen as manipulative, following prior work, our operational measurement in Studies 1 to 3 focused on the sender’s message. Although a statistical test in Study 4, using a more sender-specific measurement, showed no significant differences in our findings (see Appendix D)—suggesting this did not influence our results—future researchers who build on our work should use more sender-specific measures.

Further research is needed to examine the roles of sender characteristics in different cultures. The current research was sampled from a Western, educated, industrialised, affluent, and democratic society. Basic cognitive processes may differ between participants from different societies (Heinrich et al., 2010), cautioning against generalising our findings and conclusions to other societies is essential. Specifically, if studying societies other than the U.S. or equivalents of the Western world, such as countries or cultures that are, for instance, higher in relevant cultural dimensions such as power distance or individualismcollectivism (Hofstede, 2001), one might expect the studied effect estimates to change and become more or less salient (i.e. power or similarity becoming more critical than expertise). Testing our model with participants from other societies is beyond the scope of our research. However, we acknowledge the importance of culture and specifically call for examining how culture may change how recipients respond to senders of security messages.

## 7 CONCLUSION

Security messages are essential for motivating users to take necessary precautions against cyber risks. Although previous research has mainly examined the content of such messages, our research underscores the critical influence of sender characteristics on the effectiveness of security messages. By integrating SIT with IMI, we provide a comprehensive understanding of how cybersecurity expertise, power, and similarity impact message outcomes. Our findings reveal that these characteristics have varying effects across public, academic, and corporate settings. Specifically, cybersecurity expertise enhances message outcomes in public and educational contexts, whereas power is more effective in corporate environments. Additionally, IMI plays a significant role in explaining negative responses, offering a nuanced view of message acceptance and rejection. These insights emphasise the importance of tailoring the selection of message senders to their audience, contributing valuable knowledge to cybersecurity communication.

# Appendix B. Study 3

## Study 3: Baseline Scenario

Lawrence is an insurance service agent of a local auto insurance company. As part of his work of processing clients’ insurance claims, he receives a lot of emails with important documents from clients, authorities, and surveyors (e.g., pictures, accident explanations in MS Word or PDF, and policy reports in PDF, and sometimes Excel summarizing repair costs and remaining values).

Lawrence’s performance is tracked by how many claims he can process in a day. If he processes more claims than his colleagues in each quarter, he receives a small financial bonus. Since the pandemic, he works from home. To do so, Lawrence’s company has set up remote access to his work computer using CITRIX. Based on Lawrence’s company’s information security policy, he is required to use the remote access to his work computer to access emails and process claims. This ensures that confidential documents will remain on the company’s secured computers and networks.

To Lawrence, the remote access is cumbersome and frustrating. Not only is the connection sluggish, but frequently breaks and requires him to reconnect—each time needing to send a message to his phone to obtain a two-factor authentication token—which considerably slows him down and thus negatively affects his performance.

He knew he wasn’t the only experiencing these problems. Everyone he talked to hated the remote access and soon a simple workaround was informally established: To avoid frustration with the unreliable connection, people at his organization began relaying work emails to their private email accounts, which they could access from their home computer. Furthermore, through the relay, they could also send emails and they would appear as coming from their work email.

Although the workaround was against the formal information security policies of his organization, virtually everyone Lawrence knew at his company was using the workaround and he was also using it himself, too. He never heard of anyone being reprimanded for using it. In general, employees were rarely sanctioned for non-compliance with information security policies, and if sanctions occurred, they were no more severe than a mild slap on the wrist.

## Appendix D. Study 4

## Study 4: Baseline Scenario

Lawrence, a first-year computer science student, is working on a programming assignment in the campus library. The university has a clear IT security policy prohibiting the use of its Wi-Fi network for peer-to-peer (P2P) file sharing, such as downloading movies, music, or software via torrenting applications. One day, Lawrence received an email from Brian.

Brian was a fellow student at the same university.

He had no expertise in cybersecurity

He held no authority over Lawrence.

Lawrence had also heard rumors from other students that Brian occasionally used the university network to download movies.

## Condition 2: Security officer (Highest in Expertise)

One day, Lawrence received an email from Brian.

Brian was a staff security analyst in the university’s information security group.

He had significant expertise in cybersecurity.

However, his role did not grant him any authority over Lawrence.

Lawrence had also heard rumors from other students that staff members, including individuals like Brian, occasionally used the university network to download movies.

## Condition 3: Chancellor (Highest in Power)

One day, Lawrence received an email from Brian.

Brian was the Chancellor of their university.

Brian had no expertise in cybersecurity.

However, as the Chancellor, Brian had significant authority over Lawrence.

Lawrence had also heard rumors from other students that Brian, the Chancellor, occasionally used the university network to download movies.

## Study 4: Measurement

The measurement was the same as in Study 3, except that it used a slightly reworded IMI measure that only contained items in reference to the message sender rather than mixing the message sender and message content. The reworded measure was:

1. Brian tried to manipulate Lawrence in ways that I don’t like

2. I was annoyed by Brian tried to inappropriately control Lawrence

3. Brian was fair in what he said (Reverse coded)

To validate that this change did not meaningfully impact our results, we also collected an additional 100 samples that used the original measure but otherwise used the identical research design of Study 4. We then compared the models using the original and the sender-specific measure by examining their model fit using the compareFit function in R. The results show very small differences between the two models: ∆Chi-square = -34.083, ∆RMSEA = -.05, ∆CFI = -.056, ∆TLI = 0.07, ∆SRMR = -0.026. We compared the parameter estimates of both models using the Wald Chi-square test; we found no evidence (p = 0.31) that the estimates were statistically different. Thus, while the sender-specific measure improved model-data fit slightly, we found no evidence that these differences influenced our findings. Hence, we are confident that this did not confound our results
