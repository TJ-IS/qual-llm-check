## Introduction

Crypto-assets are regarded as a new investment class in many countries (HMT, 2023). The debate in the US is whether jurisdiction should be controlled by The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) or The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Turf wars exist and Executive Order 14067 was signed on 9 March 2022, calling for all agencies to coordinate regulatory efforts (The White House, 2022a). Despite the absence of clear and consistent policies, investment in crypto-assets have grown significantly in the last decade. From a market capitalization of \$14 billion in 2016, it surpassed \$3 trillion by the end of 2021, albeit failing to less than \$1 trillion in January 2023 (FT, 2023). So far, it is estimated that 16 percent (40 million) adult Americans have invested in, traded, or used crypto-assets (The White House, 2022b). Worldwide, some 300 million people own some form of cryptoasset (Chohan, 2022) with the figure only expected to rise.

The growth of emerging, inchoate, crypto-assets challenges regulators to develop oversight mandates fit-for-purpose for rapidly evolving decentralized finance (DeFi) in which cryptoexchanges are largely free from regulatory controls. For this study, we adopt a qualitative, inductive method to capture stakeholder views and opinions on crypto-asset regulation and technology, broadly defined.

A phenomenological perspective asserts that much of the world is socially constructed (Berger and Luckman, 1966; Schutz, 1967) which includes how people perceive and respond to emerging technology. How people view the world influences all areas of social life (Weick, 1979). Accordingly, regulation of crypto-assets occurs in complex historical, social, and political environments. Policy is infused with value-laden terminology, underpinned by moral frameworks and assumptions. Ethical principles govern regulatory agendas with power and influence wielded through exercising agency and control over regulated entities. To guide our grounded approach, we began with a broad research question: What views and opinions do stakeholders groups have on emerging, inchoate crypto-asset technology?

Empirical data is generated from different crypto-asset stakeholders. Specifically, we organize our discussion around primary and secondary data from four distinct stakeholder groups. Comparatively, the groups reveal contrasting agendas, views, and narratives on the crypto-asset ecosystem. The implications for policy and practice are significant. Comparisons are drawn between traditional regulatory approaches (rules- and principles-based) and decentralized (digital) financial systems, which foster social norms of unbridled innovation and individual freedom to own digital assets, unrestricted by state intervention. Findings show reducing governments’ role in regulating technology – presented theoretically as a social choice between institutional maintenance and individual freedom – may have unintended consequences for stakeholder groups.

The research contributes to the information systems literature in three ways. First, responding to calls to develop theoretical and empirical work on blockchain and crypto-assets (Rossi et al., 2019) we present a deeper, more nuanced understanding of different stakeholder perceptions of crypto-asset regulatory policy and practice. Four stakeholder positions, underpinned by different normative and operational priorities, impact regulatory oversight. The findings are relevant for policy makers as regulatory decisions become increasingly instantiated into computer code, hidden from broader regulatory scrutiny and oversight.

Second, the literature combines studies from information systems, finance, and law to analyse the technology-legal complexities of crypto-asset regulation. Drawing from only one discipline gives researchers the advantage of familiarization with the extant literature, theories, and methods. The disadvantage is important and relevant themes, debates, and issues are not covered or linked. For example, information systems researchers are less familiar with definitions and applications of legal terminology on digital assets and regulation, and those trained in finance and law have limited operational knowledge of digital technology (Micheler and Whaley, 2020). Different literature streams reveal asymmetries in the technical, financial market, and legal literacy among stakeholder groups. Specifically, how regulators play catch-up in policy making for emerging technologies, and how regulated entities are impacted by regulation (Currie et al, 2022).

Third, the research offers a theoretical contribution using the methodology developed by Gioia and colleagues (Gioia et al., 2012). From the literature and empirical study, three concepts emerge: inchoate technology; regulatory intervention; and social norms of innovation. The aggregate concepts build a data structure to interpret and explain the impact from policy, and practical complexities of crypto-asset regulation. We show how inchoate technology, infused with social norms which support individual freedom to innovate and invest in digital assets, is unimpeded by government intervention. This scenario plays out as crypto firms enjoy free reign to develop new financial instruments within a neo-liberal free market and, by extension, obfuscate governments’ role in protecting consumers against the adverse effects from crypto trading (e.g., rug pulls, wash trading, co-mingling of customer funds).

## Literature Review

There is no universal definition for crypto-assets. The SEC proposes the following:

“An asset that is issued and/or transferred using distributed ledger or blockchain technology (‘distributed ledger technology’), including, but not limited to, so-called ‘virtual currencies,’ ‘coins,’ and ‘tokens.’” (SEC, 2022, p. 273)

The complexity of the crypto-asset ecosystem creates definitional challenges, with claims that coins and tokens are different asset classes. The SEC and CFTC view “these terms as synonymous” (SEC, 2022, p. 53). Our research focuses on tokens shaded in grey: exchange tokens, commonly referred to as cryptocurrencies (such as Bitcoin, BTC); and the sub-set, asset referenced tokens, also called stable coins. To date, much of the literature focuses on BTC, an exchange-token, and the first “cryptoasset designed to work as a medium of exchange” (Azar et al., 2022, p. 27). Introduced in 2009 it has drawn academic and practitioner interest on a range of topics: an empirical study on the investment opportunities of cryptocurrencies (Cunha and Murphy, 2019); determinants of exchange token rates (Li and Wang, 2017); price discovery (Alexander and Heck, 2020); pump-and-dump manipulation (Dhawan and Putnis, 2021); liquidity and transaction costs (Dyhrberg et al., 2018); asymmetric volatility (Baur and Dimpfl, 2018); users’ hidden attentions (Glaser et al., 2014); pseudonymity (Fabian et al., 2016; Yin et al., 2019); the rise of the decentralized autonomous organization (Hsieh et al., 2018); media impact on value (Mai et al., 2018); the extent to which it is untethered (Griffin and Shams, 2020); social collectives shaping markets (Briedbach and Tana, 2021); its replacement to profit maximizing firms (Huberman et al., 2021); and use as a socio-technical system (Knittel et al., 2019).

Under the theme crime and crypto-assets, the extant literature examines financial market crime, including money laundering (Albrecht et al., 2019; Brenig et al., 2015; Dupris and Gleason, 2020; Dyntu and Dykyi, 2018) and market manipulation (Chen et al., 2019; Eigelshoven et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2020). Such crime varies from “mild and victimless,” to very serious crimes of “human trafficking, drug running, slavery, (and) terrorism” (Chohan, 2022, p. 15). One report suggests, crypto-asset linked crime increased to a record high in 2021 (measured by value), with illegal addresses receiving \$14 billion in cryptocurrencies, up 79% from \$7.8 billion, in just one year (Reuters, 2022). Against growing concerns about the lack of regulation of digital-assets, leading computer scientists and academics signed a letter “in support of responsible FinTech policy” delivered to US lawmakers voicing serious concerns about crypto-assets and blockchains (Chipolina, 2022).

An under researched theme in the academic literature is crypto-asset transactions, DeFi and regulation. Fiat currencies are centralized, meaning each country controls its currency supply through a single administrative authority (e.g., Federal Reserve). Decentralization means “the broad dispersion of the ability to exchange data and information within communication systems” (Vergne, 2020, p. 4). Assets, such as BTC, are stored by individuals in a crypto wallet. A custodial wallet, provided by a centralized crypto exchange is analogous to a customer depositing cash in a bank. Security breaches and crime can occur at these venues. Assets saved in a decentralized non-custodial wallet pose greater risks to the customer as the loss of the wallet password prevents access to the assets. Using decentralized applications (or dApp’s), peer-to-peer (P2P) operations mimic and enhance financial activities, a process known as DeFi (Eikmanns et al., 2023). Internet connected computers, controlled by algorithms, avoid central oversight. DeFi and crypto exchanges challenge traditional methods of regulation for financial innovation which rely on either, the regulation of activities, entities, and instruments, or the regulation of middlemen and gatekeepers (Nabilou, 2019).

Contemporary examples of inchoate technology include: blockchain (Rossi et al., 2019); exchange tokens (Bjerg, 2016); and artificial intelligence (Collins et al., 2021; Wagner et al., 2021). The inchoateness of these technologies is under-theorized in the literature, yet relevant to studies on digital transformation (Wessel et al., 2021) digital business strategy (Bharadwaj et al., 2013) and digital-assets (Birch et al., 2021), which examine either the effects or processes of novel digital technologies in specific societal and organizational settings. Theory-driven crypto-asset and empirical research on emerging technology usually begins after the technology has gained traction in the market (Rotolo et al., 2015).

Discourse on emerging, or disruptive technology, is informed by innovation literature which looks at disruptions in markets and businesses (Christensen, 1997; Paech, 2017). A broader sociological analysis examines social relations and norms, institutions, epistemic paradigms, foundational concepts, and values.

If irreversibility is the hallmark of technologically driven disruptive transformational change, research needs to consider the effects and processes of social change. For example, first-order disruption is a localised change within a market or industry (Christensen, 1997; Hopster, 2021). Second order disruption affects a much broader range of social norms and operations, including social relationships, organizational structures, institutions, public policy and regulation, and the physical environment (Schuelke-Leech, 2018). In the following three sub sections, we review the extant literature on emerging technology, regulation and innovation, ethics, and social norms.

## Emerging Technology

Emerging technology offers high potential but limited shared understanding and value among stakeholders (Cozzens et al., 2010, p. 364). Following the early stage of development, many technologies move beyond the conceptual stage to gain traction within 10-15 years (Stahl, 2011). Five distinct features of emerging technology include: (i) radical novelty; (ii) relatively fast growth; (iii) coherence; (iv) prominent impact; and (v) uncertainty and ambiguity (Rotolo et al., 2015). The term inchoate technology provides additional analytical clarity to emerging technology, since it specifically refers to technology that is not fully formed or developed, rudimentary, highly differentiated, inherently unstable, non-neutral, and potentially results in unintended consequences for policy makers, regulators, and businesses (Gervais, 2010). It is not cryptographic hash functions (e.g., SHA-256) that are inchoate (Courtois et al., 2014), these are well defined and proven. Rather, it is the systems wrapped around them which have allowed crypto-assets to be manipulated or external attacks to happen.

The inchoateness of financial technology, especially in the post-2008 financial crisis period, adds fuel to debates about how to regulate technology, spawning new areas for research, in financial technology or FinTech, and its subsets: regulatory technology or RegTech (Butler and O’Brien, 2019); and supervisory technology or SupTech, (Toronto Centre, 2017; FSB, 2020). An observation is the risk from digital-assets “stem less from the threats of their illicit uses as virtual currencies and more from the opportunities presented by their underlying blockchain technologies” (Campbell-Verduyn, 2018, p. 284). In June 2022, a Financial Actions Task Force report discussed how cross-chain bridges transfer virtual assets between different networks, laundering billions of dollars of illicit money (FATF, 2022). Hacks have targeted crypto exchanges with the largest, Binance, suspending operations when \$570m was stolen (FT, 2022). Adoption and diffusion of emerging technology increases over time, decreasing its novelty value by becoming more integrated in society. During the inchoate phase, technology is volatile and unpredictable, presenting unprecedented challenges to mature markets and businesses, as well as to new entrants (start-ups) (Gervais, 2010).

The temporal aspects of inchoate technology underpin theoretical maturity models (Becker et al., 2009) which show the transition of information technology moving from one stage to the next, reaching a final stage of maturity (Nolan, 1979). Maturity models are useful for describing the technical features of each stage (Pereira and Serrano. 2020) but they lack a nuanced stakeholder analysis of how technology transitions the various stages. To date, the literature on competing social and institutional agendas on emerging technology is limited in the management and IS literature. Accordingly, our research interest is the intersection between emerging, inchoate technology and government regulation.

## Regulation of Technology

Governments, in developing regulatory policy for emerging technology, face normative and operational questions. First, based on the initial evaluation of the potential impact of technology, is ex-ante regulation feasible? or an ex-post laissez-faire approach? Second, if the technology is viewed as harmful to society, markets, and citizens, what options are available to regulators to influence, limit, or even prohibit, its use?

Regulatory intervention of inchoate technology significantly increases complexity and risk of achieving a satisfactory policy outcome. Traditional policy-making models do not adequately mitigate the risk of regulatory failure, “proportional to the level of inchoateness of the technology” (Gervais, 2010, p. 665). Regulatory oversight of information and communications technology (ICT) consists mainly of two types: (i) rules-based regulation which prescribes a legal framework or set of prescriptive rules about how to behave; and (ii) principles-based regulation sets out controls, measures, and procedures, requiring each organisation, or individual to self-regulate to achieve the desired outcome (Black, 2008; Black, 2010; Currie et al., 2018). Historically the SEC provided detailed, prescriptive rules, alongside the CTFC as a principles-based regulator. The two narratives derive from different political philosophies on the regulation of technology (Decker, 2018).

Rules-based regulation confers enhanced reputational credibility for compliance, and potential sanctions and fines for rule-breaking behaviour (intended or otherwise). The regulator sets the rules, aiming for clarity and conciseness. The regulated adhere to the regulatory mandate (Arjoon, 2006). The advantage is regulated markets and businesses conform to a set of rules which govern their activities to prevent corruption, malfeasance, non-compliance, and other undesirable behaviour. The regulator actively engages with regulated parties to build public confidence in government and institutions responsible for oversight and monitoring (Kane, 1997).

With fewer restrictions on setting mandatory rules, principles-based regulation is criticised for failing to provide certainty and predictability of outcome. Although it was not designed as a light touch approach, the aim was to achieve similar outcomes to rules-based regimes (Decker, 2018) by offering a “flexible regulatory regime which can facilitate innovation” (Black, 2010, p. 1). For established technology firms and start-ups, principles-based regulation facilitates competitiveness (Tarbert, 2020). Regulators are provided with increased scope to interpret regulatory goals and methods of supervision (Black, 2010). Regulators rely on an enforcement approach which investigates whether regulatees’ actions are consistent with the principles or goals (Decker, 2018).

Because of the high degree of unpredictability of the evolution of inchoate technology, exante effects and outcomes are not easy to predict. Over-regulation is seen as detrimental to innovation (Chohan, 2022) which will drive new business across borders into less-regulated jurisdictions, potentially undermining growth of a new financial asset class. Regulation adds additional costs (such as requiring comprehensive record keeping) and delays transaction time.

The views of the pro-innovation lobby are illustrated in a report that shows the cryptocurrency exchange platform Coinbase, and 26 lobbyists, raised around \$1.5 bn to present their case to Capitol Hill (US policymakers and legislators) about the benefits of crypto-assets (Nuttall, 2022). Accordingly, over-regulation is viewed as stifling innovation. Mandating inappropriate rules, sanctions, and fines can lead to unintended outcomes for nascent markets and technology, not in the interests of companies, and citizens (Gervais, 2010). However, removing regulatory controls to prevent risky, flawed, and unproven digital assets undermines public confidence in governments’ ability to protect citizens from crypto related crime (Chipolina, 2022).

Prior to the financial crisis of 2008, regulation of financial innovation was laissez-faire, favouring deregulation of global institutional markets (Crotty, 2009). Financial innovation in new and unregulated derivative products (e.g., credit default swaps), played a major role in facilitating the crisis (Zetzsche et al., 2017). Post-crisis, the regulatory pendulum shifted to the other extreme. Regulators became more cautious about the deleterious effects of financial innovation and technology. Against this backdrop, emerging FinTech accelerated, presenting regulators with complex and unprecedented challenges.

Inchoate technology challenges regulators to develop fit-for-purpose governance and oversight mandates. Along with crypto-assets (Feinstein and Werbach, 2021), blockchain (Beck et al., 2018; Murray, 2019; Trump et al., 2018) and artificial intelligence (Buiten, 2019) are fast evolving, with the potential to transform global markets, businesses, and consumer behaviour (Rotolo et al., 2015). An opportunity exists in information systems research for theoretical and empirical work combining policy and technology issues. Studies from finance and law provide useful analysis of the different regulatory regimes in place (Black, 2008; 2010). The sociology of finance considers emerging technology in financial markets (MacKenzie, 2018; Fligstein and Habinek, 2013; Knorr-Cetina and Preda, 2012; Lenglet, 2011). However, more work is needed on how regulators develop and oversee policy mandates for emerging technology.

A seminal study in the context of regulation and technology coined the phrase code is law (Lessig, 1999). As computer code is standardised, it becomes the de facto rule. The scenario presents the regulator with a new challenge as computer code subsumes legal code. Policy choices to impose rules- or principles-based mandates are impacted as algorithmic code masks regulatory scrutiny of technology firms’ decisions. As rules become more precise and codified, complexity increases. This leads to a greater number of operational gaps, with the potential to create internal inconsistencies in how they are applied in practice. Certainty does not depend on the structure of the rule or principle, but how it is interpretated, and how the measured variable is defined. Whether a rule, principle, or any other type of codified social norm, depends on the shared understanding between regulator and regulatee (Black, 2008). In sum, social norms among stakeholder groups point to variation, further complicating the relationship between regulation and technology.

## Innovation, Ethics and Social Norms

Social norms are standards of behaviour and/or thought: (i) that indicate what people should or should not do, or think, under specific circumstances, (ii) are enforced upon individuals by external sanctions and (iii) are internalized by some or even many individuals (DeQuech, 2009). Distinct from ideas, attitudes, and values, social norms are codified into rules and laws or informal understandings that govern individual behaviour. They are contingent upon context, social group, and historical circumstances, sustained by multiple mechanisms, including fear of being sanctioned, signalling membership in a group, or following the lead of others (Young, 2015).

In institutional theory, social norms on governance are frequently presented as rules or sanctions (Scott, 2001). Coercive isomorphism exerts pressure on organizations from other organizations (regulatory bodies) to conform to certain rules and practices and to cultural expectations from society. If a social norm exists, the threat of sanctions also exists. External coercive pressures which threaten sanctions help maintain the norm. Mimetic and normative pressures also exert pressure on individuals to conform to social norms.

Coercive isomorphism differs from mimetic isomorphism, in that uncertainty encourages change (Dimaggio and Powell, 1983). Social norms are internalized by individuals, who interpret a norm as the ethical thing to do, regardless of the threat of sanctions. Internalization of social norms, described as, internal sanctions, shape individual behaviour from an emotion response to feelings of, for example, a sense of guilt, shame, or pride (Elster, 1989, p. 104).

Social norms on inchoate technology begin in a state of flux and evolve rapidly. A moving technological target challenges social norms which may shift to circumvent regulation (Gervais, 2010, p. 28). Social norms bifurcate into rules- and principles-based. The former upholds social norms which give citizens clear and concise prescriptive behavioural templates (rules). The latter emphasize trustworthiness, ethical interpretation, and judgement to comply with the goal (principle).

Two observations on social norms on technology regulation contrast innovation practices and ethical principles (Fassin, 2000; Lobschat et al., 2021). First, the policy literature on emerging technology illustrates the social norm that “innovation is an essential driver of economic progress that benefits consumers, businesses and the economy” (European Central

Bank, 2017). Government institutions promote innovation and technology as a means of improving national competitive performance and skills (Palvia et al., 2021). The innovation social norm is especially powerful as a driver of technology start-ups that attract significant financial investment from venture capital firms for what is essentially, untried and tested (inchoate) technology. The norm that over-regulation stifles innovation and harms the economy is a powerful societal influencer (Relihan, 2018). In sum, social norms on how to balance regulation and innovation are robustly debated among government and industry, with significant implications for policy making for nascent digital markets.

Second, high-leverage finance (Nielson, 2010) enabled by technology, is implicated in the implosion of financial institutions in the 2008 financial crisis (FCIC, 2011). To repair the serious damage affecting the global economy, regulators promote ethical principles for corporate social responsibility (Gangi and Trotta, 2015; Lewis et al., 2010). Following the financial crisis, the emergence of RegTech and SupTech initiatives are designed to govern FinTech (Treleaven, 2015; Bromberg et al., 2017). However, these developments focus primarily on the technology used for regulation and compliance of financial firms (Currie et al, 2018). This differs from crypto-assets, which are primarily computer code (WEF, 2023), and not amenable to scrutiny and oversight from external entities, including regulators.

The complexity of the technology-regulatory nexus spans different literature streams. We organize the literature into themes and concepts to underpin the empirical investigation for this study (see Table 1). Our interest on social norms of crypto-asset technology positions regulation as a policy agenda to oversee emerging, inchoate technology and not the technologies used in regulation and compliance.

## Methods

A research study on the regulation of crypto-assets is an opportunity to investigate emerging technology, potentially transformational for markets, firms, and citizens. Grounded research methods speak to the shortage of theory-driven and empirical studies, as academic enquiry on emerging technology usually begins after the technology has gained some traction in the market (Rotolo et al., 2015). To achieve our research objective of building theory from empirical data (Eisenhardt, 1989) we use an inductive, qualitative method (Denzin and Lincoln, 2018). This section discusses the Gioia methodology, research context, and data collection.

## Gioia Methodology

Inductive, theory building is preferred for researching a new field of enquiry which is undertheorized with few empirical contributions. The scientific method which uses existing knowledge as a precursor for creating new knowledge, differs from the systematic inductive approach (Denzin and Lincoln, 2018). Inductive research for concept development offers the potential for originality in theorizing (Corley and Gioia, 2011). A distinction is made between constructs and concepts. Constructs are abstract theoretical formulations about phenomena of interest that are formulated and measured primarily using quantitative methods. By delineating a domain of attributes to be operationalized, constructs are quantified as variables. Concept development is a more general and less specific approach to identify attributes that describe or explain a particular phenomenon.

Concepts are precursors to constructs (Gioia et al., 2012). They are used for sense-making in a socially constructed environment (Berger and Luckman, 1966; Schutz, 1967). For a research study to provide meaningful description, explanation, and prescription, it is useful to identify concepts for theory building as a precursor to the creation and validation of constructs. Thus, informed theory-building and testing is crucial for producing insightful research studies (Corley and Gioia, 2011).

In this research study, a set of core concepts emerge from the extant literature to guide our empirical investigation. By selecting a semi-structured interview protocol instead of structured interviews, we avoid imposing, a priori, potential bias by unwittingly influencing informants’ views and opinions on crypto-assets. Instead, the research design situates informants as knowledgeable agents who define their own realities, since they, “know what 2012, p. 17).

Inductive research of this nature has profound implications for what, how, and why data is collected since it foregrounds informants’ interpretations of the phenomena under investigation, with the researcher’s role focused on providing a rigorous and convincing account of the informant’s experience (Gioia et al., 2012). Thus, the researchers intentionally selected a broad research question to gather informants’ views and opinions on emerging, inchoate, crypto-asset technology, specifically within the regulatory context.

## Research Context

The research began as an enquiry into crypto-assets, specifically looking at stakeholder views and opinions on the regulation of a new financial asset class. Nakamoto’s paper spearheading the crypto-asset phenomenon asserts, “What is needed is an electronic payment system based on cryptographic proof instead of trust, allowing any two willing parties to transact directly with each other without the need for a trusted third party” (Nakamoto, 2008, p. 1). Since its publication, academic interest in digital-assets has led to calls for theoretical and empirical contributions (Beck et al., 2018; Rossi et al., 2019). Based on the extant literature, cryptoassets are built on emerging technology with inchoate features (novel, rapidly evolving, unpredictable) with the potential to transform financial systems through DeFi (Luther, 2016). As the technology evolves independently of regulatory regimes and market drivers (Gervais, 2010), regulators face three main challenges.

First, virtual currencies satisfy the definition of a commodity (see Commodity Exchange Act (CEA), Section 1 (a)(9) giving the CFTC jurisdiction to regulate the market). In response, the SEC chair, Gary Gensler, claims only Bitcoin (BTC) is a commodity. All other crypto-assets (issued using distributed ledger technology) meet the conditions for a security (Ackerman, 2022). Under section 2(a)(1) of the CEA, the exception to the CFTC’s exclusive jurisdiction is given: “…nothing contained in this section shall (I) supersede or limit the jurisdiction at any time conferred on the Securities and Exchange Commission…” (CFTC, 2023). This enables the SEC to regulate crypto-assets, providing the definitions of an ‘investment contract’ are met. The Howey Test, deriving its name after the 1946 case – SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., is the framework set by the U.S. Supreme Court to determine whether a financial instrument can be considered an investment contract and therefore a security. If answering yes to the four questions:-

(i) is there an investment of money

(ii) with the expectation of future profits

(iii) in a common enterprise

(iv) derived from the efforts of a promoter or third party

:- the asset is considered a security.

When first sold, BTC offered as an initial coin offering (ICO) met the Howey test. As a centralized digital asset, Satoshi controlled the trading environment. The retail investor hoped the asset would become a decentralized medium of exchange, not controlled by government or financial institutions. It took many years before enough miners and validating nodes enabled BTC to become decentralized. At this stage, BTC no longer met the fourth condition of the Howey test, which is the promotion of the asset. Decentralization redefined the virtual security into a virtual commodity, thus changing the asset type for regulatory oversight.

Second, the inchoateness of crypto-assets creates legal challenges for regulators that require new legal rules (Nabilou, 2019). The technology creates legal uncertainty, with existing laws less relevant in their scope and application. Start-up companies have found innovative ways to raise funds for new coins or services through unregulated ICOs (Kher et al., 2021). Investors receive exchange tokens issued by the company, creating securities concerns (Kolhatkar, 2021). In 2017, the SEC declared tokens of a popular crypto community, the Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAO), were securities. The decision established a regulatory expectation that most ICOs are subject to the Securities Act of 1933. In addition, the cross-jurisdictional ecosystem which is emerging for crypto-assets further complicates regulatory oversight. Thus, “Cryptocurrencies may contribute to monetary and financial instability, especially if they were to spawn a large and unregulated financial system that lacks investor protection.” (Prasad, 2021). The challenge for policy makers is how to develop fit-for-purpose regulation with little or no access to the technological assets (e.g., code) of FinTech firms.

Third, the ethical dimension is receiving more attention. Academic studies examine design constraints as part of a practical framework for crypto-asset and blockchain systems (Lapointe and Fishbane, 2018; Orcutt, 2019); impact of the adoption of cryptocurrencies and the digital divide (Hughes, 2017); cryptocurrencies and business behaviour (Dierksmeier and Seele, 2016); normative theory of cryptocurrencies and blockchains (Reijers and Coeckelbergh, 2018), use of cryptocurrencies for payment in society (Angel and McCabe, 2015); implications of using cryptocurrencies and blockchain technologies to implement new markets (Clark et al., 2014) and challenges of researching a field characterized by pseudonymity (Guadamuz and Marsden, 2014).

In the context of crypto-assets and ethics, regulators are cautioned against viewing technology as un-related to ethical principles, or simply as a neutral aspect of market or user behaviour (Gervais, 2010). How algorithmic code is written reflects the strategic priorities and objectives of market participants in the crypto ecosystem. Different stakeholders adopt various ideological positions in support or criticism of risk and effects. As the pursuit of ethical principles is a key part of technology regulation, it is important to elicit the views and opinions of stakeholder groups with competing policy and technology agendas.

## Data and Findings

Stakeholder typologies emerge from the literature and empirical data analysis. Interviews with stakeholder groups, which we define as interventionists, innovators, investors, and influencers, reveal a diversity of views and opinions on regulatory policy for crypto-assets and the social norms around innovation.

Regulators view their role as interventionist arising from their public responsibility to externally impose regulatory mandates, albeit constrained as, “policy always follows technology” (Int#4). Regulators describe the challenge of keeping pace with technology as, “overwhelming, as cryptocurrencies, stablecoins and NFT’s now make up the crypto ecosystem” (Int#2). Interviewees describe the regulatory environment as “constantly evolving” (Int#1). Regulations and restrictions vary depending on operations, such as payments, investments, derivatives, and tax status. Compliance officers claim traditional regulatory approaches “need to be fit for purpose” (Int#3) to oversee today’s digital assets, and suggest, “it is in the public interest that governments’ modify the existing regulatory framework with all agencies” (Int#4).

Interview data reveal regulator concerns about the complex structure of crypto markets. A regulator comments, “We need to work out how we define cryptocurrency as there is a lot of debate about whether it is a security or commodity. Turf wars exist as the SEC regulates securities and the CFTC, commodities.” He continues, “We also hear a lot about DeFi, as of intermediation in crypto markets, but the need for regulation (Int#1).

Regulators describe DeFi as an emerging phenomenon mainly for speculation, investment, and arbitrage (in crypto-assets). The Head of Compliance at one firm said, “DeFi is about creating a less regulated financial system which reduces the profits of centralized intermediaries, like my bank. But for it to be sustainable it will need to put in place AML [Anti Money Laundering] and KYC [Know Your Customer] policies” (Int#5).

The internalization of different regulatory approaches by innovators arises from coercive pressures from regulators, and normative influences on how market participants interpret oversight mandates. The crypto-assets regulatory environment is constantly shifting as policy proposals are polarized by self-regulation (principles-based) and command-and-control (rules-based). Interview data from innovators reveals mixed views about the role and purpose of regulatory approaches.

A respondent from a FinTech firm said, “There is a strong lobby in the US to reduce the level of crypto regulation. Personally, I am in favour of regulating this new digital asset class because I see systemic and technical problems which remind me of the dotcom era. A lot of crypto action is just empty speculation with some people making money and others losing vast amounts” (Inn#1). Another interviewee from a FinTech firm, comments, “The move to digital money is inevitable. Crypto is part of an ideological movement that rejects traditional financial institutions. But when more people realise that crypto is not an easy way to getrich-quick, and they lose their money, there will be an industry shake-out and I suspect the control of cryptocurrency will be centralized” (Inn#3).

Influencers occupy an important position since they interpret external policy mandates, often expressing diverse political and ideological positions. They are extremely active on social media creating relentless publicity about the role of innovation in market disruption. The hype over exchange tokens is described as “an ideology about social change”, which encourages people to “trust in technology rather than government” (Inf#3). Influencers play a vital role in shifting crypto-asset markets and valuations, both positively and negatively. Interviews show the market responds strongly to news events and items on the legal status and regulation of exchange tokens.

Crypto-asset influencers make money in different ways from sponsorships to promoting their own educational programs. Success is measured by their track record and by their ability to attract large audiences (e.g., Instagram, Twitter, YouTube, TikTok). A crypto market influencer said, “Following the collapse of some crypto valuations, reports about cryptocurrency are now more balanced. People are beginning to question some of the hype, particularly as the press has reported some serious scams” (Inf#5). Another comment came from a technology journalist who said, “Many of the big names in crypto reporting are themselves crypto entrepreneurs. They have a vested interest to hype the [crypto] market but they also want to protect their reputation as people with sound opinions” (Inf#1).

Investors, a heterogeneous stakeholder group, show wide diversity in financial literacy. This is shown in the following two comments: “I know many people my age (30 years) who think crypto will make them lots of money. I follow crypto pundits on social media, and I’ve become much more cautious about investing”. (Inv#7). “I know crypto is speculative investing, but people are making money from it, so that’s why I’ve bought some” (Inv#1). Interviews with retail investors point to a shift in ideological positions about investment, with large institutions losing trust and, dynamic crypto start-ups promoting an alternative ideology about unrestricted innovation and freedom of choice. Regulation of crypto-assets is viewed as inevitable, but secondary to the anticipated opportunities from DeFI. Thus, “I know the crypto market is very volatile, but other asset classes go up and down so I’m not too worried. You must believe in the underlying technology, which I do.” (Inv#2).

## Crypto-assets as Inchoate Technology

The dynamics between crypto-assets and inchoate digital technology is not a linear trajectory as it promises to democratize finance and broaden citizens’ financial access (Makarov and Schoar, 2022). BTC, developed for DeFi was built on three principles, each of which are incompatible with functionality and pose a challenge to regulators. First, it comprises decentralized architectures, with DLT maintained on multiple computers and synchronised in real time. Second, the validation of transactions is through public consensus mechanisms and not institutional intermediaries (banks, brokers). Third, governance and oversight are embedded in algorithmic code which presumes system maintenance, integrity, and security (Hassan and De Filippi, 2017). However, the heterogeneity of crypto-assets manifest in varying levels of inchoateness. Asset referenced tokens that work well as mediums of exchange are increasingly centralized (Prasad, 2021). Some stablecoins are backed by reserves of fiat currencies, issued by a private party that validates transactions and keeps centralized accounts, and with operating rules set by the issuer. Others such as algorithmic tokens managed by their issuers, fluctuate widely in their valuation. Regulating crypto-assets is more difficult, in part, because of the inchoateness of the technology.

The implications for stakeholder engagement with DeFi given such technological complexity are significant. To a large extent, the notion of decentralization is mis-leading, where the removal of trusted intermediaries becomes unfulfilled (Bakos et al., 2021). The crypto-asset ecosystem comprises a limited number of aggregated, geo-located mining pools that control the process of validating transactions and mining new coins (Shi, 2016). Just two blockchains BTC and Ethereum (ETH) consume most of the global crypto-asset electricity used (The White House, 2022c). Currently over 70% of all BTC and ETH mined occurs in just three countries (USA, 38%; China, 21%; and Kazakhstan, 13%) (CBECI, 2022). Due to high equipment and energy costs, a 51% attack on BTC has yet to happen, but such concentration increases the probability of a majority attack on other blockchains with less computational power (Aponte-Novoa et al., 2022). Ethereum Classic (ETC) reported three 51% attacks in August 2020 (Voell, 2020).

The promise of DeFi to create new financial products and services, broadening stakeholder access and undercutting traditional financial intermediaries is open to debate. The implications for different stakeholder groups are significant. Established institutions are coopting new technologies. Permissioned blockchains controlled by one or a small group of institutions are re-shaping traditional finance. Power will be concentrated in fewer institutions and limit competition.

Much of DeFi involves fully or even over-collateralised financial instruments, that maintain trust and financial stability, without increasing access among the wider population. By investing in unregulated crypto-assets, the unsophisticated investor, a vulnerable stakeholder group, places their trust in unproven technology, without adhering to (or unwittingly overlooking) government warnings about the risks involved. Such behaviour reinforces the narrative of trust in technology over trust in government institutions, leaving the investor little protection, potentially risking heavy losses from crypto-asset investments.

Regulators face the challenge to evaluate the risks of decentralization without increasing the concentration of power among a few institutional players. The inchoateness of technology embedded in DeFi products and services limits regulatory oversight since traditional centralized regulation is less relevant for a decentralized crypto-asset ecosystem. An interviewee opined, “It is difficult for regulators to define policy when they don’t understand the technology behind crypto” (Inv#6). Light touch regulation driven by community interest advocates self-correcting mechanisms (under the assumption the products are safe and secure) although it does not safeguard investors.

In the current legal and regulatory environment, questions arise about whether it is possible to regulate an inchoate, distributed network in a centralized environment. Decentralized permissionless blockchain-based crypto-assets are antithetical to the existing centralized structure of monetary and financial regulation. By shifting the policy debate from whether – to - how to regulate, findings support a policy for a decentralized regulatory architecture using industry intermediaries (existing and emerging). Instead of regulating technology, or the blockchains at the protocol layer, regulatory policy would target the applications and usecases of crypto-assets. Implementation of this strategy is through directing the policy edicts and interdictions to intermediaries, with enforcement by credible market participants and gatekeepers (banks, payment service providers and exchanges). In the crypto ecosystem, emerging participants, (large and centralized node operators and miners) will oversee the functions of traditional gatekeepers (Nabilou, 2019). The decentralization of regulation will thus produce its own sui generis risk profile.

The loss of trust in government agencies and banking institutions post the 2008 financial crisis is rehearsed in public and academic discourse on privacy, security, and surveillance. With crypto-assets and blockchains, the assumption that trust is instantiated in the digital ecosystem and its components, challenges existing regulatory ideologies and social norms. In the light of serious fraudulent and unethical practices (scams, rug pulls, pump and dump) the inevitable breakdown of distributed trust adds further pressure on regulators to act (Chainalysis, 2021). Accordingly, the solution to these challenges directs attention to the areas crypto-assets are designed to circumvent, or even replace. i.e., regulation, law, and governance (Werbach, 2018).

## Regulatory Intervention for Crypto-assets

Regulation of crypto-assets is underpinned by: (i) command-and-control ex-ante rules-based or, (ii) neoclassical, laissez-faire, ex-post principles-based (Black, 2008). A dilemma facing regulators arises as crypto-assets are beset by definitional problems. Regulators and industry influencers debate whether an exchange token is a security or commodity, or some other form of asset class. Interventionist policies that impose strict rules and sanctions on crypto firms (who fail to comply with regulatory mandates) are not thought to be more effective than selfenforceable policies designed to meet goals underpinned by ethical principles and practices. Findings reveal stakeholder confusion about regulatory options. The US government claims crypto-assets present, “a significant detection problem by facilitating illegal activity broadly including tax evasion.” (US Department of the Treasury, 2021) and “firms that hold customer assets should be required to ensure those assets are not lost, stolen, or used without the customer’s permission,” (Yellen, 2022).

Political pressure on regulators to design and implement either rules or principles-based policies is undermined by information asymmetry between policy makers and crypto firms. Cognizant of this problem, policy-makers are aware the desirability of the goal generates a normative impetus for regulatory intervention, despite little guarantee of a successful policy outcome. The emphasis on the end game, rather than on how, and to what extent it is implemented, risks producing mixed results, or unintended outcomes, misaligned with the regulatory goal of investor protection.

A practical obstacle to choosing either regulatory approach is the heterogeneity of the crypto ecosystem (not limited to technologies, protocols, stakeholders, governance agendas, pressure groups, and jurisdictions). Parallels are drawn with the 2008 global financial crisis. Prior to the crisis, US regulators favoured principles-based regulation (Currie and Seddon, 2022). Financial innovation in securitization (e.g., collateralized debt obligations) was viewed positively. Post-crisis, these products were seen as major contributors to the financial crisis (Zetzsche et al., 2017). The interconnectivity of the global financial system was a further factor in undermining regulatory oversight (FCIC, 2011).

The rhetoric of principles-based regulation derives from the narrative of a “utopian world” (Black, 2008). Regulation is targeted, focused, and harmonized, across multiple jurisdictions. New financial asset classes are expected to flourish as innovators are given the flexibility and freedom to run their business without over-zealous or intrusive government intervention. This study shows crypto influencers adopt similar rhetoric, with strongly held views that, “overregulation stifles innovation” (Inf#2). Rejecting this narrative, political pressure on regulatory agencies demands more stringent oversight policies to reign-in “the bad guys” (Int#2) (e.g., crypto firms that operate outside existing regulatory laws). In sum, diverse stakeholder groups ask government to take a “truly responsible approach to technological innovation” to protect individuals from “predatory finance, fraud, and systematic economic risks” (Chipolina, 2022).

## Institutional Maintenance or Social Change

Findings reveal wide diversity in social norms held by the four stakeholder groups. Societal and institutional pressures for maintaining or challenging social norms depend on multiple factors. Interventionists recognize the need for new regulatory policies to maintain the protection of citizens from “disruptive innovation” (Int#3). Crypto-assets challenge social norms of AML and ${ \mathrm { K Y C } } ,$ fundamental to regulatory oversight. Regulators face a balancing act of promoting innovation while implementing policies to sanction those who break the rules. The current regulatory climate poses additional problems for regulators as the crypto market comprises many moving parts. Ex-ante policy directives are preferred, but difficult to design and implement as regulators grapple with the complexities of crypto’s inchoateness. Ex-post mandates thus become the de facto norm.

Policies which apply heavy penalties after regulatory rule infringement, play a symbolic role as the regulator is actively seen to address the problem. The underlying technological obfuscations that mask functional weaknesses of crypto-assets, however, are less obvious to regulators. Accordingly, the question remains, how do you regulate inchoate technology that you do not fully understand?

A powerful social norm that undermines the crypto-asset regulatory policy agenda is how innovation is venerated in society. Supported by narratives that crypto-assets increase financial inclusion (e.g., for the unbanked) and data transparency (e.g., for investors) innovation rhetoric obfuscates the cautionary warnings from government agencies, central banks, and some sections of the media. Such warnings are used to increase financial literacy among citizens about the difference between fiat currencies (issued and guaranteed by the state) and cryptocurrencies (which are highly volatile and frequently operated by unregulated firms). Unlike sovereign currency, cryptocurrencies lack key attributes such as monetary policy to back and stabilize token values. Networks have no obligations and prevailing legal cross-jurisdiction is complex (Blundell-Wignall, 2014). Despite this, confidence in exchange tokens is strong, particularly among first time financial market investors, many of whom are young people (Generation Z) with no memory of the 2008 financial crisis.

On 29 August 2022, The Federal Reserve announced the launch of FedNow, a service provided to financial institutions allowing their customers to securely and instantly, send and receive payments (The Federal Reserve, 2022a). Unlike e-CNY, a digital version of the renminbi being developed in China, FedNow is not presented as a CDBC. The Federal Reserve Governor has said this “…will help transform the way payments are made through new services that allow consumers and businesses to make payments conveniently, in real time, on any day, and with immediate availability of funds for receivers” (The Federal Reserve, 2022b). The need for financial regulation is more important now than ever before as FinTech drives product transformation. Today, there is no decentralized regulatory solution,

To summarize, the erosion of public trust following the 2008 financial crisis, coupled with the promise of new investment opportunities in untested, inchoate digital assets, is ideologically supported by pro-innovation and techno-libertarian rhetoric, which elevates trust in technology above trust in institutions (e.g., banks and government agencies). Lessons from past regulatory failures offer only partial solutions in the crypto ecosystem, with unforeseen regulatory challenges likely to generate new risks for investors and the public at large. Terminology in the crypto-ecosystem, specifically, assets, cryptocurrency, coins, tokens, digital investments, all give the illusion of the brave new world of the DeFi miracle.

The fallout from the crypto winter of 2022, however, tarnishes the miracle of get-rich-quick

schemes from crypto-assets, leaving stakeholders with nothing more than a mirage.
