## A R T I C L E I N F O

Keywords: Artificial intelligence Data Technology and organizing Technology implementatior

## A B S T R A C T

While the self-learning nature of AI systems that use machine learning calls for sustained cocreation between developers and users during development, implementation and use, informa tion systems and management scholars still largely build on a long-established tradition of separating technology development from use. Instead, the self-learning nature of AI calls for letting go of this tradition to separate between development and use, which is starting to happen in practice but has not yet found appropriate theoretical and methodological tools among re searchers. In this paper we show some real-life attempts to develop sustained collaboration among developers and users, based on empirical cases of five organizations. In particular, we propose how blurring boundaries makes data production, explainable AI and AI deployment fields of practice where development and use intertwine. We suggest embracing the blurred boundaries of AI implementation in our theorizing, understanding the different parts of AI as fields of practice where development and use come together in the co-creation of AI and work.

## 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to a field in computer science concerned with creating systems that can accomplish tasks that normally require human intelligence (Nilsson, 1982). Recent technological developments – i.e., increasing datafication and computing power – have made mainstream AI implementation possible in organizations. Workers are therefore increasingly confronted with AI systems that can perform knowledge-intensive tasks previously left to humans. Accordingly, a large number of scholars have turned their attention towards the potential organizational impacts of AI systems (e.g., Faraj, Pachidi, & Sayegh, 2018; Gal, Jensen, & Stein, 2020; Glaser, Pollock, & D’Adderio, 2021; Kellogg, Valentine, & Christin, 2020; Von Krogh, 2018).

What is unique about AI systems is that they use learning algorithms, which can autonomously make connections between large amounts of data points and adjust their parameters accordingly (also referred to as the self-learning nature of AI). Self-learning is a continuous process, which makes AI systems inherently dynamic and changing, depending on the data that is “fed” to them. This means that the ability to self-learn makes AI systems never fully “developed” and it thus becomes difficult to determine where “development” turns into “use”. Yet, it is this distinction that existing research on technology and organizing largely depends on, with scholars mostly focusing on either development or use (Bailey & Barley, 2020; Leonardi, 2009).

In this paper, we unpack how, in the case of self-learning AI systems, it becomes a necessity to theorize beyond the traditiona boundaries of development and use. More specifically, building on empirical examples of five different cases, we put forward that, in the case of AI implementation, fields of practice emerge where development and use intertwine. We end the paper by reflecting on the theoretical and methodological future of studying AI implementation when boundaries between development and use become increasingly blurred.

## 2. From developers and users as coexisting, to co-creating AI systems

In recent years, AI systems have been increasingly implemented in organizations. We now see tools that, for example, help radi ologists detect tumors that are sometimes invisible to the human eye (Aerts, 2018; Kim, Koopmanschap, Mehrizi, Huysman, & Ran schaert, 2021) or that provide legal support by digging through lengthy documents and pinpointing where specific information can be found (Zhang, Nandhakumar, Hummel, & Waardenburg, 2020). Because of the prevalence and potential of AI systems in organiza tional settings, information systems and management scholars have become increasingly interested in how these tools are developed and implemented, and with what consequences for work and organizing (e.g., Benbya, Pachidi, & Jarvenpaa, 2021; Faraj et al., 2018; Gal et al., 2020; Kellogg et al., 2020; Van den Broek, Sergeeva, & Huysman, 2021; Von Krogh, 2018; Waardenburg, Huysman, & Sergeeva, 2022). Specifically, scholars are intrigued by AI systems' unique capability to self-learn and consider them to be a funda mentally new phenomenon because of this. While the ability to self-learn makes AI systems inherently dynamic and never fully “developed”, the study of these tools still largely builds on a long-established research tradition of separating technology development and use that hampers a full understanding of the organizational consequences of technologies that continue to learn

The scholarly tradition to separate the two “sides” started with the specialization of more technical occupations around the midtwentieth century (Barley & Bailey, 2020). Most of the work studied before that time was industrial and physical, such as construction work and mine work, but the emergence of computers called for more technical occupations. As “technology developers” created and used increasingly sophisticated tools, they became more specialized and established their own technical “language” and shared practices. Understanding the world of technology developers therefore became a research field of its own and created a distinction between studies focusing on either technology development or on use (Barley & Bailey, 2020). Continuing this separation tradition, most current AI research, in management and information systems, looks either at the activities that go into creating AI systems (e.g., Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Agrawal, Gans, & Goldfarb, 2018; Domingos, 2015; Siegel, 2013), or the organizational consequences of implementing them (e.g., Balasubramanian, Ye, & Xu, 2020; Christin, 2020; Curchod, Patriotta, Cohen, & Neysen, 2020; Jarrahi, 2019; Jarvenpaa & Valikangas, ¨ 2020; Kellogg et al., 2020). These studies consider the groups of actors involved as coexisting; their activities are studied as taking place largely independent of each other, remaining within the realm of expertise of each group, often in a consequential order, influencing either the development, or the implementation and use of the tool (Glaser, Pollock, & D’Adderio, 2021).

However, the perspective of coexistence falls victim to critiques against separation – also known as duality of technology (Orli kowski, 1992) or failure to cross the “implementation line” (Leonardi, 2009). Scholars using such critiques argue against the assumption that, both empirically and theoretically, a line can be drawn that separates technology development and its organizational consequences. They claim that maintaining such an artificial line is problematic, as it hinders scholars to see and unpack the sociotechnical processes involved. such as how a technology is made to fit the intended user context and with what (unintended) consequences for both the users and the tool (e.g., Orlikowski, 1992). Studies adopting these critiques typically ask scholars to “more specifically engage with technology [when theorizing] new forms of organizing” (Faraj & Pachidi, 2021, p. 1).

Specifically focusing on AI, this argument has recently been taken up by scholars in the field of work and technology (e.g., Bailey & Barley, 2020; Barley & Beane, 2020; Faraj & Pachidi, 2021) and in recent special issues in information systems and management journals - e.g., MIS Quarterly (Vol. 45, 2021), Journal of the Association for Information Systems (Vol. 22, 2021), and Organization Science (Vol. 33, 2022). For example, Bailey and Barley (2020) point out that organizational scholars interested in “intelligent technologies” (such as AI systems) have been largely preoccupied with unpacking the implications of these “solely situated, contextual, and emergent” (p. 2) tools, yet leaving out those who are involved in their development. They emphasize that the problem with keeping this divide is that, as these algorithms start to learn after they are initially coded, understanding and influencing their design and intent becomes impossible by the time AI systems are implemented in practice (Bailey & Barley, 2020).

Scholars that aim to cross the implementation line move away from considering different groups of actors as coexisting, and instead view them as co-creating AI systems (e.g., Holmstrom ¨ & Hallgren, ¨ 2021; Lebovitz, Levina, & Lifshitz-Assaf, 2021; Makarius, Mukherjee, Fox and Fox, 2020; Van den Broek et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020). In other words, instead of perceiving the activities of different groups of actors as existing next to each other, studies taking a co-creating perspective consider developer and user activities to be mutually dependent on each other. For example, to create AI systems tailored to the user context requires developers and users to collaborate by mutually learning about each other's expertise, often already early in the development phase (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021; Van den Broek et al., 2021).

Importantly, the co-creating perspective emphasizes that developers and users need to constantly share technical and work-related knowledge that requires them to continuously cross their boundaries. In doing so, studies focusing on co-creation offer important theoretical and practical insights into the complexity and “breathtaking array of possibilities and requirements for managing AI”, of which we have currently only uncovered the “tip of the iceberg” (Berente, Gu, Recker, & Santhanam, 2021; p. 1445). To move forward our research and theorizing of AI implementation, we argue that new fields of practice emerge where boundaries between developers and users dissolve. To understand why this is the case, we now first turn towards the specifics of AI systems.

## 3. AI and the limitations of traditional boundaries

What scholars have understood as AI has changed over time but, nowadays, AI systems are commonly defined by the use of learning algorithms that can autonomously adjust their parameters to generate the most optimal decisions, classifications, or predictions (Fara et al., 2018). What differentiates AI systems from other “intelligent technologies”, such as knowledge management systems, is that learning algorithms can “self-learn” by using different types of machine learning (ML) techniques (i.e., supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement). By using such techniques, these tools do not follow pre-programmed, rule-based patterns (e.g., by following a decision tree) but combine large datasets with advanced computational methods to autonomously find patterns and to adjust their settings to find better outcomes over time (Bryniolfsson & McAfee. 2017: Burrell. 2016: Davenport. 2018). For example. to teach an AI system to play various Atari games, developers used reinforcement learning and let the learning algorithm figure out by itself how to play these games. Without receiving any specific instructions, the algorithm learned to play several different games and was particularly good at playing Pong (a game where you have to remove a virtual brick wall by using a ball and a bat). Looking back at the playing history, the algorithm started off badly and missed the ball nearly every time.<sup>1</sup> Yet, slowly, the system became better, and it eventually developed new strategies to optimize the game results, obtaining the highest number of points with the least number of hits.

When management and information systems scholars study AI systems, they typically consider these tools to consist of three parts: inputs, processes, and outputs (Von Krogh, 2018). Inputs include the data that is used to train learning algorithms and the knowledge that is required to, for example, construct, clean, and prepare data sets. Processes refer to the autonomous learning and adiusting that happens after the initial algorithm has been coded. Outputs are the outcomes (i.e., decisions, classifications, predictions) generated by the learning algorithm, which can be adopted in practice, and which are also often used as new data points to further develop the AI system (Von Krogh, 2018; Waardenburg et al., 2022). Below, for each of the three parts, we briefly discuss the most significant challenge as discussed in current literature, typically located on either the developer or the user side.

First, studies on inputs and their challenges usually address the content of data by unpacking what is being captured and which decisions are made with regards to the production of datasets. Since the algorithms used for AI systems learn by continuously looking for patterns in data, one of the key requirements for the development of AI systems is the availability of comprehensive datasets (Brynjolfsson & McAfee, 2014; Davenport & Harris, 2017). As “information technology has become increasingly efficient at capturing and storing task-related data across the organization” (Von Krogh, 2018, p. 404), scholars emphasize that the size and reach of datasets has grown to include more and more data points from a variety of (sometimes unexpected) sources (Newell & Marabelli, 2015). For example, Brayne (2017) describes how the Los Angeles Police Department included data from pizza chains, e.g., Papa Johns and Pizza Hut, into their datasets to get more extensive contact information such as personal phone numbers of suspects

While it is typically claimed that the bigger the datasets, the better the opportunities for uncovering new patterns or relations between existing phenomena and the faster a learning algorithm can develop or improve, some scholars are becoming increasingl critical towards this perspective (Boyd & Crawford, 2012). For example, they argue that the higher the number of sources, the more difficult it will be to integrate them into one dataset (Brayne, 2017), and emphasize that not just the quantity but also the quality of data can have fundamental consequences for the functioning of AI systems (Lebovitz et al., 2021). Moreover, scholars bring forward ethical considerations (e.g., Crawford & Schultz, 2014; Lyon, 2014; Mai, 2016; Tene & Polonetsky, 2013; Van Dijck, 2014). For example, they question the motives for data collection and the kind of data collected and used (e.g., Boyd & Crawford, 2012; Brayne, 2017; Zuboff, 2019), and they criticize the objectivity of data by emphasizing that categorization is dependent on human judgment and will therefore never represent reality (e.g., Barocas & Selbst, 2016; Elish & Boyd, 2018; Gitelman, 2013; O'Neil, 2016; Pine & Liboiron, 2015; Vad Karsten, 2020). Being critical or not, most studies regarding the inputs of AI take the dataset as their starting point to unpack how they are constructed and cleaned. They do not pay much attention to how organizations can or should prepare for the need to produce data, thereby leaving the topic of how data sets are constructed largely in the realm of developers (Parmiggiani, Østerlie, & Almklov, 2022).

Second, existing research generally approaches the ML processes of AI systems as a technical topic that can only be dealt with by developers. There are many different methods to achieve ML, ranging from relatively straight-forward logistic regressions to complex, deep-learning, neural networks. The decision for the use of one of these techniques is initially made by developers, but one of the difficulties of ML is that it becomes very hard for humans to understand or follow how its code adiusts over time and how the system thus arrives at its conclusions – also called the “machine reasoning” process (e.g., Anthony, 2021; Burrell, 2016; Christin, 2020; Zhang, Yoo, Lyytinen, & Lindberg, 2021). The code of learning algorithms, and how it changes over time, is therefore described as a “black box”, which is closed off or “opaque” to the users (Ajunwa, 2020; Introna, 2016; Pasquale, 2015), and in the case of more advanced learning algorithms, such as neural networks, also to the developers (Faraj et al., 2018; Lindebaum & Ashraf, 2021; O'Neil, 2016; Zhang et al., 2021).

The black-boxed nature of learning algorithms is a particularly hot topic in computer science, where the aim is to find technical solutions to make learning algorithms more transparent. This is often referred to as obtaining “technical explainability”, which means finding automated ways to make model properties and decision factors visible (e g Barredo Arrieta et al 2020: Doran, Schulz, & Besold, 2017). There are also some scholars (including some computer scientists) who argue that technical explainability is infeasible (e.g., Ananny & Crawford, 2018; Gillespie, 2014). Most of the time, these scholars suggest that a better (technical) solution would be to avoid the use of machine learning altogether in cases where the application of AI systems can have fundamental individual or societa

consequences (Burrell, 2016).

Finally, scholars mainly take a user-centric perspective on the outputs of AI and focus on their consequences for work and organizing. Ultimately, combining large amounts of data with the ability to autonomously create connections and find patterns be tween data points enables AI systems to arrive at insights that potentially transcend or augment human knowledge (Agarwal & Dhar, 2014; Agrawal et al., 2018; Davenport & Kirby, 2016; Leavitt, Schrabram, Hariharan, & Barnes, 2020). Because of this, some scholars claim that AI systems have the potential to either take over or to enhance human knowledge-intensive tasks, such as image recognition or making predictions (Domingos, 2015; Haesevoets, De Cremer, Dierckx, & Van Hiel, 2021; Leavitt et al., 2020). The current scholarly debate therefore largely focuses on the consequences of AI systems for its users (e.g., Manyika et al., 2017; Marton ´ & Ekbia, 2021). For example, some scholars claim that it is best to “stop training radiologists now” (Mukherjee, 2017, p. 12), since learning algorithms are becoming better at recognizing malignancies and do this with greater accuracy than human radiologists. Others argue that the ability for AI systems to perform knowledge-intensive tasks does not, by definition, mean that these systems are taking over work or that the insights are dutifully incorporated into the decision-making processes (e.g., Brayne & Christin, 2021). Yet others claim that AI systems can also be used to automate simple and routine processes in more efficient, effective, or objective ways (Raisch & Krakowski, 2021), which leaves room for domain experts to engage in more meaningful activities, such as personal contact with clients or more intel lectually challenging and knowledge-intensive tasks (e.g., Makarius, Mukherjee, Fox, & Fox, 2020; Murray, Rhymer, & Sirmon, 2021).

Also, some scholars take a broader perspective on the consequences of AI and argue that the work that is affected by the imple mentation of AI systems is not always that of the primary “targets” (e.g., Baptista, Stein, Klein, Watson-Manheim, & Lee, 2020). They argue that the consequences of AI systems are usually more widespread and unintended due to the collective and practice-based nature of knowledge, meaning that when one, relatively small, aspect of knowledge-intensive work changes – e.g., by having an AI system offer simple suggestions – large “ripple effects” can occur (Baptista et al., 2020; Orlikowski & Scott, 2016). For instance, Sergeeva, Faraj, and Huysman (2020) show how the introduction of a surgical robot had consequences that went beyond the work of surgeons, as it reconfigured the complete surgical team, transforming their coordination mechanisms and changing existing occupational roles and relationships. While existing research on the outputs of AI offers important insights into the complex and widespread nature of its consequences, what scholars overlook when taking a user-centric perspective is the dynamic, self-learning nature of AI. To improve their fit, learning algorithms need input data from the context in which they are used, reflecting the unique capability of AI systems to learn and adjust their code based on the context in which they have been implemented. Even though the initial code might thus be the same, each learning algorithm becomes different over time, depending on the input data of its user context, which makes the user context inherently intertwined with the further development of the tool

In sum, the ability to use new inputs to adjust and improve the initial coding over time and generate better outputs – i.e., the selflearning capability – implies that, more than for other intelligent technologies, the inputs, processes, and outputs of AI systems need to be intertwined and are continuously adiusting. This makes it increasingly difficult to determine when development turns into use Therefore, the development, implementation, and use of AL is a continuous process of co-creation that consists of a mess of activities that blur traditional boundaries between developers and users. As mentioned above, recent studies have emphasized that a prereq uisite for co-creating AI systems is that developers and users share their, respectively, technical and work-related knowledge (Lebovitz et al., 2021: Van den Broek et al.. 2021). However, scholars taking a knowledge-in-practice perspective have extensively argued that sharing such knowledge is not easy or straightforward, as most knowledge is implicit and embedded in practices (e.g., Brown & Duguid, 2001; Carlile, 2004; Cook & Brown, 1999; Tsoukas, 2003). To understand how knowledge can be shared, these scholars have identified and studied “fields of practice” (e.g., Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Lave, 1988) in which actors that belong to the same community can share practices to act knowledgeably in a specific context (Levina & Vaast, 2005). For example, orthopedic surgeons belong to the community of medical doctors and can engage in the field of practice of orthopedic surgery. Similarly, AI developers traditionally belong to the community of computer scientists and engage in the field of practice of AI development. Fields of practice are dynamic, meaning that when members of different communities translate knowledge across boundaries – also referred to as “boundary spanning” (Carlile, 2002, 2004; Dougherty, 1992) – and engage in new shared practices, new fields of practice can emerge that allow for knowledge to be shared between communities (Levina & Vaast, 2005). The emergence of new fields of practice is particularly interesting in the case of AI co-creation, where traditional boundaries between development and use dissolve. Below, we use examples from five empirical cases to dive into what such new fields of practice would look like.

## 4. Three emerging fields of practice

For the past five years, we have been studying the development, implementation, and use of AI systems in a variety of organizations (Kim et al., 2021; Van den Broek et al., 2021; Waardenburg et al., 2022; Waardenburg, Huysman, & Agterberg, 2021). By frequently engaging with organizations for multiple years, we observed how shared practices were established in which boundaries were blurring and fields of practice were emerging. This made us realize that unpacking the consequences of AI systems for work and organizing requires a shift in how we think about, and study, the separation between technology development and use. Below, we use examples from our published and unpublished research of five of our cases (see Table 1) that are illustrative of letting go of the traditiona distinction. We group the insights around the three commonly known parts of AI we discussed above: (1) inputs, (2) processes, and (3) outputs – and propose to unpack these parts as fields of practice in which traditional distinctions dissolve and new practices emerge.

## 4.1. Inputs: Data production as a field of practice

There are various activities and actors involved in the production of data, often performed by actors who are traditionally considered as outside of the realm of technology development. MultiCo, one of the largest fast-moving consumer goods organizations, shows how performing data production activities takes users outside of their traditional fields and moves them into the development ot AI systems. While the company hired an external technology developer (NeuroYou) to create an AI system for recruitment purposes, specifically targeting the first round of the talent program selection process, over time many other organizational actors became involved. For the AI system to produce a prediction about the personality fit of the candidate, applicants needed to play fifteen online games through which traits such as concentration, emotional intelligence, and leadership qualities are assessed. While MultiCo hired an external developer, located overseas, to create the AI system, a key part of the development of the tool actually happened at the headquarters of MultiCo. This was because the algorithm had to be trained using data that included traits of MultiCo's employees to make accurate and organization-specific predictions. This type of data was not yet available, so 300 employees were asked to play the same online games that the candidates would have to perform. In addition, to create a “best performance” model, the developers asked the HR-professionals to provide performance data of each of these 300 employees to NeuroYou, data that was not ready yet and called for an active engagement of HR in developing data as input for the AI tool.

Blurring boundaries for data production also has implications for the expertise required of those involved in the production of data. For example, to prevent the data collection process from harming privacy and security, the HR-professionals at MultiCo had to learn more about data and statistics. They therefore took courses about topics such as sample selection, data quantity and quality, and data legislation, e.g., the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This newly acquired expertise enabled the HR-professionals to regularly give necessary feedback to the developers about the pitfalls and shortcomings of the data they produced. Also, updating algorithms calls for feeding them with new data. When (parts of) this data needs to come from the organization in which the tool is deploved, data production, and thus being part of the development process of AL systems, becomes a sustained, long-term activity for users.

The predictive policing case provides a nice illustration of how the need to constantly produce new data for an algorithm to continue to learn and adjust, required radical adjustments to existing work practices of the police officers. The predictive policing AI system was developed using data about crimes to be predicted (e.g., number of burglaries, area of the crime, method used), as well as demographic and geographic data (e.g., number of inhabitants, average age, average household size). For the algorithm to learn over time, demographic and geographic data were not suitable, because these statistics remained relatively stable over time. To learn, the algorithm thus depended heavily on new crime-related data points. The production of this data impacted the work of patrol officers, because they now had to register in the police database the details of each crime call they went to in the streets to make sure that there was sufficient crime data to further train the AI system. Crime data production thus became a core and ongoing task for patrol officers, making their activities part of the continuous development of the AI system.

Data production thus breaks the boundaries between development and use in the case of AI systems. and while the police case showed that this calls for a shift in the work practices of users, boundaries can also be blurred by establishing new data-related roles that become responsible to produce training data. For example, at the radiology department, an AI system was developed to predict the growth rate of ear tumors. The hospital already had a large dataset with ear tumor scans available, but just having these images at hand turned out not to be enough to train the algorithm. Each tumor had to be identified in the scans by outlining it manually, so that the algorithm could learn to recognize and distinguish images of tumors. To develop the algorithm thus required skills to identify the tumors in the first place and to draw the right contours around them. Clearly, this part of the data production could not be performed by the developers since they were not medically trained to identify tumors in scans. On the other hand, drawing contours around tumors was also a technical skill that radiologists did not have. The hospital therefore created a new role for which they hired people trained in so-called “technical medicine”, an education including both medical technologies and medicine. The members of this socalled “Imaging Services Group” aided the developers and the users by performing both technical and medical practice of data production.

Beyond the data gathering, engineering, and preparation tasks that are commonly performed by AI developers, and data engineers in specific, the need to produce data thus also implies a long-lasting, substantial task for actors that are not commonly associated with technology development. To not overlook the full range of activities involved in the inputs of AI systems when studying AI imple mentation, we therefore propose to understand data production as a field of practice that includes data-related practices of a range of

actors that all become part of the development process of AI.

## 4.2. Processes: Explainable AI as a field of practice

We described above how scholars often consider explainability to be a technical problem that resides in the algorithm. In practice, however, explaining machine reasoning goes beyond uncovering the technical details, towards a more multidisciplinary approach that pays attention to how organizational actors make sense of learning algorithms in practice and how they are explained in the context of organizations (Hafermalz & Huysman, 2022). BankCo provides an example of how developers and users step outside the boundaries of their fields to understand each other's capabilities and needs, e.g., developers aiming to understand what level of technical detail users appreciate and users getting sufficient understanding of ML methods. BankCo developed an AI system to classify potential money laundering activities with the aim to increase the effectiveness of detecting suspicious transactions. To generate targeted alerts that can be used by financial analysts for further investigation, the developers included both supervised and unsupervised ML techniques. To perform further investigations, the financial analysts needed insights into the reasons why alerts showed up in the first place. Because the ML techniques, with their typically black-boxed nature, were making it too difficult for them to decipher these reasons, the financial analysts approached the developers and asked them to help. After brainstorming about what could be a fitting solution, they together came up with the idea to create a “translation machine". This solution did not give full transparency to the data and model used, as this was both legally infeasible and considered irrelevant by the financial analysts but produced overviews such as the top three indicators of an alert of a top 20 of the most suspicious transactions. These overviews served as explanations for the financia analysts as to why certain activities were classified as potential money laundering. The example of BankCo thus shows how developers and users together become actively involved in the “unboxing” of machine reasoning, finding ways to interpret and translate how the algorithm arrives at insights.

Establishing explanations of machine reasoning that make sense to organizational actors also brings to the fore the role of inte grating technical knowledge and work-related expertise. The case of the recruitment AI system at MultiCo shows that one way in which such knowledge sharing can take place is by creating a common language that eases the sharing of ideas. To make conversations possible with HR-professionals, the developers organized various meetings where they could discuss the terminology used. For example, the HR-professionals initially struggled with the psychometric terms used by the developers to identify specific character traits (terms such as “gregariousness”). The developers were unaware that these terms were complex and non-intuitive to the HRprofessionals and the meetings provided opportunities to unpack these terms and come to a common understanding. In addition, the developers used the time to present and explain the ML techniques they used, which gave them the opportunity to show and discuss different variables that the algorithm designated as “predictive” – that is, the traits and skills that distinguish the successful from the unsuccessful employees – and receive feedback on those variables from the HR-professionals.

The case of MultiCo also provides a good example of how blurring the boundaries between development and use in creating explainable AI can trigger the emergence of new “algorithmic brokerage” roles. These so-called “algorithmic brokers” (Gal et al., 2020; Kellogg et al., 2020) can perform “translation tasks” that help developers and users to become more familiar with each other. At MultiCo. to not put too much demand on HR. the organization eventually designated a so-called “people analytics" team to work as algorithmic brokers and perform translation tasks between the developers and HR. The members of this team were both technically savvy, understanding how the algorithm was constructed, and could understand recruitment work so that they could make the pre dictions about applicants usable for HR. For example, they could help HR understand and contextualize predictions of applicants by comparing them with data about existing high and low performing employees.

Yet, the role of algorithmic brokers can also pose a danger, as their importance and potential influence can easily be overlooked (both in research and in practice). In such cases, instead of dissolving boundaries between development and use, algorithmic brokers can end up solidifying those boundaries and distancing developers from users. For example, in the case of the police, the organization kept their traditionally siloed structure for the development of the predictive policing AI system. This meant that the AI developers and the police stayed within the boundaries of their practice, both depending on a group of “intelligence officers” to act as algorithmic brokers and interpret crime predictions. As neither the developers, nor the users had any understanding of each other's work, the intelligence officers were given a free pass to develop whatever interpretation they felt suited. This eventually led to the intelligence officers bypassing the AI system altogether, without anyone noticing it. Instead of using the predictions generated by the AI system, the intelligence officers provided the police with suggestions based on their own understanding of police work. Because the police were unaware of how the AI system worked, and because the suggestions created by the intelligence officers fit so well within the police context, nobody questioned their sources

Explaining machine reasoning processes is thus as much a technical as it is a social activity that requires developers and users to step outside the boundaries of their practice. While existing research mainly focuses on unpacking the black box of AI systems, we argue that researchers should study explainability as a field of practice, which includes the emergence and consequences of a common language and/or new brokerage roles. Understanding explainability as a field of practice also allows scholars to pay attention to the dangers of new brokerage roles, as unpacking their practices can bring to light that they might inhibit this field to emerge, thereby further black-boxing machine reasoning

## 4.3. Outputs: AI deployment as a field of practice

Finally, the self-learning nature of AI systems calls for a sustained collaboration between developer and user, even when the tool is fully deployed in practice. The case of the police with its use of predictive policing is helpful to understand what happens when such sustained collaboration is not maintained. Here, the data science team built a supervised learning algorithm, using a relatively simple logistic regression analysis which mainly depended on crime data to learn and produce predictions, up to a week in advance, about where and when crime might happen. Police managers then used these outputs for their operational decision-making such as when to schedule patrol officers and where to send them to. When only considering its use, and not how the algorithm further develops, one could say that the tool appeared to be a success; every time the police used a prediction to prevent crime, no crime would happen. However, a couple of weeks later, the same crime rates would surge again, and the prediction would shift back to the same issue. This inconsistency happened because of the inherent intertwinement of the actions of the police and the way the AI system learned, which both the police and the developers were unaware of. Whenever the police put efforts into preventing a specific type of crime, the rates of this crime would go down. Consequently, no new data points would be made about that crime, meaning that for a next round of predictions, other data points were used which shifted the outputs. Because the initial crime issue was typically only prevented shortterm, but not solved long-term, the rates would go up again over time, shifting the AI outputs back to its original prediction. The AI outputs therefore worked as a pendulum, sending the police from pillar to post, instead of creating a long-term and sustained solution. The reason for this was the disconnect between the developers and users after the AI system was deployed in practice. Therefore, the developers were unaware of how the daily police practices influenced the further development of the learning algorithm. This example thus shows that, also after an AI system is deployed, developers cannot stay within the boundaries of their practices but must actively go out and use their knowledge of the everyday work of users to anticipate how these work practices can (unintentionally) influence the learning process and further development of the AI system.

The case of AviaCo. one of the world's largest air transportation organizations. how the deployment of AI can actually become a long-term, sustained solution. In this case, the developers created an AI system for meal consumption prediction on board of aircrafts in close collaboration with its users – the logistics experts of the catering services department. It helped significantly that managers of AviaCo explicitly set as a key requirement that the predictions had to seamlessly fit with the work of the users and not add any additional workload. To better understand the stressful work of the logistics personnel, who typically drove around the airport all day to make sure all planes were ready in time, the AI developers joined them in the field. This activity, that blurred the boundary between the developers and users, not only gave the developers direct insights into the needs and demands of everyday logistics work, in addition it created invaluable connections through which both continued to share their knowledge and (user) experience

In addition, AI deployment is a longitudinal process. In other words, to generate the best outputs, AI system deployment calls for time to train the system and adjust to the context in which they are applied. Users therefore cannot just adopt the outputs in their everyday work practices but will be confronted with tools that need to learn and improve over time. For example, at MultiCo, HR frequently struggled with the predictions generated by the recruitment AI system. This was especially the case when the system predicted a candidate as “successful”, while then being rejected by HR in the following round. The HR managers did not understand why the system did not generate the right outcomes and they sat together with the developers to discuss their issues. During this meeting, the HR managers learned that working with AL systems requires patience, as the developers taught them that vou cannot expect an optimal prediction right away. Instead, after deploying the system, it needs to be fed with new data to learn from the mistakes it will make in the beginning. After this, the HR managers felt more informed about the basics of how AI systems learn over time and better realized the time that was needed from their side. In addition, this made them decide to stay closely involved in the further developments of the tool.

The consequences of AI deployment thus not only apply to the user context, but also feed back into the further development of the tool itself. While existing research mainly looks at the consequences for the users, we have seen that understanding AI deployment calls for analyzing the longitudinal process through which the user practices and the learning processes of algorithms become intertwined.

## 5. Discussion: Digital futures and the study of blurred boundaries

In this paper, we discussed AI systems' unique ability to self-learn, which takes away the traditional separation between devel opment and use. Building on the latest research interest in co-creation of AI and our insights from five different empirical cases, we proposed that new fields of practice emerge, in particular around data production, explainable AI, and AI deployment. In addition, the insights from the empirical cases highlight that maintaining these fields requires sustained, long-term practices that, because of the self-learning capability of AI systems, are inherently intertwined and continuous (see Fig. 1 for a schematic overview). Acknowledging that the boundaries between development and use dissolve in the case of self-learning technologies has implications for how we theorize and study AI implementation.

## 5.1. Next steps in theorizing AI implementation

Traditionally, management and information systems scholars consider practices as linked to a specific community, i.e., as “recurrent, materially bounded, and situated action[s] engaged in by members of a community” (Orlikowski, 2002, p. 256). Together, multiple practices constitute a field that allow members of a community to act knowledgeably in a specific context and makes it possible to set boundaries around who is part of the community and who is not (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Lave, 1988; Levina & Vaast, 2005). Some previous research has argued that new fields of practice can emerge when actors from different communities act as “boundary spanners” and actively engage in translating knowledge from one community to the next (Levina & Vaast, 2005). The emergence of fields of practice in the case of AI builds on and expands this idea, emphasizing that multiple communities (and not just a few representatives acting as boundary spanners) are involved and intertwined in the performance of practices. i.e.. the work of users becomes part of AI development, and the work of developers becomes part of AI usage (we expand upon the new power relations and potential power struggles that this may include below). Studying self-learning AI systems thus requires scholars to move towards understanding a field as a collective of practices performed by actors residing in multiple, different communities.

Unpacking the emergence of fields of practice that consist of actors belonging to different communities builds on and expands recent research interests in the co-creation of AI systems, where scholars emphasize the need for developers and users to respectively share their technical and work-related knowledge (e.g., Lebovitz et al., 2021; Van den Broek et al., 2021). Knowledge-in-practice scholars have for long argued that knowledge – or better, ‘knowing’ – is implicit and embedded in practices, which makes it very difficult to codify and transmit (e.g., Brown & Duguid, 2001; Carlile, 2004; Cook & Brown, 1999; Tsoukas, 2003). Knowing typically “sticks" (Brown & Duguid. 2001) within a group. or community, of actors who share the same practice, expertise, and occupational language (Brown & Duguid, 1998; Carlile, 2004). Knowledge sharing is thus easy between actors belonging to the same community and participating in a similar practice but is hard to share with actors inhabiting different communities. Unpacking the fields of practice that emerge in the co-creation of AI systems thus requires scholars to move beyond understanding developers and users as bounded communities that share domain-specific knowledge across boundaries, towards what Brown and Duguid (1991) referred to as “communities-of-communities” that perform shared practices to co-create new machine learning tools.

Finally, understanding the boundaries between development and use as blurred in fields of practice has important consequences for how we perceive power relations with and around AI systems. Typically, fields are considered as important sites for communities to produce capital – either economic, cultural, social, or symbolic – that can be used as a power base in relation to other communities (Bourdieu & Wacquant, 1992; Levina & Vaast, 2005). In line with this, existing research that kept the separation between developers and users alive argued that actors who associate with (developing) more objective technologies could gain legitimacy and power over others (e,g., Anthony. 2018: Bourgoin. Bencherki and Farai. 2020). Accordingly, many have warned of workers' subordination to learning algorithms (e.g., Noble, 2018; O'Neil, 2016). However, when boundaries blur and both developers and users become increasingly associated with AI systems, such power relations no longer hold, and new power dynamics are likely to emerge. For example, users can make use of their newly gained, more technical knowledge to take charge of the further development of AI systems and influence the decision-making processes around this. We encourage future research to further unpack the power relations involved in the fields of practice of AI, as well as towards other organizational decision-makers.

## 5.2. Methodological implications for studying AI systems in practice

This paper is aimed at furthering existing research on technology and organizing, and specifically AI implementation, by unpacking how the self-learning nature of AI requires scholars to theorize beyond the traditional boundaries of development and use. This also calls for a mirroring approach where researchers themselves let go of their research boundaries and empirically immerse themselves in both the technical world of AI and the work-domain of the users.

We argued that understanding data production implies that researchers need to carefully identify where data is being produced which practices and decisions are involved, and with what consequences. Studying the initial development of AI systems thus not only means the data cleaning and preparation work of AI developers (i.e., studying data-engineering practices), but especially the users intentional and unintentional data construction practices that reside in their daily work, e.g., registering activities after work shifts or the digital traces left when driving around customers. Because initial data production typically occurs before the AI system is created this means studying data work before an initial version of the AI system is developed, let alone implemented.

We also emphasized that explaining machine reasoning is a field of practice that extends beyond making the technical details of the AI system transparent. We discussed that explaining can be facilitated by algorithmic brokers and we believe that such a brokerage role should also be taken on by researchers studying AI and organizing. Recently, Bailey and Barley (2020) argued that studying intelligent technologies like AI systems requires multidisciplinary studies performed by large research teams in which different types of scholarly expertise come together (e.g., engineering, sociology, computer science, management). Such large teams would indeed benefit from the specialized expertise of their various members. Yet. the danger of such distribution of tasks is that difficulties in bridging epis temological differences can hamper a socio-technical analysis. This is not only for reasons of not understanding each other but also because of institutional differences, such as differences in publication regimes among computer scientists and organizational scholars. It would therefore be preferable for a single researcher to have both “technical” knowledge of AI and “social” knowledge about the user context, as this would give researchers the absorptive capacity to identify (during fieldwork) what is important beyond the immediate context. This thus calls for programs to educate socio-technically engaged scholars who can ask the “right” questions (e.g., “Which data work practices are fundamental for developing the AI system?”; “Who are involved in explaining the outcomes of AI?”; “How do the work practices I observe influence the further learning process of the algorithm?”), understand the answers, and observe the most relevant activities at often unexpected places. To encourage socio-technical engagement for scholars, the curricula for (graduate students studying AI in the context of organizations should be extended beyond their disciplinary field, e.g., by including computer science courses in management education, and by including organization science courses in the curricula of computer science.

Finally, since how the outputs of AI are deployed in practice influences the further development of the algorithm, researchers themselves should prepare for a practice-based, and longitudinal endeavor that often requires ethnographic methods to fully capture the complexity of AI implementation. Such ethnographies are highly demanding, since studying the consequences of AI imple mentation will not be fully captured by looking at one individual group of workers or one research site. Instead, researchers must embed themselves in two or more research sites, often at the same time, for a long duration of time, to understand how developers, tools, and organizational actors learn, change, and become intertwined.

## 6. To conclude: Towards engaged scholarship

In this paper, we emphasized that, due to the self-learning nature of AI, it is increasingly impossible both in research and in practice to maintain fixed boundaries between development and use. We have brought forward different instances in which boundaries blur in practice to give a glimpse into what the digital future of organizing for self-learning AI systems will look like when creating helpful, instead of harmful. AI systems. At the same time, we are well aware that, not only in research but also in practice. these boundaries still persist. Such boundaries are particularly problematic in practice, as this means that once the “AI train” has started rolling, it will be difficult to stop its autonomous development and to unpack its increasingly black-boxed reasoning. As Bailey and Barley (2020, p. 10) have pointed out: “by the time these technologies have been adopted and implemented, we will have lost opportunities to influence their design and intent”. This implies that scholars, being aware of the importance of removing boundaries, can no longer be the fly on the wall watching the train derail. Instead, we practice-based researchers need to become “engaged scholars” (Van de Ven, 2007) and connect with the research context, since we are in the best place to argue for and support practitioners in creating the fields of practice necessary for AI to become a sustained part of the organization. In a world where self-learning tools are becoming the norm, we call for scholars to not only study the blurring boundaries in the field but also actively try to blur the boundary that has traditionally been present between us and our fields of study.
