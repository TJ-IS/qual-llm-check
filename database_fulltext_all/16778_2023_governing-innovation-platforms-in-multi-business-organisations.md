# Governing innovation platforms in multi-business organisations

Maximilian Schreieck <sup>a,b</sup>, Manuel Wiesche <sup>c</sup> and Helmut Krcmar <sup>b</sup>

<sup>a</sup>The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, USA; <sup>b</sup>Technical University of Munich, Department of Informatics, Munich, Germany; <sup>c</sup>Chair for Digital Transformation, TU Dortmund University, Dortmund, Germany

## ABSTRACT

Innovation platforms enable companies to create broad and dynamic portfolios of applications, which can be developed internally and externally. An increasing number of established companies from traditional industries have begun to explore the potential of innovation platforms. These companies are often organised as multi-business organisations (MBOs). When introducing innovation platforms, they face tensions between the corporate centre that runs such platforms and business units that develop applications on the platform. Established approaches to information technology (IT) governance in MBOs are inefective in addressing these tensions because they focus on cross-business-unit synergies, rather than on innovation from business units. To explore this issue further, we conducted a longitudinal case study of an automotive manufacturer that introduced an innovation platform in its infotainment system. The company faced four tensions: rigidity, alteration, mistrust, and competition. To address these tensions, the company evolved its IT governance, allowing for more flexibility for business units, supporting their development eforts, and prioritising them over external complementors. The role of the corporate centre evolved from a gatekeeper to an enabler of innovation. Our findings link the literature on IT governance in MBOs with that of platform governance, providing insights into how MBOs benefit from innovation platforms.

## 1. Introduction

An increasing number of established companies across industries have introduced innovation platforms to create flourishing ecosystems of applications (Khanagha et al., 2020; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Sandberg et al., 2020; Sebastian et al., 2017; Svahn et al., 2017). Innovation platforms provide an extensible software core on top of which third parties – also referred to as complementors – create applications (Cusumano et al., 2019; Tiwana et al., 2010). Emulating the success of IT companies such as Google and Apple with their mobile application platforms, a wide range of companies from traditional industries including banks, insurance companies, logistics providers, automotive manufacturers, and equipment manufacturers, seek to establish innovation platforms. For example, Deutsche Bank launched an innovation platform to attract fintech start-ups as providers of innovative applications in 2016<sup>1</sup> while Siemens launched the Siemens Mindsphere platform for applications in the Industrial Internet in 2017.<sup>2</sup>

Establishing innovation platforms is challenging for established companies (Schreieck et al., 2021; Yofie et al., 2019) and, in particular, for those that are organised in the form of multi-business organisations (MBOs). Indeed, many established companies are

MBOs; that is, they comprise a corporate centre and strategic business units, which address specific markets and are granted some autonomy from the corporate centre (Gaba & Joseph, 2013; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Tanriverdi, 2006). To coordinate the corporate centre and business units, MBOs apply information technology (IT) governance, allocating some decision rights to the corporate centre and some to the business units, and setting up an “accountability framework for encouraging desirable behaviours in the use of IT” (Ross et al., 2006, p. 121). In so doing, MBOs balance centralised IT governance (e.g., the corporate centre defining company-wide standards for IT implementation and usage to increase eficiency) and decentralised IT governance (e.g., leeway for business units to adapt and enhance the companies’ IT to best address their customers’ needs). MBOs typically rely on corporate IT platforms – technology assets and services that are shared across business units (Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018, p. 5212; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015) – to leverage synergies across business units and enable business units to run their processes eficiently (Weill & Ross, 2004). Corporate IT platforms are a means to govern the activities of business units, similar to innovation platforms that are a means to govern the activities of external complementors. However, the goal of corporate IT platforms is to leverage cross-businessunit synergies in MBOs (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015), whereas the goal of innovation platforms is to enable bottom-up innovation in the platform ecosystem.

When MBOs introduce innovation platforms, business units are no longer simply users of corporate IT platforms; instead, they can develop innovative applications themselves, potentially alongside external complementors that join the innovation platform. Thus, whilst IT governance must still allocate decision rights and accountability to the corporate centre and business units, it should also support bottom-up innovation from business units. Introducing innovation platforms requires a flexible approach to IT governance that goes beyond the dichotomy of centralisation and decentralisation to consider the dynamic interactions among the corporate centre, business units, and complementors (cf., Pearlson et al., 2016; Schwarz & Hirschheim, 2003; Tiwana et al., 2013). While the extant information systems (IS) literature on platform governance provides insights into ways to enable bottomup innovation on platforms, the focus lies primarily on external complementors. For example, tensions that arise from control mechanisms applied by the platform owner or from competition within the ecosystem have been discussed for external complementors but not for internal business units that develop applications on innovation platforms.

Further insights are needed into the tensions that arise between a corporate centre that is responsible for innovation platforms and business units that develop applications on such platforms. Discussions on how these tensions can be addressed with IT governance are also necessary. Consequently, we pose the following research question: How can MBOs apply IT governance to navigate the tensions between the corporate centre and business units when implementing innovation platforms?

To address this research question, we conduct a longitudinal qualitative case study of an automotive manufacturer that implemented an innovation platform in its infotainment system. The manufacturer introduced a federal IT governance approach which, with the corporate centre acting as a gatekeeper, focused on enforcing central requirements for application development in business units. However, this approach led to four types of tension between the corporate centre and business units: (1) rigidity, (2) alteration, (3) mistrust, and (4) competition tensions. Only when the manufacturer evolved its IT governance approach to a more enabling style were these four tensions resolved (cf., Adler & Borys, 1996). Specifically, the corporate centre granted more flexibility to business units, improved boundary resources, further developed the platform on the basis of feedback from business units, enhanced knowledge sharing among business units, and prioritised internal business units over external complementors.

These findings add to the literature streams of IT governance in MBOs (e.g., Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018; Weill & Broadbent, 1998; Weill & Ross, 2004; Williams & Karahanna, 2013) and of platform governance (e.g., Constantinides et al., 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2014; Tiwana et al., 2010). We argue that IT governance in MBOs must go beyond a rigid structure when addressing the tensions that emerge from implementing innovation platforms. We also show the implications for platform governance when internal business units develop applications on innovation platforms alongside external complementors. Our findings open several avenues for future research, such as how the collaboration between internal and external complementors can best be leveraged, as MBOs expand their innovation platforms.

## 2. Theoretical background

In this study, we analyse how MBOs can implement IT governance to navigate the tensions between a corporate centre and business units when implementing an innovation platform. Before reporting on our empirical study, we review two streams of literature that provide a theoretical foundation for this question: (1) IT governance in MBOs, and (2) platform governance.

## 2.1. IT governance in MBOs

MBOs are composed of strategic business units, which address specific markets and are granted some autonomy from the corporate centre (Gaba & Joseph, 2013; Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Tanriverdi, 2006). To be successful, MBOs must coordinate activities across diferent business units (Tanriverdi, 2006). IT and its governance have emerged as keys to a successful coordination of activities in MBOs (Weill & Ross, 2004).

IT governance should address the inherent tradeof between the centralisation and decentralisation of IT in MBOs (Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018; Williams & Karahanna, 2013). On the one hand, the centralisation of IT across business units helps leverage synergies. An IT infrastructure based on agreedupon standards across business units can minimise company costs and help clearly articulate customer relationships (Boh & Yellin, 2006; Weill & Broadbent, 1998). Beyond IT infrastructure, the centralisation of further IT resources and IT management processes contributes to synergies in MBOs (Tanriverdi, 2006, 2005). On the other hand, the decentralisation of IT ofers business units the flexibility and agility needed to respond to the needs of customers by developing local IT capabilities (Weill & Broadbent, 1998).

Along a continuum from centralised to decentralised IT governance, various archetypes have been identified (Weill & Ross, 2004). On the one extreme, purely centralised IT governance, either led by IT or business executives, is referred to as monarchical IT governance. However, in MBOs, a purely centralised approach jeopardises business units’ competitiveness because they cannot react flexibly to changes in their environment and markets. On the other extreme, purely decentralised IT governance, referred to as feudal IT governance, leads to high costs engendered by duplicated IT infrastructure and processes across business units. To combine the advantages of centralised and decentralised approaches, federal IT governance – also referred to as hybrid IT governance (e.g., Brown, 1997; Brown & Magill, 1994) – has emerged as the most common approach to balancing centralisation and decentralisation in managing IT functions (Sambamurthy & Zmud, 2000; Weill & Ross, 2004).

Federal IT governance can be defined as an approach that centralises parts of the IT infrastructure, processes, and decision rights whilst leaving other parts decentralised at the business-unit level (Boynton & Zmud, 1987; Weill & Ross, 2004). Federal IT governance is supported by corporate IT platforms because they standardise key IT functions across business units whilst making corporate IT capabilities available to business units via shared services (Reynolds & Yetton, 2015; Queiroz, Coltman et al., 2018; cf. “internal platforms” in Gawer, 2014).

However, federal IT governance becomes more challenging as an increasing number of MBOs have implemented innovation platforms or transformed their corporate IT platforms into innovation platforms. From a governance perspective, corporate IT platforms and innovation platforms support the coordination of decentralised entities through a central entity. In the case of corporate IT platforms, the corporate centre coordinates business units; in that of innovation platforms, the platform owner coordinates actors that develop applications on the platforms, typically external complementors. However, the goal in each case is diferent. With innovation platforms, MBOs aim to enable bottom-up innovation by business units and external complementors (Parker et al., 2016; Tiwana, 2014), rather than leveraging synergies across business units and providing corporate IT capabilities to business units.

Consequently, when MBOs implement innovation platforms that are to be populated by internal business units and external complementors, tensions arise between the corporate centre and business units.

Nevertheless, the former must grant the latter enough room to come up with bottom-up innovation. The IT governance of MBOs must go beyond the dichotomy of centralisation and decentralisation of decision rights and accountability (cf., Pearlson et al., 2016; Schwarz & Hirschheim, 2003; Tiwana et al., 2013) to navigate tensions between the corporate centre and business units that relate to innovation platforms. Whilst the literature on federal IT governance has yet to consider innovation platforms, research on platform governance provides helpful insights.

## 2.2. Platform governance

The challenge of governing innovation platforms has been addressed in the IS literature on platform governance (e.g., Constantinides et al., 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Hein et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2014). This literature has focused on innovation platforms as a means to leverage innovation from external complementors (e.g., Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu et al., 2018; Tiwana, 2014) and establish flourishing ecosystems of applications (Shipilov & Gawer, 2019; Tiwana, 2014). Innovation platforms, as the core of the ecosystem, are therefore separated from the applications in the periphery by a decoupling layer (Benlian et al., 2018), the main elements of which are application programming interfaces (APIs) that define a standardised approach for applications to access the software core (e.g., Yoo et al., 2010). Examples of innovation platforms not only include Google’s Android platform for mobile applications and PlayStation as a platform for video gaming but also platforms by established companies, such as Deutsche Bank with an open banking platform for applications related to financial services and Siemens with Mindsphere, an innovation platform for applications in the Industrial Internet. All such platforms enable innovation by external complementors that rely on the platforms’ APIs.

The overarching challenge of platform governance is the trade-of between granting complementors a suficient degree of freedom whilst retaining control of the ecosystem to ensure a desired level of quality of the applications in the ecosystem (Constantinides et al., 2018; Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Tiwana, 2014; Wareham et al., 2014). On the one hand, the goal of platform owners is to stimulate the activity of complementors in the ecosystem by fostering a high degree of openness towards complementors, granting them a high degree of autonomy, providing resources to facilitate development, and giving them a suficient share of the joint revenue (Tiwana, 2014; Eaton et al., 2015; cf., Dhanaraj & Parkhe, 2006). For example, Apple’s iOS is potentially open to anyone who wants to develop an application. Within certain boundaries, developers are free to choose what application to develop, and they can draw on rich resources to help in their development (e.g., Xcode, Apple’s integrated development environment [IDE]). Third-party developers are allowed to keep 70% of the revenue gained from the sales of their application.<sup>3</sup>

On the other hand, platform owners need to ensure a suficient level of quality on the platforms, as they will be held responsible for issues caused by the thirdparty applications (Roger & Vasconcelos, 2014). To maintain control, platform owners should establish application requirements and implement various formal and informal control mechanisms to ensure that the requirements are fulfilled (Goldbach & Benlian, 2014; Goldbach et al., 2018). For example, Apple conducts code reviews of third-party applications before they are published on the App Store and displays ratings from users on the App Store, thus helping users avoid low-quality applications.

Building on these findings on platform governance, IS scholars have studied established companies that have implemented innovation platforms or evolved corporate IT platforms into more open innovation platforms (Gawer, 2014; Gawer & Cusumano, 2002; Sandberg et al., 2020; Svahn et al., 2017). They contend that such a strategy increases the complexity of interactions as a hierarchy-based organisational logic evolves into an ecosystem-centred organisational logic (Gawer, 2014; Sandberg et al., 2020). Consequently, the focus of IT governance shifts from hierarchies to “ecosystem governance” (Gawer, 2014, p. 1244) or “innovation governance” (Svahn et al., 2017, p. 249), thereby generating competition between established companies and external complementors (Gawer, 2014; Svahn et al., 2017; see also Förderer et al., 2018).

Whilst previous literature examined the challenges arising when established companies implement innovation platforms, they do not study the internal dynamics of these companies that are configured as MBOs. The role of internal business units that develop applications on innovation platforms and their relationship with external complementors once the platforms become more open should be analysed further. This suggestion raises questions that go beyond platform governance and that overlap with questions about IT governance in MBOs; for example, how the corporate centre can best support business units to develop innovative applications on platforms and how relationships with potentially competing external complementors can be managed. When Deutsche Bank launched its innovation platform in 2016, it had to address governance issues, such as how to attract and manage contributions from fintech startups, how internal business units would bring their existing and new applications onto the platform, and to what extent these applications would compete with external applications.

Diferentiating governance issues related to internal business units versus external complementors is crucial, given that internal business units that develop applications on innovation platforms difer from external complementors. Firstly, their performance is more important to MBOs than the performance of unrelated external complementors. Even if external complementors fail to attract customers for their applications, the situation does not inflict immediate and irreparable harm on MBOs. However, the case where an internal business unit invests in an unsuccessful application has a direct negative impact on the performance of MBOs. A strict IT governance approach to account for this issue may, however, limit the innovativeness of business units. Secondly, business units have typically developed applications that may need to be migrated onto platforms. The additional efort it requires may cause resistance from business units. Therefore, the corporate centre may need to implement support mechanisms and consider each business unit’s individual situation. Thus, even though some aspects of governing external complementors, such as providing boundary resources, apply to internal business units, the specific mechanisms of platform governance cannot be directly applied to governing internal business units.

## 2.3. Linking perspectives to investigate the governance of innovation platforms in MBOs

In sum, the literature streams of IT governance in MBOs and platform governance provide indications of how innovation platforms can be governed in MBOs. However, each literature stream also exhibits limitations as regards innovation platforms (Table 1).

On the one hand, we argue that although IT governance in MBOs has been widely discussed (e.g., Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018; Williams & Karahanna, 2013) – mostly along the lines of federal approaches to IT governance (Weill & Ross, 2004) – how IT governance changes once MBOs implement innovation platforms on which business units develop applications alongside external complementors remains unclear. The focus needs to shift from increasing eficiency and reducing costs through IT standardisation to enabling bottom-up innovation by business units. Furthermore, the focus on the structure of IT governance in MBOs leaves little room for discussions of what a flexible IT governance approach based on interactions between the corporate centre and business units might look like and how it can adapt to change (cf., Pearlson et al., 2016; Schwarz & Hirschheim, 2003; Tiwana et al., 2013).

On the other hand, IS literature on platform governance provides insights into tensions that arise when established companies implement innovation platforms (Gawer, 2014; Gawer & Cusumano, 2002; Sandberg et al., 2020; Svahn et al., 2017); but the focus lies on tensions between established companies and external complementors, rather than internal tensions between the corporate centre and business units. As MBOs implement innovation platforms to leverage the innovative power of their business units, the extent to which these findings remain applicable is unclear.

We suggest that linking these perspectives to address inadequacies that result from relying on either one of the perspectives in isolation (Locke & Golden-Biddle, 1998) is key to addressing our research question on how MBOs can apply IT governance to navigate the tensions between the corporate centre and business units when implementing innovation platforms.

## 3. Method

We applied a qualitative research approach to study the implementation of an innovation platform by an established European automotive manufacturer (Yin, 2014). A qualitative approach captures and describes organisational phenomena that emerge from interactions among actors, matching our aim to study interactions between the corporate centre and business units within MBOs (Gioia et al., 2013; Goldkuhl, 2012; Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Specifically, case studies are useful for gaining in-depth insights from instances of a phenomenon (Yin, 2014).

We selected the automotive manufacturer $\mathrm { C A R ^ { 4 } }$ for our study because its platform project – an automotive application platform to ofer digital applications in the vehicles’ infotainment systems – was central to the company’s strategy and thus benefitted from management support and suficient funding. The platform unit acted on behalf of the corporate centre whilst diferent business units developed applications to be provided on the platform (for more details, see section Case Summary: Launching an Automotive Application Platform). This setting allowed interesting insights into how CAR applied IT governance to manage its innovation platform. Our longitudinal study, covering a time span of almost four years, enabled us to investigate how the IT governance approach evolved over time.

## 3.1. Data collection

Our longitudinal case study relied on primary and secondary data. For the primary data, we conducted 32 semi-structured interviews with key informants from CAR in three series between April 2017 and February 2021. The first series of interviews (n = 14; April 2017–June 2017) took place during the development phase of the automotive application platform, that is, before its launch in July 2018. The second (n = 8; November 2018–December 2018) and third series (n = 10; November 2020–February 2021) were conducted after the launch of the platform. By conducting the interviews in three series before and after the launch of the platform, we were able to observe how tensions emerged and how CAR’s approach to IT governance evolved, not only in the direct aftermath of the platform introduction but over the course of the following two years.

Informants were either employed in the platform unit or in business units or were external IT providers that collaborated with business units (Table 4, Appendix A.1). Interviews were structured into three sections (Appendix A.2). The first section covered the informants’ roles and their relationship with the platform project. The second section focused on the platform project and how it evolved. The third section shed light on the governance of the platform, covering collaboration among the corporate centre, internal business units and, where applicable, external complementors. We adapted the interview guide according to the background of each informant, and we recorded and transcribed all interviews.

We acknowledge that our primary data were socially constructed and thus were not free of bias. For example, interview partners might have held biased views regarding the success of CAR’s platform or they might have been pursuing a personal agenda (Klein & Myers, 1999; Schultze & Avital, 2011). To address the issue of bias and to gain further insights, we collected secondary data. Our secondary data included sources from CAR, such as company blog entries, press releases, meeting minutes, and presentations from community meet-ups, as well as external sources, such as tech blog entries, press releases of other parties, and market reports. In total, we gathered 51 documents of diferent types (Table 5, Appendix A.1). Finally, during all phases of data collection, we engaged in informal exchanges with employees at CAR to verify ambiguities in our findings or to probe specific issues that arose during preliminary analysis of the data.

## 3.2. Data analysis

We started our analysis by building a comprehensive case history of the platform project at CAR (Yin, 2014; cf., Hannah & Eisenhardt, 2018). The case history was prepared by the first author and discussed within the author team. We discussed and resolved ambiguities by revisiting the data and, in some instances, by acquiring additional secondary data or by consulting informants at CAR.

Building on the case history, we analysed the case data focusing on the relationships between the platform unit that acted on behalf of the corporate centre and internal business units that developed applications. We applied iterative coding techniques through the grounded theory methodology to make sense of our case data (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Urquhart, 2013; Wiesche et al., 2017). In the first round of open coding, we coded slices of the data that described tensions in the relationships between the platform unit and internal business units, including CAR’s eforts to navigate these tensions.

In the subsequent step of axial coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) and following the paradigm of abstraction and generalisation (Klein & Myers, 1999), we relied on the constructs of tensions and resolution to structure our codes into categories and make sense of the relationships among the codes. The resulting categories included tensions between the platform unit and business units and the resolutions of these tensions (Tables 6 and $^ { 7 , }$ Appendix A.3). To improve the reliability of the coding, we discussed the emerging categories of tensions and their resolutions within the author team and with a key informant at CAR. Finally, relying on selective coding, we made sense of our findings by summarising how CAR’s approach to IT governance evolved over time, as the company navigated the tensions that emerged.

## 4. Findings

In the following, we first describe the implementation of CAR’s automotive application platform in a case summary. Then, we provide details on the tensions related to IT governance and how CAR resolved them. Last, we summarise how CAR’s approach to IT governance with regard to its automotive application platform evolved over time.

## 4.1. Case summary: launching an automotive application platform

CAR is an established automotive manufacturer based in Europe. From its origins in the early 20th century, CAR has evolved into a leading manufacturer of luxury vehicles with a global production and sales network with Europe, China, and the US being the most important markets. CAR’s revenue has so far been largely based on a product-centric pipeline business model: CAR designs automobiles and assembles them with a major share of the parts being provided by specialised suppliers. The cars are then sold or leased to consumers and businesses. With a production output of more than 2 million vehicles per year, CAR has been consistently profitable since the 2007–2008 financial crisis.

However, similar to the automotive industry as a whole, the rapidly changing customer expectations in the age of digitalisation have exerted increasing pressure on CAR’s business model. Specifically, younger generations tend to view mobility as a service, rather than as linked to car ownership. This mindset is demonstrated by the popularity of ride-hailing services, such as Uber; car-sharing services, such as Car2Go; and multimodal mobility aggregators, such as Google Maps or Daimler’s Moovel. Furthermore, customers increasingly value digital services in their cars and are oftentimes willing to switch their car brand to benefit from improved digital services and their integration (NTT, 2020).

In response, CAR strived to transform itself from an automotive manufacturer to a mobility service provider. As one service owner (interview #14) explained, this strategy is associated with a shift in CAR’s oferings, “from the car as nucleus to the customer as nucleus”. This shift includes providing cars through diferent channels, such as car-sharing and a seamless integration of the car into the digital services used by consumers:

I believe that development will go very much in the direction of sharing in some form. As a result, I believe that the digital services in the vehicle will become even more important. How do I actually integrate my world into the vehicle? Especially considering that I will use diferent vehicles. (Service owner, interview #7)

The relevant interface through which CAR engaged with customers is the automotive infotainment system, which is typically placed centrally in the dashboard (also referred to as the automotive head unit). In the past, this infotainment system had ofered access to various services, such as radio, navigation or information about the car’s status. To provide a broad set of digital services via its automotive infotainment system, CAR integrated an automotive application platform in the system that served as an innovation platform for customer-facing applications (Figure 1).

The automotive application platform used data gathered through an automotive bus which acts as an interface to automotive control devices, such as the climate control and the interior lighting systems. On top of this data, the automotive application platform provided a set of APIs and supporting tools, such as an SDK, a runtime environment, and an application update mechanism. The head of the platform unit summarised this feature as follows:

[On the platform], you can access a subset of all of the car’s interfaces, and they are all homogenised. At the level of the car itself, interfaces are much broader and heterogeneous. However, with [the homogenised interfaces], everything is modularised [. . .]. The more modular it is, the better diferent teams can perform [application] development. (Head of the platform unit, interview #1)

After finalising the development of the automotive application platform throughout 2017 and the first half of 2018, CAR rolled out its automotive application platform in new cars from July 2018 onwards (Figure 2). The platform was developed by a dedicated platform unit, which was set up by and acted on behalf of the corporate centre. A number of diferent business units developed applications for the platform, such as a telematic services application, an online entertainment application, and a parking services application (Figure 1). In addition to these internal applications, some applications, such as an email client and music streaming, were developed in collaboration with external complementors to further enhance the ofering of digital services for CAR’s customers. A member of the platform unit illustrated this idea.<sup>.</sup>

We provide this platform as an interface to the vehicle and as an interface to interact with users. [The platform] enables applications by business units that want to bring specialised applications into the vehicle, something similar to navigation applications, a parking application [. . .], voice control or a personal assistant. We just ofer [business units] a platform, so that they can provide these new functions in the vehicle as quickly as possible with a short time-to-market and that we can thereby create added value for customers. (Functional owner, interview #14)

With the automotive application platform, CAR’s customers were able to access a set of applications that were pre-installed whilst also having the choice to instal further applications as they became available. The platform was a game changer because it was now possible to add applications at any point during the cars’ lifecycles, rather than only during production and through major software updates performed during routine maintenance appointments.

After the launch in July 2018, CAR continuously improved the platform to account for new requirements, issues that came up in the field and feedback from business units. For example, the platform unit improved the APIs and the documentation. It also enabled business units to directly contribute to the platform’s SDK.

Regarding IT governance, the platform unit was tasked by the corporate centre not only to develop and maintain the platform but also to manage the contributions of business units and external complementors to the platform ecosystem. Thus, business units that developed applications were not subordinate to the platform unit. Instead, they were located in diferent business lines of the CAR organisation (Figure 3). This setup represents a federal IT governance approach in which the platform unit possesses the decision rights required to manage the platform, whereas business units are generally free to develop the applications they deem promising. However, in a company that was traditionally organised in a hierarchical way, such an approach to governing the automotive application platform was prone to tensions that the platform unit had to resolve over time.

## 4.2. IT governance tensions and their resolutions

The automotive application platform was of strategic importance to CAR; the federal IT governance approach helped CAR establish the platform in an existing organisational structure. The platform unit, acting on behalf of the corporate centre, had the support of the management to push the platform forward. However, the implementation of the federal IT governance approach led to four tensions between the platform unit and business units that developed applications on the platform, triggered by: (1) rigidity, (2) alteration, (3) mistrust, and (4) competition (Table 2).

Rigidity resulted from the rigid central requirements that the platform unit defined for applications on the platform. Given that the applications would be used in cars and whilst driving, the platform unit had to ensure that drivers could use applications safely and without unnecessary distraction. To do so, the platform unit defined, for instance, how the user interface had to be designed in the applications. Business units could only use predefined design elements and any divergence from these elements would be declined in the release process of the applications. However, business units felt limited by these requirements and by the lengthy release processes that ensured adherence to the requirements:

So, for us, the UI is separate from the platform. This factor distinguishes the [CAR platform] from, for example, an Android or iOS, where each application brings its UI individually. In our case, it is an interface, you use this prefabricated kit. [. . .] Of course, we do not think it is a good thing. It deprives the applications of great freedom. The biggest bottleneck for developing a new application is currently the UI. We need this native integration, which gets you into lengthy, long-cycle development processes. They can no longer be updated through the system updates afterwards. That is not in our interest. We want as many use cases as possible. (Product owner in application development team, interview #25)

As a result, some business units were less motivated to contribute to the platform and to develop new features for their existing applications. Other business units explored workarounds, trying to make adaptions to their applications’ user interface whilst avoiding scrutiny in the release process.<sup>.</sup>

[Business units] stand in front of these walls and just drill holes through it or build a ladder to climb over it. Then, they always say, ‘oh, now we have a lot of possibilities,’ and everyone climbs over that ladder. Until in the next generation, someone takes that ladder away [. . .]. A back-and-forth situation happens among people who need to keep the system running, who must guarantee stability and security and those who develop the use cases. Little communication occurs among these groups. I have only ever noticed that the sides annoy one another. (Member of the platform unit, interview #23)

Over time, the platform unit realised that the central requirements posed a serious risk for the platform. Not only were they reducing the ecosystem’s innovative potential but also made the platform more vulnerable and dificult to maintain due to workarounds. Reacting to feedback from business units, the platform unit gradually granted them more flexibility:

I think [standardisation] is a continuous process. In some places, we gave too little freedom. We recently had that issue coming up that we should ofer more flexibility—that was the feedback from the application developers. I think that is such a continuous process. (Functional owner, interview #14)

Whilst the platform unit could not lower its standards for safe user interfaces, they made it easier for business units to explore and test new features without having to undergo the full release process. Instead, business units could push their applications to a limited number of cars in the company’s own fleet to obtain feedback from their colleagues.

What I find really cool—and that has just been added —is that you can easily—and without going through the release circle—push application versions onto vehicles. What we always do is that we now also unlock the application for the colleagues from the department and then they have the opportunity to look at the application again. They know the function well and find mistakes relatively quickly. This is really good and extremely helpful. (Product owner for music applications, interview #29)

A second measure to resolve the tension was to provide improved boundary resources that helped business units adhere to the requirements. For example, the corporate centre developed a software development kit and a basic development portal that included documentation such as style guides and automated code checks:

With regard to our applications here internally—considering that they are [. . .] now developed in units across the whole company—we need style guides, how applications have to look like and how to use things. It means we must know what we are allowed and not allowed to do. Then, we must perform automated checks of these specifications, as far as possible. (Head of the platform unit, interview #1)

These measures reduced the potential for frustration, as business units were less likely to face rejection during the release process for not adhering to all requirements.

The second tension, alteration, resulted from platform changes that created additional efort for business units. Specifically, in the phase before the launch of the platform, business units developed applications for a platform that continued to change. Even small alterations to the platform could lead to issues in the applications. In these cases, where the issue came from and whether business units or the platform unit should resolve the issue were often not immediately clarified:

The tools just are not always ready. For example, a human–machine editor gets a new version, in which little has changed but a user still receives some generic error message and then they must find out what it is about. Some features suddenly do not work anymore, and the user cannot tell if it is a bug or maybe it is intentional. It is then changed because many things happen in the flow. (Software developer and product owner, interview #2)

This back-and-forth situation was frustrating for business units who had invested so much efort in the development of applications, resulting in a blame game between business units and the platform unit:

We were there from the beginning and grew together [with the platform]. This factor always led to discord because everyone kept passing the buck to each other:

‘The platform has to solve this, no, this is an application issue’ and so on. (Product owner for telematic services, interview #26)

In the phase before the launch of the platform, the platform and the initial set of applications were developed in parallel, which made it almost impossible to avoid platform changes that afected business units. However, after the launch, based on the experience of this challenging process, the platform unit worked on improving the handling of platform changes. On the one hand, the platform unit increased its collaboration with business units to further involve them in the process of improving the platform. Specifically, the platform unit aimed to engage with business units before platform changes were made and to find solutions that considered the requirements of the business units:

We asked [business units] beforehand. We have captured the mood, and we have done that several times. We have also done surveys in the community. [. . .] We realised that when we make these decisions from the ivory tower, the probability that these decisions are right is low. We have this community, so why not use it? (Member of the platform unit, interview #23)

On the other hand, the platform unit continuously improved the boundary resources that business units relied on to develop applications on the platform. For example, the documentation available in the developer portal was enhanced and a starter application served as a blueprint for business units:

The starter application serves as a reference application, which uses all the possibilities of the platform, that is, all these core functions and core services, as an example, just as a normal application would use them. The application has a sample function. Hence, an application that wants to use this feature is fast because it already has a running example. For example, how do I use voice control, how do I use certain UI components that the platform ofers? These features allow me to look at the platform beforehand, by just using and experiencing the example in the starter application. It also supports end-to-end tests. You can really test the features from the users’ perspective in an automated way. (Technical lead in the platform unit, interview #17)

By relying on these improved boundary resources, the applications across all business units interacted with the platform in a more consistent way. Evaluating how platform changes would afect the application landscape and how they had to work with business units to prepare the changes was thus easy for the platform unit.

The third tension refers to the mistrust between business units and the platform unit. Before the launch of the platform and in the early phase of the platform, business units and the platform blamed each other for errors. As a result, business units did not feel that their needs were being suficiently considered by the platform unit:

I do not know where that comes from in our application team, but much scepticism about other teams and about the platform always exists. [. . .] The perception that the platform unit does not consider the application teams remains. (Product owner for telematic services, interview #26)

This mistrust was further illustrated by a possessive mentality that emerged in business units. In several instances, business units had developed useful tools or building blocks for applications that could be reused or even be included in the platform core. When the platform unit approached them, business units felt that the platform unit was “taking something away” (Product owner for telematic services, interview #26) from them and they were reluctant to collaborate.

To address the mistrust in the ecosystem, the platform unit involved business units in the process of improving the platform and fostered knowledge sharing among business units. The platform unit realised that its role was not only to develop the platform as a software artefact but also to cater to the needs of business units to enable innovation on the platform. Improvements to the platform would only make sense if business units were aware of them and were able to build on them. The platform unit’s more supportive role thus included a better communication strategy, quick reactions to issues faced by business units, and a general mindset that business units were customers of the platform that needed to be satisfied. A member of the platform unit summarised the supportive role as follows:

That is why I think that approaching people in a cooperative way and explaining the benefits were important, and that we also showed them ways to get there. We did not say ‘do it,’ we showed them how to do it gradually. We did that with every single application [team], we approached them and looked at the code, and worked with them to develop a plan on how to migrate to these new standards. That worked well. (Member of the platform unit, interview #23)

Furthermore, the platform unit fostered knowledge sharing among business units to create a sense of community and to leverage synergies. After the phase of the platform launch that left little time for knowledge sharing, the community began to take of, supported by the platform unit’s efort of community management. Formats such as regular community meet-ups, a question-and-answer (Q&A) forum, and a dedicated co-innovation space were the basis for further informal knowledge sharing in the community, as illustrated by one team manager in a business unit:

The community idea is important. Being part of something bigger is a completely diferent driver in terms of motivation compared with just saying you are doing your work and nothing beyond that, you are not seeing the bigger picture. You learn so much from it. This is something my development team has always said in the past, which was also important to me, that they are not only ‘code monkeys’ who just kind of hack down their lines of code but also they can have fun with it and identify with the product. (Product owner for parking services, interview #27)

The intensifying knowledge sharing among business units also leveraged synergies across business units, as developers learned from one another by discussing problems they faced or by running ideas past one another.

The platform unit holds regular meetings to exchange ideas. An active developer forum is also conducted; ‘active’ because it is really used for an exchange of ideas within my developer team and across teams. This is a very important channel from my developers to other development teams. It benefits from my developers putting time into it, not only to educate themselves but also to educate others. This case is a give-and-take scenario at this point. (Product owner for parking services, interview #27)

Over time, the sense of community among developers across business units grew. It reduced mistrust between business units and the platform unit because the members of the platform unit were part of the community as well, organising meet-ups and moderating the Q&A forum.

The fourth tension arose from potential competition from external complementors that business units feared they would face on an increasingly open platform. Specifically, business units were concerned that external complementors would not have to adhere to all standards and guidelines and would therefore enjoy more freedom in developing applications.

Many discussions have been going on because clearly, such a generic weather application can be developed much faster than when it is developed on our platform. The fact that some applications are native makes sense. An example is a music application, where many things run in the background [. . .]. If it works extremely well now and if it is well received, then one can ask whether, in the long term, one still needs native music applications at all. (Product owner in application development team, interview #25)

For product owners of applications on the platform, uncertainty was created because it was unclear which applications were at risk of future competition from external complementors. Product owners also realised that the decision was not up to them: “I do not think you are going to be able to do much. I will just have to develop another application then”. (Product owner for a portfolio of applications, interview #28).

However, CAR began with a limited opening of the platform. Only selected partners (a provider of an email client and a provider of music streaming) were approved to build applications and the platform unit controlled which applications were published at what time. Furthermore, CAR restricted the access that external complementors had to the data provided by the central automotive bus system:

An internal [developer] can get more permissions than an external one who may only get read-only permission and non-critical system access. (Software developer, interview #2)

Thus, CAR planned to focus on car-centric use cases with internal applications for which internal business units would be prioritised. Meanwhile, general use cases can be covered by external complementors. Specifically, use cases that required interaction with car functions or that were an integral part of a user’s in-car experience were seen as core areas to be covered by internal business units:

[Applications developed by CAR internally are] all about full integration into the vehicle. The question is: what belongs to this portfolio? What is part of this CAR experience? For instance, an application that provides country information is something that is mobility-specific. (Product owner for music applications, interview #29)

## 4.3. Evolution of the IT governance approach of CAR

CAR had introduced its automotive application platform to create an ecosystem of innovative applications. As part of a federal IT governance approach, the platform unit, on behalf of the corporate centre, was responsible for the automotive application platform, whereas various business units were responsible for creating applications on the platform. This approach represented a departure from CAR’s traditionally centralised approach to IT governance where business units would develop applications on the basis of central product planning. However, the federal IT governance approach, in its initial, rigid form, did not contribute to a smooth start of the innovation platform, as the tensions reported in our case study illustrate.

The tensions were interrelated and developed over time. Given the initial rigid federal IT governance approach of CAR before and during the launch of the platform, the rigidity tensions emerged first. The alteration tension also developed during the platform launch because CAR had to make frequent changes to the platform before establishing a more stable version. These two tensions fuelled mistrust, as business units did not feel that their needs were being suficiently considered by the platform unit. The more stable the platform became, the more relevant the competition tension grew because CAR began to gradually open the platform to external complementors. The tensions had to be addressed by the platform unit, resulting in an evolved federal approach to IT governance evolved (Table 3).

CAR’s initial federal IT governance approach was rigid and included clear-cut responsibilities and sharing of decision rights between the platform unit and business units. Acting on behalf of the corporate centre, the platform unit was responsible for developing and improving the architecture of the automotive application platform and for defining the central requirements for applications on the platform. The platform unit thus acted as a gatekeeper, intent on maintaining high-level quality, security, and driver safety. Business units that created applications on the platform possessed decision rights on what applications they wanted to develop but had to follow the central requirements with regard to how they should develop their applications. As illustrated by the findings of our case study, the rigid federal IT governance approach led to tensions and risked quelling innovation on the platform, thwarting the platform’s initial purpose. Under these circumstances, opening the platform to external complementors would have further alienated business units and potentially led to a failure of the platform project.

By addressing these tensions in the two and a half years after the platform launch, the federal IT governance approach evolved. In general, CAR kept the federal structure with the platform unit that is responsible for developing and improving the platform and business units that develop applications. However, the rigid structure created by CAR’s use of federal IT governance was relaxed and the focus shifted to dynamic interactions between the corporate centre and business units, leading to a more flexible and adaptable IT governance approach. The platform unit assumed the role of an enabler of business units, rather than of a gatekeeper that potentially blocks their work. In developing and improving the platform architecture, the platform unit considered the feedback and input of business units. Feedback included suggestions on how to improve APIs and what documentation to provide. Input went beyond feedback, comprising instances when business units developed tools for application development that the platform unit later made available as a boundary resource to all business units (e.g., a debug tool initially developed by a business unit was included in the platform’s SDK). Furthermore, the platform unit defined central requirements and approved applications in a more flexible way. Without jeopardising quality, security or driver safety, the platform unit established mechanisms through which business units could test features earlier without having to undergo full reviews, develop new UI elements, and obtain early feedback on their application through automated code and UI checks. Taken together, as an enabler, the platform unit focused on supporting the business units in developing their applications.

Based on this evolved approach to IT governance, the role of business units was also enhanced. They continued to possess the decision rights on what applications to develop, but they now had the opportunity to provide feedback to the platform unit and to contribute to the improvement of the platform. By doing so, business units not only helped the platform unit improve the platform in a way that facilitates future innovation on the platform but also made members of business units feel a valuable part of the platform strategy. This development was further supported by an increasing knowledge sharing among business units that created a sense of community among them. As a result, rather than engaging in finger pointing and blame games, the platform unit and business units shared a vision of the automotive application platform and contributed jointly to achieving this vision.

The evolved approach to federal IT governance turned out to be an important prerequisite for considering an increasing degree of openness towards external complementors. Given that the platform unit had found a way to enable fruitful collaboration with business units, it was able to focus this collaboration on car-centric use cases. Thus, internal business units were prioritised to build applications for these use cases. This approach delayed the involvement of external complementors. Nevertheless, it opened up space for them to innovate without competing directly with internal business units. Furthermore, CAR was able to set up and test all resources that developers needed to implement applications (e.g., standardised APIs, an SDK, and documentation) with internal business units before making them available to external complementors.

## 5. Discussion

Our analysis of the tensions that MBOs such as CAR face when implementing an innovation platform and how they can navigate these tensions contributes to the IS literature streams on IT governance in MBOs (e.g., Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018; Weill & Broadbent, 1998; Weill & Ross, 2004; Williams & Karahanna, 2013) and platform governance (e.g., Constantinides et al., 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Huber et al., 2017; Tiwana, 2014; Tiwana et al., 2010). Furthermore, it yields several avenues for future research.

## 5.1. Implications for literature on IT governance in MBOs

With regard to literature on IT governance in MBOs, the findings from CAR’s innovation platform project show that a federal IT governance approach can be helpful to coordinate the corporate centre, which is responsible for the innovation platform, and business units that develop applications; but when implemented as a rigid structure, this approach is prone to tensions that may quell bottom-up innovation in business units. Thus, for MBOs to tap into the innovative potential of business units on innovation platforms, the federal IT governance approach must consider flexibility and adaptability. In line with more recent literature (Pearlson et al., 2016; Tiwana et al., 2013), we challenge the traditional view of IT governance as a structure of decision rights and accountability along the dichotomy of centralisation and decentralisation, suggesting that dynamics and interactions within organisational entities and even across organisational boundaries must be incorporated.

Whilst a federal structure corresponds to what we would expect from literature on IT governance in MBOs (e.g., Fonstad & Subramani, 2009; Williams & Karahanna, 2013), it leads to tensions between the corporate centre and business units when rigidly implemented. According to literature on IT governance in MBOs, corporate IT platforms support federal IT governance models because they leverage synergies among business units to increase eficiency and reduce costs (e.g., Weill & Broadbent, 1998). When MBOs implement an innovation platform, the goal, however, shifts: the focus now lies on enabling business units to create innovative applications on the platform (cf., Adler & Borys, 1996). Thus, a rigid approach to federal IT governance with the corporate centre acting as a gatekeeper threatens the agility and flexibility of business units (Queiroz, Tallon et al., 2018; Tanriverdi, 2006), creating tensions between the corporate centre and business units, ultimately hindering bottom-up innovation on the platform.

The way CAR resolved these tensions showed that the structure of decision rights in federal IT governance is only part of the game (cf., Pearlson et al., 2016; Schwarz & Hirschheim, 2003; Tiwana et al., 2013). Firstly, the corporate centre should manage its relationship with business units, assuming a role as an enabler, rather than as a gatekeeper of the development activities of business units. The corporate centre thereby acts on the requirements of business units, provides the resources they need to develop applications, and engages in a continuous knowledge sharing with business units to improve innovation platforms. In sum, when MBOs implement innovation platforms or transform corporate IT platforms into innovation platforms, the focus of federal IT governance shifts from leveraging synergies to fostering innovation.

Secondly, our findings underscore the importance of knowledge sharing among business units. Previous literature highlighted the need for business units to specialise, whereas knowledge sharing among business units is only necessary to create synergies in crossfunctional processes or to integrate enterprise data (Boh & Yellin, 2006; Weill & Broadbent, 1998). However, our research shows that with the implementation of innovation platforms, knowledge sharing among business units becomes increasingly important. Knowledge sharing is crucial for identifying innovative solutions that often extend across the traditional boundaries of business units. Business units that target diferent market segments may identify new use cases by sharing data on how customers use their applications and by sharing ideas, which they may initially have discarded, with other business units. In the case of CAR, new formats, such as developer meetups and a dedicated co-innovation space, created a sense of community among employees from diferent business units and provided a fruitful ground for ideas. In addition, knowledge sharing strengthens the position of business units to provide feedback to the corporate centre about the innovation platform. The developer meet-ups and the related Q&A forum have helped CAR obtain valuable feedback and evaluate whether a certain issue has been shared across business units.

Lastly, IT governance in MBOs, and in particular federal IT governance, must be reconsidered when MBOs implement innovation platforms that are open to external complementors. This kind of openness has not been considered previously in the IT governance literature, which has tended to focus on internal governance. In line with calls to include further stakeholders when studying MBOs (Chan & Reich, 2007; Galliers, 2004), we show that opening an innovation platform in an MBO to external complementors raises questions regarding the segment of the application portfolio that should be provided by internal business units and the extent to which it should be protected from external competition. Handling competition between internal business units and external complementors will thus represent an additional decision domain in Weill and Ross' (2004) approach to IT governance.

## 5.2. Implications for literature on platform governance

With regard to literature on platform governance, we reveal that established platform governance mechanisms must be adapted when an innovation platform targets business units within an MBO that develop applications, rather than only external complementors.

Firstly, our findings reveal that a focus on output control as a means to ensure quality on the platform is risky for MBOs that want to incentivise internal business units to develop applications on their innovation platforms. When applications are rejected or when business units need to put considerable efort into complying with corporate requirements, business units become frustrated. This type of frustration can also emerge among external complementors. When it happens within an MBO, the frustration is arguably critical, given that employees in business units will likely continue to be opposed to the platform. Consequently, MBOs should be more attentive to each business unit’s expectations and needs when it comes to developing applications. Control should be implemented early on during the process of application development, rather than through approval processes at the end. As the case of CAR illustrated, involving business units in setting up and improving these processes helps prevent frustration. This finding adds to the established knowledge in IS research on the balance of autonomy and control on innovation platforms (Constantinides et al., 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Huber et al., 2017; Wareham et al., 2014) by considering the fact that internal business units can also develop applications on innovation platforms and may respond diferently to control mechanisms.

Secondly, our findings indicate that knowledge sharing among internal business units is crucial to develop innovative ideas and to share best practices during the process of creating applications. In the platform governance literature, knowledge sharing among complementors has received scant attention thus far. Although previous studies revealed that knowledge sharing helps complementors learn from one another – for example, at developer conferences or in communities (Förderer, 2020; Huang et al., 2018) – fostering knowledge sharing has not been discussed as a specific goal of platform governance. This implication links to literature on IT governance in MBOs that has long pointed to the importance of synergies among business units, albeit with a focus on eficiency, rather than innovation (e.g., Tanriverdi, 2006; Tanriverdi & Venkatraman, 2005).

Thirdly, studies on platform governance suggest that the openness of a platform is of critical importance to create a flourishing ecosystem (Adner, 2017; Jacobides et al., 2018; Tiwana, 2014). However, our research shows that a high degree of openness in the innovation platforms of MBOs leads to competition between internal business units and external complementors. Surprisingly, this competition can have positive efects, such as business units focusing on their key use cases and continuously improving their applications to remain competitive. However, competition can also have negative efects, such as business units fearing that their applications will become obsolete or will not be used. When MBOs manage competition by prioritising internal business units over external complementors in critical use cases (as CAR did for car-centric use cases), external complementors are excluded from what are potentially the most profitable use cases and may decrease their motivation to contribute to the innovation platform. Thus, the optimal degree of openness must consider the efects on internal business units and external complementors.

## 5.3. Future research

By linking the literature streams of IT governance in MBOs and platform governance, we derive several avenues for future research on the governance of innovation platforms in MBOs.

First, future studies can further explore the tradeof between prioritising internal business units on an innovation platform and risking the alienation of external complementors. The platform governance literature has shown that providing applications on their own platforms is a valid strategy for platform owners to capture value (Förderer et al., 2018; Hagiu et al., 2020; Kang, 2017); our results confirm that this strategy is valid for MBOs. However, hosting external complementors, even if they compete with applications provided by internal business units, can also be beneficial under certain circumstances (Hagiu et al., 2020). At the same time, protecting their own oferings from third-party competition reduces the perceived openness of platforms (Benlian et al., 2015), potentially making them less attractive for complementors. Identifying what services can be the most beneficial when ofered by internal business units, considering the existing portfolio of the applications of business units, will therefore enhance our findings.

Secondly, our findings on IT governance in MBOs, which implement innovation platforms, can be used as a starting point to study strategic IT alignment in MBOs that open up to external complementors. Strategic IT alignment will no longer be solely focused on the internal alignment of IT and business strategy (Brown & Magill, 1994; Gerow et al., 2015; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993; Queiroz, 2017). It will also need to consider the IT and business strategies of complementors. It creates a challenge of ecosystemlevel alignment that goes hand-in-hand with the governance of the ecosystem. Future research can evaluate the implications that a federal IT governance approach has on ecosystem-level alignment.

Thirdly, further avenues for future research emerge from our study’s limitations. Qualitative studies risk biases from researchers and participants, such as interview partners. We reduced the bias risk by choosing interview partners with diferent perspectives on CAR’s platform project, by discussing findings iteratively within the author team, and by triangulating our findings with secondary data. In addition, qualitative case studies limit the generalisability of the results (Lee, 1989; Yin, 2014). For example, tensions observed in the case of CAR might partly result from path dependency, even though they did not become explicit in the empirical data. Other companies might exhibit diferent path dependencies. We alleviated this issue by providing a rich case summary that lays out the context of our study. Doing so helped evaluate the extent to which our findings might apply to other companies (Cheng et al., 2016; Davison & Martinsons, 2016). We argue that companies from traditional industries, which establish innovation platforms, will face similar tensions as CAR and benefit from our findings on how IT governance can help navigate these tensions.

These two limitations of our qualitative research can be mitigated by investigating a larger number of MBOs (Avgerou, 2019). For example, Yofie et al. (2019) analysed 250 platform cases, many of them involving MBOs. Comparing IT governance approaches across these cases via surveys will be an intriguing next step. With such analyses, we can distinguish between successful and unsuccessful instances of navigating the governance tension in MBOs that implement innovation platforms and gain insights into what approaches to IT governance MBOs should avoid.<sup>5</sup>

## 6. Conclusion

In this study, we explore how MBOs govern innovation platforms to leverage the innovative potential of internal business units. The case of the automotive manufacturer CAR showed that implementing an innovation platform creates tensions between the corporate centre and business units in MBOs that stem from (1) rigidity, (2) alteration, (3) mistrust, and (4) competition. To navigate these tensions, MBOs must implement IT governance that goes beyond a rigid structure for decision rights and accountability. By considering the dynamic interactions between the corporate centre and business units, an evolved approach to IT governance allows for flexibility and adaptability, enabling bottom-up innovation in business units, and laying the groundwork for opening the innovation platform to external complementors. With our findings, we link the literature streams of IT governance in MBOs and platform governance and ofer suggestions for established companies that aim to implement innovation platforms. Given that an increasing number of established companies are introducing platform strategies, our findings contribute to the discussion of how they can be successful and why some of them fail with their platform strategies.

## A.2 Interview Questions

We provide a general version of our interview guidelines. The questions were modified for individual interview partners.

## Part 1: Background

● What is your role in the company? How long have you been at the company?

● In what way is your role related to the platform project?

● What is a platform for you? What is your understanding of the CAR platform?

● What impact does digitisation have on the automotive industry? What challenges arise for CAR and how does the platform project relate to that?

## Part 2: The platform project

● What is the background and motivation for the platform project at CAR?

● How did the platform project evolve since its launch?

● Who are the main stakeholders involved in the platform project, both internally and externally? In what way are they involved?

● What are key challenges that have occurred (or still occur) in the platform project?

● What is your vision of the platform in five years?

## Part 3: Governance

● What technical changes did the platform strategy entail and what efects did that have?

● What organisational changes did the platform strategy entail and what efects did that have?

● What is the process for applications to become available on the platform?

● Could you describe a current application project on the platform? What actors are involved, what issues arose?

● How far has the platform been opened to external actors? What efects did that have on internal activities?
