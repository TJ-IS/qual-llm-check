## Abstract

The failure rate of fintech platforms is disproportionately high, which may be because (1) there is a lack of published knowledge on the appropriate strategies to adopt and/or (2) the traditional prescriptions for strategy may be less relevant in the context of fintech platforms. To ascertain either or both of these possibilities, we conducted a comprehensive review of the fintech literature and found that not only is there a relative paucity of research on Fintech Strategies, but there are also important limitations associated with the existing works. To address these limitations, we first identified the unique characteristics of fintech platforms and the strategic implications of those characteristics. Next, we adapted a framework made up of six conventional core logics of strategy and juxtaposed the prescriptions of those logics with the unique characteristics identified. Finally, we constructed a research agenda consisting of a number of open questions based on our analysis to provide directions for future research in this area. The agenda suggests that fintech platform strategies have to account for competing institutional logics stemming from the platforms' dual identity and the tensions between conforming for legitimacy and differentiating for competitive advantage. There is also a need to account for an exceptionally dynamic and unpredictable regulatory landscape, as well as the responses and competitive actions of influential market incumbents.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Fintech is an umbrella concept that is broadly defined as the design and delivery of financial products and services through information technology (Leong et al., 2017). While the first use of the term can in fact be traced back to th 1970s, it is only since the mid-2010s that the term has become entrenched in the modern business lexicon (see Schueffel, 2016). There are two main reasons for the hype surrounding the present fintech wave. The first stem from the maturation and convergence of a number of different technologies that have amplified its commercia potential (Gomber et al., 2018a), including, among others, Blockchain, Internet-of-Things, data analytics and artificia intelligence (Chen et al., 2019). The second derives from the benefits that it is expected to generate. For consumers, fintech increases access to and lower the costs of, financial services (see Demirgüç-Kunt et al., 2017). For financia institutions, fintech enhances the efficiency of their operations and increases the reach and richness of their service (see Currie & Lagoarde-Segot, 2017). For entrepreneurs, fintech can lead to the emergence and exploitation of new business opportunities as it enhances the transparency and timeliness of financial information (see Gozman et al., 2018). In addition, the impact of fintech is not restricted to the financial sector but can be felt across a variet of industries as well (e.g., driving new ventures in professional services and e-commerce – see Leong et al., 2017) Attesting to the current prominence of the phenomenon in the contemporary business landscape, it is estimated that the cumulative investments in fintech globally was around US\$210 billion in 2021,<sup>1</sup> while industry analysts project that the global fintech market will grow at a compound annual growth rate of around 20% over the coming years to reach around US\$305 billion by 2025.<sup>2</sup>

Mirroring its meteoric rise to prominence in the global financial sector, the research attention on fintech is simi larly growing at an exponential pace (Jinasena et al., 2020). This is evidenced by the numerous special issues on the topic published in recent years across a variety of fields, including information systems (e.g., Gomber et al., 2018b), finance (e.g., Goldstein et al., 2019), economic geography (e.g., Knight & Wojcik, 2020) and philosoph (e.g., Coeckelbergh et al., 2018). The cumulative body of work on the topic, however, is currently fragmented and full of controversies (Knight & Wojcik, 2020, p. 1490), with a lack of consensus surrounding even basic issues suc as the definition of the term (Schueffel, 2016), as well as the novelty and revolutionary potential of the phenomenon (Knight & Wojcik, 2020). While this may be a reflection of the fledgling state of the research area, it is clear that there remains a myriad unanswered questions (see e.g., Fosso Wamba et al., 2020; Gomber et al., 2017) that must be addressed to enhance our understanding of the phenomenon and how to fully unlock its business potential.

In particular, while the revolutionary and disruptive potential of fintech is currently a matter of contention (Knight & Wojcik, 2020), it is clear that in recent years, digital technologies have been applied in ways ‘that have led to new entrepreneurship, unbridled innovation, and significant growth in interest by venture capital firms to becom involved’ (Gomber et al., 2018b, p. 13). This has brought about the emergence of thousands of new startups globally,<sup>3</sup> many of which are providing novel platform-based services (Haddad & Hornuf, 2019) in areas such as payments (e.g., mobile payments and cryptocurrencies), financing (e.g., P2P lending and crowdfunding), wealt management (e.g., social trading) and exchange services (e.g., stocks and derivatives trading). And yet, in spite of thi flurry of entrepreneurialism, the failure rate of these new fintech platforms has been projected to be extremely high (Muthukannan et al., 2020), with estimates of around 90% as the firms progress over the various venture capita funding rounds over a period of typically 5 to 6 years.<sup>4</sup> By contrast, the five-year mortality rates for new ventures in advanced economies are historically around 50% (see Levie et al., 2011)

As the strategy of a firm, defined as a systematic blueprint that determines how a business is going to compete what its goals should be, and what policies will be needed to carry out those goals’ (Porter, 1980, p. xxiv), is traditionally held as one of the fundamental determinants of the firm's survivability and profitability (Block et al., 2014 Linton & Kask, 2017); the discrepancy in mortality rates suggests two possibilities. First, there may be a lack of publi shed knowledge for fintech platforms to consult and refer to on the strategies that could ensure the survival and success of their ventures. Second, it could be that the traditional prescriptions for strategy are less relevant or effectiv in the contexts of fintech (see Kazan et al., 2018), platform-based business models (see Van Alstyne et al., 2016) or both. In particular, there is growing recognition that the global fintech sector is not only hypercompetitive, but it is also already or quickly becoming saturated.<sup>5</sup> These market influences suggest that an effective competitive strategy is needed even more to help the fintech platforms ensure their survival and navigate the adverse contextual condi tions surrounding their businesses (Chanias et al., 2019).

While the task of addressing the complex and multi-faceted issues implied by these two possibilities is beyond the scope of a single article, the objective of this article is to lay the groundwork for future research by ascertaining either or both of them based on what is currently known. To this end, we will first begin by describing our approac to conducting a review of the fintech literature. Next, we will identify the key research themes in this domain and assess the state of existing knowledge surrounding fintech platform strategies, before attempting to establish if fintech is a distinctive context of theoretical importance. This will be carried out by exploring the unique characteris tics of fintech platforms and the implications of those characteristics on the formulation and deployment of strate gies. Third, we will construct a theoretical lens based on an existing typology of strategies to gain an overview of th strategic options that are available to a fintech platform. Finally, we will assess if there are limitations in the finding and prescriptions of existing fintech studies when they are juxtaposed with our theoretical lens and present a research agenda to steer future studies in the area of fintech platform strategies toward addressing those limitations We will emphasise here that our unit of analysis is a fintech platform as opposed to a fintech firm, and while fintech platforms tend to be more commonly associated with start-ups (Cortina Lorente & Schmukler, 2018; Haddad & Hornuf, 2019), they may be owned and operated by larger and more established entities as well (see e.g., T. Tan et al., 2020). In addition, it is possible for a single fintech firm, whether a start-up or an established entity, to own and operate multiple platforms even as each platform is typically regarded as a distinct line of business that coul require different competitive strategies (Leong et al., 2017). The overall approach we adopted is consistent with similar agenda-setting works (e.g., de Reuver et al., 2018; Yoo et al., 2010) in the field of information systems (IS). Th rest of the article is organised into four sections, corresponding to the objectives indicated above.

## 2 | REVIEW METHOD

As our intention is to take stock of the existing knowledge on fintech platform strategies, our review of the literatur may be may be classified as a narrative review, which has its unique advantages including affording researchers wit ‘judgement and discretion to classify, critically assess and determine importance of individual studies and their con tributions to knowledge’ (see Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2015, p. 164) that could enhance the originality of insights

This type of literature review, however, has been criticised for being opaque and difficult to replicate (see Paré et al., 2015). To address these potential limitations, we synthesised the approaches of a number of previous scholars (i.e., Günther et al., 2017; Lagna & Ravishankar, 2022; Senyo et al., 2019) to enhance the systematicity and transpar ency of our review (see Paré et al., 2016). In particular, our approach to reviewing the literature consists of fiv stages: (1) Definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria, (2) literature search, (3) dataset refinement, (4) analysis and synthesis and (5) presentation of findings (see Figure 1). The following stream of reporting elaborates on the activi ties and sub-components of each of these stages.

## 2.1 | Definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria

To ensure the quality of our review, our criteria for inclusion were targeted at works from peer-reviewed sources (see Webster & Watson, 2002). Consequently, books, dissertations, case studies and book reviews were omitted from our review (see Senyo et al., 2019). Following the approach of Lagna and Ravishankar (2022), we made the decision to exclude conference articles as well as many of them have been further developed into the journal article that are already included in our sample, so the overlaps and potential for double counting would have been signifi cant. We also defined our search terms as ‘Fintech’, ‘Financial Technology’ and ‘Financial Technologies’ to capture as broadly as possible, the articles related to our phenomenon of interest.

To ensure the relevance of the articles included in our dataset, we refined our sample by manually reviewing the title abstract and keywords of the sampled articles (see Günther et al., 2017). ## 2.4 | Analysis and synthesis

The analysis and synthesis of the dataset begins with an in-depth reading of each sampled article (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014). A narrative strategy (see A. Langley, 1999) was then adopted where a member of ou research team would create textual summaries of each article that were subsequently checked by the othe members. Based on these summaries. the sampled articles were iteratively grouped by affinity into a number of research themes (see similar approach in Lagna & Ravishankar, 2022). Where there was disagreement about how an article should be categorised, the research team would review the focal article carefully, and either nego tiate consensus to resolve the conflicting categorisation, or modify the emergent set of themes (i.e., creating new themes, or modifying/clustering existing themes) if an article cannot be clearly categorised. When the latter occurs, the categorisation exercise will be restarted to examine the previously classified articles against th newly created or modified themes.

This iterative process is in line with Webster and Watson's (2002) recommendation for a concept-centric review and as will be elaborated in our subsequent Findings section culminated in the synthesis of four research themes There was a fifth category of articles that we labelled as Others because they were broader in scope and did not fit with any of the research themes. These include agenda setting works, literature reviews and research opinion (e.g., Currie & Seddon, 2017; Mention, 2019). With the classification of existing works in place to assist with the identification of conceptual connections and disconnections, we performed a critical assessment of the articles to identify explicit or hidden contradictions and missing explanations, and thereby identify or construct white spots or gaps’ (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014, p. 267). We then proceeded to problematise the literature domain b scrutinising the existing assumptions (see Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011) surrounding the strategies of fintech platforms and the competitive environment in which they operate, before developing the arguments and suggestions fo future research that we present in the following stream of reporting (in line with the prescriptions of Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014).

## 3 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 3.1 | The existing research on fintech and Fintech Strategies

From our review of the literature, the emerging works on fintech can be broadly classified into four main themes (refer to Table 1). The first theme is centered on Fintech Drivers, and studies in this area are typically seeking to identify the factors the facilitate the development of the overall fintech movement, the adoption of fintech, as wel as the potential inhibitors of these outcomes (e.g., Ma & McGroarty, 2017; Muthukannan et al., 2020). The second theme, which the majority of existing studies fall under (see Gomber et al., 2018b), focusses on Fintech Operations This stream of research attempts to shed light on how the operations of various fintech businesses should be structured and how fintech innovations emerge (e.g., D'Acunto et al., 2019; Gozman et al., 2018). The third theme i Fintech Implications. Studies aligned with this theme are generally aiming to identify the economic and social implica tions of fintech, both positive and negative, and provide an explanation for how these implications arise (e.g., Foley et al., 2019; Gomber et al., 2018a). The fourth and final theme is centered on Fintech Strategies, and studies aligned with this theme are typically seeking to uncover the competitive strategies and business models that are effective for fintech firms, as well as the consequences of those strategies (e.g., Chong et al., 2019; Kazan et al., 2018). For a summary of selected articles aligned with these themes, please refer to Appendix B.

Our classification of the existing works on fintech suggests that Fintech Strategies is a theme that has received comparatively less attention to date. And more importantly, a closer examination of the articles aligned with thi theme suggests that many of them tend to be descriptive (e.g., Chong et al., 2019; Gimpel et al., 2018; I. Lee & Shin, 2018) and/or based solely on anecdotal evidence (e.g., Broby, 2021; Dhar & Stein, 2017). For instance, Dha and Stein (2017) presented a compelling argument for the integration of technology and business processes while maintaining open access to develop a ‘complete’ fintech platform. And yet, notwithstanding the academic and practi cal significance of their suggestions, their arguments are not corroborated by empirical evidence, and the question of how to integrate has not been fully addressed

One exception is the work of Chanias et al. (2019), who theorised on the process through which a digital trans formation strategy may be formulated and continuously shaped, but their findings and prescriptions are limited to the context of traditional financial institutions (i.e., fintech platforms tend to be new ventures that do not have existing structures and processes to transform digitally). Another exception is the work of Kazan et al. (2018), who describe three strategies that can be used by fintech (i.e., mobile payment) platforms depending on their platform profile: (1) the germination strategy, which allows firms to nurture private business networks by capturing valu without intervention from third parties, (2) the orchestration strategy, which emphasises co-opetition (see Bengtsson & Kock, 2000, p. 411) and entails a deliberate platform design that is highly integratable with existing mobile banking applications and (3) the transformation strategy, which is centered on product and process innovation. However, the focus of their work lies in the elaboration of the platform profiles and their antecedents. Consequently, these strategies are only described very briefly. The questions of why these strategies are appropriate, how the strategies should be deployed and how do the strategies contribute to the performance of fintech platforms hav not been adequately addressed in their study.

Overall, in relation to the first of the two possibilities discussed in the previous section, our literature review sug gests that the corpus of published works on Fintech Strategies is limited, which could contribute to a lack of knowl edge among fintech platforms on the strategies that are appropriate and effective for the survival and success of their ventures. More specifically, research on the other themes may be useful for providing indications on how to increase the adoption of fintech platforms (i.e., Fintech Drivers), how the operations of the platforms should be con figured for efficiency and effectiveness (i.e., Fintech Operations) and the economic and social impact of the platform (i.e., Fintech Implications). However, without an adequate understanding of Fintech Strategies, the platforms coul still struggle to navigate the competitive dynamics and adverse market conditions that characterise the global fintech landscape (Chanias et al., 2019). But is a distinct set of strategic prescriptions for fintech platforms required (i.e., the second of the two possibilities discussed earlier)? To address this question, we turn our attention to exploring what the existing literature is saying about the unique characteristics of fintech platforms and the implications of thos characteristics on the formulation and deployment of strategies.

## 3.2 | The unique characteristics of fintech platforms and their strategic implications

From our review of the existing literature, we uncovered at least four unique characteristics of fintech platforms that can have profound implications for their strategies. The first characteristic is that a fintech platform may be either mor of a tech firm or more of financial firm (see Stulz, 2019), but it is nevertheless a hybrid organisation that has to contend with the conflicting demands of this dual identity. To illustrate, Leong et al.'s (2017) study of a microloans platform sug gests that even as the platform was seeking to grow rapidly and exponentially as many digital platforms do, there was a need to temper the pursuit of growth with the typical prudence of a financial firm to minimise operational risk (e.g., probability of loan defaults) and ensure the welfare of the customers that they were serving. In other words fintech platforms are likely to be confronted with competing institutional logics (see Thornton & Ocasio, 2008), and unlike many tech firms or generic digital platforms that employ strategies to prioritise growth and network effects (se Van Alstyne et al., 2016), the strategies of fintech platforms must balance growth with caution so that the risks associ ated with the provision of financial products and services are controlled to an acceptable level (Xiao et al., 2020)

A second unique characteristic of fintech platforms is that as compared to other businesses, their customers ar more likely to stem from the vulnerable segments of society that incumbent financial institutions have traditionall neglected, especially in the context of developing countries (Cortina Lorente & Schmukler, 2018; Realini & Mehta, 2015). As a result of concerns over exploitation, fintech platforms tend to be subjected to a greater extent of public scrutiny (Currie et al., 2018), which implies that the attainment of cognitive legitimacy is crucial. Cognitiv legitimacy is defined as the extent to which a firm is aligned with societal norms and expectations (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994). Without cognitive legitimacy, owners of the resources required by a fintech platform (e.g., investors on a wealth management platform) may be reluctant to transact or even be associated with the firm, which increases the difficulty and costs of resource acquisition (Deephouse & Suchman, 2008). The heightened public scrutiny means that ‘the range of acceptability’ (Deephouse, 1999, p. 152) of a fintech platform's actions is likely to be narrower which can constrain the scope of its strategic options even as it is seeking to differentiate itself from its competitors.

A third unique characteristic of fintech platforms is that they are emerging within, and threatening to disrupt, an industry that is regarded as a key pillar of economic stability (Lagarde, 2018). And because of its societal importance, financial markets all across the globe tend to be heavily protected and regulated and the emergence of fintech inno vations can be perceived as a threat to the extent of control and influence exerted by regulatory authorities and policymakers (Zetsche et al., 2017). A recent example is the failed initial public offering (IPO) of the Chinese fintech giant Ant Financial, which operates the Alipay and Yu'ebao platforms (T. Tan et al., 2020) that have ‘fundamentally changed the way hundreds of millions of Chinese citizens spend, save, invest and borrow money’.<sup>6</sup> A root cause commonly cited for the failure of what has been dubbed ‘the biggest IPO in history’ is that Ant's business model presents systemic finan cial risks that the Chinese government may not be able to control.<sup>7</sup> The case of Ant Financial illustrates that even as fintech platforms may be seeking to set themselves apart from their competitors according to classic strategic thinking (Porter, 1996); there is nevertheless the need for socio-political legitimacy, which is defined as the extent to which a busi ness conforms to recognised principles, or accepted rules and standards (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994). Otherwise, the platforms may not be able to access the life-sustaining resources (e.g., regulatory permits and licences) required for their surviva (Deephouse & Suchman, 2008). In addition, fintech platforms also have to navigate and rapidly adapt to an unpredictabl regulatory landscape, where strict regulations can emerge abruptly to threaten their viability (see Xiao et al., 2020). The strategies employed by fintech platforms must account for both of these considerations.

A fourth unique characteristic of fintech platforms is that the incumbent firms they are threatening to disrupt tend to be larger, more established and more influential on the whole. This is because of the extensive capital and resource requirements associated with setting up a financial institution (Clifton et al., 2017; Morrison & White, 2005), which has traditionally precluded the participation of firms that are smaller or lacking in resources before the fintech era. The scale and influence of the incumbent firms presents an exceptional threat to the fintech platforms because it implies that there are an array of options for responding to the strategies and competitive actions of the fintech platforms at their disposal (P. Langley & Leyshon, 2020), including imitation (Chen et al., 2019) takeovers (Dranev et al., 2019) and strategic partnerships (Muthukannan & Gozman, 2019). This suggests that th strategic horizon of fintech platforms has to be expansive and long-term, and their strategies must account for the measures and countermeasures of the influential incumbents that they are trying to dislodge.

In summary, in relation to the second of the two possibilities discussed in the Introduction section, the unique characteristics of fintech platforms identified in the existing literature suggest that a more nuanced view of their strate gies is called for. More specifically, the traditional prescriptions for strategy may be less relevant or effective in the con text of fintech platforms for a number of reasons. First, the strategies of fintech platforms must strike a balance between the competing institutional logics stemming from its dual identity as both a tech and financial firm (see Stulz. 2019). Second. there is a need for their strategies to maintain cognitive legitimacy even as differentiation ano competitive advantages are sought over their competitors (see Currie et al., 2018). Third, their strategies have to emphasise socio-political legitimacy and the navigation of an unpredictable regulatory landscape (see Zetsch et al., 2017). Fourth, strategic planning for fintech platforms must account for a longer-term perspective that enables the anticipation of, and reaction to, the competitive actions of influential incumbents (see P. Langley & Leyshon, 2020).

## 4 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATION

## 4.1 | The six core logics of strategy

Now that it has been established that (1) the published knowledge on Fintech Strategies is likely to be inadequat and (2) the prescriptions for strategy have to be more nuanced and account for the unique context of fintech plat forms, we will construct a theoretical lens based on an existing typology of strategies to gain an overview of the stra tegic options that are available to a fintech platform, which are discrete choices for competing that can be pursue in isolation (see Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999) or combination (see Zhao et al., 2017). This will lay the groundwork for an evaluation of the findings and prescriptions of existing studies in relation to their relevance to fintech platform strategies. The typology we have selected is a framework consisting of six core logics of strategy (refer to Table 2).

This typology is developed based on prior work by Lengnick-Hall and Wolff (1999) who identified what they ter med the Capability, Guerilla and Complexity Logics. Sambamurthy et al. (2003) relabelled the first two logics as th Logic of Leverage and the Logic of Opportunity respectively and added the Logic of Positioning. The typology was extended further by B. Tan et al. (2010), who added the logics of optimality and social congruence. It is important to note here that these strategic logics are conceptually distinct from the notion of institutional logics (see Reay & Hinings, 2009; Thornton & Ocasio, 2008). A strategic logic refers to ‘a set of articulated principles that specify strategic goals, frames, competencies, and expectations for success’ (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999, p. 1109), while an insti tutional logic refers to ‘the socially constructed, historical patterns of material practices, assumptions, values, belief and rules by which individuals produce and reproduce their material subsistence, organise time and space, and pro vide meaning to their social reality (Thornton & Ocasio, 1999, p. 804).

The reason for selecting this framework is that its coverage is one of the most extensive, and while the six logics may not exhaustively account for all possible strategies, they would represent the more conventional options that are accessible for fintech platforms. In the following stream of reporting, we will describe the six core logics and explain their theoretical underpinnings. We will also present examples drawn from the literature on digital platform to illustrate the workings of each of the core logics, which is the body of knowledge that is likely to yield the most relevant insights since the fintech platforms are a subset, albeit a unique form, of digital platforms (Dhar & Stein, 2017; Gomber et al., 2018a; Kazan et al., 2018).

## 4.1.1 | The Logic of Positioning

The first logic is the Logic of Positioning based on Michael Porter's (1980, 1996) classic conceptualisation of competi tive strategy, which posits that a firm should seek to acquire and defend a unique market position relative to the external environment so as to achieve superior performance (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). This logic emphasises the creatio and maintenance of a novel value proposition that accounts for environmental opportunities and threats (Hoope et al., 2003), which suggests that a firm must have a strong grasp of the industry in which it operates and react to th competitive forces stemming from the constituent stakeholders accordingly (Collis & Montgomery, 2008).

An illustrative example of the Logic of Positioning from the literature on digital platforms is the ‘coring’ strategy described by Gawer and Cusumano (2008, p. 30), which is centered on tailoring the value proposition of a digita platform to solve an essential problem confronting the industry and erecting switching barriers against competing platforms (i.e., acquiring and defending a unique market position). A study of the application of this strategy by th Chinese tech giant Alibaba revealed that beyond enabling a competitive edge, coring can also facilitate the attractio and retention of platform entities (i.e., suppliers, customers and business partners) and the attainment of self sustaining critical mass (B. Tan et al., 2015).

## 4.1.2 | The Logic of Leverage

The second logic is the Logic of Leverage (Sambamurthy et al., 2003) based on the resource-based view of the firm (Wernerfelt, 1984), which suggests that superior performance is a result of leveraging firm-specific strategic resources and capabilities (Barney, 1991). Like the Logic of Positioning, strategies based on the Logic of Leverage are centered on the development of a novel value proposition. The difference, however, lies in how the value proposi tion arises. The former emphasises deliberate market positioning based on a consideration of external opportunities and threats, while the latter focusses on the management of internal strengths and weaknesses (Spanos & Lioukas, 2001). According to the Logic of Leverage, firm-specific resources and capabilities that competitors do not own and would find it hard to imitate should be leveraged to generate unique value and establish sustainable com petitive advantage (Barney, 2017)

In the digital platforms literature, previous studies have shown how firm-specific resources and capabilities suc as an established parent firm, an experienced senior management team, in-depth market knowledge and the backin of institutional investors can provide platforms with competitive advantages (B. Tan et al., 2015; Xiao et al., 2020). This is because these resources and capabilities may not only serve to attract and retain new platform entities (i.e., customers, suppliers and complementors), but these entities can align themselves and coordinate value creatio around the seed proposition of the platform as well (Moore, 1993). In addition, the platform may be able to reinforce its boundaries by allowing the platform entities to utilise those resources and capabilities, which serves as both a par ticipation subsidy and a disincentive for leaving the platform (Rietveld et al., 2019)

## 4.1.3 | The Logic of Opportunity

The third logic is the Logic of Opportunity epitomised by Agility Theory (see Sambamurthy et al., 2003), which empha sises the development and deployment of the capabilities of sensing and responding to market opportunities (Overb et al., 2006). The focus of strategies aligned with this logic is rapid and continuous innovation (D'Aveni, 1994) as asset and competencies are repeatedly recombined to this end for the purpose of disrupting the competitive equilibrium (se Ozalp et al., 2018) and creating a chain of temporary competitive advantages (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000)

Previous studies on digital platforms provide plenty of indications for strategy that are aligned with the Logic of Opportunity. For instance, the study of Cennamo and Santalo (2013) suggests that a software-based platform can seek to stimulate competition among complementors and increase the rate of innovation development. And if this is coupled with complementarity between input control and the extent of modularisation in terms of the platform's extensions, the variety of offerings and the performance of the platform may be enhanced further (Tiwana, 2015) This suggests that when a digital platform is marshalling its platform entities as the lead innovator, strategies aligned with the Logic of Opportunity can influence platform development by introducing a continuous stream of fresh idea that promotes self-renewal and staves off obsolescence (Moore, 1996). Moreover, continuous innovation by the platform may motivate the other platform entities to adapt to the changes or exit the platform. With successive iter ations, the remaining entities develop the capabilities to adapt efficiently and effectively, which results in the devel opment of a robust, self-reinforcing and agile network of entities (Pierce, 2009).

## 4.1.4 | The Logic of Complexity

The fourth logic is the Logic of Complexity that has its roots in Chaos theory (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999). This logic sug gests that superior performance is a result of maintaining the health of a business network and its participating entities which leads to productivity, synergies from diversity and greater durability of the benefits derived. Strategies aligned wit the Logic of Complexity emphasise the manipulation of forces and attractors that create order in a network of organisational relationships; such as shared values, the collective vision, governance mechanisms and interaction channel (Stacey, 1995). This results in a state that has been termed the ‘edge of chaos’ (S. L. Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997, p. 29) in which the focal firm and its business network is primed for coordinated actions and continuous change (Stacey, 1995).

Prescriptions for strategy aligned with the Logic of Complexity are especially prevalent in the digital platform literature due to their networked nature. To cite one example, Huber et al. (2017) suggest that to effectively manage a network of entities and channel them toward a collective goal, there is a need for governance policies and practice that embody platform-wide values instead of the unilateral interests of the platform sponsor, so that all platform entities can develop simultaneously and synergistically. Another example is the work of Rietveld et al. (2019) which suggests that the strategies of a platform sponsor should be centered on selectively subsidising and promotin complementors that are not only ‘best in class’, but can contribute different forms of value. In doing so, th sponsor may be able to increase the loyalty of, and manage the competitive dynamics between, these complementors. Overall, by helping the platform entities develop their own capabilities through direct intervention or providing the tools and interfaces for capability development, the competitiveness of the entire platform is enhanced, which is especially relevant in the present era of inter-network business competition (Iansiti & Levien, 2004).

## 4.1.5 | The logic of optimality

The fifth logic is the logic of optimality based on Hannan and Freeman's (1977) Organisational Ecology, which suggests that firms are subject to the forces of natural selection (Oliver, 1988). Consequently, strategies aligned with this logic are centered on the attainment and retention of an optimal organisational form that is best suited for survival in a given set of environmental conditions (B. Tan et al., 2010). When the optimal organisation form is achieved and recognised by the stakeholders within the firm's external environment, the firm gains cognitive legitimacy (see Aldrich & Fiol, 1994), which makes it easier for the firm to access the life-sustaining resources that it requires (Zhao et al., 2017).

In the digital platforms literature, the prescriptions aligned with this logic is represented by the advocacy of certain traits such as modularity (e.g., Jacobides et al., 2018), openness (e.g., Wessel et al., 2017) and an appropriate pricing structure (e.g., Bakos & Katsamakas, 2008). The argument here is that these traits contribute to stronger platform performance, and if a focal platform does not already possess them, it should seek to acquire them to adapt toward th optimal organisational form, particularly if its competitors have already acquired those traits (Cennamo et al., 2018).

## 4.1.6 | The Logic of Social Congruence

The sixth logic is the Logic of Social Congruence (B. Tan et al., 2010) that stems from Institutional theory (see Tolbert & Zucker, 1999). This logic emphasises the importance of social fitness (Scott, 2014, p. 185), a specifi organisational state that renders a firm congruent with the external pressures acting upon it, including coercive pres sures from regulatory bodies, mimetic pressures from related organisations (e.g., competitors) and normative pres sures associated with professionalisation (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983). With an appropriate response to these pressures, the focal firm attains isomorphism and gains legitimacy (Oliver, 1991) to enhance the ease of resource acquisition (Deephouse & Suchman, 2008).

An example of prescriptions aligned with the Logic of Social Congruence from the literature on digital platforms is the work of Taeuscher and Rothe (2020), who suggest that platforms can attain social fitness and legitimacy by attracting high-status complementors. This approach to achieving legitimacy is distinct from simply acquiescing to social pressures (Zhao et al., 2017), but the association with high-status complementors gives rise to legitimacy nev ertheless because it confers upon the focal platform a ‘stamp of approval’ (Taeuscher & Rothe, 2020, p. 3). Another illustrative example of this logic stems from the work of Ingram Bogusz et al. (2019), who suggest that platforms can be designed from the outset to be aligned with social expectations. This can include mimicking existing relationship and hierarchies through the appropriate use of symbols, catering to the design preferences of key platform stake holders and engaging in sequential legitimacy building by gaining the approval of these stakeholders with priority.

With our theoretical lens (which depicts the conventional options for strategy that fintech platforms can deploy in isolation or combination) in place, we now turn our attention to assessing the findings and prescriptions of the existing fintech literature in juxtaposition to this lens. In doing so, we will be able to gain deeper insights into the limitations of the existing knowledge on fintech platform strategies, which will provide the foundation for the research agenda we aim to propose. As the volume of published works that are explicitly on Fintech Strategies is limited (see Table 1), we will broaden the scope of this exercise to include the findings and prescriptions of the fintech article classified under the other research themes uncovered in our literature review. This will provide a more comprehen sive coverage of what is currently known, and account for the pockets of insights that may be scattered across th existing literature in a holistic and integrative manner.

## 5 | ANALYSIS AND DISCUSSION

## 5.1 | A proposed research agenda for fintech platform strategies

In this section, we will analyse the findings and prescriptions of the existing fintech studies and present example that are aligned with each of the core logics of strategy. This is followed by a discussion of their utility and limitations in relation to the unique characteristics of fintech platforms identified previously

## 5.1.1 | Future research directions aligned with the Logic of Positioning

In relation to the Logic of Positioning, an example of the findings and prescriptions in the sampled articles of our lit erature review that are aligned with this logic is the ‘transformation strategy’ described by Kazan et al. (2018 p. 212) that can set apart a fintech firm from its competitors ‘through differentiated and cost-effective arrange ments’. They argue that the transformation strategy can be achieved through the establishment of a novel value delivery architecture that is distinct from those of existing competitors, which implies the occupation and subse quent defence of a unique market position (see Porter, 1996). In a similar vein, the studies that identify and prescribe specific business models for the various forms of fintech are also aligned with the Logic of Positioning. To cit another example from our sampled articles, Chong et al. (2019) identified the (1) platformer, (2) disintermediator, (3) mediator, (4) transformer and (5) co-innovator business models in their study of the commercial applications of Blockchain. These models are aligned with the Logic of Positioning because they represent the different ways of cre ating value, which can be configured along a vast array of dimensions to deliver a unique value proposition and account for contextual influences (Gimpel et al., 2018)

The sophistication and applicability of the findings and prescriptions aligned with this logic, however, may be improved if considerations of the unique traits of fintech platforms are incorporated. For instance, the dynamic and unpredictable regulatory landscape (see Zetsche et al., 2017) discussed previously suggests that the environmenta opportunities and threats that confront fintech platforms are constantly evolving. Consequently, an important ques tion that must be addressed is how fintech platforms can continuously make sense of this environmental flux and craft a value proposition that is not only unique in the moment, but can co-evolve with the changing external condi tions as well (see Muthukannan et al., 2020). In addition, the dual identity of a fintech platform (see Leong et al., 2017) suggests that a more careful consideration of the objectives of occupying and defending a market posi tion is needed. For example, should the market position and its associated value proposition be aimed at attractin as many customers and suppliers as quickly as possible for exponential growth (see Van Alstyne et al., 2016) or should considerations of financial risks take precedence? The very definition of an attractive market position may have to be carefully rethought. Finally, the presence of incumbent firms with ‘deep pockets’ (Haddad & Hornuf, 2019, p. 96) would complicate the defence of any attractive market position captured by a fintech platform How a smaller, more resource-constrained fintech platform can defend its occupied market position from the influ ential incumbents would also be an important avenue for future research.

## 5.1.2 | Future research directions aligned with the Logic of Leverage

With regard to the Logic of Leverage, one prominent example of the findings and prescriptions that are aligned wit this logic are those of studies that explore how established tech giants are entering the global fintech arena (se P. Langley & Leyshon, 2020). To illustrate with an example from our sampled articles, the work of T. Tan et al. (2020 presented an account of how Ant Financial leveraged firm-specific resources and capabilities in the form of the mas sive user base and accumulated technological capabilities of their parent company (i.e., Alibaba) to develop Yu'e Bao a wealth management platform that grew to become one of the largest and most widely used in China. More specifi cally, they explained that ‘Yu'e Bao elicits high levels of trust from grassroots consumers because it is embedded in Alipay, which has spent ten years building its credibility’ (Alipay is Alibaba's mobile payment platform – see T. Tan et al., 2020, p. 189). This suggests that fintech platforms may have access to certain resources and capabilities that their competitors (i.e., smaller fintech start-ups and incumbent financial institutions in the case of Yu'e Bao) do not possess and cannot imitate (see Haddad & Hornuf, 2019), and the leverage of these resources and capabilities can provide a clear competitive edge to contribute towards the commercial success of a fintech platform.

As in the case of the previous logic, the findings and prescriptions aligned with the Logic of Leverage can be more nuanced if the unique characteristics of fintech platforms are accounted for. For instance, the classic conceptualisation of the resources and capabilities that can generate competitive advantages suggests that they have to be valuable, rare inimitable and organised for exploitation (Barney & Clark, 2007). But in the light of a fintech platform's dual identity what does it mean for a fintech platform that is primarily more of a tech firm (e.g., T. Tan et al., 2020), or more of a financial firm (e.g., a fintech subsidiary set up by an incumbent financial institution - see Zhang et al., 2020)? It is likel that the strategic resources and capabilities at the platforms' disposal will be different in the two contexts because the are path dependent (Schreyögg & Kliesch-Eberl, 2007). And what if the fintech platform is a startup that has to contend with the more established and resource-rich incumbent firms? The leverage of strategic resources and capabilities b fintech startups, and how they can prevent the influential incumbents from eroding the resultant advantages with thei competitive actions (see Muthukannan & Gozman, 2019) have not been adequately explored to date. In addition, if fintech platforms are subject to greater public and regulatory scrutiny (Currie et al., 2018), what are the implications of this narrower range of acceptability (see Deephouse, 1999) for the competitive actions that arise from the leverage of strategic resources and capabilities? Addressing these areas would provide a fuller understanding of the resources and capabilities that should be prioritised and exploited for a variety of fintech platforms

## 5.1.3 | Future research directions aligned with the Logic of Opportunity

For the Logic of Opportunity, an example of the findings and prescriptions among our sampled articles that are aligned with this logic are those of studies on the mechanisms of fintech innovation. To illustrate with an exampl from our sampled articles, Gozman et al. (2018) described the mechanisms of disintermediation, access extension financialisation, hybridisation and personalisation for effecting innovation. They noted that these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive but are instead mutually reinforcing and interdependent (see Gozman et al., 2018, p. 174) which suggests that each mechanism represents a discrete option that can be employed in combination or on it own by a fintech platform for effecting and sustaining a continuous stream of innovations (see Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). The aforementioned study of Kazan et al. (2018) provides another example of a prescription aligned with the Logic of Opportunity in their recommendations for how a fintech platform can configure its value creatio and delivery architectures. This configuration process is implied to be aligned with the Logic of Opportunity becaus according to Kazan and his colleagues, these architectures would influence how fintech platforms engage their net work partners in the co-production of innovations. And through a combination of modular and architectural innova tion, a fintech platform and its network of business partners may be able to disrupt the competitive equilibrium an erode the competitiveness of the market incumbents (see Sambamurthy et al., 2003)

But as discussed earlier, the unique characteristics of fintech platforms may render continuous innovation and sustaining a series of temporary competitive advantages exceptionally challenging. On one hand, the influentia incumbents who have been disrupted by the fintech firms can be expected to respond vigorously (Chen et al., 2019) which increases the pressure on fintech platforms to innovate even more quickly to stay ahead, while simultaneousl trying to avoid costly innovation failures (see Jinasena et al., 2020). On the other hand, the customers of fintech platforms may be from the more vulnerable segments of society and severely resource-constrained (Cortina Lorente & Schmukler, 2018; Realini & Mehta, 2015), which suggests that engaging them in the co-production of innovation may not be straightforward. Moreover, the pursuit of innovations tends to unfold under greater public and regula tory scrutiny (Currie et al., 2018), which could hinder the process or make its diminishing rate of returns even more salient (see Taeuscher & Rothe, 2020). Accounting for these considerations could yield deeper insights into th nature and mechanisms of fintech innovation, and how the latter can be recombined or adapted to enhance the per formance of a fintech platform.

## 5.1.4 | Future research directions aligned with the Logic of Complexity

In relation to the Logic of Complexity, an example of the findings and prescriptions in the fintech literature that are aligned with this logic is the ‘orchestration strategy’ described in the work of Kazan et al. (2018, p. 213). This strategy emphasises inclusivity and the management of co-opetitive dynamics to align the interests of a fintech platform with those of its net work partners and coordinate value delivery to enhance their joint market reach. In a similar vein, the studies of fintech eco system development among our sampled articles are typically aligned with the Logic of Complexity as well. For instance Muthukannan et al. (2020) described how the conditions within a fintech ecosystem can be made conducive for its constit uent entities through the mechanisms of (1) adaptive tensions, (2) self-organisation, (3) emergent structuration, (4) resource accumulation, (5) strategic localisation and (6) collectivisation. They argue that these mechanisms should be selectively acti vated at specific points of the fintech ecosystem's development, which would enable fintech platforms and their networ partners to mature and co-evolve in a synergistic and concurrent manner (see Leong et al., 2017)

The findings and prescriptions aligned with this logic, however, can be sharpened by accounting for some of the unique characteristics of fintech platforms discussed earlier. For instance, if fintech platforms have to interact and transact with a wider array of entities because of its dual identity (I. Lee & Shin, 2018), a key question that is yet to be addressed is how fintech platforms can manage their relationships and interactions with these entities, while catering to what woul naturally be a more diverse range of interests. Moreover, in relation to entities such as the market incumbents or regula tors that that wield significant influence over the fintech platforms, future studies could explore how to manage the co opetitive dynamics with the former (Kazan et al., 2018) and maintain the goodwill and support of the latte (Muthukannan et al., 2020). Conversely, for peripheral entities that have less influence but are nevertheless part of the fintech platforms' business network, future studies can also look into how fintech platforms can help these entities, partic ularly those that are resource constrained, to develop their capabilities so that they are able to contribute more meaning fully toward a collective cause (see B. Tan et al., 2015). The management of the relationships with both influential an peripheral entities will be crucial to the fintech platforms' ability to harness collective resources and generate synergies which could be especially crucial given the realities of the present networked economy (see Rietveld et al., 2020).

## 5.1.5 | Future research directions aligned with the logic of optimality

With regard to the logic of optimality, the findings and prescriptions among our sampled articles that are aligned with this logic include those of studies that are centered on specific features or traits that are held to be ideal for fintech platforms. One of these studies is that of Ondrus et al. (2015), who argue for the importance of selective openness, where openness specifically in terms of service provision, technology interoperability and user interaction woul increase the market potential of a fintech platform. In other words, they are suggesting that most, if not all, fintech platforms should be open on the provider, technology and user levels as this would translate to numerous benefits including greater access to shared resources, the distribution of the burden and costs of infrastructure development the realisation of synergies with interoperability, as well as the generation of innovation and value co-creatio opportunities. Another of these studies is that of Leong et al. (2017), who describe the development of a digita hybrid model at a Chinese fintech platform, which combined both product and service offerings to minimise operational risks and lock in the platform's customers. The digital hybrid model is prescribed as one that fintech platform should consider because it will create a closed ecosystem that keeps the entities of a fintech platform within its boundaries and reduces the necessity of multi-homing (see Barua & Mukherjee, 2021). Both of these studies from our sampled articles resonate with, and extend, Dhar and Stein's (2017) advocacy of integrating technology, business processes and open access.

## 5.1.7 | Overarching directions for future research

In summary, our review of the findings and prescriptions related to fintech platform strategies in the existing literature (summarised in Figure 2) suggests there are a number of significant research gaps that future studies on the topic should be looking to address. Toward laying the groundwork for these studies, we will conclude this section by proposing a number of overarching directions for future research on top of the suggestions aligned with each logic of strategy presented earlier (see Table 3 for an overview of our proposed research agenda). I doing so, we hope that this will contribute toward a more sophisticated and holistic understanding of fintech platform strategies.

First, our review suggests that there has been little research attention to date on the implications of the dua identity that fintech platforms are inevitably confronted with (see Stulz, 2019). To address this gap, future studie can explore how the competing institutional logics associated the dual identity can be effectively managed. As dis cussed earlier, this knowledge is important because it will have profound implications for the fintech platforms' mar ket positioning, their leverage of firm-specific resources and capabilities, the stakeholder interests that they have to cater to, as well as the optimal traits that they may seek to acquire.

Second, fintech platforms tend to be subject to a greater extent of public scrutiny (Currie et al., 2018), but how they can navigate the potentially conflicting objectives of conforming for legitimacy and differentiating for competi tive advantage has not been examined to a significant degree. Addressing this gap could provide indications to fintech platforms on combining and striking a balance between the strategies that would differentiate them from their competitors, and those that would make them more similar (e.g., acquiring optimal traits or acquiescing to socia pressures), which would be crucial to enhancing their market performance and ensuring their survival (Zhao et al., 2017).

Third, while it is generally acknowledged that fintech platforms across the globe tend to be facing regulatory uncertainty (see Zetsche et al., 2017), there has been little research to date on how the dynamism and unpredictability of the regulatory landscape can be managed. Given that sociopolitical legitimacy (see Aldrich & Fiol, 1994) is ultimately what is sought by the fintech platforms in responding to, or complying with, regulations there is a need for a deeper understanding of how sociopolitical legitimacy can be achieved and maintained against the backdrop of a regulatory landscape that is constantly evolving.

Fourth, as the market incumbents that fintech platforms have to contend with tend to be large, established and influential entities (see Clifton et al., 2017; Morrison & White, 2005), this suggests that the strategies of fintech platforms will have to account for the incumbents' responses and countermeasures, which can run the gamut from being aggressive (see Dranev et al., 2019) to cooperative (see Muthukannan & Gozman, 2019). The vast range of potential responses (P. Langley & Leyshon, 2020) suggests that future studies should look into how fintech platforms can anticipate or rapidly react to the competitive actions of the influential incumbents, which will ensure that the are able to retain their competitive edge as opposed to being crowded out by the incumbents' resources and inher ent advantages.

Finally, our review of the literature suggests that the existing findings and prescriptions pertaining to fintech platform strategies tend to be aligned with singular logics and do not account for how the strategies may be com bined, evolve over time, or selectively adopted under certain contingencies. Addressing this gap is important because many of the unique characteristics of fintech platforms suggest that the formulation and deployment of an appropri ate strategy is a moving target. Consequently, future studies can investigate how strategies tend to evolve as th fintech platforms grow and mature along their developmental trajectory across progressive levels of maturity. The specific Fintech Strategies that are most appropriate to the different types of fintech platforms (e.g., payments, financing and wealth management – see Haddad & Hornuf, 2019) and contextual conditions would also be a fertil ground for future research, since it is highly unlikely that a single strategic logic, or combination of logics, will be universally effective under all circumstances (B. Tan et al., 2010).

## 6 | CONCLUSION

## 6.1 | Summary

At the outset of this article, we noted that the failure rate of fintech platforms is disproportionately high (Muthukannan et al., 2020), especially in comparison to other forms of new ventures (see Levie et al., 2011). As the strategy of a firm is typically regarded as one of the key determinants of its survivability and profitability (Bloc et al., 2014; Linton & Kask, 2017), we proposed that the extraordinary failure rate could be due to two possibilities First, there may be a lack of published knowledge for fintech platforms to consult and refer to on the strategies that could ensure the survival and success of their ventures. Second, the traditional prescriptions for strategy may be les relevant or effective in the context of fintech platforms.

To ascertain either or both of these possibilities, we first conducted a comprehensive review of the literature, which revealed that the existing articles on fintech can be conceptually classified into four main themes. In addition by focussing on the works that are explicitly centered on the theme of Fintech Strategies, we were able to establish that not only is there a relative paucity of research on the topic, but there are important limitations associated with these works (e.g., they are primarily descriptive in nature and/or not corroborated by empirical evidence). This sug gests that in relation to the first possibility, the existing literature is poorly equipped to provide indications on th strategies that are appropriate and effective for fintech platforms.

Next, by identifying and delving into four specific characteristics of fintech platforms that have been discussed in the literature and reflecting on their implications for the platforms' strategies, we have established that the tradi tional prescriptions for strategy may be less relevant or effective in the context of fintech platforms in relation to th second possibility. Based on these characteristics, we then elaborated on why fintech platform strategies shoul account for (1) competing institutional logics (see Stulz, 2019), (2) the tensions between conforming for legitimacy and differentiating for competitive advantage (see Currie et al., 2018), (3) a dynamic and unpredictable regulatory landscape (see Zetsche et al., 2017) and (4) the responses and competitive actions of influential market incumbent (see P. Langley & Leyshon, 2020).

Finally, by constructing a theoretical lens based on an established typology of strategies and mapping the find ings and prescriptions of existing fintech studies to this theoretical lens, we developed a research agenda consisting of a range of open research questions that are important to address to advance our understanding of the topic.

## 6.2 | Theoretical and practical implications

Our work has significant implications for theory and practice. In relation to the former, while there have been a hand ful of other literature reviews published on the fintech phenomenon in recent years (e.g., Fosso Wamba et al., 2020 Knight & Wojcik, 2020), the focus of our work on fintech platform strategies is unique. As such, our review has not only complemented these works to provide a fuller perspective of the state of IS-related fintech articles, but we hav revealed a number of white spots or gaps (Boell & Cecez-Kecmanovic, 2014, p. 267) in the existing literature tha are specific to our focal topic as well. For example, we found that many of the relevant studies tend to be descriptiv (e.g., Chong et al., 2019; Gimpel et al., 2018; I. Lee & Shin, 2018) and/or unsubstantiated by empirical evidence (e.g., Broby, 2021; Dhar & Stein, 2017). By presenting a systematic blueprint for future research covering a broad range of issues in relation to fintech platform strategies, we have not only charted a course for addressing these knowledge gaps, but we have established the case for the necessity and significance of further research in this area. In doing so, our efforts could catalyse and provide directions for future research, and contribute to a more sophisti cated, relevant and comprehensive understanding of the strategic options that are available to fintech platforms

In addition, by synthesising the pockets of insights that are scattered across the existing fintech literature, ou review has produced a number of novel insights and conceptual innovations. For instance, not only does our work represent one the earliest in the IS discipline to discuss the unique characteristics of fintech platforms in an in-dept and integrative manner, but to the best of our knowledge, it is also the first to relate these characteristics to the plat forms' strategic options. The contextualisation of the conventional options for strategy (see Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; B. Tan et al., 2010) to the realm of fintech platforms, highlighting the assumptions and prescriptions that must be reconsidered or accounted for more fully, is a distinctive contribution as well. The arguments and insights presented in our work are therefore important because not only are they mor nuanced and tailored to this emerging business form, but they suggest that our existing understanding of competitiv strategies should be critically re-examined and adapted for the fintech era.

Finally, our work also extends the discourse initiated in the literature review of Lagna and Ravishankar, who urged that ‘there are opportunities to study business strategies and their operationalization’ (Lagna & Ravishankar, 2022 p. 69). While their focus is on the very real potential of fintech for promoting financial inclusion (Leong et al., 2020), it should be acknowledged that the majority of fintech offerings are designed and catered to customers in develope markets who are technologically savvy and financially literate (Fuster et al., 2019). These customers are primarily looking for convenience (Ryu, 2018) and can be considered relatively wealthy (e.g., Ioannou & Wojcik, 2022). In this sense, the scope of the arguments and prescriptions presented in our article may be considered to be more general as they are relevant to fintech platforms that are profit or socially oriented, in both developed and developing contexts Moreover, juxtaposing the underlying assumptions of Lagna and Ravishankar's (2022) and our review brings up the classic dilemma that confronts organisations with a social orientation (Smith et al., 2013), which is what should com first: the organisation's business or pro-social objectives? Lagna and Ravishankar (2022) appear to suggest that strateg making should be approached with financial inclusion firmly in mind, while our work echoes the arguments of studie conducted on other digital platforms (e.g., F. T. C. Tan et al., 2017) in suggesting that the realisation of a fintech plat form's pro-social objectives would naturally follow the attainment of business viability. A comparison of the implica tions and relative advantages of both of these approaches (i.e., to pursue financial inclusion explicitly and deliberatel or not) would certainly be a worthwhile topic for future fintech scholars to investigate

With regard to its practical implications, our work can have a significant impact on at least two groups of stakeholders relevant to the fintech space. The first stakeholder group would, of course, consist of the owners and managers of fintech platforms. For this stakeholder group, our work has not only provided indications of the strategi options that are available to them, but the additional considerations that have to be accounted for as they pursue strategies aligned with a particular logic or combination of logics. For instance, applying the Logic of Positioning, fintech platform may be able to carve a lucrative niche within the financial services sector for itself. However, th open questions listed in our proposed research agenda suggest that the occupation of an attractive market position may be transient unless it can defend that position from influential incumbents (see Muthukannan et al., 2020) or adapt its business rapidly to regulatory changes (see Zetsche et al., 2017). This suggests that a combination of strate gic logics, taking into account the unique characteristics of fintech platforms and the prevailing contextual condi tions, may be required. One possibility, for instance, could be to leverage complementary business partners to fill th gaps in the focal platform's portfolio of resources and capabilities (i.e., incorporating the Logics of Leverage and Complexity).

In addition, our agenda suggests that even if a platform has achieved and is able to sustain, differentiation vis-à-vis its competitors, it has to effectively combine logics because cognitive and sociopolitical legitimac (see Aldrich & Fiol, 1994) may be crucial as well, especially when the regulatory environment remains in a state of flux (see Xiao et al., 2020). In other words, a platform must maintain a delicate balance between trying to stand apart from its competitors and conforming to the expectations, regulations and norms of the stake holders in its organisational environment (Taeuscher & Rothe, 2020). By providing a more nuanced view of the strategic options that are available, our work should be especially useful for the majority of fintech platforms that are facing existential challenges (see Muthukannan et al., 2020) because it can help them identify th appropriate actions or remedial measures to take in order to set their ventures on the path of commercia success.

The second stakeholder group that can be potentially informed by our study consists of the regulators and policymakers who are charged with overseeing the financial sector. While the innovations associated with the emerging fintech platforms have the potential to bring about significant economic and social benefits (Gozma et al., 2018; Leong et al., 2017), if the majority of these platforms are failing (Dietz et al., 2015; Finextra, 2018) it could mean that this potential may be diminished or left unrealised. For this stakeholder group, our study can help them to identify ways of better supporting the fintech platforms, such as providing advice on strategy or establishing a regulatory sandbox to encourage innovation where sociopolitical legitimacy is granted by default (see E. Brown & Piroska, 2021). This could serve to reduce the likelihood of commercial failure, actualise th potential for economic and social gains and safeguard the collective resources that have been invested in th fintech platforms.
