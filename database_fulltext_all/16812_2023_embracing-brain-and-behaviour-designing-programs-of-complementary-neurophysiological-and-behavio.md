## Abstract

NeuroIS—the methods and knowledge of neuroscience applied to the information systems (IS) domain—has become an established research field within the IS discipline. A key advantage of NeuroIS is its ability to provide insights into human cognition beyond those obtained using behavioural techniques alone. Nevertheless, in neuroscience, there is renewed interest in examining behaviour together with neurophysiological methods to better inform our understanding of neural processes. In this research opinion article, we argue that in the field of NeuroIS, there is an opportunity for hybrid programs of study that combine neurophysiological and behavioural methods in a complementary manner. We outline four strategies for designing complementary neurophysiological and behavioural experiments in a research program: (1) observe the relationship between neural processes and behavioural change; (2) combine neurophysiological and behavioural methods to enhance internal, external, and ecological validity; (3) extend, rather than replicate, experiments based on theory; and (4) use neurophysiological and behavioural experiments together to evaluate IT artefact design. By applying these strategies, researchers can more effectively design programs using complementary neurophysiological and behavioural methods, which, in turn, can help to provide richer insights into the phenomena under study as well as accelerate the advancement of IS knowledge.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

NeuroIS—the methods and knowledge of neuroscience applied to the information systems domain—has become an established research field within the IS discipline since its introduction in 2007 (Riedl et al., 2017, p. 700). A key advantage of NeuroIS is its ability to provide insight into human cognition and affect beyond that obtained using behavioural techniques alone. Prior to the advent of modern cognitive neurophysiological methods,<sup>1</sup> cognitive scientists and psychologists relied heavily on the observation of behaviour to infer cognitive and affective processes (Miller, 2003). In contrast, cognitive neuroscientists today can observe neural activity directly and infer these pro cesses without any reference to behaviour. Nevertheless, there has been renewed interest in the field of neurosci ence in terms of combining behavioural observations together with other neuroscientific methods to better inform our understanding of the neural processes (Berman, 2018; Gomez-Marin & Ghazanfar, 2019). For example, a review by Krakauer et al. (2017) in the neuroscience literature explicated the need for neuroscience studies to emplo measures of both behavioural and brain processes in a complementary manner.

In view of this trend, there is an opportunity for NeuroIS researchers to use hybrid programs of research that complementarily combine neurophysiological methods with behavioural methods, such as field and laboratory experiments. This approach allows researchers to explain cognitive and affective processes from a neural perspective while also observing downstream behavioural effects. It also has the potential to enhance research programs in several dimensions, including the conceptual, methodological, theoretical, and—through the evaluation of IT artefact design—artifactual. However, despite these advantages, our search of the IS literature reveals that scholars rarel combine neurophysiological and behavioural methods in the same research study. One reason for this may be a lack of guidance from the literature. Although guidelines exist for conducting behavioural studies (Dennis & Valacich, 2001; Straub et al., 2004) and NeuroIS studies (Dimoka et al., 2012; Riedl & Léger, 2016; vom Brocke & Liang, 2014), there is little guidance on effectively combining the two in a single research program. Similar but dis tinct approaches have been made for combining neuroscientific approaches with computational model (Kriegeskorte & Douglas, 2018) and real-world behavioural paradigms (Matusz et al., 2019).

We respond to the call of Krakauer et al. (2017) by developing strategies for designing and conducting complementary experiments using both behavioural and neurophysiological methods within a single research program These strategies are listed in Table 1.

We derived these strategies through years of designing and conducting complementary neurophysiological and behavioural experiments, and they are thus distillations of the lessons we have learned. In addition, we solicited feedback to refine these strategies via workshops and conferences

We provide concrete examples of how these four strategies may be implemented by highlighting articles in bot the NeuroIS and consumer neuroscience literature, the latter of which features a more frequent combination of neu rophysiological and behavioural methods in a single study. We selected these papers for two reasons. First, each implements at least one of the four strategies of combining neurophysiological and behavioural methods in a complementary way. Second, each article was published in a different premier journal in the IS or marketing fields and thus was vetted for its methodological rigour and theoretical contribution.

## 2 | REVIEW OF KEY STUDIES OF NEUROIS AND RELATED SUBFIELDS

Before presenting our research strategies, we first indicate the need for our approach by reviewing key studies in the domain of NeuroIS—the methods and knowledge of neuroscience applied to the information systems (IS)—and related ‘neuro’ subfields. In so doing, we follow Alvesson and Sandberg's (2011) problematization methodology and typology of assumptions.

In particular, we examine the root metaphor assumption type to identify and evaluate assumptions underlyin the existing NeuroIS literature. Root metaphor assumptions are

…associated with broader images of a particular subject matter. Within management studies, for example, it is common to see organisations as ‘cultures’ in terms of a unitary set of values and beliefs shared by organisation members. However, at the root metaphor level, authors have questioned assumptions around unity, uniqueness, and consensus, and they have emphasised differentiation, fragmentation, discontinuity, and ambiguity as key elements in culture. (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 255

Thus, root metaphor assumptions fundamentally colour how scholars in a stream of literature understand a topic or phenomenon. Because of the fundamental nature of root metaphors, the assumptions underlying them are often not recognised or are taken for granted

However, once a root metaphor in the literature is identified, it can be challenged by ‘detecting or producin alternative possible confrontational metaphors’ (Alvesson & Sandberg, 2011, p. 257) that contrast with the root met aphor and promote different understandings. When an alternative metaphor is proposed, the assumptions of th root metaphor are revealed and can be reevaluated. This exercise can lead to the identification of opportunities fo advancing research in an area.

## 2.1 | The root metaphor of ‘opening the black box of the brain

A root metaphor used in seminal articles of NeuroIS and related neuro disciplines is that the brain is a black box that can only be opened and understood through neurophysiological tools. Table 2 provides a sample listing of arti cles that have used this metaphor.<sup>2</sup> For example, Camerer et al. (2004) explain that neuroeconomics ‘opens up the “black box” of the brain’, and Moore (2005) suggests that consumer neuroscience takes scientists ‘closer to opening the black box of the consumer's mind . Likewise, in a NeuroIS context, Dimoka et al. (2007) state that advances in neuroimaging techniques now make the direct and objective measurement of human thoughts, beliefs, and feeling possible for the first time, opening the “black box” of the human brain’ (emphasis added).

The black box root metaphor has coloured subsequent NeuroIS literature that references these seminal articles and perpetuates its use. To name a few, Hannah et al. (2013) argue that the cognitive revolution in leadership and organizational studies has brought greater attention to the mental processes of leaders’, but ‘this revolution has been limited largely to conjecture of what occurs inside the “black box” of leaders’; Quazilbash et al. (2019), suggest they ‘contribute towards progressing NeuroIS research by using neurophysiological tools to report human behavior in IS [and] thereby help in opening the black box of human brain and thus in designing better systems with improved perceived usefulness’; and Luna-Nevarez (2021) states that ‘[s]cientists and marketers are most interested in prying into the subconscious mind, which remains largely a black box, but it's where all the action is’. Clearly, ‘opening the black box of the brain’ is a major motivation for using neuroscience tools in IS research.

An implicit assumption of the black box root metaphor in NeuroIS and related neuro-management disciplines is that without neurophysiological methods, we cannot understand what is happening inside the black box. In reality, however, there are two ways to understand the black box of the brain: (1) through examining it directly using neuro physiological methods (i.e., opening the black box) and (2) by examining its inputs and outputs as manifested through behaviour. While opening the black box of the brain using neurophysiological methods is immensely valuable, our assertion is that combining behavioural observation with neurophysiological methods can increase understanding overall.

To be fair, none of the articles listed in Table 2 argue for the exclusion of behavioural methods. However, we argue that the black box root metaphor leads to a focus on neurophysiological methods that can overlook the valu of behavioural methods to complement neurophysiological methods. Indeed, our review of the IS literature reveals that scholars rarely combine neurophysiological and behavioural experiments within the same research study or stream. 3

## 2.2 | The alternative metaphor of the car mechanic

Following the methodology of Alvesson and Sandberg (2011), we propose the metaphor of a car mechanic as an alternative to the black box metaphor. In this metaphor, in addition to looking under the hood to examine a car engine directly in order to diagnose a problem, the mechanic drives the vehicle, listens, and observes how it performs. The two approaches—of inspecting the engine directly and observing how it performs—are complementar and using them both enables trained mechanics to more accurately pinpoint the problem than does using eithe approach alone.

## 2.3 | Complementary strengths of neurophysiological and behavioural methods

We define neurophysiological methods as those which measure physiological processes that may occur outside conscious awareness. In NeuroIS research, these typically include measures of neural correlates using functional mag netic resonance imaging (fMRI) or electroencephalograms (EEGs) but can also include other, more overt (physically external) measures, as gained by the tracking of eye movements, for example. Common to all these measures is the identification of physiological processes that are not apparent from the (everyday) observation of behaviour. In othe words, these techniques give information on how the brain responds to specific task demands and contributes to specific behaviours. Neurophysiological methods are able to provide important insights that are complementary to those afforded by more traditional behavioural methods. Mather et al. (2013), list several of these insights, namely as enabling (1) the functional localization of cognitive functions, (2) specification of the markers of mental processe involved when participants perform specific tasks, (3) identification of the distinctiveness of representations of dif ferent cognitive states, tasks, or representations, and (4) clarification of whether two tasks or cognitive states engage similar or distinct neural circuits or representations (e.g., trust vs. distrust in Dimoka (2010)). Additionally, neurophys iological methods can give insights into individual differences and how these might relate to other physiological o demographic markers. Finally, they allow us to test the predictions of cognitive or computational models of how th brain approaches specific cognitive tasks

In contrast, we define behavioural methods as those that record overt or volitional action on the part of research participants. These methods typically involve either observation by the researcher or self-report by the participant and might include laboratory-based tasks, naturalistic observation, field studies, or surveys. Compared to neurophysiologica methods in general, behavioural methods have practical advantages, such as their relatively low cost and ability to b administered remotely and at scale. Additionally, they have conceptual advantages, such as affording access to introspec tion (e.g., response confidence or thought processes). Behavioural methods might include (combinations of) computer based tasks (in the laboratory or on the participants' own devices), questionnaires or surveys, or qualitative interviews.

## 3 | DESIGNING A RESEARCH PROGRAM WITH COMPLEMENTARY NEUROPHYSIOLOGICAL AND BEHAVIOURAL STUDIES

In this section, we outline some strategies and provide related practical suggestions for designing and implementing programs involving complementary neurophysiological and behavioural studies. These strategies have been selected for both theoretical and practical reasons, and each capitalises on the different strengths of their associated methods. We emphasise at the outset that neither the strategies nor the associated practical suggestions are meant to be used as ‘checklists’ or criteria in the review process for individual articles. Rather, these strategies ar intended for application at the research program level, which may encompass multiple research articles

## 3.1 | Strategy 1: Observe the relationship between neural processes and behavioural change

The first and possibly most fundamental strategy for incorporating complementary neurophysiological and behav ioural methods is to observe the relationship between neural processes and behavioural change. Typically, the term ‘behavioural change’ refers to differences in behavioural responses in the context of an experiment (e.g., choosing one option over another or a difference in reaction times in an experimental task). More generally, this could also refer to changes over the medium- or long-term, such as changes in an individual's stress management strategy or long-term habituation to repeated stimuli. The neuroscience literature has recently shown renewed interest in incor porating behavioural testing with other neuroscientific methods. For example, Krakauer et al. (2017) argue that sim ply understanding the parts of the brain involved in a certain behaviour is not sufficient to explain that behaviour. These authors quote the early computational neuroscientist David Marr, who said that ‘trying to understand perception by understanding neurons is like trying to understand a bird's flight by studying only feathers. It just cannot b done’ (1982, p. 27). We echo this sentiment here. The complementary strengths of behavioural and neurophysiologi cal methods allow one to address the question: ‘How does the brain produce a specific behavior?’ Just describing the neural processes is insufficient to provide an answer. Rather, one must leverage the neural data (what is the brain doing) with behavioural data (why is it doing it) in order to arrive at a complete understanding of the process.

The ultimate aim of many NeuroIS studies is a deeper understanding of human behaviour in an IS context. Whil neurophysiological measures give information about research participants that would otherwise be unobservable they do not provide a complete explanation of participants' behaviours. Due to the observational nature of most commonly used neurophysiological methods, it is often necessary to incorporate behavioural methods to answer fundamental neural research questions. Krakauer et al. (2017) cite several examples of neuroscientific findings that were later revealed to be incomplete or incorrect because results had been interpreted without reference to corresponding naturalistic behaviours. This was shown to be true of bradykinesia in Parkinson's Disease (Mazzoni et al., 2007), sound localization in barn owls (Grothe, 2003), and electrolocation in weakly electric fish (Fortune, 2006). We propose that the same principle holds in NeuroIS: in the absence of behavioural data, neurosci entific techniques may yield incomplete explanations of the phenomena under investigation.

An example of a NeuroIS study that provided incomplete explanations because it did not use both a behavioura and neurophysiological experiment is Anderson et al. (2014). This study was valuable in that it measured habituatio to security warnings by observing diminishing neural activity in the brain using fMRI, thus providing a neural explanation for why people ignore repeated warnings. However, as a consequence of not observing behaviour, the study was not able to test this neural explanation by showing the influence of decreased neural activity on security warn ing disregard behaviour. As a result, the authors could only infer the impact of their findings on security warning dis regard behaviour. In recognition of this limitation, later studies by Anderson et al. incorporated behavioura experiments to demonstrate behavioural impact.

One area where this dual approach may be of particular use in neurophysiological studies is in reducing the number of instances of unwarranted reverse inference, in which mental states or processes are inferred from a certai pattern of neural activation (Poldrack, 2006). Reverse inference is problematic because researchers may lack a thor ough understanding of the mental states or processes of their subjects and because a particular pattern of neura activation may be associated with a number of cognitive processes. However, both Hutzler (2014) and Poldrac (2006) acknowledge that reverse inference is not logically fallacious per se. Rather, both Hutzler and Poldrack point out that reverse inference as it is generally practiced (what Hutzler calls ‘the careless use of reverse inference , [2014, p. 1062]) is problematic, as authors rarely have the additional information needed to make a bette estimate of the probability of an observed pattern of brain activity given conditionals such as the task demands o the behaviour involved.

One way to appropriately use reverse inference is by observing behaviour. Poldrack advises that the relationshi between mental events and fMRI activation can be strengthened by ‘choosing experimental tasks that maximise the prior probability of a particular process being engaged’ (2006, pp. 62–63). Further, knowing the characteristics of task demands can set a conditional on the probability of observing activation in a certain brain region. For example, Hutzler (2014) expands on the formulation of reverse inference offered by Poldrack (2006) by specifying the need to understand the task setting in which the brain activation is observed. According to Hutzler, this improves th precision of reverse inference as it narrows the number of possible alternative explanations’ (2014, p. 1063)

A complementary behavioural experiment may therefore allow researchers to gain additional insight into the cognitive processes of their subjects and thus gain a better understanding of the mental event associated with a par ticular pattern of activation observed in the neurophysiological portion of a study. Krakauer et al. (2017) note that behavioural experiments are often needed either before or after conducting neural experiments in order to close a mutually-beneficial knowledge loop . In that way, the behaviour under investigation can be better defined throug pilot or preliminary testing. Similarly, behavioural testing can be informed by the results of the neural data

Another conceptually related reason for combining neurophysiological and behavioural research methods is to use neurophysiological data to attempt to predict overt behaviour. This may involve a descriptive approach wher the neurophysiological data are predictive of behavioural responses in the sense that changes in neurophysiologica responses are correlated with changes in behavioural measures (Vance et al., 2014 detailed in Section Risk perception study in NeuroIS). A better understanding of how neural responses map onto specific behavioural outcomes wil help in the development of neuroadaptive systems and brain-computer interfaces

## 3.1.1 | Examples of implementing Strategy 1

## The decoy effect in consumer neuroscience

A complementary behavioural study may be usefully conducted either before or after a neurophysiological experi ment, depending on the circumstances, and desired outcome. From the marketing literature, Hedgcock and Rao (2009) provide an example of the latter. They conducted a behavioural experiment to confirm the attraction (or decoy) effect, which describes a situation in which an individual faces a trade-off between two choices; a third irrelevant option (the decoy) is then introduced that increases the attractiveness of one of the original options du to its similarity (Huber et al., 1982). Following a behavioural experiment, an fMRI experiment was conducted to compare rival explanations for the attraction effect.

Importantly, the approach used by Hedgcock and Rao (2009) was different from simply conducting a behavioura pretest as a dry run for a neurophysiological experiment (Dimoka, 2010). While pretests are generally helpful in iden tifying errors in the experimental protocol or in validating instrumentation (Dimoka et al., 2012), Hedgcock and Rao (2009) designed their behavioural experiment to be a peer of the neurophysiological experiment, with insights from both experiments used to inform the other. This is an effective use of this strategy to confirm (or disconfirm purported theoretical mechanisms or when ruling out competing explanations for behavioural effects.<sup>4</sup>

## Risk perception study in NeuroIS

Vance et al. (2014) employed a similar strategy in a risk perception study, which allowed them to compare the predictive power of both physiological (EEG) measures and behavioural measures of risk perception. In this study, the author examined the influence of users' security risk perceptions on their adherence to security warnings. They first collected traditional measures of the participants' overall perceptions of risk via questionnaires in a manner that the participant did not obviously associate with the main experiment. They then measured their implicit reaction to risk and losses using EEG and a software implementation of the Iowa Gambling Task (IGT), a behavioural paradigm that has been widely use to measure participants' decision-making abilities (Bechara et al., 1994; Toplak et al., 2010). In the IGT, participants learn through trial and error to select advantageous card decks (with modest rewards but minimal losses) over disadvantageou card decks (with larger rewards but much larger losses). In particular, the authors measured the P300 event-related potential component, which is often associated with surprise or expectation. Consistent with previous research, the authors found that the P300 had a greater amplitude with larger losses than with smaller losses.

Immediately following the EEG study, the same subjects moved to a different room and used their own laptop computers to engage in a behavioural image classification task. Interspersed with this task were security warnings that the subjects could heed or ignore. The authors also simulated a realistic security incident that made participants think a hacker was stealing files on their computer. Security warning disregard behaviour was compared in the task both before and after the simulated incident. Finally, the subjects completed a post-test survey in which they again indicated their overall perceptions of risk. The difference in reactivity to the losses, as measured in the EEG part of the study, more accurately predicted security warning disregard than the behavioural self-report. However, after the simulated computer security incident (on the participants' own laptop computers), the EEG and questionnaire mea sures predicted the participants' security warning disregard equally well

The addition of the behavioural experiment allowed the authors to demonstrate that the EEG measures of risk perception could predict security warning adherence behaviour when the self-reported risk perception measures could not. Critically, the behavioural experiment also allowed the authors to simulate a security incident in which they led participants to believe their personal laptop was being hacked. Interestingly, after the simulated security incident, th participants' behaviour aligned with their self-reported measures of security risk. Thus, the behavioural experiment illustrated not only that self-reported and neurophysiological measures may differ but also how these differences could be brought into correspondence through a behavioural change. This provided a more complete explanation of the participants' behaviour than either the EEG or behavioural experiment could have done on their own.

## 3.1.2 | Practical considerations when implementing Strategy 1

As noted above, a complementary behavioural study is useful, as it reduces the need to make reverse inference about mental states from neural activation patterns. As a practical example of using behavioural data to counte reverse inference, consider an experimental condition in which researchers observe increased activation in the hip pocampal formation. Based on previous research that shows the hippocampus to be critically important in long-term memory encoding (Kircher et al., 2007; Powell et al., 2005; Shrager et al., 2008), one might conclude that th observed increase in hippocampal fMRI activation indicates that participants are more engaged in memory encoding than the comparison condition. However, this conclusion is not necessarily valid, as other studies have indicated that the hippocampus is also involved in memory retrieval (Eldridge et al., 2000; Kirwan et al., 2009; Rugg & Vilberg, 2013), spatial navigation (Boccia et al., 2014), the Default Mode Network (Greicius et al., 2009), and even when imagining the future (Addis & Schacter, 2012; Kirwan et al., 2014). In this case, researchers could counter the problems associated with reverse inference by adding a recognition memory test following the fMRI scan, in which they could explicitly test for increased long-term memory encoding in their condition of interest. Alternatively researchers could behaviourally test the experimental design in a pilot test to demonstrate that memory encoding i involved by the nature of the task.

Another practical consideration is how to interpret results that differ between neurophysiological and behav ioural studies. When results agree, researchers can have high levels of confidence that their findings have been cor roborated, because a triangulation of methods can potentially overcome the biases or weakness inherent in a singl method (Dimoka et al., 2011) In contrast, a case in which results differ is more complex, because a variety of causes may be at work. First, it is possible that the implementation of one of the methods was flawed, or alternatively implementations of both methods were flawed.

Second, neurophysiological and behavioural methods may measure different dimensions of the same construct— or perhaps even distinct constructs. For example, Tams et al. (2014) found that the salivary stress enzyme α-amylase and self-reported measures of stress were insignificantly correlated, indicating little if any overlap of the two methods However, using hierarchical regression, they found that both the self-reported and α-amylase measures of stress sig nificantly and independently predicted task performance, a theoretically related outcome. They concluded that ‘th physiological measure captured the unconscious component of technostress while the psychological measure capture the conscious component’, and that they were therefore complementary (Tams et al., 2014, p. 737)

Third, if the results of two methods are not collected concurrently, then variation in their results may be due t differences in people, settings, and treatments, any of which could have interacted with the data (Shadish et al., 2002) Whatever the reason, when results differ, we recommend that additional studies be performed to determine the caus of the differences observed across studies. Although this may initially pose a setback, it may also lead to interestin findings, such as latent interaction effects, that will lead to greater theoretical insights and contributions.

## 3.2 | Strategy 2: Combine neurophysiological and behavioural methods to enhance ecological and external validity

Neurophysiological methods have great potential to provide insights into human cognition beyond those obtained using behavioural techniques. While traditional self-reported measures ‘only capture conscious, declared opinions (Venkatraman et al., 2015, p. 438), neurophysiological methods can observe automatic or unconscious mental processe that are not subject to perceptual biases (Dimoka et al., 2011). However, the advantages of neurophysiological method typically come at the price of intrusiveness. Defining this as ‘the extent to which a measurement instrument interfere with an ongoing task , Riedl, Davis, and Hevner (2014, p. xxvi) explain that intrusiveness has three dimensions, thus:

1. Invasiveness: ‘the extent to which the recording device of a measurement instrument has to be inserted into or attached to the body’;

2. Natural position: ‘whether a person is able to carry out a task in a natural position’;

3. Movement freedom: ‘whether a person is able to move during task execution’.

This conceptualization of the dimensions of intrusiveness is presented graphically in Figure 1.

An fMRI, for example, is considered highly intrusive because although sensors do not need to be attached to the body (low invasiveness), subjects have very low degrees of natural position and movement freedom inside the MR scanner. Conversely, an EEG may be considered moderately intrusive because subjects enjoy a high degree of natura position and relatively high movement freedom for computing tasks, but electrodes are attached to their bodies which is highly invasive. Where there is a high degree of intrusiveness, ecological and external validity typically suffer as a result.

In contrast, behavioural methods provide limited insight into the neurocognitive processes that underlie behaviour, yet they are strong where neurophysiological methods are weak. That is, they have low intrusiveness, potentially high ecological validity, and, in the case of field experiments, high external validity.

As researchers, we aim to maximise internal, external, and ecological validity. Because all methods are strong in some aspects of validity and weak in others (McGrath, 1981, p. 438), there is a benefit in using multiple, complemen tary methods across research studies as part of a larger research program that ‘answer[s] several interrelated ques tions and potentially [includes] more than one research study’ (Venkatesh, 2011, p. 14). Nowhere, we would argue is this approach more advantageous than in the case of coupling neurophysiological and behavioural methods Although there is some overlap between external and ecological validity, they are distinct concepts. In the followin (sub)sections, we distinguish between the two and provide illustrations.

## 3.2.1 | External validity

External validity is the generalizability of findings to other situations and people (Crano et al., 2015)—or, phrased alternatively, external validity concerns inferences about the extent to which a causal relationship holds over varia tions in persons, settings, treatments, and outcomes (Shadish et al., 2002, p. 83). Cronbach and Shapiro (1982) advo cate for the significance of external validity by establishing a notational system called UTOS to refer to the units of study (such as the individuals), treatments, observations, and settings covered in a study. Shadish et al. (2002) explai that external validity may not hold if any one of these four dimensions changes.

Researchers can increase the external validity of a study or a follow-up experiment if they vary the units, treat ments, observations, or settings and still obtain similar results. For example, if the results of neurophysiological and behavioural experiments are similar, this increases confidence in the applicability of the theory or hypothesis being tested across different units (people), treatments, outcomes, and settings. On the other hand, if the experiments do not agree, this could indicate that an important boundary condition is at work. Uncovering boundary conditions can lead to a study's greater overall theoretical contribution

The setting of a neurophysiological study can be somewhat limited depending on the tools used. Currently, fMR studies need to be conducted in a very controlled environment. In the past, EEG studies were also very sensitive to movement and electromagnetic interference; however, newer EEG tools, such as Emotiv headsets, allow for more movement. With this newer technology, some neurophysiological studies can be moved outside the laboratory set ting. When the tools used for a neurophysiological study limit the experiment to a laboratory setting, it is often help ful to pair the study with a second experiment conducted outside the laboratory. For example, researchers ma conduct a field experiment in a corporate setting or even collect data from individuals in their own living spaces usin services such as Amazon Mechanical Turk.

Moreover, researchers can advance a program of research by varying the units and treatments under study. If an orig inal study has been conducted using undergraduate students as subjects, for example, the researchers can use an onlin service, such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, or even a representative sample of the population recruited, using Craigslist o another such tool. The subject pool can be expanded to include users with varying levels of expertise in both the subjec matter as well as the technology used or, additionally, to compare frequent with casual users. The number of subject required for a study increases with added treatments, but external validity also increases as more variables are tested

## 3.2.2 | Ecological validity

Ecological validity concerns whether an ‘effect is representative of what happens in everyday life’ (Crano et al., 2015, p. 136). In other words, an ecologically valid study uses methods, materials, and settings that are realistic approximations of the world being studied (Brewer, 2000). If the experiment is conducted in a setting that is unlik the natural setting, it may be hard to generalise the results (i.e., make inferences beyond the bounds of the experi ment). While some have argued that, unlike internal and external validity, ecological validity is not essential to th overall validity of a study (Shadish et al., 2002), the increased generalizability and applicability of results this afford merit the effort needed to increase it.

As explained, NeuroIS studies especially face challenges with ecological validity. Again, the fMRI methodolog requires that users lie still in a supine position while being scanned, so any realistic interaction (e.g., with a computer is limited. This artificiality results in a high degree of intrusiveness, both compared to behavioural and other neuro physiological methods (Riedl et al., 2014).

Similarly, the stimuli associated with various neurophysiological tools and the requirement for stimuli repetition may reduce the ecological validity of the experiment. For example, participants may see screenshots of computer interactions rather than interacting with an actual computer. Therefore, Dimoka et al. (2012, p. 682, 695) advise researchers to ‘replicate [NeuroIS experiments] in a more traditional setting and compare the corresponding behav ioural responses to test for external validity’, advising that ‘the richness provided by multiple sources of measure can be used to enhance the ecological validity of IS studies .

In the context of a global pandemic, Demazure et al. (2021) creatively devised a way of conducting remote EEG data collection that increases the ecological validity, although at the cost of some internal validity. Their framework which includes thorough training, guides this remote data collection. In a related study, Vasseur et al. (2021) pilo tested remote electrodermal activity and electrocardiogram data collection. Innovative progress towards improvin the ecological validity of neurophysiological tools is continuing.

## 3.2.3 | Examples of implementing Strategy 2

Visual attention study in consumer neuroscience

Hagtvedt and Brasel (2016) conducted an interesting set of studies examining how sound frequency and colour lightness can guide visual attention in a marketing context. They began this research project by conducting four separate eye-tracking studies in which they were able to manipulate different shapes, colours, the lightness of objects, highand low-frequency tones, and the placement of images and pictures with sounds. Relevant to Strategy 2, in order to increase ecological validity, the researchers next conducted a field study in a supermarket, where they tried to vali date the results from the prior four experiments. They varied the colour, light, tone, and placement of the items based on the prior studies. Their findings confirmed the earlier results that high-frequency music and dark shelf spaces significantly increased purchasing behaviour. This series of studies combined the detailed attention results allowed in eye-tracking studies with a realistic field study of purchasing behaviour.

## Short-term habituation study in NeuroIS

Another example of the strategy of using complementary studies to increase the ecological validity of an experiment is to be found in the work of Anderson et al. (2016). The researchers first used fMRI to examine participants' neura responses to standard as compared to modified, polymorphic variations of security warnings. They were able to demonstrate that participants were significantly more resistant to habituation to polymorphic warnings than conven tional warnings in the regions of the brain related to attention.

However, due to the ecological validity limitations of fMRI, Anderson et al. (2016) conducted a follow-up behav ioural laboratory experiment in a more realistic setting. Specifically, they used mouse-cursor tracking to unobtrusively follow attention and habituation while participants completed a task on their (own, personal) laptops in th laboratory. The fMRI experiment also informed the behavioural experiment, as the four most effective polymorphi warning variations based on neural activation levels were used in the behavioural experiment. The researchers agai found evidence of habituation: participants given polymorphic warnings habituated more slowly rate than those given standard warnings. The realism in the structure of the behavioural laboratory experiment increased the overal ecological validity of the paired set of studies. In addition, the broader subject pool for the behavioural laborator experiment increased external validity.

## 3.2.4 | Practical considerations when implementing Strategy 2

We recommend that researchers brainstorm a complementary behavioural experiment while planning their neuro physiological experiment instead of simply planning one as an afterthought to mitigate a perceived validity gap. Simi larly, researchers should also consider designing multiple experiments to improve both internal and external validity It is wise to select methods that complement or compensate for the validity weaknesses of the other. For example researchers would do well to use broad samples in their behavioural studies to compensate for using narrow sample in their neurophysiological experiments, perhaps using a field experiment rather than another laboratory experiment.

Likewise, it is advantageous to design realistic scenarios to compensate for artificial laboratory contexts and so increase ecological validity. Schmuckler (2001) advocates for experiments that are as realistic or natural as possible across the three dimensions of ecological validity: the research setting, stimuli, and task. Researchers aiming to improve the eco logical validity of an experiment can seek a research setting that is closer to real-life—for example, a field study rathe than a study in a laboratory. Similarly, an effort can be made to include stimuli and objects that mirror the participant' everyday setting, such as using the participant's own computing device rather than a laboratory computer (as above) Finally, researchers can seek tasks that are common in the daily life of the participants. Examples of realistic tasks includ conducting a search on an e-commerce site, replying to emails, and working in business software applications.

## 3.3 | Strategy 3: Extend, rather than replicate, experiments on the basis of theory

There is a clear value in replicating an experimental design for a variety of reasons (Dennis & Valacich, 2015). For neurophysiological studies, Dimoka et al. argue that no single neurophysiological measure is usually sufficient on it own, and it is advisable to use many data sources to triangulate across measures’ (2012, p. 694). However, a behavioural experiment that closely replicates a neurophysiological experiment may share many of the same limitations (Riedl et al., 2014). This can be a missed opportunity to gain additional insights that complement those already gained through the neurophysiological experiment.

An alternative to replication is extension, which Berthon et al. define as a duplication of a target study in whic one or more key parameters are altered (2002, p. 419), such as the theory, method, or context. We argue for th benefits of complementary experiments in which behaviour is observed while neurophysiological data is collected (Strategy 1), thus changing the ‘method’ parameter. We also argue for the value of using a more naturalistic task, set ting, mode of interaction, or broader sample when compared to the neurophysiological experiment (Strategy 2), thus also changing the context parameter. Further, due to the contrastive nature of many neurophysiological method and the need to gather many trials, it is sometimes desirable to change the parameters of the experimental task itself to fit a more naturalistic setting (Strategy 2). Extending a neurophysiological experiment in terms of both its metho and context can result in a behavioural experiment that is considerably different from its neurophysiological counter part. In these cases, it is especially important that theory be used effectively to serve as the ‘linchpin’ that ties the disparate neurophysiological and behavioural experiments together.

Theory can be used to generalise across different people, treatments, outcomes, and settings (Shadish et al., 2002), as ‘generalization[s] about [phenomena], interactions, and causation are thought to be at the core of a theory’ (Gregor, 2006, p. 616). Similarly, Lucas explains that ‘through their abstractness, theories apply across settings and populations; empirical findings do not. Thus, the key component of external validity is theory’ (2003, p. 238). Therefore, theory can be used to make connections between two experiments that are otherwise quite different

Extension experiments also provide a theoretical advantage by testing different aspects of a theory, thereby providing a richer overall theoretical contribution. For example, in addition to studying whether an independent variable or intervention explained by a theory results in a pattern of neural activity in a neurophysiological experiment researchers may also study how the same independent variable or intervention influences actual behaviour in a com plementary experiment. In this way, researchers can observe more of the theoretical causal chain that links stimulus neurophysiological activity, and behavioural response. By examining multiple links in the causal chain, researcher can provide a more complete test of theory as well as a more thorough explanation of its operation in a given context.

## 3.3.2 | Practical considerations when implementing Strategy 3

When designing complementary studies in a research program, we recommend testing the same hypotheses, or close analogs, in both the neurophysiological and behavioural studies so that they can be directly compared. Althoug studies can still be complementary with divergent hypotheses that examine different facets of a problem, a theoreti cal extension will be clearer if the hypotheses build upon or reinforce each other. This also enables researchers to relate the findings of both experiments more easily. Hypotheses that are parallel to each other provide the strongest ‘connective tissue’ between studies.

Additionally, the hypotheses for both experiments can either exist at the same or different links in the causa chain. On the one hand, testing the neurophysiological and behavioural operationalizations of a theory at the sam stage of the causal chain can corroborate a hypothesis. On the other hand, testing a theory at different stages of a causal chain provides a more complete view of the theory involved. For example, for theoretical models in whic intention is antecedent to behaviour (Sheeran, 2002), corresponding hypotheses can be designed at both the intentional and behavioural stages and tested in both neurophysiological and behavioural studies. In this way, the two studies would be tightly coupled by theory while also providing a more complete test of the theory.

Finally, developing compatible hypotheses across multiple studies is most easily done prior to the execution of any of the studies. This requires planning and foresight, but it best ensures that the hypotheses will meaningfull extend each other. Designing a follow-up study after an initial study has already been conducted will typically pro vide fewer degrees of freedom, because the parameters of the first experiment have already been finalised. It ma also prove more expensive because researchers may need to go to greater lengths to ensure that they can obtai results complementary to the findings of the previous study.

## 3.4 | Strategy 4: Use the neurophysiological and behavioural methods together to evaluate IT artefact design

NeuroIS has great potential to build and evaluate the design and use of IT artefacts. Riedl et al. observed that NeurolS can contribute to the.., design of ICT artifacts that positively impact economic and non-economic variables such as productivity, satisfaction, adoption, and well-being’ (2014, p. ii). In addition, vom Brocke et al. explained that ‘the consideration of neuroscience may improve IT artifacts' alignment with users' perceptual and information processing mechanisms, particularly the brain’ (2013, p. 1). This includes both the building and evaluation of IT arte fact designs (vom Brocke et al., 2020). Nevertheless, as powerful as NeuroIS tools and knowledge are in the evalua tion of the design of IT artefacts, we argue that combining NeuroIS with behavioural experiments—particularly field experiments—in a complementary way is particularly powerful in the evaluation of IT artefact design that interface with people.

This is for at least two reasons. First, although NeuroIS can evaluate the design of an artefact and explain why neural responses to an IT artefact should influence behaviours, it is desirable to verify that the IT artefact actuall does influence relevant real-world behaviours. Riedl and Léger observe that neurophysiological methods ‘can be used as a mediator between the IT artifact and IT behaviour’ (2016, p. 20), and Dimoka et al. suggest ‘the brain areas associated with the desired effects can be used as an objective dependent variable which the IT artifacts will be designed to affect’ (2011, p. 700). However, having determined how IT artefact designs influence neurophysiologica responses, it is also important to observe their downstream, behavioural effects. Thus, combining NeuroIS with behavioural experiments provides a more complete evaluation of an IT artefact design.

Second, in addition to showing that an IT artefact design performs successfully in the field as well as in a labora tory environment, as previously mentioned in connection to Strategy 2, NeuroIS methods typically require a high degree of intrusiveness that preclude their deployment in the field (Riedl et al., 2014); thus, it is difficult to observ how IT artefact design influences behaviours in their natural setting. Additionally, NeuroIS methods can be time consuming in that subjects are typically examined individually (Dimoka et al., 2012). This makes it difficult to conduct large trials with many subjects, as is required when testing many design variations. For these reasons, it is desirable to pair NeuroIS experiments with behavioural field experiments that can evaluate IT artefacts in real-world setting with large numbers of subjects. Such ‘road testing’ achieved through field experiments can offer a culminating validation of IT artefact designs.

## 3.4.1 | Examples of implementing Strategy 4

## Product attention study in consumer neuroscience

Although IT artefact design is mainly studied in the IS field, marketing also includes the concept of artefact desig (Luchs & Swan, 2011). One element of artefact design common in marketing is the precise presentation or configuration of products to consumers. For example, in a study exploring the influence of product positioning among an arra of products, Atalay et al. (2012) conducted two eye-tracking studies and found that brands positioned in the horizon tal centre of an array of products received more attention and were more likely to be chosen. In the first study, par ticipants were presented planograms (a diagram that indicates the placement of retail products on shelves) with fictitious brands arranged horizontally. Participants perused products on the screen of the experimental computer as if they were on the store shelf and hit the enter key when they were ready to choose a product. The results indicated that horizontal centrality had an impact on visual attention (received more eye fixation and greater total fixation duration). In a second eye tracking study, the authors further explored whether the results of the first study wer explained by the horizontal centrality of the brand within an array of products or by centrality on the compute screen. The results corroborated those of the first eye tracking study: brands in the horizontal centre of the product array received more frequent eye fixations and a longer total duration of fixations overall.

In a follow-up behavioural study with tangible products, the authors again found the centrally located items per product category were chosen more often. Products were arranged on a shelf in a physical retail context. Participants were positioned in the middle of the display, while an array of focal products they were asked to choose from were positioned either to their right or left. The rest of the display was populated with filler prod ucts. The results indicated that the brand located in the centre of its associated array of products was chose most often.

Together, the eye tracking studies paired with the behavioural study demonstrate not only why the product placement design artefact featuring centrality influences product choice—because it draws more visual attention— but also show that these results held in a realistic behavioural experiment in which brands were placed centrally in a product array in different parts of the retail shelves, overall increasing product choice. This study is one example of several marketing studies (Deng et al., 2016; Hong et al., 2016; Telpaz et al., 2015) that have used both neuro and behavioural methods to evaluate product display design and positioning

## Dual-task interference study in NeuroIS

In a dual-task interference study, Jenkins et al. (2016) conducted an fMRI experiment to show that interruptions caused by security messages reduced participants' neural processing relevant to a task and that this similarly reduce their warning adherence in a behavioural task conducted with the MRI scanner. This research also demonstrated that neural activation and related warning adherence behaviour improved when the security messages were timed to minimise interruptions. They complemented these findings with a field experiment that evaluated nine different timing designs for an actual security message in Google Chrome. The experiment involved 856 Mechanical Turk users who used Google Chrome on their own personal computers in their natural work settings. The researchers showed that, by using three behavioural measures—accepting or dismissing the security message, mouse speed, and mouse cursor deviation—timing of a security messages to reduce interruptions can significantly reduce warning dis regard behaviour. Together, the NeuroIS and behavioural experiments guided the evaluation and final selection of superior IT artefact designs.

## 3.4.2 | Practical considerations when implementing Strategy 4

There are several practical considerations when evaluating an IT artefact design across neurophysiological and behav ioural studies. First, one should consider whether or not to use the same user interface design for both the neurophys iological and behavioural experiments. Using the same user interface design is desirable in many situations, because i helps ensure consistency between neurophysiological and behavioural studies, allowing researchers to confidently relate the results of the two. However, in other situations, it is desirable to optimise the IT artefact for one study but not the other. For example, researchers try to minimise movement in an fMRI machine to reduce noise in the data. Fo this reason, participants view a static image of the IT artefact rather than interacting with the actual dynamic IT arte fact. In such cases, it may be beneficial to evaluate a different version of the IT artefact (i.e., an actual functioning IT artefact within a realistic context) to maximise realism in the behaviour study. When using a different IT artefact (or presenting it in a different context), one should strive to ensure that the design manipulation is similar across stud ies. For example, if one manipulates a property of an IT artefact design (e.g., the timing of a notification) in a neuro physiological study, one should manipulate that same property similarly in the behavioural study. As long as the sam phenomenon of interest is manipulated between the two studies (i.e., the same hypothesis is tested), the other proper ties of the IT artefact design and presentation can differ so as to optimise control or ecological validity.

Second, researchers should carefully consider which dependent variables in the complementary study are best suited to evaluate the success of an IT artefact. This decision will largely determine the methods chosen. Typically, in selecting a dependent variable for complementary neurophysiological and behavioural studies, one should consider which combination of dependent variables will provide the most robust evaluation of the IT artefact. This often includes choosing dependent variables that assess different elements of the IT artefact (e.g., behaviour and attention) to provide a comprehensive evaluation. For example, if an IT artefact was created to attract users' attention neurophysiological-dependent variables may be used to evaluate activation changes in the brain regions associated with visual attention, such as the posterior parietal cortex, or else use gaze fixations or pupil dilation as measure through an eye tracker. A behavioural study can then be conducted to observe the behavioural consequences of the increased attention.

Third, the purpose of conducting a complementary study is to gain insights that are not otherwise available through a single behavioural or neurophysiological study alone. Thus, when choosing complementary methods fo neurophysiological and behavioural studies, one should consider the primary insights one hopes to gain through the complementary study. For example, if one designs a complementary neurophysiological study to gain insight into why an IT artefact influences behaviour and hypothesizes that the reason for the change in behaviour is that th artefact captures the users' attention, one should choose a neurophysiological methodology that can measure changes in users' attention, such as eye tracking or fMRI. If one hypothesizes that an IT artefact influences behaviou because it reduces stress, one might consider measuring cortisol levels (a hormone associated with stress responses) Likewise, in a behavioural study, one should consider the key insight one hopes to gain from the study. If, fo example, ecological validity is the key insight, one should consider performing a field study to maximise realism.

## 3.5 | General guidance on study sequence for all four strategies

For all four strategies, the behavioural study can generally be conducted before, after, or during (simultaneously with) the NeuroIS study. Dimoka (2012) argues that there is a value in conducting the behavioural component before th neurophysiological component of the study as this can allow the researchers to pilot-test the experimental design in a cost-effective way. Several iterations of behavioural pilot tests can save time and money. Additionally, pilot tests allow researchers to refine hypotheses of psychological constructs or mental events that occur while participants perform a task, thus reducing the need for reverse inference when interpreting the results of a neurophysiologica study.

Alternatively, a behavioural experiment may follow a neurophysiological experiment with the purpose of observ ing behavioural effects that stem from neural processes or demonstrating the impact of the neural processes in terms of behavioural change. This is often done in the context of verifying the neurophysiological findings in a realistic or ecologically valid setting. Behavioural interventions identified in the neurophysiological study can be validated in a follow-up behavioural study, which, in turn, may inform further neurophysiological studies—for example, see the dis cussion of Hedgcock and Rao (2009) above (Section The decoy effect in consumer neuroscience).

Finally, conducting behavioural and neurophysiological studies at the same time can powerfully demonstrate causation. While some neurophysiological methods are intrusive and require a laboratory setting, other tools, such a eye-tracking and facial electromyography, may be performed simultaneously. As Riedl et al. (2010, p. 255) note, ‘tools to measure psychophysiological responses are less intrusive, as participants usually sit in front of computers in a quiet environment, but still involve the use of sensors attached to the body’. As technology improves, neurophysio logical methods will become less intrusive and easier to incorporate into behavioural designs. For example, one physiological measurement that can be administered remotely and at scale is facial emotion analysis. Facial emotio analysis uses computer vision to analyse video frames to detect non-verbal facial expressions indicative of emotions including feelings of happiness, sadness, anger, disgust, fear, and surprise (Kumar et al., 2017)

## 4 | DISCUSSION

This article reflects renewed interest from neuroscientists to study behavioural to complement and enhance their neurophysiological findings (Berman, 2018; Gomez-Marin & Ghazanfar, 2019). We respond to the call of Krakauer et al. (2017) by developing strategies for designing and conducting complementary experiments using both behav ioural and neurophysiological methods within a single research program. In so doing, this article makes severa contributions to the topic of research design.

First, while previous researchers have pointed to the value of using neurophysiological and behavioural method in a single study to triangulate a finding (Dimoka et al., 2011) or measure different dimensions of a construct (Tam et al., 2014), our scope is broader. The strategies proposed here encompass conceptual and methodological (Strategy 1), validational (Strategy 2), theoretical (Strategy 3), and artifactual (Strategy 4) contributions. We have described how complementary neurophysiological and behavioural studies can be designed within a single research program Thus, we propose that researchers combine neurophysiological and behavioural studies so as to capitalise on th comparative strengths of these approaches while compensating for their comparative weaknesses

Second, we have described how to design such research programs. Although many principles described in this arti cle have been articulated previously or may appear commonsensical, few NeuroIS researchers have taken advantag of the complementary approach that we describe—despite its clear benefits.<sup>5</sup> One of the reasons for this may be that there is little practical guidance on how to combine neurophysiological and behavioural methods in a NeuroIS research program. To address this need, we have outlined four strategies that IS researchers can follow when designing a research program that comprises both neurophysiological and behavioural methods. Additionally, all four strategie highlight research opportunities that are unique to combinations of neurophysiological and behavioural methods.

Third, we have offered practical considerations to take into account when implementing each strategy (see Table 3). Rather than encompassing prescriptive guidelines, our strategies represent points of decision that researchers are likely to face when implementing the relevant strategy as well as the strengths and weaknesses of these choices. These were derived from our own experience in carrying out complementary neurophysiological and behavioural research programs. Some of these insights were hard-won in that we sometimes tried multipl approaches before finding viable solutions. Furthermore, the degrees of freedom become fewer after one or more studies have been completed. Consequently, it is both more cost-effective and more efficient to consider thes points early in the design phase of a research program

Fourth, we have illustrated these strategies using published examples from both the NeuroIS and consumer neuroscience literatures that follow these strategies. Each example article was vetted in the review process by a premie IS or marketing journal, demonstrating that these strategies are useful for enhancing the contributions and publishability of NeuroIS and consumer neuroscience research. In each case, the articles made a greater contribution by following one or more of the complementary strategies than would have been possible using either a neurophysi ological or behavioural method alone. In our experience, reviewers are sometimes sceptical of the value of NeuroIS The addition of a complementary behavioural experiment helps to demonstrate the value of NeuroIS by showing th behavioural impact of neurophysiological results. In addition to providing proof of value, these articles show, in con crete terms, how the strategies suggested might be implemented. Further, we have provided multiple examples fo different ways in which the strategies can be realised.

Finally, we recognise that not all neurophysiological experiments require a behavioural complement—or vic versa. Some specific research questions can be answered succinctly using a single methodology. However, broader research questions can benefit from a programmatic approach that examines the same problem from different meth odological angles. Further, because all methods have relative strengths and weaknesses, only a programmatic, com plementary approach can provide strong internal, external, and ecological validity. Thus, the acquisition of knowledge can be accelerated beyond the normal accretion of uncoordinated studies by scholars in a single field of research.

## 5 | CONCLUSION

In this paper, we have presented the argument that the acquisition of knowledge can be accelerated by designing research programs comprising complementary neurophysiological and behavioural methods. This potential lies in the fact that neurophysiological and behavioural data provide different perspectives on phenomena being studied and thus provide a more complete understanding of them than does either methodological approach alone. We outline four strategies for designing research programs that efficiently combine the relative strengths of neurophysiologica and behavioural methods and discussed practical considerations for their implementation together with example from the literature.

Our focus in this paper has been to examine the NeuroIS literature and reflect on the value added by conducting studies using both behavioural and neurophysiological tools. In order to broaden our investigation, we have also examined the marketing literature, which is closely related to IS and also has a tradition of using neurophysiologica methods (i.e., consumer neuroscience). This paper could also have examined research in other business fields—o even medical or psychological research. We encourage future researchers to examine such opportunities. We hop that these insights will encourage IS researchers to design research programs that integrate neurophysiological and behavioural methods and thus obtain insights that would not be possible using either method alone.
