## Abstract

The emergence of Fintech platforms has revolutionized the way financial services are provided. And yet, in spite of their growing prominence in the global financial sector, there remains a lack of understanding of the competitive strategies that are appropriate for these platform-based businesses, and the implications of those strategies for their performance. Examining four case studies of some of the most successful Fintech platforms in China from the theoretical perspective of the core logics of strategy, we develop a theoretical framework that suggests that the nature of the competitive strategies deployed by a Fintech platform should be contingent on (1) the extent to which their services can be differentiated, as well as (2) the tangibility and physical presence of the platform’s service offerings. More specifically, our framework presents four different combinations of competitive strategies that Fintech platforms can adopt contingent on the nature of their services along these two dimensions. In addition, beyond the two dimensions, our framework suggests that all Fintech platforms should be underpinned by a common strategic core consisting of strategies that align the platform with social pressures. These pressures include those exerted by the government, the market, and society in general. With its findings, it is hoped that our study will provide specific guidance for Fintech practitioners on the appropriate competitive strategies to adopt in order to set their platforms on the path of commercial success.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

Fintech is a portmanteau formed by combining the words “finance” and “technology.” The term has a long and storied history, first popularised in the early 1990s when Citicorp initiated the “Financial Services Technology Consortium” project to foster collaboration with its business partners (Hochstein, 2015). With the passage of time, the meaning of the term has evolved. Today, the term Fintech is an umbrella concept that is broadly defined as the design and delivery of financial products and services through information technology (Leong et al., 2017). The phenomenon has gained prominence in the contemporary business landscape, particularly since the Global Financial Crisis of 2007–2008, which has provided the impetus for regulators and firms in the financial sector to strive for an appropriate balance between harnessing the business potential of Fintech while managing its associated risks (Arner et al., 2016). According to an analysis by KPMG (2021), global investments in Fintech reached US\$210 billion in 2021, while industry analysts project that the size of the global fintech market will be approximately US\$305 billion by 2025, growing at a compound annua growth rate of around 20% over the next few years (Business Insider, 2022).

However, despite their global significance, new Fintech ventures are extremely likely to fail (Muthukannan et al., 2020) with an estimated failure rate of around 90% over a period of 4–6 years (Finextra., 2018). In comparison, the 5-year mortality rate for new start-ups in developed economies has historically been around 50% (Levi et al., 2011). This significant disparity in mortality rates may be indicative that the traditional wisdom surrounding how Fintech firms can ensure the performance and survival of their ventures is inadequate to address the challenges associ ated with this new mode of delivering financial services. This, in turn, may stem from at least two gaps in the existing literature.

As will be elaborated on in our literature review, the first gap is that an overwhelming majority of the new Fintech firms tend to be platform-based businesses that connect suppliers of financial services to the consumers tha demand them (Gozman et al., 2018; Pollari, 2016). There is, however, little research to date on the determinants an mechanisms that underpin their performance, and the conventional prescriptions for new entrepreneurial venture that are non-platform-based businesses (i.e., product manufacturers or service providers—McDougall & Robin son, 1990) may be less appropriate or relevant for these firms (Van Alstyne et al., 2016). In addition, a second gap is that among the handful of emerging studies in this area, none of them have examined the competitive strategies tha are available and appropriate for new Fintech platforms extensively

A dedicated study of the competitive strategies of Fintech platforms is required because the traditional prescriptions for strategy do not account for at least four unique characteristics of Fintech platforms (Ng et al., 2022). First Fintech platforms must strike a balance between its conflicting dual identity as both a growth-oriented technology firm and a risk-averse financial firm (Stulz, 2019). Second, Fintech platforms are more likely to serve customers from the vulnerable segments of society that incumbent financial institutions have traditionally neglected (Cortina Lorent & Schmukler, 2018). Third, as Fintech platforms are threatening to disrupt the financial services sector that tends to be regarded as a key pillar of economic stability in most countries across the globe (Lagarde, 2018), they may b more likely to face abrupt legislative changes and an unpredictable regulatory landscape (Zetsche et al., 2017)

Fourth, the Fintech platforms typically have to contend with incumbent firms that are larger, more established, and more influential on the whole (Clifton et al., 2017). The insights that such a study could generate will be potentiall useful for Fintech practitioners who wish to learn from the experience of their peers across the various Fintech sec tors in addition to from their own businesses. Without this knowledge, it may be especially difficult for the fledgling Fintech platforms to navigate the numerous challenges that confront them, such as their resource constraints (se Baker & Nelson, 2005), the competition with established incumbent financial institutions, and the “liability of new ness” (Singh et al., 1986, p. 171) as a new business form.

To address these gaps, the purpose of this study is to examine in-depth the nature and implications of the competi tive strategies of successful Fintech platforms. To this end, we conducted four case studies of some of the most success ful Fintech platforms in China, which represents one of the most vibrant, yet exacting, Fintech markets in the world (Knight & Wojcik, 2020). With these case studies, we aim to develop a theoretical framework that will shed light on th interplay between the competitive strategies and their performance implications for Fintech platforms, and generat practical insights on how the disruptive potential of Fintech may be realised. Accordingly, the research question that ou study aims to address is “What competitive strategies are effective and appropriate for Fintech platforms?”

## 2 | LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 | The existing research on Fintech

With the Fintech revolution that is currently sweeping across the globe (Cojoianu et al., 2020), digital technologies are being applied in unprecedented ways “that have led to new entrepreneurship, unbridled innovation, and signifi cant growth in interest by venture capital firms to become involved” (Gomber et al., 2018b, p. 13). Fintech is expected to deliver a number of significant benefits. For consumers, Fintech increases access to, and lowers th costs of, financial services (Demirgüç-Kunt et al., 2017). For financial institutions, Fintech enhances the efficiency of their operations, and increases the reach and richness of their services (Currie & Lagoarde-Segot, 2017). For entre preneurs, Fintech can lead to the emergence and exploitation of new business opportunities as it enhances the transparency and timeliness of financial information (Gozman et al., 2018). In addition, the impact of Fintech is not restricted to the financial sector, but can be felt across a variety of industries as well (e.g., driving new ventures in professional services and e-commerce—see Leong et al., 2017).

As a result of its profound economic and social implications, the rise of Fintech has been meteoric, and many believe that this momentum will be sustained as long as firms delivering novel value propositions continue to emerge (SCMP Research, 2020). Commonly cited Fintech success stories from across the globe include M-Pesa; a mobil money transfer system originating in Kenya (Mbiti & Weil, 2014) with over 20 million users and processing over 1.7 billion transactions per year (McGrath, 2018), as well as the Lending Club; a firm credited for adapting the sharing economy paradigm to the financial landscape, which grew to a point where they were able to provide US\$21 billio of credit in 2016 (Tottman, 2016)

Reflecting its prominence in the global financial sector, the amount of research on Fintech in the Informatio Systems (IS) academic literature is similarly growing. In particular, the emerging articles on Fintech<sup>1</sup> can be classified into three main themes (refer to Table 1). The first theme is centered on Fintech drivers, and studies in this area ar typically seeking to identify the factors that facilitate the development of the overall Fintech movement, as well a the potential inhibitors that must be overcome (e.g., Muthukannan et al., 2017). The second theme, which the majority of existing studies fall under (e.g., Gomber et al., 2018b), focuses on Fintech operations. This stream of researc attempts to shed light on how the operations of various Fintech businesses should be structured, and how Fintec innovations emerge (e.g., Gozman et al., 2018). The third theme is Fintech implications. Studies aligned with this theme are generally aiming to identify and economic and social implications of Fintech, both positive and negative and provide an explanation for how these implications arise (e.g., Gomber et al., 2018a).

However, despite the increasing research attention on the phenomenon and the number of Fintech studies that are emerging, our review of the literature reveals at least two clear gaps in relation to our research question. First even though a significant majority of new Fintech firms are platform-based businesses (Dhar & Stein, 2017

Gozman et al., 2018), there is currently very little research on how to ensure the performance of these firms. Ther have been a significant number of studies on the development of new entrepreneurial ventures over the years (e.g., the eight archetypes of competitive strategies for market entry—see McDougall & Robinson, 1990), and there i research emerging on digital platform development (e.g., the six platform development strategies described in Tan Pan, Lu, and Huang (2015)), but as a Fintech platform tends to be an amalgamation of both a new venture and a digi tal platform, the findings and prescriptions of these studies may be inappropriate or less relevant because the condi tions associated with both contexts must be considered together (Dhar & Stein, 2017; Van Alstyne et al., 2016)

Second, of the handful of published articles on the performance of Fintech platforms, none of them have delved extensively into the competitive strategies of these firms and the implications of those strategies on their perfor mance. For instance, Kazan et al. (2018) describe three strategies that can be used by Fintech (i.e., mobile payment) platforms depending on their platform profile: (1) the germination strategy, which allows firms to nurture private business networks by capturing value without intervention from third parties, (2) the orchestration strategy, which emphasises “coopetition” (Bengtsson & Kock, 2000, p. 411) and entails a deliberate platform design that is highly integratable with existing mobile banking applications, and (3) the transformation strategy, which is centered on product and process innovation. However, the focus of their work lies in the elaboration of the platform profiles and their antecedents. Consequently, these strategies are only described very briefly. The questions of why these strate gies are appropriate, how the strategies should be deployed, and how the strategies contribute to the performance of Fintech platforms have not been adequately addressed in their study

In a similar vein, Dhar and Stein (2017) argue strongly for the integration of technology and business processes while maintaining open access to develop a “complete” Fintech platform. Nonetheless, notwithstanding the aca demic and practical significance of their suggestions, their arguments have not been empirically corroborated, and the question of how to integrate is again not fully addressed. An understanding of the competitive strategies that are appropriate and effective for Fintech platforms and the performance implications of those strategies is important because many of the platforms tend to be start-ups (Gozman et al., 2018) and represent a new business form. Consequently, this understanding can help these firms to avoid costly missteps in terms of the competitive actions taken grow their business effectively, and overcome the many challenges (see e.g., Baker & Nelson, 2005; Sing et al., 1986) associated with their fledgling status.

To address these gaps, the aim of this paper is to examine the competitive strategies of successful Fintech plat forms and how those strategies contribute to their performance. Accordingly, we develop a theoretical lens based on the literature on the core logics of strategy (Ng et al., 2022) so as to construct a sensitising device (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 75) to guide our subsequent inquiry.

## 2.2 | The core logics of strategy

Although a Fintech platform has a vast array of competitive strategies to choose from (e.g., Kazan et al., 2018), som scholars (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Ng et al., 2022; Sambamurthy et al., 2003; Tan et al., 2010) believe that the essence of most strategies can be distilled into a number of distinct logics (refer to Table 2).

The first logic is the Logic of Positioning (Sambamurthy et al., 2003), which suggests that the acquisition and defence of an attractive market position in relation to contextual threats and influences are critical to superior firm performance (according to the classic conceptualisation of competitive strategy in Porter, 1996, 2001). This ha implications for the performance of a Fintech platform as it implies the creation and maintenance of a unique valu proposition, which can facilitate the attraction and retention of platform entities (i.e., suppliers, customers, and busi ness partners) and the attainment of self-sustaining critical mass (Tan, Pan, Lu, et al., 2015).

The second logic is the Logic of Leverage (Sambamurthy et al., 2003) based on the Resource-Based Theory (se Barney & Clark, 2007). This logic posits that superior performance is a result of leveraging firm-specific strategic resources and capabilities (Barney, 1991). While the Logic of Positioning emphasises deliberate market positioning based on a systematic consideration of environmental opportunities and threats, the Logic of Leverage focuses on the man agement of organisational strengths and weaknesses (Tan et al., 2010). For Fintech platforms, this can influence their performance because the novel value proposition may not only serve to attract and retain new platform entities, but these entities can align themselves and coordinate value creation around the seed proposition of the platform (Moore, 1993). Moreover, the Fintech platform may be able to delineate and reinforce the boundary of its network by sharing its resources with its members, which serves as both a participation subsidy and a disincentive for leavin the platform (Tan, Pan, Lu, et al., 2015).

The third logic is the Logic of Opportunity which has its roots in Agility Theory (see Sambamurthy et al., 2003) This logic emphasises the development and deployment of the capabilities of sensing market opportunities and responding to them with the appropriate competitive actions rapidly and effectively (Overby et al., 2006). The focus of strategies aligned with this logic is continuous innovation (D'Aveni, 1994) as assets and competencies are repeatedly recombined for the purpose of sustaining a continuous stream of temporary competitive advantages (Eisenhard & Martin, 2000). With a Fintech platform acting as the lead innovator, strategies aligned with the Logic of Opportunity can influence platform performance by introducing a continuous stream of fresh ideas that promote self-renewa and stave off obsolescence (Moore, 1996). Moreover, continuous innovation by the platform may motivate the othe platform entities to adapt to the changes or exit the ecosystem (Pierce, 2009). With successive iterations, th remaining entities develop the capabilities to adapt efficiently and effectively, which results in the development of robust, self-reinforcing, and agile network of entities (Tan, Pan, Lu, et al., 2015).

The fourth logic is the Logic of Complexity epitomised by Chaos Theory (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999). This logic posits that superior performance is a result of a mutually reinforcing business network with a vibrant set of partici pating entities, which gives rise to productivity, synergies from diversity, and greater durability of the benefit derived. A Fintech platform may pursue strategies aligned with the Logic of Complexity by attempting to manipulate the underlying forces and attractors that create order in a network of organisational relationships; such as share values, the collective vision, mechanisms of control, and platforms for interaction (Stacey, 1995). This keeps the plat form at the “edge of chaos” (Brown & Eisenhardt, 1997, p. 29) so that the platform is primed for innovation and continuous change (Stacey, 1995). Alternatively, the platform may focus on helping the other platform entities develop their own capabilities through direct intervention, or providing the tools and interfaces for capability development (Iansiti & Levien, 2004). The result of these community-oriented measures may be the development of a health Fintech platform characterised by symbiotic relationships. collective strategies, and orchestrated actions (Lengnick Hall & Wolff, 1999).

The fifth logic is the Logic of Optimality which stems from Hannan and Freeman's (1977) Organisational Ecol ogy. This logic suggests that there are forces of natural selection at work in any given organisational environment (Oliver, 1988). Consequently, firms should prioritise the attainment and retention of an optimal organisational form that predisposes them for survival in a given set of environmental conditions (Tan et al., 2010). The optima organisational form in the context of Fintech platforms could consist of the traits of technology-business integration openness (Dhar & Stein, 2017), platform interoperability (Kazan et al., 2018), and the establishment of a digita hybrid model” (Leong et al., 2017, p. 95) that combines both product and service offerings within a single platform.

The sixth logic is the Logic of Social Congruence (Tan et al., 2010) based on Institutional Theory (see Tolbert & Zucker, 1999). This logic emphasises “social fitness” (Scott, 2014, p. 185), which aligns a firm with the external pressures acting upon it, including coercive pressures from regulatory bodies, mimetic pressures from related organisa tions (e.g., competitors), and normative pressures associated with professionalisation (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983) Social fitness ensures the performance of a Fintech platform because it suggests that the platform has responde appropriately to these pressures (Oliver, 1991) for the attainment of legitimacy. For instance, when online lenders were banned from targeting students in China in 2017 (Zhuang, 2017), the platforms that had targeted this segment of borrowers had to adapt their business or exit the industry (Weinland & Ju, 2018). In other words, the responses to institutional pressures as a result of the Logic of Social congruence can entail radical changes to the composition of platform entities, the revenue streams exploited, as well as the nature of the platform's operations

The six logics all represent potential options for Fintech platforms in relation to their competitive strategies. Yet when taken together, the propositions of the six logics appear to be contradictory (Tan et al., 2010). On one hand the first four logics (i.e., positioning, leverage, opportunity, and complexity) are advocating differentiation—settin apart an organisation (and its associated business network) from its competitors for the attainment of competitiv advantage (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). On the other hand, the latter two logics (i.e., optimality and social congruence) are advocating conformity, making an organisation (and its associated busines network) more similar to the competition so as to achieve legitimacy (Tan et al., 2010). This contradiction has bee referred to in previous studies as the paradox of homogeneity (or heterogeneity), and has the potential to creat confusion and leave practitioners “in a void in terms of practical discretion” (Durand & Calori, 2006, p. 93).

At this juncture, we will not attempt to resolve the paradox conceptually because alternative arguments exist in the literature (e.g., Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Leonardelli et al., 2010; Tan et al., 2010), and to take a stand now may limit our openness and flexibility in terms of the collection and analysis of data subsequently (Walsham, 1995) Instead, we integrate the six logics of strategy in a single theoretical lens, which will serve as a “sensitising device” (Klein & Myers, 1999, p. 75) to guide our study. This is because each logic is unlikely to be adequate in isolation, and in tandem, the combination of logics will likely provide greater robustness and accuracy (Langley, 1999).

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

The multiple-case research approach was selected for our study because it is especially ideal for studying the under explored phenomenon (i.e., the nature and implications of the competitive strategies of Fintech platforms) that is th focus of our study (Pan & Tan, 2011). Based on our research objectives, we identified two criteria as the basis for our case selection. First, the case organisations must be Fintech platforms that have achieved a significant measur of commercial success because we are looking to develop a theory based on proven, if not best, practices (Tan, Pan & Zuo, 2015). Second, a variety of competitive strategies should have been deployed by the selected Fintech plat forms towards their commercial success as this affords us a wider array of possibilities in our theorising (Tan et al., 2010). Based on these criteria, we selected four highly successful platforms in China representing a variety of Fintech service categories (see classification in Gozman et al., 2018), including wealth management, collateralise loans, crowdfunding, and microfinancing, for the purpose of our study. Each of the selected platforms conforms to the criteria of an “extreme” (Gerring, 2008, p. 653) or “revelatory” (Yin, 2003, p. 43) case because they are all market leaders (i.e., among the Top 10 firms in terms of market share) in their respective service categories. Moreover, the fact that all of the cases share the same environmental context (i.e., they are all based in China) is especially advanta geous because many contextual variables are kept constant, which helps to rule out possible alternative interpreta tions of the data (e.g., where cross-case differences may be attributed to contextual influences).

## 3.1 | Background of selected cases

Our first case study is on Wealth Management Company (WMC—a pseudonym), a Fintech platform based in Hang zhou whose business is centered on providing customers with carefully curated financial products that are safe and simple to understand to invest in. Established in 2015 against a backdrop of market scepticism because of a signifi cant number of fly-by-night platforms and high-profile Fintech scandals (e.g., Ezubao, once China's largest P2P lender, was found to be a Ponzi scheme—see Sheng et al., 2017), WMC sought to provide greater assurance by reducing information asymmetry, simplifying the process of investing, and providing comprehensive risk disclosures This unique value proposition was evidently very well-received by the market. As a reflection of its commercia success, WMC was recognised among the Top 10 wealth management platforms in 2016 and an “exemplary Fintech platform” at the 6th China Finance Summit in 2017. By 2020, the platform had more than 2.3 million registered users with a cumulative transaction volume of over US\$5 billion.

Our second case study is on Loans R Us (LRU—a pseudonym), another Fintech platform from Hangzhou whose business is centered on providing collateralised car loans on a peer-to-peer (P2P) basis. Prospective borrowers are required to put up the vehicle they are purchasing as collateral for their loan, which would be assessed at a physica workshop for condition and value. They would also have to provide personal details such as their identity card and bank account information, as well as their credit records and academic qualifications to create a comprehensive borrower profile that will determine the size and interest rate of the loan. Lenders can then view the information on th borrower, vehicle, and the proposed loan on the LRU platform to decide if they would like to invest in specific loans LRU's commercial success is evidenced by its rapid growth, employing over 2000 workers and establishing an expansive network of over 100 physical workshops and branches within 5 years of its inception. By 2018, LRU wa recognised among the Top 5 P2P car financing platforms in the country. Its annual operating income and net asset value are estimated at US\$24 million and US\$201 million respectively in 2019.

Our third case study is on ProjectStarter.com (PS.com—a pseudonym), a third Fintech platform from Hangzho that is among the Top 5 largest rewards-based crowdfunding platforms in China. On PS.com, project owners can submit their crowdfunding proposals, and if a project is approved, PS.com will help the owner design and develop crowdfunding campaign and publish it online. Supporters who subscribe to the projects are subsequently rewarded with discounts or early access to the product/service developed after the project is completed. As one of the most recognised platforms of the crowdfunding industry in China, PS.com has achieved a significant measure of commer cial success and business growth over the years. Within 3 years of its inception, PS.com has accumulated over 20 million fans, 1.1 million registered users, and a total of 6.3 million subscriptions to the projects offered on its plat form. The cumulative value of all the subscriptions on the platform to date is over USD\$750 million.

Our final case study is on Microfinancing Inc. (MI—a pseudonym), a Fintech platform based in Shanghai that is one of the oldest, and among the Top 5 microfinancing platforms, in China. Catering primarily to customers with lim ited credit histories and would therefore find it difficult to obtain loans from banks and other financial institutions, MI allows individual borrowers to submit loan applications with an interest rate algorithmically determined by a pro prietary risk assessment system. Individual lenders can then decide if they are willing to invest in the loans based on the interest rate on offer. Attesting to MI's commercial success, MI is currently listed on the New York Stock Exchange after attracting over US\$25million in investments from established venture capital firms and institutiona investors such as Sequoia Capital and the Alibaba Group over several funding rounds since its inception. By 2020, i has a revenue of over US\$1.1 billion with over 115 million registered users. It is estimated that one out of every 20 people in China has used MI's services

## 3.2 | Data collection

Data collection unfolded in two phases for each case study. In an initial preparatory phase conducted about 1– 2 months prior to entering the field, we gathered data from a variety of secondary sources including news articles internal corporate documents, and industry reports. The information from these “nontechnical” (Strauss & Corbin, 1998, p. 52) sources was used to enhance our knowledge of the focal case organisation and its competitiv strategies over the years, which formed the basis for formulating our interview questions (Darke et al., 1998). The preparatory phase was followed by a field visit phase that entailed the participation of a team of investigators. The advantage of involving multiple investigators is that we were able to triangulate our observations and the interpreta tions of the data we were collecting (Klein & Myers, 1999). As part of this phase, we visited the offices of each cas organisation to conduct interviews with members of its top management, as well as various senior managers and executives of its key business units (see Table 3). Additional interviews were also conducted with a number of external informants who were intimately familiar with the case organisation and the contextual conditions it was operating in to verify the information that we were receiving. The informants for each case study were identified via chain referral sampling (see Biernacki & Waldorf, 1981) based on a set of preliminary interview questions that we sent in advance to our “gatekeeper” (Pan & Tan, 2011, p. 165) within the organisation. This method of identifying informants was adopted because we, as external researchers, do not possess enough inside information to identif the most appropriate informants for the questions we had independently (Pan & Tan, 2011).

## 3.3 | Data analysis

Data analysis was performed concurrently with data collection to take advantage of the flexibility that the case research method affords (Eisenhardt, 1989). The voluminous and poorly organised qualitative data was first con densed into manageable form via narrative and visual mapping strategies (Langley, 1999). The narrative strateg entailed the construction of a “story” that represented our account of what happened at each of the case organisa tions. The visual mapping strategy, on the other hand, involved documenting our emergent theoretical ideas in series of diagrammatic sketches. After the narrative and the visual maps have been constructed, they were verified with the relevant informants. This was to ensure the validity of both our interpretation of the informant's account and the theory that we were developing (Pan & Tan, 2011).

Following the development of the narrative and the visual maps, we then organised and coded the data collected using the grounded theory techniques of open, axial, and selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). More specifically open coding was used to assign conceptual labels to data (e.g., excerpts from interviews) to create a pool of first-order concepts (see van Maanen, 1979). To illustrate, an informant from MI stated that the organisation “had to innovat within the boundaries of the regulations,” which was coded as the first-order concept of “Innovating within regulatory boundaries.” Following the derivation of the first-order concepts, axial coding was then used to relate the first-order concepts to a number of second-order themes (Gioia et al., 2013). The initial set of second-order themes consists of the six logics of strategy identified earlier in our literature review. There were, however, some first-order concepts that emerged from our data that could not be easily classified under any of the six core logics discussed earlier. This necessitated modifications to our coding scheme (i.e., add a new, or modify/delete an existing, second-order theme— see Strauss & Corbin, 1998), and coding was restarted each time a modification was made. More specifically, we found that we had to create two additional second-order themes (e.g., the “Logic of Envelopment” and the “Logic of Materiality to be discussed in our Findings and Discussion sections) to fully account for the first-order concepts from our dataset that could not be classified. For instance, organizing offline activities, a first-order concept derived from open coding, was categorised under the Logic of Materiality as it neither differentiated the fintech platform from its competitors (Durand & Calori, 2006), nor was it an attempt to conform to environmental or social pressures (Ta et al., 2010). The new and existing second-order themes that emerged from axial coding are summarised in Table 4.

Following axial coding, the second-order themes were then abstracted via selective coding into aggregat dimensions based on the pattern of strategies deployed across the four cases (Gioia et al., 2013). For example, a strategies aligned with the “Logic of Social Congruence” were deployed across all four case organisations, we cat egorised this second-order theme under an aggregate dimension we termed the Strategic Core, which we concep tualise as the common core of strategic logic that underpin the commercial success of Fintech platforms. B recursively iterating between (and thus constantly comparing) theory and data (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007, p 30) in this way, our theory was inductively derived and gradually shaped. This process continued until the state of theoretical saturation is reached (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), which refers to the state where the inductively derived model can comprehensively account for the case data and “incremental learning is minimal because the researchers are observing phenomena seen before” (Eisenhardt, 1989, p. 545).

## 4 | FINDINGS

Our findings across the four case studies indicate that Fintech platforms, as an emerging business form, are indeed confronted with numerous challenges associated with the liability of newness (Singh et al., 1986, p. 171), including a dynamic regulatory landscape, rapidly changing market expectations, and the continuous emergence of new com petitors and substitutes as the global Fintech sector continues to develop and evolve (Gomber et al., 2018a Muthukannan et al., 2020). Consequently, to navigate the environmental uncertainty associated with these challenges, a commonality among the four case organisations studied is that all of them were pursuing strategies aligned with the Logic of Social Congruence (see Tan et al., 2010) to meet societal expectations and stay within the “the range of acceptability” (Deephouse, 1999, p. 152) as defined by the regulatory authorities. Some of the strategies employed across the four case organisations are summarised in Table 5, which include innovating within regulatory boundaries, protecting the welfare of more vulnerable customers, and maintaining frequent interactions with author ities and policymakers.

Beyond this common strategic core, each of the case organisations also pursued distinct strategies that appear to be influenced by contextual conditions and the nature of their Fintech services. We will elaborate on the natur and implications of these strategies in the stream of reporting that follows.

## 4.2 | Case 2: LRU's competitive strategies

In the case of LRU, our informants indicated that the success of its collateralised P2P lending business may be attrib uted to strategies that are in line with what we term the “Logic of Envelopment,” which is defined as the provision of complementary services to cater to the customers' needs holistically and reducing the need for going beyond the boundaries of a platform to obtain those services. A similar example was described in the study of Leong et al. (2017), where a student microloans platform was found to have expanded its offerings to include complementar services like online shopping, employment search, and wealth management. This resulted in a closed loop that allowed its customers to borrow, spend, earn, and invest within the platform's ecosystem. Similarly, in the case of LRU, the platform was able to minimise the necessity of multi-homing (see Barua & Mukherjee, 2021) where it stakeholders have to participate on multiple Fintech platforms to have their needs met by catering to the comple mentary needs of its customers. This differentiates LRU from its competitors through the convenience of one-sto services and keeps its stakeholders within the boundaries of LRU's platform. The Deputy CEO of LRU described th complementary services provided by the platform in line with this strategic logic:

“We are providing more services surrounding cars to our users (as compared to our competitors). First, car insurance… If our users are unable to pay for car insurance, they can use our services to get funding from us. In this way, we charge interest from the borrowers, and at the same time, we earn commission from a (third-party) insurance company. Second, our borrowers can use parking spaces as collateral as well. In China, parking spaces have title deeds, and if borrowers cannot repay their loan, we can sell the parking space. Third, we provide loans for second-hand car dealers. Those second-hand cars will be under our names, and if they cannot repay their loans, we can sel their cars.”

Another type of competitive strategy that was effectively used at LRU is aligned with what we term the “Logic of Materiality, which is defined as the deliberate investment of effort and resources to emphasise the tangibility and physical presence of a platform's services. As indicated earlier, most Fintech platforms are inherently service pro viders that connect suppliers of financial services to the consumers that demand them (Gozman et al., 2018 Pollari, 2016). As such, by enhancing the tangibility and physical presence of its services, LRU was able to instill a sense of familiarity among its customers (Mittendorf, 2018) and increase its perceived credibility through the transfe of trust from its offline presence to its online business (Hahn & Kim, 2009). The Head of Collateral and Loan Opera tions at LRU described the platform's strategies and initiatives in this regard:

“We have offline branches as the “gateway” (to our business). Borrowers have to come to one of our offline branches for evaluation. Our customer service officers will try to gain a complete understanding of the borrowers, including their financial situation, before explaining to them about their responsibilities. Since customers will provide their car as a collateral, after evaluation, our technicians wil assess borrower's car, and install a GPS device in the car to keep track of the car's real-time location…

This (physical presence) increases the confidence of our borrowers in our business since they know they can come to our branches at any time if there are problems”

## 4.3 | Case 3: PS.com's competitive strategies

The evidence from our interviews suggests that the success of PS.com's rewards-based crowdfunding busines stems from its unique value proposition and the market position that it is occupying. In particular, PS.com was able to differentiate its services by directly assisting project owners in the development of media content to package their proposals, making their project pitches more engaging to increase the likelihood of funding success. Moreover, as one of the earliest crowdfunding platforms in China, it was able to utilise the recognition of being a first-mover to defend the market position it had acquired (see Makadok, 1998) in line with the Logic of Positioning (see Sambamurthy et al., 2003). The Chief Operating Officer of PS.com described this strategy:

“Our market position is unique in two ways. First… we were able to create complementary new media content… to attract around 200 million fans on social media, who were subsequently converted to become our initial customers. By using this strategy, we can acquire customers at a very low cost as compared to our competitors. Second, in terms of project selection, unlike our competitors who usually focus on project viability, we emphasize deep engagement. This is because we want to provide our consumers with an upgraded consumption experience.”

In addition, a second strategic thrust that was crucial to the success of PS.com was centered on increasing thei physical presence and the tangibility of their services. The rationale for this is that PS.com's management wanted to increase the platform's perceived credibility in the eyes of their investors. In particular, it sought to incorporat offline events and activities to complement the crowdfunding projects on their platform, so that investors are not only able to browse the project information and listings online, but can participate in those activities to deepen their familiarity and trust in both the platform and the project owners (K. C. Lee et al., 2007) in line with the Logic of Materiality discussed earlier as well. The Operations Manager of PS.com provided an illustration of thi strategy

“We organize plenty of offline activities, including homestay industry national conferences and F&B events, to promote our products and services. In addition, we set up stalls at shopping malls to showcase products that our project owners developed… Furthermore, we have “speed-dating” events to match our users to potential investors in a Ferris wheel, providing investors an opportunity to know more about our owners' projects. Lastly, we also organise events with government officials. We connect homestay operators to the local governments, which is an effective way to promote and increase awareness for their homestay business.

## 4.4 | Case 4: MI's competitive strategies

According to our informants at MI, the success of its microfinancing business is due to its superior risk assessment capabilities, which stem from a proprietary AI-enabled system that it had developed. Through the system, MI is able to compute an interest rate based on a wide variety of parameters such as the borrower's previous transactions on third-party e-commerce platforms, phone number, search history, and even the brand of mobile device/computer that he/she is using. The leverage of this firm-specific capability is in line with the Logic of Leverage (Barney & Clark, 2007), which allows MI to present a loan offer with an interest rate that is commensurate with risks withi seconds of receiving an application. Borrowers would not have to wait days for risk assessment as they would if the were borrowing from a traditional bank, and the instantaneity of MI's services would also prevent them from poten tially submitting applications with other platforms. In addition, the technical sophistication of MI's system would also provide these stakeholders with greater assurance of MI's operational capabilities and credibility at the same time An external industry analyst we interviewed described MI's proprietary risk assessment system:

They built a proprietary system in-house so that they can assess the risks of a loan with speed and precision. This system is underpinned by cutting-edge technologies such as big data and artificia intelligence. This system is a risk assessment tool that can automatically analyze the risk of loan defaults by considering a range of information about the borrower, which is very innovative as not even the traditional banks have done it before”

Another strategy that many of the informants interviewed cited as crucial to MI's success is the way that it tried to cater holistically to the needs of its borrowers and lenders. The primary needs of borrowers and lenders are to obtai a loan and earn interest from the loans they make, respectively, but MI has been deliberate in not only catering to these needs but also complementary needs as well so that their borrowers and lenders do not have to go elsewhere to fulfil those needs. This strategy is an embodiment of the Logic of Envelopment (Leong et al., 2017) described pre viously in that it is aimed at catering to the needs of MI's borrowers and lenders comprehensively and eliminating the need for multi-homing (see Barua & Mukherjee, 2021) for these stakeholders. For instance, a Product Manager at MI described the additional value-adding features that serve to keep its borrowers within the boundaries of the platform:

“On our app, we have a feature named “My Financial Activities”. For this feature, we provide different payment services for our customers. Not only can they pay for their credit card and utility bills, but they can also top up their mobile phone credits. With these functions embedded in our app, it increases the usage rates of our app. Hence, this app is very practical, customers do not only use it to repay debt, but they can also use it for other purposes.”

## 5 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

By bringing together our findings across the four case studies, a typology of competitive strategies that may be especially effective for the various types of Fintech platforms may be inductively derived to address our research ques tion. More specifically, the typology can be represented in the form of a 2x2 framework (refer to Figure 1), and suggests that the nature of the competitive strategies employed by a Fintech platform should be contingent on th (1) heterogeneity and (2) corporeality of its services. We define the former as the extent to which the offerings of the focal Fintech platform can be differentiated, and the latter as the tangibility and physical presence of the Fintech platform's offerings.

To illustrate, MI and LRU are characterised by low service heterogeneity. In the case of MI, the primary differen tiating factor for the microfinancing services it offers for both borrowers and lenders is the interest rate that is algorithmically defined by its proprietary risk assessment system. Similarly, in the case of LRU, the main point of consideration when borrowers and lenders are deciding if they should engage LRU's services is the interest rate on offer. In other words, service heterogeneity is constrained because both of these platforms are only able to differen tiate their services from other platforms through their interest rates. Under these circumstances, customer retention would naturally be challenging (Reimann et al., 2010) for both of these platforms (i.e., customers can defect to a competitor if the competitor offers a better interest rate). To compensate for low service heterogeneity, our finding from both the MI and LRU cases suggest the need for strategies aligned with the Logic of Envelopment (Leong et al., 2017). In doing so, a Fintech platform may achieve what we term Retentive Differentiation, which is whe a Fintech platform is able to distinguish itself from its competitors through its superior ability to retain its customers.

Conversely, PS.com and WMC are characterised by high service heterogeneity. In the context of PS.com, heterogeneity stems from the sheer variety of crowdfunding projects (e.g., ranging from homestay to art and new agricultural projects) that can be proposed or are available for investors to invest in. In the case of WMC, heterogeneity is reflected in the array of financial products that are available for investment, which can be related to a variety of industries or assets. Under this condition, our framework suggests that a Fintech platform should pursue strategies aligned with the Logic of Positioning. This is because the scope for varying its services would represent a potentia range of options for crafting a value proposition that is both unique and valued by a Fintech platform's customer (see similar discussion in Vanderstraeten & Matthyssens, 2012). This would enable the Fintech platform to attain “Attractive Differentiation,” which is when the platform is able to stand out from its competitors and attract more platform participants based on the unique value that it offers (Gawer & Cusumano, 2008).

On a different dimension, MI and WMC are characterised by low service corporeality because the services of both of these platforms are completely virtual in nature without a physical component. At MI, borrowers submit their applications electronically and have their loans approved online without ever having to visit MI's offices. In addition on both of these platforms, the loan or financial product that investors are able to invest in are also completely digitised. With low service corporeality, our framework suggests that a Fintech platform should pursue strategie that are aligned with the Logic of Leverage (Sambamurthy et al., 2003) because of the virtualised nature of its services can be the source of legitimacy challenges (see Carayannopoulos, 2009). And by leveraging its firm-specific resources and capabilities (i.e., the proprietary risk assessment system and the reputation and track record of its founders in the cases of MI and WMC, respectively) to demonstrate the platform's strengths relative to its competitors (Barney & Clark, 2007), the Fintech platform may be able to provide its stakeholders with greater assurance of the viabilit and sustainability of its business towards the attainment of Cognitive Legitimacy, defined as the extent to which a new venture or business form is accepted and taken-for-granted by its stakeholders (Aldrich & Fiol, 1994)

On the other hand, LRU and PS.com are characterised by high service corporeality. In the context of LRU the tangibility and physical presence of its business are reflected in its network of over 100 physical workshops and storefronts across China. For PS.com, the corporeality of its business is underpinned by the fact that many of its projects are related to tangible products or facilities that its customers can physically visit. With high servic corporeality, our findings from both the MI and LRU cases suggest the need for strategies aligned with the Logic of Materiality (see K. C. Lee et al., 2007). This is because by leveraging the physical aspects of its business, a Fintech platform may be able to signal that it is enmeshed in the more traditional economic and social systems with which consumers are familiar” (Stewart, 2003, p. 9), which would translate to Cognitive Legitimacy as wel (Mittendorf, 2018).

Finally, our framework suggests that successful Fintech platforms are underpinned by a common strategic core consisting of strategies aligned with the Logic of Social Congruence (Tan et al., 2010). This is evident in the way all th four case organisations studied pursued strategies that sought to align each organisation with the evolving regulator landscape and societal expectations (e.g., taking steps to protect the more vulnerable segments of society). The stra tegic core represents the set of basic conditions that must be in place for a Fintech platform's survival, and a founda tion upon which other competitive strategies may be built on. Our conceptualisation here is aligned with the view that businesses should seek an optimal balance of differentiation and conformity (Zhao et al., 2017), and without this strategic core, differentiation is arguably pointless because other entities may be reluctant to provide the Fintec platforms with the resources they require for their survival (Durand & Calori, 2006). The finding from our case stud ies suggests that the strategic core provides the means for a Fintech platform to attain both Regulative and Normativ Legitimacy. Both forms of legitimacy refer to the recognition that a Fintech platform is “right and proper” (Tyler, 2006 p. 376), with the former stemming from its conformity to legal requirements by authorities, such as government or public agencies, and the latter from the alignment of the platform's espoused values with the beliefs and norms tha represent the prescriptive, evaluative, and obligatory pillars of social life (Scott, 2014).

## 5.1 | Limitations and future research

Our study is not without its limitations. First, even though we have conducted multiple case studies, a general criti cism of the case research approach is the issue of external validity or generalisability (Walsham, 2006). An issue that limits the generalisability of our study, in particular, may lie in its singular context (i.e., Fintech platforms from a spe cific country with the same regulatory regime and cultural backdrop). While it must be acknowledged that statistica generalisation is impossible in four cases, this study invokes the principles of analytic generalisation (see Silva & Hir schheim, 2007) or what some researchers refer to as “generalizing from description to theory” (Lee & Baskerville, 2003 p. 235). In any case, future research can be directed at statistically validating the propositions of our theoretica framework, so that the potential boundary conditions (e.g., participating platform entities, structure of platform as discussed in Kazan et al., 2018) of our model can be explored.

A second limitation is that although we were able to identify the combinations of strategic logics that are likel to be effective for four different permutations of service heterogeneity and corporeality, we must acknowledge that we are nevertheless constrained by the limits of the data collected and that other variations may be possible. Indeed strategic choices tend to be both context and path dependent (Jarzabkowski, 2004), and since both of these aspects are highly variable, it is impossible to rule out other possibilities in terms of the nature and combination of the strategic logics employed. And while we found the dimensions of service heterogeneity and corporeality to be prominent in our collected data, we cannot exclude the possibility that there could be other factors, platform characteristics and/or service dimensions that could influence the effectiveness of the platforms' strategies. While it is certainl impossible to capture all possible strategies and combinations within a single study, we submit that our theoretica framework can nevertheless serve as the baseline model for subsequent studies to build upon because we have developed what we believe to be cogent and plausible explanations (Klein & Myers, 1999) for our findings that reso nate with many previous studies (e.g., Barney & Clark, 2007; Leong et al., 2017, among many others). In any case the variations of strategic logic and other influencing factors that have not been explored in our study would cer tainly be a fruitful avenue for future research

## 5.2 | Theoretical implications

At the outset of this paper, we noted that there is currently little research on the nature of the competitive strategie that are appropriate for a Fintech platform, and the implications of those strategies on its performance. This study i one of the earliest to contribute towards addressing these gaps and, in the inductively derived framework we pres ented, we have made several important theoretical contributions to the Fintech literature

First, in uncovering how the competitive strategies may be influenced by the characteristics of Fintech services our study has not only shed light on the competitive strategies that are more likely to be effective but specifie which strategies should be deployed for different types of Fintech platforms as well. More specifically, our framework suggests that a Fintech platform should pursue strategies aligned with the Logic of Envelopment (Leong et al., 2017) or the Logic of Positioning (Sambamurthy et al., 2003) when service heterogeneity is low or high respec tively, and the former would lead to retentive differentiation, while the latter would affect attractive differentiation In addition, our framework suggests that a Fintech platform should pursue strategies aligned with the Logic of Lever age (Barney & Clark, 2007) and the Logic of Materiality (Stewart, 2003) when service corporeality is correspondingl low or high. Both of these logics would translate to cognitive legitimacy through different mechanisms. The former would provide assurance of the viability and sustainability of the platform's business through the exploitation of firm-specific resources and capabilities, while the latter would enhance familiarity and facilitate the development of trust by emphasising the tangibility and physical presence of the Fintech platform's services. With these findings, our study extends the state of existing knowledge by not only demonstrating, as previous scholars have (e.g., Kaza et al., 2018), those different competitive strategies are required for different types of Fintech platforms, but ou study has explicitly identified four previously unexplored contingency conditions that would have a defining influ ence on the effectiveness of the Fintech platforms' competitive strategies as well.

But are the combinations we found unique or specific to Fintech platforms, or are they applicable to non-plat form-based businesses, and/or other digital platforms as well? For non-platform-based businesses, previous articles on their competitive strategies have suggested that a singular strategic logic may be sufficient (Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999), or they could switch between logics that emphasise differentiation or conformity depending on the state of the external environment (Tan et al., 2010). For platform-based business models in general, previous articles on their competitive strategies have typically been centered on scalability (Huang et al., 2017) and a growth-at-all costs mentality (Parker & Van Alstyne, 2016). By contrast, each of the combinations we uncovered represents a blend of differentiation and conformity (Durand & Calori, 2006), which is likely required due to the Fintech platforms dual identity (Stulz, 2019). More specifically, some scholars (Ng et al., 2022) have suggested that a Fintech platform tends to be confronted by competing for institutional logics as it is simultaneously a growth-oriented technology firm and a risk-averse financial firm (e.g., risk aversity could curb growth). Consequently, we submit it is highly plausibl that our findings are specific to the context of Fintech platforms because they account for this inherent contradic tion, although ascertaining this would be beyond the scope of our study. Nevertheless, validating the specificity o generalisability of our findings would certainly be a promising direction to pursue for future scholars of Fintech plat form strategies.

Second, while the uncertainty surrounding the global Fintech sector is widely acknowledged because of th shifting regulatory landscape (Ringe & Ruof, 2020) and the sheer volume and variety of Fintech innovations that ar constantly emerging (Gozman et al., 2018), the existing literature does not provide much guidance on how this uncertainty can be navigated. Towards addressing this knowledge gap, our study suggests that Fintech platforms can navigate uncertainty by pursuing strategies aligned with the Logic of Social Congruence (Tan et al., 2010). Our stud also hints at the various forms that these strategies can take, including innovating in line with regulatory boundaries and societal expectations, adopting a pro-social orientation, and maintaining frequent interactions with the authori ties and policymakers. The depth and specificity of our empirically grounded prescriptions are uncommon in the exis ting literature (e.g., Dhar & Stein, 2017; Kazan et al., 2018), and provide a foundation that future studies into the competitive strategies of Fintech platforms or Fintech platform performance can build upon

Third, beyond its implications for the Fintech literature, our study has implications for our understanding of th core logics of strategy (e.g., Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Sambamurthy et al., 2003) as well. In particular, our study suggests that there are the Logics of Envelopment and Materiality that currently fall beyond the existing taxonomie of strategic logics (e.g., Tan et al., 2010). The two logics are conceptual innovations that are distinct from those iden tified previously for different reasons. In the case of the Logic of Envelopment, the logic enables a platform to stand apart from its competitors not via a unique market position, the exploitation of firm-specific resources and capabili ties, continuous innovation, or the unlocking of synergies among network entities. Instead, the logic emphasises the retention of customers within the platform's boundaries (Leong et al., 2017). In other words, the Logic of Envelopment presents a novel mechanism for achieving an alternative, retentive form of differentiation, which is distinct from the principles implied by the other strategic logics. On the other hand, the existing view of the core logics of strategy is that they tend to be either centered on differentiation or conformity (Tan et al., 2010). The Logic of Mate riality identified in our study, however, does not contribute significantly to either. Instead, it is merely a deliberate strategic choice to emphasise the tangibility and physical presence of a firm's offerings so that its stakeholders ar more likely to recognise the firm as legitimate and credible (K. C. Lee et al., 2007). Consequently, this paper can potentially serve as a catalyst for further research on these newly identified logics of strategy, and, by com plementing the existing taxonomies (e.g., Lengnick-Hall & Wolff, 1999; Tan et al., 2010), a more holistic and com plete picture of the strategic options for Fintech platforms, or other contemporary business forms in general, ma emerge.

Fourth, this study hints at a possible solution to the paradox of homogeneity (see Durand & Calori, 2006) in the context of Fintech platforms. In particular, our study reconciles the conflicting perspectives of differentiation and conformity by suggesting that the attainment of Optimal Distinctiveness (see Zuckerman, 2016) could be a viable solution. This represents a departure from the “contingency solution” (Tan et al., 2010, p. 195), which suggests that the effectiveness of strategies that lead to either heterogeneity or conformity is contingent on whether the environ: ment is in a state of equilibrium or revolution, where strategies that lead to heterogeneity are more effective in the former state, while those that lead to conformity are more effective in the latter state. Our proposed solution also extends the “strategic balance” perspective (see Deephouse, 1999), which suggests that firms with a moderate amount of differentiation have the best organisational performance. More specifically, our study reveals that it is no the amount of differentiation that needs to be uncritically curtailed, but rather the efforts to distinguish a Fintec platform from its competitors should be based on the heterogeneity of its services (i.e., whether differentiation should be targeted at attracting or retaining customers) and tempered by legitimacy considerations.

## 5.3 | Practical implications

In spite of these limitations, we believe that the results of our study have significant implications for practice, particu larly for two groups of stakeholders operating within the global Fintech space. The first stakeholder group would, of course, consist of the entrepreneurs and managers of Fintech platforms. For this stakeholder group, our study can be a source of empirically supported prescriptions on how to overcome some of the key challenges that they may b facing (e.g., see Barkema et al., 2002). In particular, by describing and explaining the competitive strategies that ar especially effective for the different types of Fintech platforms, this study should be especially useful for the majorit of Fintech practitioners who have been unable to translate the resource investments in their platforms into eco nomic returns, because it can help them identify the appropriate competitive strategies to adopt to re-align thei Fintech platforms to the path of commercial success.

The second stakeholder group that can be potentially informed by our study consists of the government authori ties and policymakers who are concerned with the development and maintenance of a vibrant Fintech ecosystem While it is almost inevitable that the majority of Fintech start-ups will fail (Dietz et al., 2015; Finextra., 2018), for thi stakeholder group, our study can help them to identify the ventures that are more likely to succeed because they ar deploying proven strategies that are appropriate for the Fintech services that they offer. Resources can then subse quently be allocated or diverted to these ventures with priority so that their probability of survival is increased fur ther. Conversely, our study can also help government authorities and policymakers to identify the ventures that are less likely to succeed. The appropriate help can then be given to these firms (e.g., advice on strategy or establishing a regulatory sandbox for new Fintech variants), so as to preserve the resources that have been invested in the estab lishment of these ventures and, potentially, mitigate the rate of Fintech failure.

## SAMPLE INTERVIEW GUIDE

## Thematic Interview Guide for Interview with MI's Chief Strategy Officer

## Organisational Background:

• What is the business of MI?

• How did the idea for this business come about?

• Can you briefly describe the history of MI?

• How would you describe the culture of MI?

• Does the culture of MI have an effect on its business model, its internal operations, and its relationship with its business partners?

## Industry/Contextual Conditions:

• How would you describe the microfinancing/Fintech industry in China?

• How has the industry evolved over the years?

• Who are the key players (not just competitors, but suppliers and complementary service providers as well)?

• What is your relationship with these key players?

• How would you describe the regulatory environment surrounding the microfinancing/Fintech industry?

• What is the role of the government?

• How have the regulators promoted the growth of the industry?

• What are some of the measures that the regulators have employed to regulate the industry?

## Competitive Strategies:

• What are the competitive strategies that were crucial to MI's commercial success?

• How do these strategies differentiate your organisation from your competitors?

• Have these strategies evolved over time? If so, could you explain how the strategies have evolved?

• How does MI cope with the uncertainty in the market and regulatory environment?

• What are some strategies/measures to ensure that MI is able to respond to change quickly and effectively?

• How would you describe the management style/philosophy of MI?

• How did this management style/philosophy come about?

## Platform Performance:

• How would you describe MI's performance?

• What are MI's main competitive advantages? Where do these advantages originate?

• What resources are required for MI's operations? Has it been easy to acquire those resources?

• Microfinancing is a relatively new concept in China. What are your strategies to promote the acceptance of microfinancing among your stakeholders?

• What were the key phases of business development? Could you describe the main operational challenges associated with each phase and how they were overcome?
