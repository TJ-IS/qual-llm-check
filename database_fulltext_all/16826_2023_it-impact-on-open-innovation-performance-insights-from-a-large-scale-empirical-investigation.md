# IT impact on open innovation performance: Insights from a large-scale empirical investigation

## Abstract

Motivated by prior inconclusive findings on the effectiveness of open innovation initiatives in generating business value and the complexity of open innovation, this study draws on the IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective to propose that the mobilization of Information Technology (IT) resources can positively affect the development of open innovation initiatives and, in turn, improve firms’ open innovation performance. Our empirical analysis of more than 1,100 large Spanish firms confirms that firms' IT resource investments open innovation performance in terms of product and process innovations. This research contributes to the business value of IT and to Information Systems (IS) research on open innovation by evidencing the enabling role of IT investment in achieving open innovation performance gains.

## 2. Theoretical background

## 2.1. IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective

The literature on the business value of IT is a mature research line within the IS field that has widely focused on exploring the role of IT in generating business value (Melville et al., 2004; Dong and Yang, 2015; Liu et al.,

2020). Within this research line, the IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective is found to be a well-established perspective to explain how a firm’s IT resources affect firm performance through other intermediate capabilities (Benitez et al., 2018b; 2018c). This perspective suggests that IT resources per se do not necessarily improve firm performance directly, but indirectly through other intermediate organizational capabilities such as flexibility, absorptive capacity, talent management, or organizational learning (Benitez and Walczuch, 2012; Benitez et al., 2018b; Braojos et al., 2020). For example, Mithas et al. (2011) suggest that IT-enabled information management capability (i.e., human IT resource) influences firm performance through a collaboration software (i.e., IT resource) results in greater team collaboration and manufacturing performance (i.e., increased product quality, reduced cycle time and product developme t) Following this theoretical perspective, some studies also show the role of IT in enabling innovation ou es (Malhotra et al., 2005; Joshi 2018). However, understanding of what happens inside the black box of IT-enabled open innovation is still limited.

Therefore, this research draws on the IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective to study how a firm’s investments in technological and human IT resources (i.e., IT resources) do not only improve firm performance performance) directly, but also indirectly through other intermediate organizational capabilities, namely the capability to execute open innovation initiatives. Against this background, the core constructs related to our study are: IT investment, open innovation initiatives, and open innovation performance. IT investment is defined as the investment in technological and IT human capital. While technological IT capital refers to investment in hardware and software, IT human capital refers to investment in IT professionals. Open innovation initiatives refer to “the use of purposive inflows and outflows of knowledge to accelerate internal innovation, and expand the markets for external use of innovation, respectively” (Chesbrough, 2003; p.1). Open innovation performance is defined as the outcomes of the process of changing existing internal products and processes and/or developing new products and processes together with other

external sources (Benitez et al., 2018a).

## 2.2. IT, business innovation activities, and open innovation activities

A major area within the literature on business value of IT has widely recognized the positive relationship between IT and innovation (e.g., Hopkins and Brynjolfsson, 2010; Xue et al., 2012; Ravichandran et al., 2017; Trantopoulos et al., 2017). One research line suggests that the positive impact of IT on innovation is mainly due to the support of knowledge activities. For example, Joshi et al. (2010) suggest that IT provides a set of knowledge capabilities that contribute to firm innovation, while Benitez et al. (2018a) state that IT infrastructure influences innovation performance through knowledge ambidexterity. On the other hand, there is literature that argues that this relationship is not only mediated by knowledge activities, but through other mechanisms. For example, Chen et al. (2015) show that IT capabilities affect product innovation performance through corporate nizational virtues enabled by IT influence organizational capabilities, which in turn improve organizational innovation. Moreover, other body of work also contributes to this line of research finding different associations between IT and innovation outcomes. For instance, prior literature revealed that the combination of IT and research and development (R&D) indirectly that IT can mitigate the diminishing returns to R&D considered, this large body of work provides evidence that IT plays a crucial role in firms’ innovation outcomes.

A topic within the Innovation field that attracts the attention of practitioners and academics alike is the which the advent of new digital technologies enables firms to increasingly rely on external knowledge sources to innovate and compete (Boudreau and Lakhani, 2009). Firms such as Procter & Gamble, Ford, General Electric, Acciona, and Philips are waving the flag of open innovation and working openly at unprecedented levels to gain a competitive advantage (Dahlander and Wallin, 2020; Cui et al., 2022). The core of open innovation is to open up the innovation process and exploit and take advantage of external knowledge for innovation success (Cui et al., 2018). Although still in an early stage, IS research started to investigate the relationship between IT and open innovation. For example, it was shown that IT moderates the value of search in multiple external knowledge sources for process innovation performance (Trantopoulos et al., 2017). Similarly, Cui et al. (2015) suggest that

IT flexibility and IT breadth, when aligned, enhance innovation radicalness and volume, and that IT integration and IT depth, when aligned, only enhance innovation volume, with breadth and depth being the two components of external search openness. However, there are also studies that, despite highlighting the importance and power of openness in combination with IT, caution about the negative effects of open approaches and question their effectiveness (e.g., Schlagwein et al., 2017). Contributing to this body of literature, Gomez et al. (2017) found that the relationship between open innovation strategies and invention/product innovation is stronger when the firm invests in IT, but that this relationship becomes negative when firms use open innovation strategies more l opposing arguments, together with the limited IS research on the effect of IT and the use of external knowledge sources on firms process innovation, open up an unexplored area that needs more in-depth development and theorization. ## 3. Research model and hypotheses development

## 3.1. IT investment and open innovation initiative

As a key organizational resource with increased penetration into innovation activities, we argue that firms can invest in software capital such as enterprise systems, common digital platforms, or databases will be better able to effectively manage and integrate knowledge from external sources (Benitez et al., 2022). Previous research has shown that collaborative and flexible IT infrastructures enable the creation of business value in the form of accelerated internal innovation by providing fast and consistent access to external knowledge flows (Bharadwaj et al., 2007; Cui et al., 2015). Second, previous IS research also shows that investing in enterprise systems such as supply chain management or customer relationship management systems allows firms to establish close and long-term relationships with suppliers and customers (Hendricks et al., 2007). Such relationships will allow firms to, first, have immediate access to expertise in some cases, and second, be better able to find and exploit new and unknown external knowledge, fresh ideas and competence such as technological advancements or new customer preferences (Braojos et al., 2020), supporting the deployment of open innovation initiatives. Third, open innovation is about embracing new partners, which is also one of the common challenges of open innovation (Dahlander and Wallin, 2020). Investment in integrated systems has been demonstrated to enable a firm “to share information, coordinate activities, and align processes with its partners” (Cui et al., 2015; p. 351), allowing better integration and communication between users within the firm e.g., employees, and outside the firm e.g., universities (Gold et al., 2001; Trantopoulos et al., 2017). Therefore, an integrated system provides a stable foundation for shared business operations that supports open innovation initiatives. Finally, investment in IT applications supports the acquisition and sharing of both internal and external insights and provides more transparent processes to all the firm’s stakeholders (Trantopoulos et al., 2017), thus increasing the likelihood that the firm searches for knowledge from different sources to accelerate internal innovation. Moreover, these IT applications allow a better understanding of the required knowledge, leading to a more “targeted and cost-efficient” search of external sources (Kleis et al., 2012; Trantopoulos et al., 2017). Against this background, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1 (H1): There is a positive relationship between IT investment and open innovation initiatives.

## 3.2. Open innovation initiatives and open innovation performance

Following prior arguments on the business value of open innovation, we argue that open innovation initiatives external flows of knowledge to accelerate internal inn ation will enrich their knowledge repository, adding new options and ideas that may be not available ally, and thus increase the likelihood of finding new ways to develop new products and processes w nal parties (March, 1991; Katila and Ahuja, 2002). This is for firms that are able to leverage and exploit external knowledge are likely to be more tolerant to changes, more adaptable and more innovative than those that are not (Laursen and Salter, 2006). In addition, firms who draw from external sources are in a better position to build and maintain good and long-term collaborations with third parties (Laursen and Salter, 2006), and may foster higher levels of creativity because their existing knowledge is exposed to new approaches and distinct modes of reasoning (Cui et al., 2015; Gomez et al., 2017). Therefore, and under these circumstances, these firms are more likely to be innovative and achieve more gains from their open innovation initiatives. Third, firms conducting innovation initiatives with other firms or institutions will reduce the costs and risks associated with innovation activities due to the potential sharing of R&D expenses, which will facilitate the successful deployment of open innovation initiatives. In addition, these firms will benefit from existing external knowledge that they would otherwise have to develop themselves at considerable costs (Gomez et al., 2017). Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2 (H2): There is a positive relationship between open innovation initiatives and open innovation performance.

## 4. Research design

## 4.1. Sample and data collection

To study our conceptual model, we collected information from the Spanish Technological Innovation Panel (PITEC). PITEC is a panel database that is the result of the joint efforts of the Spanish National Statistics Institute (INE) and the Spanish Science and Technology Foundation (FECYT) and is included in the General Plan for Statistics on Science and Technology promoted by the Statistical Office of the European Communities to quantify the innovation activities of Spanish firms (PITEC, 2020). The data was extracted from the Spanish Community Innovation Survey (CIS), which draws from Yale survey, the SPRU innovation database, the OsloManual and the FrascatiManual (OECD, 2002, 2005; Laursen and Salter, 2006; Barge and Lopez, 2014). Moreover, PITEC and similar panel databases have been validated and used in prior studies such as Trantopoulos et al. (2017) for Swiss firms, Ghisetti et al. (2015) for European companies, or Gutierrez et al. (2022), Trigo and Vence (2012) and Barge and Lopez (2014) for Spanish firms. To study our conceptual model, we deliberately focus on Spanish firms. The Spanish market is gaining strength in terms of innovation. For example, 90 of the 100 top companies in R&D have a subsidiary in Spain<sup>1</sup>. Additionally, according to the 2019 European Innovation Scoreboard, Spain scores high in sales impact/sales of new-to-market and new-to-firm innovations, which refers to the turnover generated by new products and products being ranked second in Europe. The report from Sopra Steria also revealed that Spain showed to have the highest number of respondents with a dedicated internal business unit to manage open innovation projects (Open Innovation Report, 2023). Hence, the Spanish market is an appropriate context in which to develop this research. Moreover, PITEC is eminently suitable for our purposes as it is the most extensive database to explore business innovations in the Spanish market with more than 460 variables over time and the inclusion of lags for explanatory variables (Gutierrez et al., 2022).

To test the hypotheses, the study sample is obtained as follows: (1) the original sample includes information from a panel of more than 12,000 Spanish firms since 2003; (2) we eliminated firms that joined the panel after 2010; (3) we also removed firms that showed deficiencies (i.e., missing information on key constructs and (4) we only retained firms with at least 251 employees. deliberately focused on large-sized firms because they tend to have sufficient resources to explore and innovate, but are, ceteris paribus, less flexible and innovative (Zhao et al., 2016). The survey items used from the PITEC database include information about investments in IT for innovation, open innovation initiatives carried out by a firm, and the output of open innovation initiatives. The items used in our study are detailed in the next subsection.

## 4.2. Items

## 4.2.1. Composite model

All concepts involved in this study (i.e., IT investment, open innovation initiatives, and open innovation performance) are considered artifacts and are modeled as composite constructs. Composite constructs, also called emergent variables, can serve as proxy for concepts that are understood as theoretically justified and human-made constructions (Benitez et al., 2020; Castillo et al., 2021, Yu et al., 2021). Following Braojos et al. (2020, p.6), composites represent “emergent, strong, complex, and firm-made concepts” where the indicators are regarded as ingredients of the concept, making up or composing the concept rather than causing it. While behavioral concepts are usually modeled as common factors in a reflective measurement model, artifacts are preferably modeled as composite constructs in a composite model (Henseler, 2017). We view the “ingredients” of the IT investment, open innovation initiatives and open innovation performance as a descriptor of different aspects of the constructs which are necessary to understand the constructs (Benitez et al., 2020).

## 4.2.2. IT investment

Using data from the PITEC survey, we operationalize IT investment with two indicators: (1) natural logarithm of investment in technological IT capital (hardware and software) in Euros in 2010, and (2) natural logarithm of investment in IT human capital in Euros in 2010. ## 4.2.4. Open innovation performance

Open innovation performance is specified as composite construct using two indicators referring to (1) open product innovation performance and (2) open process innovation performance. These indicators show who developed the product or the process innovation in the period 2012-2014. The possible range of answers is: 0, if this question is not answered; 1, if these innovations were developed by other firms or institutions (other firms from the same group and consulting firms included); 2, if the innovations were developed just by the firm; 3, if the innovations were developed by the firm, through the adaptation or modification of goods or services/process originally developed by other firms or institutions; and 4 if the innovations were developed by the firm, together with other firms or institutions.

## 4.2.5. Control variables

To study the effects of IT investment and open innovation initiatives on open innovation performance, we controlled for the effects of past firm performance, firm size, industry, firm age, and technological development. All the control variables were taken from the PITEC database. The use of these control variables is consistent with previous research on open innovation (Cui et al., 2015; Gomez et al., 01 ; Gutierrez et al., 2022). We also control for these variables to check whether the impact of IT investment on open innovation performance remains natural logarithm of the revenues in 2011. Similarly, firm size was measured as the natural logarithm of the average total number of employees for the firms tend to have more resources to invest in innovation activities. We also control for industry differences as some sectors may be inherently more innovative than others and therefore the output of open innovation according to the National Classification of Economical Activities and industry as single-indicator constructs to control for potential industry effects. Firm age was measured as the natural logarithm of the number of years the firm was operating until 2014 and accounts for the potential effect of experience on innovation performance (Gutierrez et al., 2022). Finally, we also controlled for technological development as we expect that those firms dedicating more R&D resources to technological development will be better able to obtain gains from open innovation initiatives. Technological development was measured as the average percentage of the internal expenditure on R&D for technological development for the period 2012-2014.

## 5. Empirical analysis and results

## 5.1. Estimation strategy

We followed an estimation strategy composed of two steps. In the first step, we run a confirmatory composite analysis to evaluate the composite constructs involved in the model. In the second step, we estimate our proposed model. In both steps, we used PLS-PM for parameter estimation as implemented in the statistical software ADANCO (Version 2.3.2; Henseler and Dijkstra, 2015). In doing so, we used ADANCO’s default options for PLS-PM.

## 6.1. Key findings

However, understanding how to handle the complexity brought by open innovation is challenging for both academics and business leaders. In particular, prior research on open innovation has cautioned about the effectiveness of open approaches (Gomez et al., 2017; Schlagwein et al., 2017), suggesting that there may be complementary assets or underexplored mechanisms playing a pivotal role in achieving open innovation performance gains. Consequently, this research draws on prior IS research on open innovation to help reconcile differing perspectives and arguments about the IT-open innovation-performance relationship. With this purpose in mind, this paper analyzes the role of IT investment in enabling open innovation initiatives and its subsequent effect on open innovation performance in terms of product and process innovation performance. Prior research is limited in two ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, only Gomez et al., (2017) have conceptualized the IT variable as a pure IT investment reported by firms. This is important as IT resource investments represent a significant portion of firms’ overall investment (Joshi et al., 2021). In contrast, prior research has considered IT capabilities or IT-enabled capabilities and their impact on open innovation outcomes (Cui et al., 2015; 2022). Second, prior IS research on open innovation has mainly focused on innovation performance. This research focuses on open innovation performance, a component of innovation performance, given that open innovation efforts are not always translated into business value (Dahlander et al., 2021). Based on our analysis of more than 1,100 large Spanish firms from the PITEC database, we found that IT investment facilitates the firm’s open innovation performance and that this relationship is partially mediated by the capability of the firm to execute open innovation initiatives. ## 6.2. Theoretical contributions to IS research

This research explores and extends one of the research streams that most fundamentally contributes to the IS discipline: the field of business value of IT (Liu et al., 2020). First, to deepen our understanding of the enabling role of IT to draw knowledge from external sources to accelerate internal innovation, we built on the well-established IT-enabled organizational capabilities perspective. Following this theoretical approach, this study provides a comprehensive but parsimonious theory on the role of IT investment in the execution of open innovation initiatives and open innovation performance, where the aim is to “explain much by little” (Friedman, 1994, p.153) and allow the replication and explanation of the research model (Sharma et al., 2019). IT is shown to have a pivotal role in enabling and supporting open innovation initiatives. This paper is different from previous research in terms of how the IT variable is conceptualized and operationalized. In this study, IT investment is considered in terms of both technological (software and hardware) and human IT capital. ## 6.4. Limitations and future lines of research

Our study presents shortcomings that suggest some promising future research opportunities. First, although the items are able to capture the nature of our studied constructs, we recognize that they are constricted by the PITEC survey. Second, we focus on Spanish firms, this fact may limit the generalizability of results. Future IS research may expand our study to a broader context to examine whether there are significant differences among countries. Third, we have not used information on the role of different types of technologies, thus, we have not analyzed the different potential effects of particular technologies on the execution of open innovation initiatives and open innovation performance. Laura Ruiz is an Assistant Professor of IS at NEOMA Business School, France. She holds a double Ph.D. in IS from the Rennes School of Business, Rennes, France, and the University of Granada, Granada, Spain. Jose Benitez is a Full Professor of Information Systems (IS) at EDHEC Business School, France. His teaching interests and instructional expertise cover managing digital business transformation, digital innovation, the business value of digital technologies, IT management, IT strategy, theory development, and quantitative research methods in IS research at Ph.D., Executive MBA, business world and society.
