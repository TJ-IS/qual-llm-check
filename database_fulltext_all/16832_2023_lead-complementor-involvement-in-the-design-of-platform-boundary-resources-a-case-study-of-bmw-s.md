# Lead complementor involvement in the design of platform boundary resources: A case study of BMW's onboard apps

## Abstract

An increasing number of companies have implemented digital platforms to attract complementors who create innovations on the platform. Establishing such digital platforms is a challenge for incumbent companies because they lack related experience and capabilities. In particular, boundary resources that are the interface between the platform and complementors need to be well-designed to attract complementors and keep them engaged. We propose that lead complementor involvement helps incumbent companies to improve the design of the platform boundary resources. In a multi-year action research study, we established lead complementor involvement in the boundary resource design of a digital platform for automotive onboard apps at the BMW Group and evaluated how boundary resources improved. Along three episodes, we illustrate that lead complementor involvement was effective and we summarise our findings in four propositions: (1) lead complementor involvement facilitates platform emergence for incumbent companies by enhancing boundary resource design, (2) lead complementor involvement is more efficient in higher platform layers, (3) lead complementor involvement is facilitated by an increase of intensity and frequency of interactions between complementors and platform owner and (4) establishing a complementor community helps to continuously identify opportunities for lead complementor involvement. We contribute to the information systems literature on platform emergence and design by focusing on the process of designing boundary resources and by considering the heterogeneity of complementors. Furthermore, we contextualise the concept of lead user involvement in the domain of digital platforms and compare and contrast lead complementors and lead users.

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The rise of digital platforms has resulted in a remarkable transformation of numerous industries (Cusumano et al., 2019; Parker et al., 2016; Tiwana, 2014). Digital platforms are software-based systems that offer a core functionality and interfaces through which complementary apps interact with the core (Tiwana et al., 2010, p. 676). The generative capabilities of digital platforms enhance the development of innovative third-party apps by complementors (also referred to as third-party developers, e.g., Tiwana, 2014). For example, Apple's success has been fueled by hundreds of thousands of complementors who developed apps for Apple's mobile operating system iOS. As a consequence of this ‘platform revolution’ (Parker et al., 2016), the locus of value creation is no longer within linear value chains but within a digital platform ecosystem, that is, the ecosystem of complementors who engage in generative activities on a digital plat form (de Reuver et al., 2017; Hein et al., 2019).

Incumbent companies are forced to break with traditional product-centric development practises to compete with platform-centric firms (Sandberg et al., 2020; Svahn et al., 2017; Yoo et al., 2010). As one example the automotive industry—which is still one of the economically most important industries in Germany and othe European countries (e.g., Statista, 2021)—is heavily affected by the disruption caused by digital platforms While in the past, customers mostly considered characteristics such as design and safety when choosing a car brand, today, they also expect the availability of digital services in their cars (NTT, 2020). Therefore, car manufacturers strive to embrace digital innovation by implementing digital platforms in their vehicles (Svahn et al., 2015; Svahn et al., 2017). However, the emergence of digital platforms (de Reuver et al., 2017; Iyer et al., 2007) is challenging for these incumbent companies because they lack experience in platform design and have to adjust existing and create new capabilities (Schreieck et al., 2021; Svahn et al., 2017; Tilson et al., 2010; Tiwana et al., 2010). For example, automotive companies are used to closely collaborating with suppliers and managing them in a top-down manner; they have little experience working with loosely-coupled complementors that create bottom-up innovation on a digital platform.

A key element of platform design is boundary resource design—that is, the design of the resources that the plat form owner provides to support the development efforts of complementors (Eaton et al., 2015; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013). While the role of boundary resources has been well studied (Förderer et al., 2019; Karhu et al., 2018), there is little understanding of how boundary resources can be designed to best address the require ments of complementors. Recent research that focuses on complementors of digital platforms indicates tha complementors are heterogenous, in particular concerning their capability to develop high-quality apps and the degree of guidance they need (e.g., Binken & Stremersch, 2009; Förderer, 2020). We argue that involving some of the more capable complementors in the process of designing boundary resources helps platform owners to implement platforms that are more attractive for complementors overall.

While involving complementors in boundary resource design has not been discussed in information system literature so far, involving users in the product design is an established concept (Schreier & Prügl, 2008; von Hippel, 1986) that has been applied in information systems as well (e.g., Bano & Zowghi, 2014). We transfer the concept of lead user involvement in product design to the context of digital platforms, arguing that involvin lead complementors in the early stages of boundary resource design can be promising, particularly for incumbent companies that lack experience in and capabilities as a platform owner. In analogy to lead user involvement we define lead complementors as those complementors whose requirements concerning boundary resourc design are representative of a broader group of complementors but who face these requirements earlier than other complementors (cf., von Hippel, 1986). Lead complementor involvement then refers to granting lead complementors early access to boundary resources and engaging with them to gather feedback to improv boundary resource design. To study the potential of lead complementor involvement, we pose the researc question: ‘How can an incumbent platform owner involve lead complementors to enhance boundary resourc design?’.

Towards this end, we conducted an extensive action research study of an emerging digital platform for automotive onboard apps. The study is embedded in the development division of the BMW Group and provides rich insights into lead complementor involvement in the design of three different platform boundary resources, each represented by one episode of the action research study: (1) the abstraction of platform application programming interface (APIs), (2) the development of a release management tool for apps and (3) the establishment of contribution routines for a software development kit (SDK). We derive four propositions for lead complementor involvement: (1) lead com plementor involvement facilitates platform emergence for incumbent companies by enhancing boundary resource design, (2) lead complementor involvement is more efficient in higher platform layers, (3) lead complementor involve ment is facilitated by an increase of intensity and frequency of interactions between complementors and platform owner and (4) establishing a complementor community helps to continuously identity opportunities for lead com plementor involvement.

With these findings, we contribute to the information systems literature on platform emergence and platform design in the context of incumbent companies. First, we show the potential of lead complementor involvement to improve the boundary resource design and provide conditions when lead complementor involvement works best Second, we highlight that lead complementor involvement acknowledges the differences between complementors— who have often been interpreted as homogenous in literature. Their heterogeneity can be leveraged for lead com plementor involvement and thereby represents a starting point for further research on platform design and gover nance. Furthermore, we contextualise the concept of lead user involvement in the domain of digital platforms and compare and contrast lead user and lead complementor involvement

## 2 | THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS

The starting point of our work is the challenge of platform design, specifically boundary resource design and the potential role of complementors therein. Based on the observation that the involvement of complementors in boundary resource design has rarely been studied, we draw on lead user involvement to establish the concept of lead complementor involvement. The notion of lead complementor involvement emerged during our action design research study and we refined it as part of an iterative back-and-forth between the empirical case and literatur (Gioia et al., 2013). For better readability, we establish the concept of lead complementor involvement upfront, a part of the theoretical foundations.

## 2.1 | Boundary resource design and the role of complementors

A digital platform enables the development of apps by complementors (Tiwana et al., 2010) and provides access to these apps for users of the platform (Gawer, 2009). Interactions between the complementor and the user side of the platform create network effects which are a major driver of the success of platform business models in comparison to traditional pipeline-based business models (Parker & Van Alstyne, 2017). Considering a newl emerging digital platform, the platform owner needs to attract complementors as well as users and kick-off interactions between both sides. Resolving this chicken-and-egg problem represents a major challenge in th design of digital platforms (Evans & Schmalensee, 2010). For example, Apple's App Store on iOS is only attrac tive to users if it provides enough apps—and app developers only invest in creating a new app if there is a suffi ciently large user base.

In this paper, we focus on boundary resource design, addressing the complementors' side of the platform Before any digital platform can attract users, apps need to be available on the platform. While platform owners can develop their own apps to kick off the platform (Stummer et al., 2018), resources are often limited and complementors are needed to create a larger and more diverse set of apps. Platform owners need to attract complementors by creating incentives for collaboration to increase the reach of the digital platform (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013).

To do so, platform owners first have to enable complementors to develop high-quality apps by providing resources that complementors can draw on to develop apps (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2010). These boundary resources include technical resources such as a developer portal, access to APIs along with their documen tation and blueprints for building blocks of apps, as well as social boundary resources such as formats fo exchange among complementors (cf. Förderer et al., 2019). By designing boundary resources, the platform owner transfers design capabilities to complementors while retaining control of the value creation on the plat form (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013).

Information systems research has shown the central role of boundary resources in enabling and controllin complementors (Förderer et al., 2019; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018). However, we have a limited understanding of how boundary resources can be designed in the first place to best address the require ments of complementors and how complementors can be involved in the process. When using boundary resources complementors engage in tuning to adapt them to their needs and the platform owner might react to these tuning activities by changing boundary resources (Eaton et al., 2015). But this is a reactive approach that does not involv complementors in the design process of boundary resources. Recent work that focuses on incumbent companies challenges when designing digital platforms acknowledge the importance of complementors for generativity and innovation (e.g., Sandberg et al., 2020; Svahn et al., 2017) but they do not address the interactions with complementors and how they could be leveraged by the incumbent companies for boundary resource design.

While there is a lack of research on boundary resource design, an ongoing area of interest is how platforms interact with complementors in a broader sense (Cennamo & Santalo, 2019; Eaton et al., 2015; Kapoor & Furr, 2015; Rietveld et al., 2020). For a long time, the underlying assumption has been that complementors are homogenous and that they are attracted to digital platforms based on indirect network effects: the larger the potential market size on the side of the users, the more attractive the platform. However, this assumption changed in recent years. A signifi cant body of research has emerged, for example, showing that superstar complements play a disproportionate rol in technology adoption (Binken & Stremersch, 2009). Other research looked at the effect of complement quality on technology adoption (Kim et al., 2014), and how platform owners can foster complement quality (Förderer, 2020) Such research shows the heterogeneity of complementors concerning their capabilities and indicates the need to consider the differences among complementors when designing a platform.

We argue that platform owners can leverage the heterogeneity of complementors by involving capable complementors in boundary resource design in the emerging phase of a digital platform. In particular for incumbent com panies that lack experience in boundary resource design, involving complementors could prove beneficial.

## 2.2 | Lead complementor involvement

While the involvement of complementors for boundary resource design has not been considered so far, scholars have comprehensively investigated the role of external communities in the innovation process for product design (Altman et al., 2022). Multiple methodologies have been identified that help companies enable co-creative innovation in their product design with external partners to build competitive advantage (Chesbrough, 2003). Among them, lead user involvement is one of the most influential concepts (von Hippel, 1986; see also Schreier & Prügl, 2008). Lead users are users who present strong needs that wil become general ones in the future (von Hippel, 1986, p. 791). Involving those users in product design has been shown to enhance innovation and improve products. Lead user involvement has been applied in multiple infor mation systems studies on innovation in different areas such as banking (Oliveira & von Hippel, 2009), mobil services (Kankanhalli et al., 2015) and telecommunication (Magnusson et al., 2003; for a systematic literatur review see also Bano & Zowghi, 2014)

We propose that it can be beneficial for platform owners to involve lead complementors in the process of boundary resource design, just as it can be beneficial to involve lead users in the process of product design. Simila to lead users, lead complementors are complementors whose requirements with regard to boundary resource design are representative of a broader group of complementors but who face these requirements earlier than other (cf. von Hippel, 1986). Platform owners can involve lead complementors in boundary resource design by granting them early access to the digital platform and engaging with them to gather feedback on boundary resource design For example, the developers who built the first apps for the iPhone were pioneers on the iOS platform and wer followed by a larger number of complementors in subsequent years. Any issues with development tools, APIs or doc umentation that they found, and that were subsequently fixed by Apple, might have helped the next generation of complementors build new apps faster and easier. However, from the perspective of information systems research we have little insight into how, and if at all, that process of complementor involvement unfolded and prove beneficial.

On a conceptual level, lead complementor involvement is closely related to lead user involvement However, the specific mechanisms of lead complementor involvement cannot directly be borrowed from lead user involvement because lead complementors differ from lead users along several dimensions (Table 1). First a platform typically is a more complex system than a product: while a product serves a clearly defined purpose a platform supports the development of highly heterogeneous apps (Tilson et al., 2010). The design of boundary resources needs to reflect this heterogeneity, making the involvement of lead complementors a mor complex endeavour as well

Second, the relationship between lead users and the company they work with is different than the relationship between lead complementors and platform owners. In general, users are only loosely or not at all coupled with th company that they buy products from, whereas complementors are already part of a digital platform ecosystem that is orchestrated by the platform owner via platform governance (Hein et al., 2019; Tiwana, 2014). Thus, whereas companies need to design campaigns to identify suitable lead users among their customer base, lead complementor can be more easily identified by the platform owner among the complementors in its ecosystems.

Third, the scope of the involvement differs. Lead complementors will not just contribute to the design of a single product, they will enter an ongoing collaboration with the platform owner to develop apps on the digital platform While the characteristics of the collaboration differ from complementor to complementor, there is potential for long running relationships with lead complementors. Thus, most lead complementors might be motivated to contribute to the design of various boundary resources, while lead users often have an interest in one specific product

In sum, involving lead complementors in boundary resource design seems a promising path, in particular for incumbent companies that lack the experience and capabilities of a platform owner. The information systems litera ture does not yet guide how lead complementor involvement can be successful but lead user involvement can serv as a conceptual foundation for an empirical study of lead complementor involvement.

## 3 | RESEARCH METHOD

This study strives to evaluate lead complementor involvement in boundary resource design of emerging digital plat forms that are implemented by incumbent companies. We chose the automotive industry as the context for our study, because—in contrast to several other industries that have already experienced the rise of digital platform (Parker et al., 2016)—the automotive industry seemed to be still at the beginning of the platformisation process when we started our work in March 2017 (Svahn et al., 2017; Weiß et al., 2020).

We selected BMW as a case company because it is known as a pioneer in the implementation of digital services in its cars. Digital services that provided online content such as weather or news directly to the driver had been implemented in BMW cars since 2003. Furthermore, BMW initiated projects with established digital players, such as the first integration of the iPod in a car in collaboration with Apple as early as 2010 and the implementation of mobile apps in its infotainment system in 2011.

As an employee in the platform department, the first author of this paper was able to collect deep insights into th daily work of several software development teams at BMW. This setup allowed us to apply action research, with its goa to solve practical problems while expanding scientific knowledge on the emergence of digital platforms (Baskerville, 1999)

According to Davison and Martinsons (2016, p. 6), researchers need to thoroughly and systematically identif their own strengths and the opportunities with respect to the environment . Our collaboration with BMW enabled us to collect in-depth insights regarding the design of emerging digital platforms and the interactions of platform owners and complementors. While previous research on the relationship between platform owners and complementors has mostly relied on the evaluation of publicly available archival data and thus is limited to an outside view (Cennamo & Santalo, 2019; Eaton et al., 2015; Förderer et al., 2019; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Karhu et al., 2018; Rietveld et al., 2020), the application of action research allowed us to collect first-hand insights on the collaboration between the BMW platform team and app developers. Our active role enabled us to implement th idea of lead complementor involvement directly in the field and thereby explore its nature to expand scientifi knowledge (Baskerville, 1999). An overview of the fit of the action research approach can be found in the Appendix (Table A1). In this section, we detail the action research approach before describing the research context as a back drop to the subsequent empirical findings

## 3.1 | Implementation of action research approach

With this study, we strive for illustrating potential collaborations between a platform owner and lead complementors in boundary resource design during the emergence of a new digital platform. Action research allows for gaining dee insights into these kinds of interactions because it represents ‘a cognitive process that depends on the social interactions between the observers and those in their surroundings’ (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998, p. 91). Its strong focus on the involvement and collaboration between the researcher and the practitioners (Avison, 1998) allows a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon, in our case the involvement of lead complementors. Concur rently, it enables the implementation of theoretical rationales in a practical context. The researcher is concerne about the current state of an organisational environment and tries to introduce change while simultaneously researching the phenomenon (Babüroglu & Ravn, 1992; Baskerville, 1999). Action research enables the researcher to implement change (Baskerville, 1999) and participate in the process of shaping and adjusting the implemented mea sures according to the organisational context (Sein et al., 2011). The goal of our research project was to evaluate the potential of lead complementor involvement in boundary resource design.

To address frequent criticism regarding a lack of methodological rigour in action research (Avison et al., 1999; Baskerville, 1999; Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998; Davison et al., 2004), we applied canonical action research wit its rigorous structure represented by an iterative process model (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1998). We followed the five interdependent principles that were proposed by Davison et al. (2004) to prevent a lack of rigour whil enhancing the practical relevance of action research. First, the establishment of a sound researcher–client agreement should be the base for a solid client-system infrastructure (Baskerville, 1999; Susman & Evered, 1978). The embeddedness of the first author as an employee in the platform team provided a fundamental assurance of th cooperation between BMW and our research team. Furthermore, we addressed and aligned our goal of involving lead complementors with the affected practitioners regularly and refined our approach as required.

The second principle considers the application of a cyclical process model in the action research design. Al our insights were gained from three episodes that were conducted in the setting of the digital platform for automotive onboard apps: (1) the abstraction of platform APIs, (2) the development of a release management tool for apps and (3) the creation of contribution routines for a SDK. In total, the three episodes extended over 27 months, from July 2019 to September 2020. Each episode describes the refinement of insufficientl designed boundary resources. We selected boundary resources based on interviews with app developers in which we discussed what boundary resources required improvement. In each episode, we established an itera tive action research process that involved the five stages of diagnosis, action planning, action taking, evaluation and the specification of learning (Baskerville, 1999; Susman & Evered, 1978), which again were a base for th subsequent iteration (see Figure 1). Table A2 in the Appendix offers a detailed overview of the single steps that were conducted in each iteration.

The third principle refers to the involvement of a theoretical framework or tool that accompanies the research process. We implemented the idea of lead complementor involvement, drawing on the established concept of lead use involvement. Moreover, we gathered appropriate theoretical concepts in the diagnosis and action planning of al three conducted projects. For instance, we used the literature on APIs for enhancing the platform's APIs

The fourth principle comprises the demand for change through applied action. We evaluated all changes that we implemented in the course of the projects to show the effects of our actions. Our interventions focused on esta blishing and refining lead complementor involvement to enhance boundary resource design. Consequently, we evalu ated the evolving design of the boundary resources and subsequent effects on their utilisation by complementors All our interventions started with the recognition of insufficient boundary resource design and ended as improve ments were achieved. Thereby, we relied on the assessment of involved lead complementors. For instance, th enhanced APIs were evaluated by app developers before and after our activity based on previously defined criteria The actual impact of the changes can be illustrated by the number of apps that benefitted from enhancements of th boundary resource design: In the summer of 2020, the platform was used by more than 50 apps that were availabl to more than two million customers of BMW cars. All apps moved from the initial API design to at least one refined API (see the first episode), all apps accepted the enhanced review and release process (see the second episode) and large number of apps implemented SDK features that contained contributions of lead complementors (see the third episode).

The fifth principle is learning through reflection. The iterative process that we established specifically includes reflecting and learning in every iteration. Learnings from one iteration represented the base for adaptions and actions in the consequent iteration.

As part of our involvement in the daily work of the platform team and through additional data collection efforts, we gathered comprehensive data in form of meeting minutes of 180 team meetings and 39 workshops, more than 2000 emails, as well as 19 transcribed interviews. Table 2 provides an overview of all data collection activities that were conducted in the context of this study.

At the end of an episode, we collected manifold insights into the design process of the boundary resource. However, while we specified learnings in every iteration, our research lacked generalisable conclusions on lead complementor involvement. Therefore, we decided to exploit the data set for another, overarching analysis by th application of grounded theory procedures (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Wiesche et al., 2017). Using lead complementor involvement as the core category of our coding procedures (Glaser, 1978) revealed 28 codes that were subsequently condensed into four propositions on lead complementor involvement for boundary resource design. An overview of the codes can be found in the Appendix (Table A3). The conclusions on the phenomenon of lead complementor involvement in this paper are another testimony of satisfying the principle of learning through reflection.

## 4 | EPISODES OF LEAD COMPLEMENTOR INVOLVEMENT IN BOUNDARY RESOURCE DESIGN

The following chapter illustrates the results of our action research study. It is structured along three episodes that focus on the design of three boundary resources. The selection of the boundary resources was the result of an exchange between the platform team and the app developer community. Whenever the platform team decided to refine the design of another boundary resource, we engaged with lead complementors. Every episode begins with an overview of the initial situation followed by three iterations that describe interventions to enhance the boundar resource design through lead complementor involvement. Each episode concludes with a summary of the desig improvements.

## 4.1 | First episode—Abstraction of APIs

In August 2018, 2 months after the initial release of the platform, the platform team conducted interviews with sev eral app development teams to collect insights for potential improvements to the platform. The design of APIs tha provided access to different external modules turned out to be one major pain point for app developers. In its initia release in July 2018, the platform functioned as a simple gateway between apps and other software modules in the car. The platform exposed access to raw APIs without adding any functionality or abstraction. APIs were highl complex, lacked documentation and developers had to implement numerous lines of code with no alteration (‘boilerplate code’) to access them. Consequently, it was error-prone and time-consuming for app developmen teams to implement features that used APIs.

The implementation of some APIs requires massive efforts. You need to write tons of boilerplate code that doesn't include any functionality of the application. For example, the implementation of a notifi cation banner in the UI requires more than 350 lines of code.— External App Develope

To tackle these problems, the platform team decided to add a layer of abstraction to the APIs to facilitate the imple mentation of features by apps. A refinement of the APIs should hide the complexity of the underlying system layers fo apps. At the same time, several app developers had built their own abstractions for APIs for their local projects. In September 2018, all members of the platform core team committed to an iterative refinement process. Thereby, each AP should be considered in a dedicated iteration that should involve app developers with expertise for the respective API

## 4.1.1 | First iteration—Setup API design process

It turned out that the API that was most frequently used exposed features of the vehicle's navigation system. There fore, an external app development team that intensively relied on the utilisation of the navigation API was requested to provide support. Moreover, an internal app development team that had crafted its own code library to simplif interactions with the navigation APIs, was invited. All contacted developers declared their willingness to participat in the API design process.

It is simply more worthwhile to support the platform and to make it clear how we have to read out the current route length, for example, and how we would like it to be done than to stand alone again each time. This way, the maintenance of the abstraction is also taken over by the platform team.— Internal App Developer

In the next step, the involved app developers and the platform team jointly evaluated the existing API design to under stand its weaknesses. Several design proposals were developed and refined. Furthermore, the platform team decided to release a beta version of the SDK to collect feedback from other app development teams. The feedback revealed the need for smaller adjustments such as the renaming of single methods or attributes. Eventually, the final API was released in October 2018. While the refinement of the next API was already in the planning phase, the platform team organised a meeting with all involved members of the platform team to reflect on the approach and specify its learning. Even though the involvement of app developers was appreciated by all parties, the platform team was concerned about the high effort that were required for the refinement of the API. Therefore, the team decided to formulate API design conventions that should prevent extensive discussions on basic design decisions. The more basic decisions were stated in guidelines that were acknowledged by all involved parties, the simpler it would be to focus on specific aspects of the API. The navigation API design was taken as a role model for these basic guidelines.

## 4.1.2 | Second iteration—Shift responsibility back to the platform team

Between October 2018 and September 2019, several more APIs were refined in the platform SDK. After severa releases with multiple involved app development teams, however, the involvement of complementors decreased While selected complementors were still engaged in the initial API design proposals, the feedback on beta releases of the SDK abated. Inquiries from various app development teams revealed that the high efforts on the one side and the sufficient quality of the released APIs on the other side were reasons for this decrease.

Of course, it is always better to have an abstracted API. But you also have to consider if it is worth the effort to participate. In the end, we want to develop our app first. Everything else just comes afterward.— Internal App Product Owner

However, the platform team also stated that the involvement of app developers is not required at the same leve as at the beginning of the project. During the design of several APIs, the team learned which API design attributes were important to the app developers and in which way they could be implemented. With every iteration, the platform team understood better how the APIs should be designed.

## 4.1.3 | Third iteration—Involve platform periphery team for implementation

In November 2019, the platform team intended to enhance the design of further APIs to facilitate app development activities. The platform team decided to involve a former app development team which should be temporarily inte grated into the platform periphery as an external team. Thereby, the external team should not only be involved in the API design process but be responsible for the actual implementation.

After a short phase of orientation, an API for backend communication should be refined. After the design was validated via the already established process with further app developers, the next step was the actual porting of the API. It soon became clear that the team from the platform team periphery would have major problems. Due to the lack of established processes for the complete involvement of external teams in platform development, intensiv communication between platform developers and the peripheral team was necessary. However, the focus of th platform developers during this period was on the development of new features. Consequently, the porting took three times longer than originally estimated by the platform periphery team.

You simply need the support of the platform developers. There is too much internal knowledge that is needed to really integrate the implementation of the API. Once we're through the process, I think it's much easier to do it.— Developer in the Peripheral Platform Team

After several iterations, the abstracted API for backend communication was made available to other app devel opment teams in February 2020 in a regular platform release. In the following weeks, the abstraction of further APIs was taken over by the platform periphery team.

## 4.1.4 | Summary—Enhanced API abstraction

At the end of the episode in April 2020, the platform exposed 12 abstracted APIs with more than 250 abstracted methods. All 52 app development teams replaced the implementation of non-abstract APIs with abstracted APIs to improve the maintainability of the code and reliability. All BMW app development teams preferred to use the abstracted APIs for new projects and there were only a few use cases where APIs wer needed for which no abstraction was available.

## 4.2 | Second episode—App review and release management

Long before the initial launch of the platform, the platform team recognised the need for a gateway for the release of apps to the company's testing fleet (which was a prerequisite for any public launch of an app). The review of app was conducted by an external team that was embedded in the platform periphery.

We've had too many cases where a single app crashed the platform and the testing of all other apps was blocked. Of course, the platform also has to become more robust, but at the same time we recognized that we have to review the apps that are released.— Member of the Platform Team

In the initial phase, in the spring of 2018, the review team focused on the detection of the known issues that caused system crashes in the past. Additionally, the team scanned the code for salient code sections and bad prac tises. By August 2018, the review team had analysed all apps at least once. In a workshop with the platform team and the platform developers, the review team determined the need to take further actions to support the apps to increase stability and code quality.

## 4.2.1 | First iteration—Establish coding guidelines

The review team recognised that coding guidelines would contribute to code quality improvements. To motivate app developers to adhere to coding guidelines, the review team decided to involve app development teams in the devel opment of the coding guidelines. While multiple app development teams appreciated the efforts of the review team only a few developers were willing to participate actively in a project for coding guidelines. The benefits seemed too vague and the teams had to cope with a high workload. However, a small number of developers signalled their will ingness to get involved. It turned out that these developers were in app development teams with particularly seriou stability problems who had a special interest in improving the situation.

Yes, of course, we are happy to participate in this [project to establish coding guidelines]. It is better to spend the effort and make sure that our interests are taken into account in the guidelines than to have to spend a lot of time explaining why we cannot meet this requirement at this point in time.— Internal App Product Owner

Subsequent workshops with the review team and app developers revealed that several problems originated from a lack of standardisation. When the platform was launched, several app development teams were missing specific platform features and created their own solutions. The resulting variety of multiple features led to increasing complexity of the platform. To address these problems, the platform team needed to provide guide lines that document all limitations and requirements that need to be fulfilled by apps. The involvement of app developers turned out to be particularly valuable during the development of the guidelines. App developers provided immediate feedback on the feasibility of a guideline and the estimated efforts for adopting a guide line. Moreover, the platform team was able to validate whether the guidelines were comprehensive and refine formulations in case of uncertainty or misunderstandings even before they were published to the broader app developer community. Eventually, in November 2018, the app development guidelines were released in BMW' internal developer portal.

## 4.2.2 | Second iteration—Implement automated control mechanisms

Once the review team had implemented transparent guidelines, compliance with these guidelines had to be verified. As the number of app releases increased, a manual review of all of the data collected for each app version was no longer feasible. Not only reviewers struggled with the high workload, but also app developers complained about th number of tests that had to be performed and documented for the release of a single app version.

The solution to this problem was automated control mechanisms. In December 2018, the review team decided to build a web app that would serve as a central app review portal. App developers should upload thei source code into the tool, where it would be checked automatically regarding several criteria. Furthermore, the tool should contain an automated build pipeline that could further contribute to a more standardised app build ing and packaging process. Although the announcement of the portal was received positively by the app devel opment teams, there were individual concerns. Some app developers feared that too many parts of thei proprietary build process had to be modified to be compatible with the portal. The review team tried to under stand the problems and the motivations of the app developers and to incorporate their findings into the design of the app review portal.

Direct feedback helps us enormously. We have already built an app ourselves, but at the moment we are only in the role of reviewers. The more communication and feedback there is, the better.— Mem ber of the App Review Team in the Platform Periphery

The first version of the app review tool was released in March 2019. Eventually, in August 2019 the app review portal was established as a central release management tool.

## 4.2.3 | Third iteration—Enhance release management

By involving app developers in the design process of the app review portal, major problems with automation on the part of the apps could be avoided. Soon, the review team realised that the positive image of the tool in the app developer community could be used to develop it even further. Every time an app development team requested a new feature, the review team encouraged the involvement of the developers in the concept and design process Thereby, reguests by app development teams often resulted from discussions in BMW's internal developer forum and were jointly submitted by several app development teams

For example, in January 2020, an internal app development team requested that other stakeholders should be informed about the release of new app versions. Subsequently, the review team implemented a notification feature in the portal. A list of all new features, changes and bug fixes that were implemented in a new version was emailed to the relevant parties. The app development team proposed that the app's change log, which wa documented in a markdown file that was delivered with each app version, should serve as a source for th requested information. In March 2020, a guideline on change logs was published in the internal developer por tal. The guideline prescribed a standardised format for the file, which enabled automated fetching of the infor mation by the app review portal. In May 2020, a check that automatically rejected all submitted app packages that did not contain an appropriate change log was introduced. Eventually, in July 2020, the review team enabled other users outside of the app development teams to subscribe to specific events in the app releas process. Besides the actual information that a new version was released, the email contained a list of all fea tures and bug fixes that were released with the new app version.

All these little things, like the change log or the automated emails to the stakeholders, take a lot off my daily work. Therefore, I am also happy to get involved in the specification of the requirements for the app review portal.— Internal App Product Owne

## 4.2.4 | Summary—Enhanced app review and release management

In September 2020, the app review portal was the central tool for app release management. Since its launch in March 2019, more than 1800 app releases were processed and monitored. Besides the more than 250 app developers and app product owners, about 170 other users logged in every week.

## 4.3 | Third episode—Contribution routines for the platform SDK

Two different app development teams developed and contributed minor features to the platform SDK in 2017 and 2018. However, this engagement was not motivated by the idea of an open-source approach but was caused by missing platform capabilities that were required for critical features in the apps those teams developed. Even thoug these contributions were coordinated and aligned with platform developers, they were not appreciated by the plat form team. The team feared the loss of control over its code base and was worried about a lack of quality in the con tributed source code. The app developers on the other hand experienced massive impediments during their implementation. The absence of any contribution process or guidelines aggravated the already complicated setu and development process.

Without the necessity of offering our feature when we released the app, I think we would have simply stopped the contribution process at some point. The effort for such a small extension was just huge compared to the benefits.— Internal App Developer

However, motivated by the success of the involvement of app developers in the design and implementation of the automotive onboard APIs, the platform team discussed further possibilities to exploit collaboration with app developers in December 2018. Several platform developers confirmed the usefulness of involving app developer while also acknowledging the inherent challenge of contributions to the SDK

We consider it a good thing if the app developers themselves can contribute features to the SDK. On the other hand, it is very important to keep the platform stable. This is the challenge with externa contributions that we need to resolve.— Platform Develope

## 4.3.1 | First iteration—Establish platform contribution process

Consequently, the platform team decided to draw up a contribution process. After several iterations, the plat form SDK contribution process was officially agreed upon and promoted to the app developer community. However, no contribution was submitted for review within the next month. The platform team started to answer platform feature requests that appeared appropriate to a contribution by app developers with refer ences on the platform contribution process. An internal app developer started working on a refinement of an existing platform feature. After three rounds of iteration, the contribution was eventually accepted and merged into the platform SDK in June 2019. But the app developer reported a high level of frustration caused by the enormous communication and refinement efforts that did not seem coordinated or aligned with the platform development team.

It's just super frustrating when you have to invest so much time for such a small change. Especially if it is only a small side project that runs alongside the actual app development. I spent more time on the contribution [to the SDK] last week than on my actual work.— External App Developer

The platform team recognised the lack of guidelines on the actual code. While the propagated documenta tion was very detailed about the process of contribution, the actual content of contributions was not consid ered. The lack of coding guidelines in contributions caused differing opinions of the responsible reviewers and consequently varying remarks that triggered a frustrating ping-pong process between reviewers and contribu tors. Consequently, the platform developers decided to reiterate the documentation and enhance it with com prehensive coding guidelines.

## 4.3.2 | Second iteration—Define contribution guidelines

After this experience of failure, the platform team wanted to avoid further frustration about contribution to the platform in the app developer community. The team decided to assign a team from the platform periphery to take on the role of an app developer and create a contribution to the SDK. In February 2020 discussions with the team from the platform periphery revealed that the existing contribution guideline were still not sufficient. In a brainstorming session, it was concluded that there were classes of feature that were suitable for contributions, while others were not. While minor changes to existing features appear to be valuable and were appreciated by the platform team, major changes to the platform's core functional ity were not.

I do not think it makes sense to release all components for a contribution. There are simply topics that only we can do ourselves. You just have to understand a bit why things are the way they are. If you don't know why things are the way they are, you will end up going in circles and probably end up with the same result, wasting your and our time.— Platform Developer

However, the external developer stated that her potential contributions would focus on minor changes. Conse quently, it was agreed that major changes to core functionalities should be excluded from the contribution guidelines.

## 4.3.3 | Third iteration—Refine contribution process

In March 2020, the contribution guidelines were published to the app developer community. In the following weeks, the platform team recognised several contribution activities. While the peripheral team started anothe project for the implementation of a completely new platform feature, the external app developer, who had par ticipated in the preceding workshop, started working on the refinement of an existing platform capability. App developers from other teams indicated an interest in the contribution process. In response to several requests some minor refinements were added to the contribution guideline to increase its understandability. In Apri 2020, the requests of the app developers were submitted for review by the platform team and after a smal number of minor annotations and refactoring, these were eventually merged into the SDK's code. The app developers perceived the process as appropriate and engaging. The cooperation with the platform team appeared transparent and responsive

Still, the app developers reported that they are not willing to submit another contribution for the moment. The reason for that was a lack of tooling and support for platform development activities. While the platform team and the peripheral external team had their setups in place already, one developer that started a new con tribution struggled with the installation of partially unavailable or deprecated tools and a lack of documenta tion. This particularly affected teams that had no direct connection to the platform team. For example communication between platform developers and contributors from China was particularly difficult due to th time difference. In June 2020, another workshop for reflection was organised to specify learning from this last iteration of contributions. All participants agreed on a need to simplify the platform development setup for contributors. Therefore, it was agreed that the peripheral team will identify critical spots in the setup and work on improvements.

It cannot be the case that setting up the development environment takes longer than the actua implementation. I'm pretty sure that most app developers will otherwise move away from the idea of contributing quite quickly.— Internal App Developer

## 5 | PROPOSITIONS ON LEAD COMPLEMENTOR INVOLVEMENT IN BOUNDARY RESOURCE DESIGN

Based on an analysis of lead complementor involvement across the three episodes, we derive four propositions that capture our understanding of how incumbent companies benefit most from lead complementor involvement in boundary resource design.

## 5.1 | Lead complementor involvement and platform emergence

Our research was motivated by the challenges of an incumbent platform owner that struggled with the boundar resource design due to its lack of experience in the design of digital platforms. The three episodes of the action research study show that involving lead complementors helped the platform owner to improve the design of its boundary resources and make it attractive for complementors in the initial phase of platform emergence

For example, the first episode showed that the platform's API design was a major pain point in the app develop ment teams' daily work. Developers complained about large efforts for the implementation of small features. High complexity in the API design combined with a lack of documentation caused multiple issues in the code of the app developers that required comprehensive refactoring. It turned out that these insufficiencies were caused by th characteristics of traditional automotive software development. Instead of focusing on the creation of generativ platform capabilities, engineers were used to implementing specified functionality.

While the platform team recognised the insufficiency of the API design, it felt overwhelmed by the volume and variety of requirements of app development teams. The involvement of lead complementors was beneficial for two reasons: First, the lead complementors had expertise in the respective domain of the API. The lead complementors knew which methods and attributes required attention due to their high relevance for app developers or due to specific characteristics. Second, the design was considered from the perspective of app developers. Before, the platform team strived for an API that was simple to develop, maintain and extend. With the involvement of lead complementors, the design should be simple to implement by app development teams, and the code of the app should be simple to maintain and extend

Similarly, the second episode on the introduction of an app release process shows the relevance of the com plementors' perspective for the boundary resource design. As an incumbent car manufacturer, BMW was used to multi-year development cycles. Consequently, the release of a new product was considered a rare event. While thi still was true for cars that are assembled in a production plant, new app versions were released several times a week While the platform team was initially only interested in preventing certain bad practises, lead complementors helped the platform team to understand which exceptions to such guidelines should be considered. Streamlining the releas process facilitated the work of app developers and contributed to an increased development speed. Thereby, the col laboration of the platform team and lead complementors led to a balanced boundary resource design that worked for both sides.

A balanced boundary resource design that considers the interests of the platform owner and complementors is especially important for an emerging digital platform that needs to attract new complementors while keeping contro over its digital platform. In the emerging phase of the digital platform, the course is set on how the platform owne collaborates with complementors and whether the critical launch phase can be overcome (cf. Evans &

Schmalensee, 2010). If interested complementors join the digital platform just to be turned away by insufficientl designed boundary resources, it will be even harder to motivate them to come back.

In particular, inexperienced platform owners such as incumbent companies might struggle to take a com plementor perspective into account. Our observations show that companies such as BMW are used to a traditiona original equipment manufacturer (OEM)-supplier relationship, in which the OEM's requirements weigh more heavil than the supplier's needs. Lead complementor involvement might help incumbent companies extend their focus from the customer to the complementor side, which in turn provides benefits to customers that ultimately have the choice among a larger number of innovative apps. Even if the boundary resource design is insufficient to start with, couple with a call for feedback from complementors it might not turn them away but rather motivate them to engage wit the platform owner to improve the design.

Specifically, we argue that lead complementor involvement facilitates platform emergence in two ways. First, it contributes to enhanced boundary resource design. The improved usability of boundary resources fosters the quantity and quality of complements on the platform (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2017). Second, the involvement of lead complementors in the boundary resource design pro cess strengthens their relationship with the platform owner. The awareness that the platform owner is listening to their needs increases their loyalty. Thereby, lead complementors are trend leaders that positively influence complementor communities (Sarker et al., 2012) and inspire other complementors to join the platform (Hienerth & Lettl, 2017). In sum, we establish the following proposition:

Proposition 1. Lead complementor involvement facilitates platform emergence for incumbent companies by enhancing boundary resource design.

## 5.2 | Lead complementor involvement and platform layers

We found that the effect of lead complementor involvement varied across platform components. While lead complementor involvement for enhancing boundary resource design in higher platform layers was straightforward and improvements to the design could be implemented quickly, improvements in lower platform layers were slow and caused significantly more effort for the platform team and the lead complementors.

For example, the third episode on contribution guidelines for the platform SDK showed that seemingly mino features required several iterations with huge efforts for the involved app developers and the platform team becaus they affected the basic mechanisms of the platform (e.g., application lifecycle management or download and installa tion flows). Thus, the platform team was cautious about changes in core components implemented by app devel opers. Contrarily, the team experienced several smooth contributions from app developers for components in highe platform layers. Most of the time, they were improvements of tools or APIs contained in the SDK. The multi-laye contribution guidelines that were created by the platform team can be considered as a direct ramification of this pattern.

A second example originates from the second episode on app review and release management. The involvement of app developers in the design of initial coding guidelines required major efforts for the involved app developers as well as the platform team. The additional effort that had to be made to include app developers in the process of cre ating these fundamental guidelines appears questionable in retrospect. In contrast, the involvement of app devel opers in the design of the enhanced release management yielded major improvements relatively quickly

We argue that this observation confirms previous work on boundary resources, but adds the important insight that lead complementors can be constructive partners of the platform owner. It seems evident that changes to th boundary resource design can be made dynamically and in response to the behaviour of complementors. For exam ple, the tuning of boundary resources by complementors in the iOS platform (Eaton et al., 2015) and Google's use of boundary resources to retain control on the Android platform (Karhu et al., 2018) illustrate the flexibility of boundar resources. However, previous work takes on the perspective that boundary resources are a means to control complementors and need to be designed in a way that they cannot be exploited. Our conclusions add a novel perspective that considers the potential of complementors to not only use the boundary resources to create apps but also contribute to boundary resource design directly

However, our observations show that these contributions get more difficult in the lower layers of a platform architecture (Yoo et al., 2010). While lead complementor involvement revealed the illustrated improvements in boundary resource design it barely proved any benefit on the platform core while causing increased efforts to enabl the collaboration. We argue that this is caused by the fact that direct interactions with the platform core are exclu sive to the platform owner. Hence, lead complementors have less expertise in platform core components compared to boundary resources. On the other hand, refactoring the core components might affect all apps. Fault implementations have the potential to endanger the overall stability of the platform (Tiwana, 2014). These majo risks for changes on the platform core on the one side and the low expertise of lead complementors on the design of the platform core on the other side diminish the value of lead complementor involvement for lower platform layers. This conclusion is especially relevant for incumbent companies that lack knowledge of the design of digital platform and need to configure effective management of the available expertise. We summarise these findings in the follow ing proposition:

Proposition 2. Incumbent companies can facilitate lead complementor involvement by focusing on higher platform layers.

## 5.3 | Lead complementor involvement and platform owner–complementor interaction

For some design enhancements, several app development teams were involved as lead complementors. Their involvement turned out to differ even though it was related to the same boundary resource. We recognised that the collaboration with lead complementors from teams that had already had frequent or highly intensive interactions with the platform team before the involvement required less effort on both sides than collaborations with lead com plementors that had not had prior interactions

For example, we observed two teams contributing to the same software module in the platform SDK. Although both contributions were of comparable size, the contribution of one team could be accepted without major coordi nation, whereas the other team needed several alignments with the platform team to finally merge their code into the platform SDK. We made similar observations in the other episodes.

The frequency and intensity of interactions were influenced by three different factors. First, lead com plementor involvement was particularly effective with app developers who built complex applications that implemented many functions of the platform. These needed more support from the platform team than team that implemented simple use cases. Second, some teams were close to the platform team within the organisa tion. App developers who were part of the same organisational unit as the platform team had more interactions with the platform team, also due to organisational issues that did not concern the app development process Third, geographical distance influenced the frequency and intensity of the interactions between com plementors and the platform owner. Several teams from China contributed to the platform SDK. Although the contributions were generally viewed as valuable, it always took several iterations and great effort within th platform team until a change could be incorporated into the SDK

Independent of the reasons for different levels of platform owner–complementor interaction, we argue that highly intensive or frequent interactions facilitate lead complementor involvement. Not only is the actua process of involving lead complementors simpler if both parties are already familiar, but the platform owner wil also be able to identify areas of the boundary resource design that require improvement from its interaction with complementors. Our findings indicate that there are given factors such as technical entanglement organisation proximity or geographical proximity between app and platform that facilitate interactions and sub sequently foster lead complementor involvement. However, the involvement of complementors with lower levels of interaction should not be neglected since they might represent differing needs than complementor with a high level of interaction. Therefore, we argue that a platform owner should try to establish a minimum level of interaction with all complementors to involve as many complementors as possible

Such kind of orchestration of complementor relationships is fundamentally different from the traditionally rather distanced and formalised relationship of an incumbent OEM and first-tier suppliers. We, therefore, state the follow ing proposition:

Proposition 3. Incumbent companies can facilitate lead complementor involvement by increasing the intensity and frequency of interactions between complementors and the platform owner.

## 5.4 | Sustaining lead complementor involvement

The intensity of lead complementor involvement decreased in the course of the episodes that we studied. The more the design of a boundary resource was improved, the less the effort for engagement was justifiable for the complementors. However, by establishing a complementor community, the platform team was able to keep com plementors engaged and quickly identify new opportunities for lead complementor involvement.

For example, at the beginning of the abstraction of APIs episode, several different complementors were willing to commit themselves to a high degree and participate in multiple workshops and meetings. The perspective of improving an aspect of a boundary resource they had struggled with before motivated them to participate. The more APIs were abstracted, the less of an issue this became. Even though the refinement of APIs was continued by th platform team, the involvement of lead complementors fizzled out. The platform team had similar experiences wit the improvements of the release process. After the initial improvements in the process were established, the interest of app developers in being involved in further improvements decreased. However, this episode also showed that BMW's internal forum was an effective tool to keep complementors engaged as part of a community. Even after th release process had been improved, the forum was a platform where complementors could easily raise issues an exchange with other complementors on how to resolve these issues—thereby signalling not only new areas fo improvement of boundary resource design to the platform team but also that they could potentially get involved as lead complementors.

The observation that complementors' motivation to get involved decreases with increasing platform quality is not surprising. As long as an insufficient boundary resource design inhibits their app development activities, there is a willingness to contribute to improving the situation. Once the boundary resource design is no longer an inhibitor the complementors' focus will be directed back to the development of their apps. This observation is in line with pre vious research on tuning activities of complementors on the iOS platform (Eaton et al., 2015). The tuning of bound ary resources was most pronounced when third-party developers were dissatisfied with Apple's boundary resource design and decreased as soon as Apple had addressed the most pressing concerns—such as allowing Voice over IP functionality in third-party apps.

Furthermore, our observations confirm findings on lead user involvement, which is considered a tempora phenomenon that evolves (Hienerth & Lettl, 2017) and which works better when lead users are part of a com munity (Mahr & Lievens, 2012). However, we argue this approach is new for incumbent firms that steer sup pliers via fixed contracts while platform-centric companies are used to orchestrate and collaborate with communities of complementors (Förderer, 2020; Förderer et al., 2019). While a vibrant complementor commu nity will not prevent the decrease of lead complementors' interest in already refined boundary resources, it helps uncover new design insufficiencies, creating new opportunities for lead complementor involvement Therefore, we posit:

Proposition 4. Incumbent companies can continuously identify opportunities for lead complementor involvement by establishing a complementor community.

## 6 | DISCUSSION

The propositions presented in the prior chapter illustrate different characteristics of lead complementor involvement in boundary resource design. Figure 4 integrates the propositions and illustrates lead complementor involvement as a continuous process that resolves insufficiencies in boundary resource design.

When platform owners establish a digital platform, the platform's boundary resources are prone to design insuf ficiencies. As platform owners discover these insufficiencies, they should focus on the higher levels of the platform architecture (Proposition 2). By interacting with complementors (Proposition 3), platform owners can identify lea complementors. Lead complementors should have sufficient technical expertise, have interacted with the boundar resource, and should have some benefit from the improved boundary resource (e.g., because they use it in their ap development).

Engaging with these lead complementors then yields design improvements (Proposition 1). To sustain lead complementor involvement, platform owners should establish a complementor community (Proposition 4). The community can not only suggest further design improvements but also identify further insufficiencies in the existing boundary resources.

The model is particularly relevant for incumbent companies that lack experience in interacting with com plementors. Incumbent companies as platform owners might not fully take the complementors' perspective into account because they are not experienced in interacting with complementors. Within their existing network of stra tegic partners, IT providers and consultants, incumbent companies might find lead complementors that are alread familiar with the company's technology.

## 6.1 | Implications for literature and future research

The notion of lead complementor involvement as an approach to improve the boundary resource design of an incumbent company's emerging digital platform contributes to theory and practise. First, we enhance the information systems literature on digital platforms with a focus on platform emergence and design in the context of incumbent companies. Second, we add to the literature on lead user involvement by contextualising it in the domain of digita platforms. For each theme, we lay out questions for future research.

Concerning platform emergence, we highlight that lead complementor involvement can be a crucial strategic element in the early stages of a digital platform (cf. Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013) and add to the ongoing discussion around challenges associated with the launch of digital platforms (Cusumano et al., 2019; Evans & Schmalensee, 2010; Stummer et al., 2018; Yoffie et al., 2019). In its emerging phase, a digital platform has to overcome the chicken-and-egg problem, that is, it has to attract both users and complementors (Caillaud & Jullien, 2003; Evans & Schmalensee, 2010). Various launch strategies have been suggested to overcome the chicken-and-egg problem, such as a zig-zag strategy in which the platform owner alternately tries to attract a group of complementors and users (Evans, 2009). However, all strategies rely on the capability of the platform owner to attract complementors.

Insufficient boundary resource design in the emerging phase of a digital platform is detrimental to attracting complementors. Not only might it make attracting complementors more difficult, but it might also turn away com plementors that join the platform initially because they feel that the platform owner does not take their requirement into account. These complementors might be gone for good because it will be difficult to motivate them to try th platform for a second time—the same way it is difficult for companies to reengage with customers they lost (Stauss & Friege, 1999).

Alternatively, complementors might start tuning activities to change boundary resource design (Eato et al., 2015). Thereby, complementors drive the evolvement of boundary resources by overcoming the regime of an autocratic platform owner. While Eaton et al. (2015) illustrate the activity of tuning as an act of resistance and accommodation’ (p. 220), lead complementor involvement is based on a collaborative relationship between the plat form owner and (lead) complementors, which is only possible by a given degree of trust and willingness to collaborate from both sides. Given that lead complementor involvement and tuning of boundary resources by regular complementors might happen simultaneously, future research could the interplay of the two phenomena. For exam ple, lead complementor involvement might reduce tuning activities in the broader platform ecosystem.

In particular for incumbent companies that do not have experience in establishing digital platforms, navigating the emerging phase of a digital platform is a huge challenge (Schreieck et al., 2021; Svahn et al., 2017; Tilson et al., 2010; Tiwana et al., 2010). They might lack the capability to, first, attract complementors and, second, orches trate the activities of complementors on the platform. For example, manufacturers might see complementors as reg ular suppliers that have a clear scope of activity, defined by the manufacturer. In that case, the boundary resourc design will not grant sufficient flexibility to complementors to innovate freely on the platform. Engaging with lead complementors early on will thus help incumbent companies to establish successful digital platforms.

While our study shows the value of lead complementor involvement for single incumbent car manufacturer, we suggest that future research could extend our findings to further industries in different contexts. Incumbent indus tries such as finance, insurance, healthcare, equipment manufacturing and farming increasingly implement digita platforms (Choudary, 2021; Perlman, 2017; Sebastian et al., 2017) and might benefit from lead complementor involvement as well.

Future research could further integrate lead complementor involvement with further elements of platform gov ernance for a holistic platform launch strategy of incumbent companies. For example, platform owners also need to balance openness and control (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Goldbach et al., 2018; Ondrus et al., 2015), find the right revenue-sharing model (Oh et al., 2015; Tiwana, 2014), and involve their existing partners and customers in th digital platform (Schreieck et al., 2022). Lead complementor involvement could give incumbent companies specific insights on how to implement platform governance.

Concerning platform design, we focus on boundary resource design and have shown how lead complementor involvement can help to take into account the heterogeneity of complementors. Previous work on digital platform has called for considering complementor heterogeneity as part of platform governance (e.g., Förderer et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2017; Hurni et al., 2020; Rietveld & Schilling, 2021). For example, complementors have different expectations and requirements when they begin developing applications on a digital platform. We argue that platform owners not only need to address this heterogeneity as part of the complementors' development activities but also a part of platform design when involving lead complementors. In the case of lead complementor involvement, appreci ating the heterogeneous expertise of complementors in specific areas of the platform is crucial to identify those lea complementors that can contribute best to the improvement of a boundary resource. These lead complementors can then also help the platform owner to consider the different needs and requirements of other complementors tha are not engaged as lead complementors.

In particular incumbent companies that are part of complex business-to-business markets such as equipment manufacturers, enterprise software vendors and insurance companies can benefit from involving lead com plementors. In those industries, complementors often do not build standardised apps but rather develop vertica solutions on the platform together with the platform owner. By working with lead complementors early on, the plat form owner is in a better position to avoid fragmentation of the ecosystem caused by verticalisation and customisation. Instead, the platform owner can implement the platform and its boundary resources in a way that it can be reused in different verticals without changes to the platform's core.

In sum, we argue that platform owners can consciously exploit lead complementor involvement to improve plat form design, specifically boundary resource design. Future research on platform design could explore further oppor tunities to leverage the heterogeneity of complementors and could thereby link previous research on partner programs (Sarker et al., 2012; Kude et al., 2012) in inter-organisational networks with research on digital platform and lead complementor involvement.

Concerning the literature on lead user involvement, we provide a contextualisation of the lead user involvement concept in the domain of digital platforms and show similarities and differences compared to lead user involvement Similar to lead user involvement that enhances product design (von Hippel, 1986), lead complementor involvement also contributes to an enhanced boundary resource design in digital platforms. Both lead users and lead com plementors are early adopters of a technology that is intended to spread further later on. Their needs can be projec ted to a large extent on later adopters, and their willingness to contribute to solution-finding enables involvemen (Schreier & Prügl, 2008; von Hippel, 1986).

Lead complementor involvement differs from lead user involvement regarding the numbers and variety of involved participants. While a product has a predefined scope of purpose, a platform affords generativit (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Um et al., 2013; Yoo et al., 2010). Consequently, a large spectrum of requirements for different use cases needs to be satisfied. Depending on the specific use case, different lead complementors might be able to contribute the most. Due to its modularity (Yoo et al., 2010), a digital platform offers a vast field for lead complementors to get involved as they can contribute to selected modules of the platform. Thereby, we showed that lead complementor involvement is most efficient on higher platform layers

In contrast to a product that serves one or a few specific purposes, a digital platform has more generic characteristics (Henfridsson & Bygstad, 2013; Um et al., 2013). Its design is subject to constant changes and adjustment (Eaton et al., 2015; Tiwana, 2015). New components are added while others are constantly being developed an changed. We, therefore, conclude that the application of lead complementor involvement is possible throughout th entire lifecycle of the digital platform, but with increasing quality of boundary resource design, the complementors motivation decreases. Establishing a complementor community can help to quickly identify new areas for lead com plementor involvement and contribute to the complementors' motivation to remain engaged with the platform.

Future research on lead complementor involvement could focus on the role of the complementor community building on previous work on sponsored online communities such as the SAP community (Huang et al., 2018 Kauschinger et al., 2023). Furthermore, it would be promising to analyse the interplay of internal and externa complementors as they engage in lead complementor involvement and potential conflicts that arise as some lead complementors might find their inputs considered more often by the platform owner than others.

## 6.2 | Implications for practise and limitations

Our findings are of use to practitioners and in particular to incumbent companies that lack experience and capabili ties in designing digital platforms. We show the potential of lead complementors and guide the selection of potentia lead complementors based on their expertise and their previous interactions with the platform owner. We cautio practitioners to apply lead complementors on higher platform layers (i.e., for boundary resources) to avoid the huge effort that is required to involve complementors in the design of core components. Furthermore, we show that the value of lead complementor involvement is highest in the emerging phase of a digital platform and that an activ complementor community can help to sustain lead complementor involvement over the lifecycle of a digita platform.

Our study is subject to three limitations that provide starting points for future research. First, we acknowledge that the context of an incumbent company might influence our findings and limit the generalisability of the findings to other companies. Incumbent companies need to start from the beginning and acquire the basic capabilitie whereas IT companies and start-ups might require less feedback and fewer refinements in the boundary resourc design. However, we argue that lead complementor involvement will also have benefits for these companies—an anecdotal insights show that, for example, Google and Apple interact with complementors at developer conference and might build on their feedback. Future research could therefore compare how successful lead complemento involvement differs between incumbent companies from traditional industries and ‘digital native’ IT companies

Second, our study did not account for potentially malicious complementors that engage in lead complementor involvement only to gain short-term advantages for their own business. The platform we studied was only open to complementors inside the organisation and for commissioned suppliers, thus the risk of malicious actions was limited When a platform is more open, lead user involvement becomes riskier and the platform owner will need to vet com plementors before involving them. Studying lead complementors in more open settings will add important nuance to our findings and increase.

Third, our study focused on the emerging phase of digital platforms. While we argue that lead complementor involvement is particularly helpful in this phase, it might also yield benefits in a later phase of the platform lifecycle This might come with new challenges such as making the practises of lead user involvement more scalable to includ more complementors. At the same time, boundary resources have already matured and the motivation for com plementors to engage in lead user involvement might thus be limited. Future research could study more mature plat forms to identify these challenges and ways to address them.

## 7 | CONCLUSION

The design of digital platforms is a major challenge for incumbent companies. With the application of an action research approach, we establish lead complementor involvement as a practise to improve boundary resource design, a crucial part of platform design. The three episodes of our action research study illustrated how our research impacted the implementation of lead complementor involvement and how that enhanced BMW's boundary resourc design. As a result, BMW's digital platform has improved abstracted APIs that had been implemented by more than 60 apps that are available for hundreds of thousands of BMW customers. The platform's SDK itself, including th contributions by complementors, had become a mandatory building block of every BMW onboard app. The ap review portal enabled an automated review and release process for more than 120 app versions every month. Thes findings contribute to the information systems literature on platform emergence and platform governance b showing that platform owners can leverage the heterogeneity of complementors to identify and engage lead com plementors to improve boundary resource design. We contextualised lead user involvement in the domain of digita platforms and discussed similarities and differences between lead user and lead complementor involvement. Ou work can be the basis for further research on the cooperation between platform owners and complementors as par of the design and governance of digital platforms.
