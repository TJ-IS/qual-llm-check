# Information Support Systems for Problem Solving

David DERY $^{+}$ and Theodore J. MOCK \* $^{+}$ The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Faculty of Social Sciences, Mount Scopus, Jerusalem 91905, and \*University of Southern California, School of Business Administration, Los Angeles, CA, 90089.

The occurrence in the literature of success stories dealing with (management) information systems is rare indeed. This paper addresses some of the possible factors that make it difficult to design and implement successful information (and decision support) systems. More specifically, we consider the role of problem formulation and specification, including goal setting, in the design of information systems.

Keywords: Problem solving; Information evaluation; Decision making; Information support systems

## 1. Introduction

The design of management information systems (MIS) largely depends on what is understood by ‘management’. The rare occurrence of MIS success stories (for critique on the concept of MIS see: wildavsky, 1978, 1980; Hedberg and Jonsson, 1978; Dery, 1981) may indicate a misconception of what management is all about. The predominant view that managerial activity consists primarily of decision-making has led to growing concentration on information support for decision-making processes, routine as well as unstructured. This tendency manifests itself most clearly in the economic approach to the (decision) value of information, and more recently in the introduction of ‘decision support systems’ (DSS).

Most MIS designers would probably agree that management also consists of problem-solving. But since ‘problem-solving’ and ‘decision-making’ are often treated as similar, if not equivalent, information support for one (decision-making) is, explicitly or implicitly, viewed as adequate for the other (problem-solving). In this paper we propose to distinguish between the two. More specifically we argue that information systems that are designed with decision-making in mind do not contain the richness required for problem formulation and reformulation. We view a decision or choice problem as a pre-formulated problem. Information support for decision making would not adequately support the pre-decisional problem formulation phase. To the extent that one needs to define a new problem or redefine old problems there is little one can hope to gain from a decision support system.

In what follows, we present an overview of ‘problems’ as they are developed in management and related disciplines. This is followed by a discussion of ‘problem’ as it has been applied within information systems research. Some alternative notions of ‘problem’ are then developed and implications for information systems design presented.

## 2. Alternative Conceptions of Problems

One of the more general notions concerning information systems is that information is produced for some (expected) purpose, or, in other words, to help solve or identify some (potential) 'problem'. But what notion or notions of 'problem' facilitates the study of information systems?

## 2.1. Problems as Situations

One notion of facing a problem has to do with the lack of a ready response (Duncker, 1945; Newell and Simon, 1972); something that calls for thought prior to action. If a problem is ‘a stimulus for which an organism does not have a ready response’ (Davis, 1973, p. 12), to define a problem would be to define that stimulus situation. An example might be an entity (e.g., the federal government) that sensed its expenditures were exceeding its revenues by \$100 billion per year.

This notion of ‘problem’ is not very rich as far as information systems are concerned. Presumably an information system would primarily play a ‘sensing’ or intelligence role with respect to stimuli (e.g., sensing that water consumption is exceeding water inflow).

## 2.2. Problems as Stress (Lack of Homeostasis)

Many times a problem is simply taken to mean, as in everyday language, a state of difficulty, a set of undesirable conditions. Problems, like disease or injury, are in this view objective entities, empirical phenomena (Fuller, 1941; Tallman and McGee, 1971).

Chambers (1966), p. 37) introduces the stress notion of problem in his basic assumptions concerning individual thought and action:

‘There is a preferred condition of no strain; less strain is preferred to more strain. ... Persons are homeostatic systems; the object of action is the relief of strain ... A stimulus is any object, event, or condition giving rise to strain.

The notion of information in such a model relates first to a sensing function concerning the existence of strain. Secondly, the feedback function of information is readily apparent as a system attempts to assess the effectiveness of action in attaining homeostasis.

## 2.3. Problems as Discrepancies

A more common notion of a problem is that of a discrepancy between ‘what is’ and ‘what ought to be’ (Merton, 1966; Pounds, 1969; Kilmann and Mitroff, 1979). Problems in this view are not the equivalent of stress conditions, but are rather gaps. Clearly, discrepancy is a concept that presumes the notion of a goal and a measure of current status. This notion of ‘problem’ presupposes knowledge of both goals and alternatives.

Many formal information systems, for example accounting variance reporting systems, seem to be based on the discrepancy notion of problem. The 'feedback' value of such system (Mock, 1971) may be in assessing 'problems', in evaluating the effectiveness of alternate decision strategies, and in revising underlying decision models.

The view that problems are discrepancies, however, many overlook the crucial role of problem formulation in identifying what would count as a solution and distinct arrays of alternative strategies. The role of information systems in this aspect of problem solving is also unclear, especially for systems such as traditional accounting variance reports. To the extent that, for example, the fault lies in the organization's aspirations level rather than in the fact that this level is not realized, information support for bridging our discrepancies would not normally provide cues for adjusting aspiration levels.

## 2.4. Problems as Choice or Decision Problems

Perhaps the most frequent theoretical framework within which information systems are researched is that based on decision theory (e.g., Marschak, 1967; Feltham, 1973; Mock, 1971; Hilton, 1981; Dery 1983, 1984). In this framework choice problems (see Mason and Mitroff, 1973) confront the analyst who must choose from a given set of alternatives or acts, given the goals subsumed within a utility function and given the expected outcome associated with possible action-state pairs. In this context:

'A decision problem is defined as follows: to choose from among a set of acts $A1, \ldots, Am$ that $Ai$ which optimizes (in some sense) the decision maker's ( $Z$ 's) return $Uij$ is the utility or value to $Z$ of the outcome $Oij$ corresponding to the doublet ( $Ai, Sj$ ) where ( $Sj$ ) is the set of the 'states of nature' [50]"

(Mason and Mitroff, 1973, p. 479).

Feldman and March (1981) characterize this framework as that of information engineers and argue that it is not a particularly fruitful way of viewing information issues of real organization. Note that in this framework problems are viewed as choices to be made from assumed action alternatives and from given alternative information structures or systems. In this world view, the desired end-state is given and the ‘problem’ is which alternative (action and information system) will produce that (given) end-state. Perhaps a less restrictive notion of ‘problem’ is better suited for information system evaluation issues.

Several elaborations of the decision theory notion of problem have appeared in the literature. One involves the degree of uncertainty concerning the decision model elements defined above. Under certainty, all relationships are deterministic and known, and thus there is no role for an information system (at this stage of decision analysis). Under risk, known probabilistic relationships among some of the model elements are assumed. Again information has no role (value) in the analysis, unless risk can be resolved through sampling. Under uncertainty, some relations among model variables are assumed unknown; thus, information can be helpful in estimating these relations and in reducing uncertainty. The main role of information in the choice problem notion of ‘problem’ thus focuses on the uncertainty of future states. Solving a ‘problem’ thus involves making a decision (in choosing the most attractive alternative).

A second elaboration expands uncertainty to the decision model itself. Such ‘unstructured’ or ‘wicked’ problems assume one may be uninformed concerning, perhaps, relevant states, decision criteria, or relations among choices and outcomes. The role of information in such a formulation is much richer as both sample information and feedback may enhance our knowledge. Recently, Hilton (1981) has delineated which aspects of a choice problem may affect the value of information.

## 3. Discussion

'What determines the selection of operations to be performed? There is but one answer: The nature of the problem to be dealt with' (Dewey, 1929, p. 123).

## 3.1. Solution-mindedness vs Problem-mindedness

The view that problems are the equivalent of choice problems, that solving merely entails a choice among alternatives, may lead to what Maier calls ‘solution-mindedness’ (1962). Groups, according to Maier, look for solutions even before they are certain what the problem is. Such ‘uncritical thinking’, in Dewey’s words (1910, p. 13) leads to premature evaluation of alternatives, and tends to inhibit the exploration of novel avenues of attack. ‘Solution-mindedness’ orients toward seeking a solution away from investigating the problem. Experiments with group problem-solving demonstrated that the presentation of an idea for solution

'tended to evoke either acceptance or rejection, rather than to stimulate other ideas'

(Maier and Solem, 1962, p. 152).

‘Problem-mindedness’, on the other hand, calls for exploring the nature of the problem; it stresses the generation of alternatives rather than the choice among, somehow given, alternatives. Eventually a choice must be made, but before one chooses from a given array of alternative solutions the task is to generate alternatives. Hence comes the suggestion to turn a ‘choice situation’ into a ‘problem situation’, to clarify the nature of the problem, to define it, before proceeding to choose a solution (Maier and Salem, 1962). Weick has similarly suggested, with regard to the formulation of research problems, that by accepting the manager’s definition of the problem, the researcher is at a disadvantage for

'he takes the phenomenon that the manager point to and his way of pointing to it as the principal area in which the search for relationships should be conducted'

(1962, p. 22).

## 3.2. Problem-Solving Process

Clearly, the notion of problems as choice problems does not necessarily suggest that solving merely entails a choice among somewhat given alternatives. First, decision processes normally depict a number of stages, only the last of which is usually viewed as the act of choice. Secondly, choice problems are not of one type. The distinction between structured and unstructured decisional tasks, programmed and unprogrammed, presumably provides for the requirement to investigate the nature of the problem. Nevertheless, our contention will be that neither the stages of decision-making nor the distinction between types of decisions adequately account for the problem formulation task.

Decision-making according to Simon (1960), comprises three principal phases: 'intelligence' (searching the environment for conditions calling for decision), 'design' (inventing, developing and analyzing possible courses of action), and 'choice' (selecting a particular course of action from those available). These phases, Simon holds, are closely related to John Dewey's stages of problem solving:

(i) what is the problem;

(ii) what are the alternatives; and

(iii) which alternative is best?

(1910, p. 3).

Thus, finding an occasion for making a decision (Simon's 'intelligence phase') would, in this view, somewhat answer Dewey's question: What is the problem? Awareness of computers and their potentialities by insurance companies, banks and other firms, which is Simon's illustration of the intelligence phase, provided the occasion for a decision with regard to the introduction of computers to these firms. But what was the problem for which computers constituted a solution? More specifically, would Simon's 'design phase' exclusively consider alternative computer systems, or would it also suggest possible non-computer alternatives? Simon is of little help here. His 'design task', in this illustration, is one which had to be carried out in each company before computers could be introduced (1960, pp. 2–3). There is no mention of what the nature of the problem was, or the invention of possible courses of action. Simon's more recent work on human problem-solving is similarly focused on solving. In problem-solving, as with decision-making, individuals presumably engage in selective trial and error search within the confinement of a maze – a set of path, some subsets of which, the 'correct' paths, have rewards at the terminals (Newell and Simon, 1972; Simon, 1979). But how is the maze to be constructed, the problem to be defined?

The first stage in Janis and Mann's five-steps scheme of decision making, to use another example, is 'challenge appraisal'. Being exposed to information about a threat or opportunity that effectively challenges a current course of action, one is advised to ask: 'Are the risks serious if I do not change?' (1977, p. 172). If the answer is positive, the decision maker's step two is to survey alternative means for dealing with the challenge. Again, alternatives seem to be the object of an unspecified search process, not the product of an answer, in the first place, to Dewey's query: What is the problem?

The interpretation of problems as choice problems is closely related to the tendency to view what Katz and Kahn (1966) call ‘dilemmas’ as ‘problems’. A ‘problem’ is one which can be solved in the frame of reference suggested by its nature, by past precedents for dealing with it, or by the application of existing policy. A ‘dilemma’, on the other hand, is not soluable within the assumptions explicitly or implicitly contained in its presentation; it requires reformulation (p. 276). This distinction between ‘problems’ and ‘dilemmas’ echoes Simon’s distinction between programmed and nonprogrammed decisions (1960). Simon’s emphasis on the existence of a definite procedure for handling programmed decisions coincides with Katz and Kahn’s requirement that a frame of reference exist within which the problem type of difficulty can be solved. But whereas Simon is less instructive with regard to the nonprogrammed, or unstructured, problems that require custom-tailored treatment, Katz and Kahn tell us what would be common to all such treatments – formulation of the problem (i.e. converting a ‘dilemma’ into a ‘problem’ in Katz and Kahn language, a ‘problem situation’ to ‘choice situation in Maier’s).

By ‘unstructured decision processes’ Mintzberg et al., like Simon, refer to

‘decision processes that have not been encountered in quite the same form and for which no predetermined and explicit set of ordered responses exists in the organization’ (1976, p.246).

Unlike the textbook's ‘decision under uncertainty’ where the alternatives are given even if the consequences are not, Mintzberg et al. prefer to single out ‘strategic decisions’ – where almost nothing is given or easily determined (p. 251). Most strategic decisions, they hold, do not present themselves to the decision makers but must be identified in the streams of ambiguous data they receive. Having identified such a decision (the recognition phase), the task is to diagnose, for no strategic decision situation comes preformulated (p. 254). 'Diagnosis', they contend:

'is probably the single most important routine, since it determines in large part, however implicitly, the subsequent course of action. Yet researchers have paid almost no attention to diagnosis preferring instead to focus on the selection routines ....'

## (p. 274).

March has similarly challenged the narrow view of individual and organizational choice behavior when goals exist and organizations act on these goals, choosing from among several alternatives on the basis of available information (1971, p. 572).

## 3.3. Problems and the Role of Interpretation

Indeed, most crucial to the requirement to define problems is the view that problems are not objective entities in their own right, but are the product of interpretation (Rein and Schon, 1977; Wildavsky, 1979, Dery 1983; 1984). The tendency in ‘systems analysis’ has been to believe in the reality of an objectively-defined problem, as Eden and Sims put it, which is assumed to be self-evident to all intelligent people (1979, p. 120). The opposing view that reality does not constrain the thought of the scientists but is rather itself a projection of that thought (Kuhn, 1962) suggests that problems do not come with an identifying tag, neither as problems nor as certain types of problems.

That problems are not objective entities should not be taken to mean that difficulties do not objectively exist, as if it were up to some kind of cognition agent to decide whether dissatisfied customers, faulty products or strong competitions do actually exist. Rather, the crucial point is that to define a problem is not to say that a certain phenomenon is a problem indeed (in the sense that it poses difficulties), but to say what kind of problem it is. And, from an intervention perspective, that of a manager, the crucial question in formulating a problem is what can the manager do to improve the situation. Neither the dimensions of improvement nor the identity of alternative courses of action are predetermined.

## 3.4. 'Decision' Support and Various Inquiry Systems

To the extent that problems are not the equivalent of decision (or choice) problems, a 'decision support system' (DSS) (Keen and Scott-Morton, 1978) is not a 'problem-solving support system'. While the former stresses support for a process that begins after the problem has been defined, the latter calls for support while defining the problem. Mason and Mitroff (1973) have confused one for the other, it seems, because problem solving is considered a decision problem of the unstructured type. They do recognize that with 'wicked problem' ('wicked decision problems' in their language) the problem is that of defining the state or the nature of the problem. Nevertheless their information system's support for solving wicked problems is still support for decision problems viewed primarily as choices from sets of alternatives.

More specifically they suggest a Lockean inquiry system (basically inductive) and leibnezian inquiry system (basically deductive) as most suitable for working on well-structured problems. For the ill-structured, wicked type they offer conflictual and dialectical inquiry systems (i.e., Kantian, Hegelian, and Singerian – Churchmanian). The later inquiry systems are deemed more suitable for ill-structured problems because they require that a number of formulations, possibly conflicting, be considered. A Kantian inquiry system according to Kilmann and Mitroff (1979), requires that the decision-maker be provided with at least two different views of the problem situation. The Hegelian inquiry system calls for a debate among the two most opposing views, and the singerian–Churchmanian suggests that an integrated, or synthesized problem definition be developed (see also, Mitroff and Pondy, 1974).

## 3.5. Information Support Systems

But given that a debate among competing formulations need be promoted, the question remains open as to which inquiry system is most suitable to support the generation of (any) problem definition. Conflictual or dialectical procedures are put to work in Mason and Mitroff's scheme only after different formulations of the problem are (some-how) arrived at, not in support of the generation of these formulations. In a recent book Mason and Mitroff correctly point out that 'decision support systems' are of limited help. The need to achieve insight into the nature of the complexity and to formulate concepts for coping with it, they write, is more important than providing guidance for choosing a particular course of aciton (1981, p.16). The question thus remains as to the appropriate type of information support system for the problem formulation task. The distinction between problem and decision problems and the stress on problem definition calls for information support systems which would allow for multiple interpretation of phenomena and goal scrutinization.

## 4. Conclusion

Given the important role of problem definition in problem solving and its need to promote debate among competing definitions, inductive reasoning would appear more suitable for dealing with ill-structured rather than, as Mason and Mitroff suggest, well-structured problems. Consider two of the advantages attributed to the case-study method over that of deductive reasoning (Walton, 1972):

(1) While deductive processes stress verification of theories and ad hoc hypotheses, induction is a vehicle for the generation of new theories and hypotheses. The requirement in case studies to start with the phenomenon observed rather than with an explanatory theory enables one to go outside existing inventories of concepts and hypotheses and, more importantly, sets of alternatives and projected outcomes.

(2) Rich descriptive details, another necessary ingredient in the case-study method, allow for differing and competing interpretations of the same phenomenon. An information support system with induction in mind would stress 'raw data' rather than pre-interpreted data, different presentations of phenomena rather the pre-imposed conceptual frameworks, models and theories to interpret phenomena. Inductive inquiry systems are prerequisites for conflictual dialectical procedures.

The contention that problems are not objective entities, that they need definition, has been carried a bit too far, at least so long as one considers it within an organizational context. An organization is by definition a set of restrictions on individual interpretation of events (Katz and Kahn, 1966, p.277). The notion of ‘problems’ as ‘decision problems’ is useful to the extent that it reminds us that organizational members are not (and normally should not be) free to constantly question, examine and re-examine organizational goals, or seek remedies outside a given repertoire of intervention strategies. Our stress on the pre-decisional phase of problem formulation should not be taken as a call to de-organize, de-routinize, to always turn a 'decision situation' into a 'problem-situation'. There is no sense in immediate unlearning of what has been just acquired through experience. Rather, it suggests that organizations should provide for the ability to question organizational givens. This ability would be lacking if inquiry systems were exclusively built to assist decision-making. Information systems and inquiry procedures that are built around certain premises cannot serve as the potential falsifier of these same premises. Feldman and March's (1981) observation that organizations do not display, in their data collection and retrieval and use of information, an agreement with rational models of decision-making is significant here. It suggests, unless we assume stupidity on the part of the organizations observed, that organizational needs for information are much richer than those provided for in the narrower concentration on more or less rationale models of decision-making.
