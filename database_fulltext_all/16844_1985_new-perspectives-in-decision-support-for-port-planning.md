# New Perspectives in Decision Support for Port Planning

Hans-Herbert WAGSCHAL

Technische Universität Berlin, Fachbereich Informistik, FR 5–5, Franklinstraße 28/29, 1000 Bertin 10, Federal Republic of Germany

The Decision-Support-System (DSS) to be presented within this paper is based on research done at the department of Computer Science of the Technical University of Berlin. Among the research conducted at the subdepartment for Applied Computer Science, the project "Determination of the Capacity of Ports" was one point of focus in the years 1980–1985. Findings of this research work as well as practical experiences helped in underpinning the knowledge of the decision processes in seaports; all this contributed to the development of a practice-oriented DSS. All parts of the system, including the data base, were programmed in SIMULA and implemented on the IBM 4381–2 of the Technical University of Berlin. Before the system is applied in an actual problem situation, all parts of the system, particularly the integrated simulation model, will have to be validated. Not until this validation has been done, will the decision makers have sufficient confidence regarding the decision support of this system. This validation is still being worked on. Further research is focused on the application of the DSS for similar decision problems in other traffic and transportation systems.

Keywords: Decision-support system, Port planning, Multi-purpose terminals, Information management, Data base, simulation model, Risk analysis. Filter principle, Efficiency of traffic- and transportation systems.

## 1. Introduction

When analyzing the reasons and presuppositions for industrialization one will always find that the developing traffic situation occupies a dominant position. Seaports represent the pivotal points of international thoroughfare. Next to means of transportation and roads, an economy also needs a place where transportation goods flow in and out. Seaports are such places. They also link traffic on land and on sea, i.e. they are places of trans-shipment and storage. Especially lately, ports provide a variety of services for their clients.

In the years 1967 to 1974, maritime trade was characterized by structural change. This structural change, introducing unit-loads of equal size in the form of pallet loads, roll-trailers and containers, changed ships as well. This new technology has considerably reduced unloading and loading times.

The seaports have undergone a remarkable change with the introduction of this new technology. Container transportation gained the most in significance. The use of containers has brought about door-to-door transport with a transport "chain" of standardized load-units. Transportation capacity is determined mainly by the quality of coordination among the groups involved in the transport chain. Each group in the seaport therefore endeavors to strengthen the competiveness of the port. The strong competition each port encounters necessitates cooperative conduct by all interest groups when it comes to making important decisions.

With every trans-shipping and storing activity to be accomplished, the decision makers are confronted anew with problems concerning efficient allocation of limited resources. The reason for this lies in the complexity of the trans-shipment process as well as in the technical, economic, social and political factors. As a result, it becomes more important to give all decision makers concerned a good idea of the port's performance at present and in the future (see Figure 1).

At present, no methods are known with which the efficiency of ports may be determined and evaluated. Indeed, methods based upon computer-aided decisions were developed in the USA as early as 1968. Yet those methods were based upon restrictive assumptions; consequently, their application up till now has been limited to special problems. It is hoped that this gap will be filled by the method proposed here. It includes a DSS, fully supporting decisions regarding the planning in seaports, an approach reach combines theory and practise [9].

## 2. Field of Application

Compared to a specialized port, a seaport involved in trans-shipping, storing and combining general cargo as well as special goods is far more complex in nature, where processes and structures are concerned. Such a seaport represents, in comparison with a specialized port like a container terminal, a far more difficult—because more universal—case for planning. Indeed, a DSS designed for such a seaport can also be applied to comparable problems of other specialized ports, a characteristic which does not necessarily apply to methods developed for specialized ports. To be considered in the following the seaport in question will therefore be the more universal type of seaport, which can handle ships at several berths with the modern technology of containers, roll-on/roll-off and LASH. Likewise, it must be possible at the port to trans-ship conventional general cargo along with special goods at multi-purpose terminals.

A typical application for DSS in seaports is the field of middle- and long-range investment planning. These are chiefly transactions dealing with the purchase and removal of stock goods. Investments in this time range determine the performance of every seaport. Applications for the DSS are mainly measures in the field of the infrastructure of a seaport, such as the widening of a harbor basin or enlargement of quay segments. Furthermore, measures changing the suprastructure must be included in the planning, because of the close relationship between the suprastructure and the infrastructure. Especially the suprastructure with its cranes, storage areas, transport facilities and the personnel needed involves extensive layout-planning for trans-shipment companies.

A DSS will be applied especially when several decision makers in a seaport have to take a whole variety of complex interrelationships into consideration in making a decision. Prior to every decision comes the planning process. This process can be divided into subprocesses (See Figure 2), resulting in a network of dependencies between the objects affected by the planning and the temporal element. The objects shown sample all facilities which could constitute bottlenecks in ports.

Planning is an organizational process. This process leads from the goal-setting stage, to the analysis of the problem, through the search for alternatives, up to the valuation. It is the task within the planning process to determine alternatives of action for future events. It is expedient to conclude the planning process with a valuation in the form of a suggested decision. The final decision itself will always come about through compromise and should therefore not be included in the planning process.

## 3. Requirements

The preceding statements suggest requirements for a DSS. Beginning with the status quo of a seaport, a DSS has to secure a problem-oriented and realistic consideration of the complex context. Reference to the specific expert knowledge and experience of the decision makers involved will result in a high degree of acceptance of the measures decided upon [6]. On the different levels of the planning process, the decision support should be adjusted, based upon the degree of detail required in that stage of the planning process. When planning strategically for the long-range, annual planning periods are necessary to guarantee a survey of the totality of the planning process.

Simple alteration and determination possibilities of affecting variables should enable one to make prognoses at this level in terms of layout planning or rough calculations of bottlenecks in technical objects and personnel. With respect to plans concluded on basis of the empirical experience of planners, different types of practical knowledge can contribute to establishing a clear procedure. A 'Management by Exception' requires the DSS to point out cause-effect relations and in particular bottlenecks concerning any of the processes in the port. Effects of previously roughly outlined bottlenecks have to be recognizable in detail on the next level of planning. To ensure the acceptance of the decision, an interface to the rough planning stage, which also takes into account the experience of the decision maker is required at this stage.

A DDS is supposed to allow decision makers to ask “what-if” and “what-to-do” questions. By doing so, the results of the rough planning stage can be corroborated. Additionally, it is possible that the decision makers involved in the planning process are not necessarily interested in an optimal alternative. The consequences of single alternatives should be clearly tied to the aims.

The purpose of evaluating planned measures is finally to enable one to make a statement about the performance of the port at present and in the future. Only with these requirements will it be possible to answer the main question whether development of transshipping using a DSS, meets the demands and considers the socio-economical, technical and political conditions.

## 4. Inventory

A lot of attention is presently paid to systematic decision support in the planning of sea-ports in theory and practise. There are quite a few reasons for this:

1. Technological progress and the dynamics of economic developments restrict the margin for activities by the management.

2. The risks associated with the decision problems restrict the management's margin of decision.

3. The management is confronted with increasingly unstructured decision problems which are of importance for the future development of the port. Often expert knowledge is necessary to recognize the problem situation.

4. A growing number of people are concerned by decisions made in the field of traffic. Both international and local linking of ports require the involvement of several decision makers within the decision process. Their often differing aims have to be considered in order to achieve decisions which will be accepted and can therefore be implemented.

At the same time, however, support means currently available to the management are as a rule of limited importance; e.g. the management information systems developed in the 1960's [11]. The lack of success of these systems results from a number of fundamental problems.

## 4.1. The Problem of Data

It is either not possible to obtain the necessary data or it can only be done at great expense. Uncertainty exists all along the way regarding the relevant information for making the decision. The decision risk is virtually inestimable. Data are usually dependent on the applied program. If several decision makers are working with the same data, there is a risk that the data will be inconsistent. Hence decisions will be based on incorrect data.

## 4.2. The Problem of Modeling

A model exists to help describe, explain and solve a decision problem. Only those characteristics of reality will be covered by the model which are significant for the decision preparation [8]. A model is achieved by a process of abstracting. This abstraction enables the decision maker to concentrate on the characteristic of reality essential from his point of view. Nevertheless, a model is only of practical value when it makes sufficient allowance for how people and objects actually behave in the environment in question.

Many models applied to support decisions fail to consider the fact that they are not only used to represent and explain reality. For the very reason that models are applied in decision support, reality is reorganized and hence changed. In connection with a decision process models are therefore of special importance, because they imply decision tasks and by doing so may preclude optimal solutions.

## 4.3. The Problem of Methods

Methods are procedural rules for attaining a satisfying or optimal solution to a decision problem with a model. The methods applied so far are goal-oriented concerning the decision problems and restricted in their application because of their dependency upon specific suppositions. Within the field of decision support, methods are applied to sub-problems in the planning process. An integration of the methods is lacking, as is connection to the data. As a result, methods are used for which the necessary data cannot be attained or can be acquired only at great expense. On the other hand, data may exist which is irrelevant for the decisions. As an example, it can be stated that for the group of theoretical, analytical methods, the application to seaports is restricted to special and limited problems [1, 2, 12, 14].

## 4.4. The Problem of Decision Makers

It is seldom possible to determine the exact relationship of a decision to strategic, tactical and operative planning since the influence of company-specific characteristics is very considerable. There are always problems in differentiating decisions when an isolated survey is done. Decisions gain significance only through their interdependencies. The decisions discussed here are of an ad-hoc character; e.g., they are characterized by an insecure information base. The specific problems to be solved are unstructured. The decisions are of great importance. The planning period is a middle-or long-range one and is, therefore a higher management decision [4].

New DSS concepts take into consideration at the outset that as a rule, little computer science knowledge can be presumed for the decision makers [13]. In order to achieve a higher degree of acceptance of such systems, the software should be largely oriented to the empirical behavior of the decision makers. The ergonomics of many systems has contributed to the growing significance of decision support systems. Achieving a user-oriented dialog between the decision, maker the and machine also requires ambitious software adaptations [17]. Only then will the basis of the decision be clear and transparent to the decision maker.

Real experience shows that the success of decision support systems depends very much on the information management, models and methods used. By itself no traditionally applied method for port planning is capable of meeting the demands set. Solutions to support a decision may be found in the integration of information, models and methods.

## 5. System Architecture

## 5.1. The Conception of Layers

The Decision Support System proposed here integrates data, models and methods into one planning process [19]. In the course of this process, the data is used for different purposes, starting with the definition of goals up to the decision preparation. The layers of the models might be pictured as a framework with which the decision maker gathers information, using different tools. Using this information, he can prepare decisions. Only by this process does the data become relevant for the decisions and in the process it becomes information. The framework, i.e. the models as a whole, is the important factor in order not to lose one's bearings in the planning process. Irrespective of the particular decision tasks to be achieved, they perform two functions:

(1) They are an organizational means to help avoid losing the safest pathway in the course of planning.

(2) They connect data and methods at the right point in the planning process while involving the decision maker.

For certain decision tasks, it may be necessary to use methods and data only within one sub-model. Therefore each submodel is independent of the others. The use of a single submodel within a planning process will only be necessary for particular problems. The dialog between the decision maker and the model is illustrated in Figure 3 by the interconnection between model, methods and data.

## 5.2. Information Management

In the course of developing an information management system (IMS), the main concern was to create a conceptual distance between the abstract data and its physical representation in the storage medium. The IMS enables the decision maker to design his data on the logical level. This user's view of the data is transformed into a form that can be interpreted by the system using a data description language. The models and methods search for and process data elements using a manipulation language. This language gives the decision maker the opportunity to navigate within the data base.

![](/api/attachments/WB3XH4JH/fulltext/images/34ebc6ea2b0c19e775e108a53dd753a67071f0c042aed35990ee54e560ff5485.jpg)  
Figure 3: Conception of Layers at the DSS

The model of a seaport in a computer consists of a model representing the interrelations between separate objects and a model of their currents states. Information on the objects – quay, storage areas, cranes, transport equipment and personnel – have to be administrated by the DSS for the model level. Other objects are the ships entering or leaving the port. For this group of objects, data on ship length, number of hatches, tonnage and kinds of goods have to be administered, as well as interactions between ships and the other objects. The data base contains all objects and interrelations of the port.

To create a conceptual distance between the logical and the physical structure of the data, the Information Management System was designed as a two-layer model. The first layer is a scheme with the logical structure of the data base. A scheme interpreter analyses and codes this scheme into an internal representation. The internal data description is stored in an administration file. This scheme is designed to be a base for the construction of the physical data base structure. A program connects the scheme to each model. The models only have access to the data in the data base via this first layer.

The second layer is responsible for the internal organization of the data. This is where the data is administered in the background storage. The layer of internal administration organizes the structure of the stored records as well as their relations (see Figure 4).

With the help of this Information Management System it is possible to change the data and the structures during the planning process without problems. The concept of layers makes it possible to make these changes without necessitating essential changes on the layer of methods and models. The data elements can be located relatively quickly by means of different criteria. The data is stored to a great extent without redundancy and is administered consistently.

## 5.3. Management of Models and Methods

This model layer contains three submodels. The need of some decision makers for clear planning of the strategic area is answered with the first submodel, the Development Model. To assist the decision maker in planning effectively, different methods are offered from a library of methods. These methods can be used to obtain approximations for bottlenecks of important objects. The decision maker uses data from the data that he can locate with the help of the Information Management System. Parameters can be altered and the range of the results can be displayed. The results themselves are again stored as solution proposals in the data base.

With this kind of long-term planning, an answer to decision-relevant questions can be found fast and easily. Examples are: How many berths need to be constructed for the ships, how many cranes are needed, what kind of personnel is needed? The decision maker can choose commonly used methods from the method layer as well as methods he has developed himself. The methods can be generated and stored in this layer. Familiar methods can be called up and used in a dialog. With this layer concept the decision maker can apply his experience and knowledge interactively. The results are easy to interpret and to comprehend, since the decision maker himself chooses the methods and data. Basically, the Development Model is a simple pocket calculator which a planner can use to solve demanding problems without programming knowledge. The Development Model provides the decision maker with a wide margin for selecting and using methods and data.

![](/api/attachments/WB3XH4JH/fulltext/images/eb1a9376d448404477e8cff88c98bf453f2eeb7113d21f7e648d91e74c46b2a5.jpg)  
Figure 4: The 2 Layers of the Information-Management-System

The analytical methods used in this submodel generally result in a high degree of uncertainty in the computed results. Critical objects like cranes or transport facilities are planned separately without regard to the organizational connections. When using this submodel, no indications of the risk and the effects of the planning process are given.

In order to verify the still-uncertain results of the Development Model, the decision maker can use the second submodel of this model layer, the Traffic Model. The Traffic Model makes it possible to adopt the solution proposals ,computed earlier with the Development Model and to use them as input data. In this submodel, the critical objects considered before are represented in far more detail. The basis for this model is the eventoriented discrete simulation. The Traffic Model is designed to apply this method for two reasons:

1. Dependence on Specific Problems: The complex relations within a Traffic Model can only be surveyed easily when specific requirements involved in the problem are taken into consideration. This model can never be designed for universal application; it needs to represent reality realistically. It is important to conceive the relations to the environment and the interdependencies exactly. It is indispensable that the complex processes and the organizational interweavings be taken into account in the application proposed here. This complexity requires an event-oriented, discrete modeling.

For modeling a seaport the collection and analysis of data is of great importance. Specific variables of influence have to be determined from the data depending on the application. The formulation of relations between the objects in the model also requires extensive data collecting. Above all, it is the formal process description of the status quo that results in a problem-oriented model of the existing system.

2. Dependence on the Valuation: After verifying the still-uncertain results of the Development Model by discrete simulation experiments, one will obtain individual determinate information statements on the simulated alternatives. The decision maker perceives the probability of environmental occurrences through the simulation experiments. These occurrences are called risk situations.

This transformation from a situation of uncertainty into a risk situation is of great significance for the preparation of decisions because the decision now becomes predictable and can also be evaluated in another submodel. The conventional procedure would be to estimate the variables used on the basis of evaluated information available to the decision maker at the time of the decision. From the statistical distributions achieved in this way, samples of any size can be generated using the Monte-Carlo Method with random numbers. This procedure is simple and hence widely employed [5].

Using the principles proposed here, the decision maker has the opportunity to name criteria that he finds significant. The results for these criteria will be determined by simulation; they do not have to be estimated. A necessary requirement for this procedure is a completed modeling including the methodical aid of a simulation.

Each simulation run is to be considered a random sample. The results determined this way are condensed to a risk analysis in the subsequent Evaluation Model. More information about environmental occurrences of which the decision maker knows the probability do not necessarily result in better decisions. The question remains of how the environmental occurrences and the information on them should be evaluated. To find an answer to this question, the Evaluation Model is needed.

The simulated results of the Traffic Model are methodically closely associated to the third sub-model, the Evaluation Model. The Evaluation Model shows the results of each simulation run corresponding to the valuation of each decision maker. Very different criteria can be used for the evaluation according to the problem. When evaluating several investment alternatives, economical standards other than costs and returns are chosen, e.g. technical, economic or social criteria.

The Evaluation Model functions by the rules of the generally known cost-efficiency analysis. The procedural steps in detail are considered to be known and therefore not explained further [10, 20]. The model calls for a standardization of the evaluation. It becomes possible to include standardized criteria of different dimensions. Aggregations are no problem. The conclusions determined in this way show the performance of the existing or planned port in the view of the decision maker. It is important that the selection of goals and criteria as well as their respective significance and relation to benefits are transparent and comprehensible. This allows for the inclusion of planned changes – even of quantitative kind – in the evaluation.

The result of the last procedural steps is an aggregated quantity which clearly characterizes the performance as a summary of economical, technical and social attributes of performance. In this way, a statement expressing the decision maker's viewpoint on the ability of a traffic system to perform is developed.

## 6. Decision Making in Ports

## 6.1. Decision Support as a Filter Principle

As has been outlined in the beginning, the planning process is not capable of giving a clear answer to the question of how alternative solutions to a problem arise. Decision makers may either prefer various alternatives or have differing perceptions about the costs and benefits of the jointly proposed alternative. Likewise, it is possible that the same or similar performance requirements can be answered with different technologies or forms of organization and with different efforts.

In every case, the management or the administrative authority as the final decision maker is entitled to ask for an objective and logically founded decision base from the DSS [3]. It is clear that port planning needs to be embedded firmly into a planning process. For this planning it is necessary to form an administratively accepted and scientifically held convention which requires the legitimacy of all decision makers. One requirement for this legitimacy is the inclusion of the interest groups as decision makers in the process of decision preparation. Another requirement is to accept a limited number of optimal or at least satisfying alternatives. The filter principal shown below in Figure 5 illustrates this concept.

The aims of the different decision makers are specified during the planning. The determining of calculable criteria increasingly reduces the margin for solutions. This process of reduction is carried on until only those alternatives are left to be considered in the Evaluation Model which may at least be expected to be satisfying solutions to the problem. The necessary criteria for the filter principle do not exist at the beginning of the planning process; they are developed only when alternatives are designed with the Traffic Model.

It becomes clear that the planning process may not be dominated by the final decision. In all phases, subresolutions consciously or subconsciously exclude information. During the planning process those alternatives are extracted which are of some significance with regard to the most favorable solution to the whole problem and which meet the minimum requirements of a decision maker. This manner of performance reduces the number of theoretically possible alternatives as well as the necessary evaluation of the alternatives which fall within an economically acceptable range. Through dialog with the decision maker, the alternatives are not developed beyond practical needs but rather according to the possibilities and demands of reality. In this way, the filter principle meets the requirements for making a rational decision.

## 6.2. Interactive Decision Support—an Example

The usage of the different DSS layers and the application of the filter principle is not limited to the planning of ports and can therefore be transferred to similar problems. The aspects of the decision support which are of general interest shall be illustrated here with a real-world example.

In 1962, the planning for new general cargo facilities in the South Town Harbor completed. Operations started in 1966. The facilities were developed further until the summer of 1977. By that time, on the western side of the harbor basin, a state of development had been reached that could not have been foreseen when planning began in 1962. The general cargo facilities turned into diversified multi-purpose facilities capable of trans-shipping special goods as well as general cargo. The development was characterized by the introduction of containers. In 1978, another multi-purpose facility was constructed as Terminal 21 on the east side of the harbor basin. The operator of the port facilities, the Bremer Lagerhaus Gesellschaft, constructed 20,000 square meters of roofed-over storage area. This area was expanded by 30,000 square meters of outside storage. The city of Bremen financed the construction of the 450m. quay, from positions 150 to 600. In 1982, the facilities available for trans-shipment included seven cranes, a container terminal (80 tons) and a gantry crane for the trans-shipment outside. All in all, more than 100 people were employed, handling more than thirty transport equipments to operate the trans-shipment at the multi-purpose Terminal 21.

Because of developments in the quantity of cargo and progress in trans-shipment technologies, the demand soon arose for improvements in the performance of the existing port or for the planning of new facilities. The decision makers were trying to achieve trans-shipment facilities that met all requirements while still taking into account the changing socio-economical and technical conditions. In this case, decision support is both sensible and helpful due to the complex events in the port, the unstructured nature of the problem and future risks linked to the decision. Such a decision support is illustrated in Figure 5.

The relevant data of 1980-1981 for the South Town Harbor were analyzed with the aid of a computer. This traffic- and structure-analysis data are administered by the Information Management System. With the IMS the decision maker has the opportunity to plan the kinds and quantities of goods to be trans-shipped at each multi-purpose terminal for a defined period of time. The necessary variables are defined with the system in a dialog (Point 1 in Figure 5).

These variables can now be used to determine the approximate length of the quay or the storage area needed. To do this, the decision maker uses mathematical formulae from the library of methods (point 2 in Figure 5). The mathematical relations between the length of the quay and the determining variables can also be set up by the decision maker. The result is a solution proposal for a bottleneck object, exemplified here for the quay (Point 3 in Figure 5).

The methods used in the Planning Model reduce complex stochastic relations to simple determinate statements. At this point it becomes evident that the extensive mathematical registration of stochastic relations is only needed whenever they represent the sole basis for planning [7]. In the DSS proposed here, the modeling of complex stochastic relations is transferred to the Traffic Model. Because it is possible to verify the solution proposals afterwards, the prognosis calculations can do without stochastics. Therefore, all accidental imponderables which had to be considered for the utilization to be on the safe side can be condensed into one quantity at this solution-proposal [19]. All other bottleneck objects are computed in the Planning Model by application of the same principle.

The important aspect of these approximate calculations is the results, i.e. the solution proposal, which can now be verified. The solution proposal has to be in the right range of values to prevent extensive modeling and to reduce the number of model experiments.

In this simplified elucidation, the existence of a model of the status quo at the port to be analyzed is taken for granted. This model of the basis year is confined to the bottlenecks in the inner port corresponding to the problem in this example. The solution proposals from the planning Model are now included into this basis model as planning values. In terms of facts, this means modeling the infrastructure and the suprastructure necessary to the quay. The fact that the solution proposals at this point are just structural data should not be ignored. Additional variables and disposition rules have to be changed because different kinds of goods are trans-shipped or the productivity of the cranes and the personnel is altered by the planning (Point 4 in Figure 5).

The model experiments indicate whether the planned kinds and quantities of goods can be handled in the part of the modeled port considering the given or planned technical, economical and social conditions (Point 5 in Figure 5). To solve this problem it is necessary to have a view of the trans-shipment activities on an aggregated level when bottlenecks or short-comings in utilization can be recognized and interpreted immediately. For many statements this level is the working shift. Only within a shift will bottlenecks that energy in the course of events be recognized (Point 6 in Figure 5).

With reference to the solution proposals of the Planning Model, statements on the shift level can be related to all bottleneck objects. It solution proposals are realized, the consequences are determined this way in the context. Figure 7 shows the state of the quay during certain working shifts; each single activity and each event affects the output. The decision maker can observe bottleneck objects in the process and the detailed effects of his planning. Naturally, all detail statements can be combined and analyzed for a range of time and a multi-purpose terminal. The printer output shown in Figure 8 enables the decision maker to recognize the utilization of the quay for the different days of the week. This statement can only be interpreted sensibly when all other bottleneck objects are taken into consideration. The decision maker is then no longer condemned to idleness but can instead actively shape the trans-shipment activities. The utilization of the bottleneck objects can be adjusted. The practical requirements determining the permissible capacity of the facilities becomes recognizable and, via a number of simulation experiments, also interpretable.

Figure 9: Cost-efficiency as method of the evaluation model

Finally, Figure 9 illustrates the valuation of alternatives by the Evaluation Model. This shows how the valuation criteria can be chosen individually. Here the criteria chosen by a decision maker were merely related to the prospective yields. The degree of goal accomplishment was ascertained by a valuation function determined subjectively by the decision maker and is entered into the chart for each alternative. In this example it is not possible to check the provisional results, however, because the valuation function was not specified explicitly for reasons of clearness.

Separate valuations are carried out for each decision maker which again can be ranked for the final decision [18]. As mentioned before, certain prospective yields are specified directly by the decision maker (Point 7 in Figure 5). Risk-affected prospective yields are determined by the simulation and included automatically into the valuation (Point 8 in Figure 5).

![](/api/attachments/WB3XH4JH/fulltext/images/0bb70f729851e83bf6db42bcfd2f8bffb40bec3a467cbde00341d7188d1b8b5a.jpg)  
Figure 10: Risk analysis-graphic output

The costs and benefits illustrated in Figure 9, therefore, represent a random number and depict the most probable values of the prospective yields. If determination of the risk-affected prospective yield is not possible through simulation, it has to be estimated in the dialog. Additional information about the range of values and the probability of certain values is given by the decision maker (Point 7 in Figure 5). With this information, samples of known statistical distributions can be taken which are included for the valuation as representative values of the prospective yields. The individual benefits and the costs of each simulation experiment are grouped according to the frequency of their occurrences and are combined into a distribution function. In this way, it becomes possible for the decision maker to reckon the risk of each alternative (Figure 10). The risk analysis is, therefore, variable throughout the simulation. Hence, the decision support is not related to a single point in time, as is the case with the cost-efficiency analysis. The risk analysis of the DSS covers the whole planning period.

This way, each decision maker determines the efficiency relevant to him of each alternative and of the status quo, illustrated in a simple manner in Figures 9 and 10. The results presented for the support of the decision show the problem of aggregation and of the decision rules used (Point 9 in Figure 5). The decision makers might come to different valuations ranking the alternatives. This problem can only be solved by a detailed analysis of every prospective yield and a discussion about the subjective valuation criteria. The DSS creates the requirements for such feedback by the integrated use of data, models and methods.

## 7. Conclusion

The planning of the infra- and suprastructure is essential in large transportation and storage systems as they are represented in modern ports. For that reason it is very important to convince the management of a port of the quality of the existing and planned performance of transportation systems. Currently, however, performance is not defined clearly and comprehensively according to any standards. In addition, no techniques are known to determine and evaluate this performance. The importance of large-scale project planning in ports, on the other hand, has continually increased. This planning belongs to the typical range of application of logistics as, for example, in the conveyance and storage of goods. The proposed solution is a computer-based Decision Support System (DSS), which was developed in a large research project. It combines data, models and methods which have mainly been used in singular planning phases for determined problems. The results of the planning process are automatically condensed in the evaluation phase to a risk analysis. Each person participating in the decision process finds the most relevant performance for his special case. In this approach the traditional use of the term “capacity” of traffic systems for evaluating their efficiency has been extended to a multidimensional declaration of the economical, social and technical effects for better decision support in ports.
