# Fundamental Issues of Decision Support Systems: A Consequentialist Perspective

H.K. Klein \* and R. Hirschheim \*\*

\* State University of New York, School of Management, Binghamton, NY 13901, USA and

\*\*London School of Economics, London, UK

While considerable enthusiasm for DSS exists in many quarters, there are still a number of fundamental issues which have not been seriously addressed. Of particular concern, is how DSS is likely to affect organizations. This philosophically motivated paper analyzes the underlying assumptions of DSS and develops an approach for consequence determination. Moreover, the paper uses this approach to assess how DSS might affect various organizational elements.

Keywords: Decision support systems; Epistemology of information systems; Tacit knowledge; Consequentialist approach; Pluralist inquiry; Knowledge interests; Organizational impacts of decision support systems; Consensus formation; Power; Individual interest; Collective interest.

## 1. Introduction

Throughout the history of the development of information systems (IS) in organizations, new and better types of systems and technology have been proposed. Technological tools such as data bases, office automation, expert systems and the like have been developed over the past years with the promise that better organizational information systems could be designed and built. Similarly, the types of information systems which can be supported by the new technology have also blossomed. Initially, only routine transaction systems were supported by or implemented on the new technology. Later, reporting and control systems which oversaw the various transaction systems were developed. This gave rise to the notion of an all-embracing information system which integrated the various reporting and control systems together – the so-called management information system. Unfortunately, this latter concept turned out to be a more difficult proposition than was originally conceived, and thus led to considerable confusion and debate among the data processing and information systems community. (See, for example, Dearden 1972; Will 1973; Ackoff 1967; and Tolliver 1971.) Because of the problems associated with management information systems, numerous individuals asserted the need to change the focus of computer support for information systems away from the integrated information resource to something more appropriate to the individual decision makers. It was suggested this focus should be on the specific decision and the individual who would make the decision. Thus, the concept of decision support systems (DSS) was born. DSS has been presented as the latest in the development of information systems for organizations.

The benefits attributed to DSS have been widely written about and are briefly discussed in the second section of this paper. What has received considerably less attention is whether these claims have any validity, or more generally, what the impact of DSS is likely to be on organizations.

There appears to be an implicit assumption on the part of DSS writers that DSS are beneficial to organizations and the DSS intervention process is not inherently polemic. The purpose of this paper is to challenge some of these assumptions and to critically examine what the potential consequences might be. The approach taken is philosophically motivated and driven. We feel this to be valuable as it offers an alternative perspective to analyzing DSS consequences.

## 2. The Evolving Nature of DSS

In attempting to examine the potential effects of a new organizational intervention like DSS, some agreement about its meaning is needed. Yet defining DSS is not a straightforward matter. DSS seems to mean different things to different people. Moreover, as reported in Hackathorn (1980), there may be an inherent technical or engineering bias in our notion of DSS. He writes:

“…we need to deal with more profound conceptions of human decision making. Each of us should take care to become aware of the cultural limitations inherent within a ‘technical’ or ‘engineering’ orientation. Each of us should recognize, with deep humility, that our fundamental values may tragically disable our honest mission of improving decision making.” (Hackathorn 1980, p. 173)

Nevertheless, the DSS concept is widely used and there exists a generally ‘accepted’ view of what it is.

## 2.1. The Orthodox View of DSS

According to the ‘orthodox’ view, the notion that support systems are systems which provide information to managers to support their decision making activities, does not constitute a meaningful definition of DSS. This very wide notion of a DSS, as for instance implied in Alter (1980), is too broad to be of much value. Rather, a DSS may be more appropriately considered within the context of what precisely it is intended to support: its intentional nature.

Dempster puts and answers the question of intentional meaning of the DSS concept as follows.

"Which part of the support of human beings with information technology is the concern of decision support engineering? It is obviously the part which is adaptive, that is underspecified, and that recognizes continually that complexity and perplexity are the order of the day. Decision support systems must be resilient in the faces of changes – not only in the organizational environment, but also in the organizational structure, decision-making norms, and operating personnel. The necessity for user orientation, reconfiguration, and useful documentation is obvious." (Dempster 1980, p. 177).

This view of the nature of DSS appears to be consistent with the pioneering work of Keen and Scott Morton (1978) and Sprague (1980) while being somewhat at odds with the views held by many practitioners.

Sprague (1980) in principle agrees and stresses that DSS is a separate technology along with structured reporting and communications to support all functions of management.

“DSS, therefore, is not merely an evolutionary advancement of EDP and MIS, and it will certainly not replace either. Nor is it merely an information system aimed exclusively at top management, where other information systems seem to have failed. It is, rather, another powerful weapon in the information technology arsenal to improve the effectiveness of managers in organizations. To accomplish this objective, however, DSS depends on a unique set of characteristics and capabilities which are evolving from state-of-the-art information system technology.” (Sprague 1980, p. 3).

There appears to be full agreement of what the DSS defining characteristics are between Sprague and the key attributes suggested by Keen (1980, p.24). According to these authors DSS:

(i) Deal with semi-structured tasks;

(ii) Employ an evolutionary and ‘middle-out’ design strategy;

(iii) Draw on descriptive insights and normative models of decision making;

(iv) With the intent to improve effectiveness rather than efficiency; and

(v) Reflect an implementation strategy based on

the use of skilled intermediates, responsive service, and ‘humanized software interfaces’. This we take to be the consensus view of the nature of DSS. It is consistent with the way DSS is discussed by numerous authors (c.f., Keen and Scott Morton 1978; Alter 1980; Keen 1981; Sprague and Carlson 1982; Bennett 1983). While these characteristics are helpful for the extension or denotation of the DSS concept, it still leaves some room for disagreement over the role of models and the emphasis given to the ad-hoc, evolutionary, ‘middle-out’ strategy.

The DSS concept is somewhat ambiguous on the role of normative models. Some suggest the view that normative models are the way to improve the effectiveness of management and should be the basis of DSS. Others, are less certain. The consensus appears to be that a normative model is to be preferred whenever available. Hence DSS are normative model driven. This also seems to be the view of many OR practitioners. A middle out strategy here means that a ‘quick and dirty’ OR model or part of such a model is introduced first and then subsequently refined or extended. In the refinement and extension process, user characteristics and preferences are to be given a great deal of consideration. User characteristics are measured in terms of certain psychological difference data relating to cognitive style and other personality dimensions; c.f., Benbasat and Taylor (1978); Zmud (1979).

Numerous benefits have been attributed to DSS. In Keen and Scott Morton (1978) we find two explicit classes of benefits claimed for DSS:

(i) DSS concentrate more on improving effectiveness than efficiency;

(ii) DSS can contribute to the decentralization of the design process.

(Whether or not one admits this as an advantage is, of course, dependent upon one's preferred model of organization. But many feel such decentralization is valuable.) To these two, a third could be added (c.f., Keen 1981) by extending the concept of decentralization, as introduced under (ii), to refer not only to the decentralization of design decisions, but decentralization of decision making in general. This could lead to decision making which stimulates initiative, creativity and flexibility throughout the organization.

## 2.2. The Question of Bias in the Orthodox View of DSS

At this point it is not very clear if DSS are biased in a ‘technical’ or ‘engineering’ sense. One conjecture is that bias comes from putting a normative model at the core of the DSS concept. If one takes this to mean the type of mathematical models that currently make up the main stay of Management Science/OR, then this, indeed, could lead to a mechanistic bias (c.f., Dempster 1980, p. 176; Sheinin 1980, p. 154). However, bias may also be interjected into DSS through the nature of the people who design and build DSS. That is, they may be ‘technically’ minded and view organizational processes and problems from a ‘technical’ perspective. Thus, a mechanistic bias may indeed be an inherent aspect of DSS. (More on this later.) Moreover, although there appears to be no inherent ‘technical’ or ‘engineering’ bias in the claimed benefits of DSS (other than the fact that the benefits are derived from some technological intervention), on closer inspection, Keen’s claimed benefits (Table 1) are not explicitly social or humanistic ones. They reflect management’s concern for effectiveness and efficiency and do not strictly consider human needs.

## 2.3. The Question of Bias in the Preferred Framework of Analysis

Whatever the benefits of DSS, there is a need for a positive relationship between introducing DSS and the psychological–behavioral response of the human organization if they are to be achieved.

Hence we are led to ask what sort of relationships are presumed in the literature which provide a theoretical rationale for the claimed benefits. The usual way in which we predict the behavior of a system in science is by a cause-effect model. This would lead one to question if the DSS community has, indeed, built on a valid model of human behavior in organizations or if this is an area where ‘scientific bias’ has crept in. (Scientism is the doctrine that the same model of inquiry which is deemed valid for the physical sciences and engineering can be transferred to the social sciences without question; c.f., McCarthy 1982, p. 1.) On what sort of model of human behavior does the DSS community by and large rely?

In order to answer this question it is necessary to briefly discuss what kinds of dimensions such a model could embrace. To this end, we shall adopt a framework developed by Burrell and Morgan (1979). It is seen as a useful vehicle for structuring the tacit presuppositions of the approach to organizational analysis adopted by most IS/DSS researchers. The framework involves two dimensions: the subjective-objective dimension and the cooperative-conflict dimension. The subjective-objective dimension is concerned primarily with epistemological issues and ontological assumptions. For example, the objectivist approach to social science embraces an ontology of ‘realism’ which contends the world is made up of hard, tangible objects which exist as empirical entities. Moreover, the world exists independently from any person’s appreciation of it. It also adopts a ‘positivistic’ epistemology. This is one:

“which seeks to explain and predict what happens in the social world by searching for regularities and causal relationships between its constituent elements." (Burrell and Morgan 1979, p. 5)

The subjectivist approach, on the other hand, embraces an ontology of ‘nominalism’ which sees the world in terms of labels, names or other artificial creations which impose some form of structure on reality. The world cannot exist independently of a person’s perception of it. The subjectivist approach adopts an ‘anti-positivistic’ epistemology, which Burrell and Morgan define as one where:

"the social world is essentially relativistic and can only be understood from the point of view of the individuals who are directly involved in the activities which are to be studied." (Burrell and Morgan, 1979, p. 5)

The second dimension is that of cooperation–conflict (c.f., Dahrendorf 1959). It is concerned with whether organizational and human behavior can be categorized as generally stable or in a constant state of conflict (see Table 2).

In summarizing how a social scientist approaches his task of investigation, Burrell and Morgan state:

"All social scientists approach their subject via explicit or implicit assumptions about the nature of the social world and the way in which it may be investigated. ... Social Scientists for example are faced with the basic ontological question: whether the ‘reality’ to be investigated is external to the individual – imposing itself on individual consciousness from without – or the product of individual consciousness; whether ‘reality’ is of an ‘objective’ nature, or the product of individual cognition; whether 'reality' is a given 'out there' in the world, or the product of one's mind." (Burrell and Morgan 1979, p. 1)

For instance, it has been proposed that organizations 'are' coalitions, processes, structures, various types of systems, defences against anxiety, even 'garbage cans' (c.f., Keen and Scott Morton 1978; Huber 1980; March and Olsen 1976).

## 2.4. Conclusions and Outlook

In view of the distinctions proposed by Burrell and Morgan (1979) it appears that there exists an inherent technical-engineering bias of the orthodox DSS concept which is essentially two-fold. In analyzing human decision-making and ‘effectiveness’, the DSS literature (and the IS literature in general) is predisposed towards an ‘integrationist’ (cooperative) or conflict denial model of organizations. This can be demonstrated by investigating the amount of attention given to the kind of phenomena to which the right half of Table 2 refers. For instance, practically no IS/DSS research draws on critical social theory (e.g., Marcuse 1964; Habermas 1971; Keat 1981; McCarthy 1982) or the psychoanalysis of organizational behavior (e.g., Bion 1968; Fromm 1955; Klein 1959; Jaques 1955). The second bias of the DSS concept comes from a strong predisposition towards ‘reality modeling’ (c.f., Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982). Underlying most of the IS/DSS literature is an unreflected commitment towards an objectivist ontology and epistemology. The latter bias prevents the DSS movement from absorbing the lessons of hermeneutics, phenomenology, and existentialism when trying to understand human decision making. For instance, the problems associated with consciousness, free will and authentic motivation are largely ignored when applying a cognitivist framework to the analysis of human decision making. (Notable exceptions to this are Boland and

Day (1982), Boland (1984) and Checkland (1982) who extensively draw on the phenomenological tradition in their work.)

Of course, these comments raise many more questions than can be dealt with here. In principle, the consequentialist approach as advocated here, tries to strike an ecclective balance between various lines of thought in the social sciences. Section 3 will draw on some subjectivist writings for dealing with the epistemological problems of consequence determination in DSS development. In section 4 it will be argued that DSS development could profit if it were to draw more extensively on the writings of the subjectivists and if it were to look at the potential consequences of IS and DSS development both from an ‘integrationist’ and ‘conflict’ perspective.

## 3. The Epistemology of the Consequentialist Approach

The label ‘consequentialist’ is to suggest that the desirability of an act – its goodness and rightness – is to be determined by weighing its consequences. The consideration of intent associated with the act is secondary. Taking a consequentialist perspective in systems development means, essentially that the desirability of a proposed change is to be judged by its actual consequences regardless of whether these were intended or anticipated. In principle, taking a such a perspective requires three conceptually separate steps:

(1) Establishing that, in principle at least, it is possible to identify the actual consequences. (That this is in principle possible will be doubted by someone believing in a probabilistic universe or the possibility of human freedom. Causal determinism involves the denial of human freedom – this is one Kant's (1964) famous antinomies. But if users are granted the possibility of free choice then it must be recognized that they can react in a basically unpredictable fashion to the developer's action.)

(2) Perceiving some subset of the actual consequences, given that they are predictable in principle.

(3) Evaluating the predicted and perceived consequences.

Because of its complex nature and the desire to keep this paper within a reasonable length, it is not possible to deal here with the last of these three issues: how to determine which values are to be attached to the consequences. It leads to such questions as what is desirable, for whom, in whose eyes, for what purposes, and so on. The consequentialist position advocates that any methodology associated with DSS development should give explicit consideration to addressing the value question. The concern in developing an appropriate evaluation approach must be with ‘emancipating’ the interests of the system stakeholders, such that the resulting system design is sensitive to both the human needs of the stakeholders and to the ‘ethics of the system as a whole’, as this latter concept has been defined by Churchman (1968). Further details are discussed in Klein (1980).

## 3.1. Identifying Actual Consequences

The identification of actual consequences is difficult at best and impossible and worst for two basic reasons: the issue of causation and the difficulties with measuring important aspects of human behavior. Both of these issues arise in predicting the impact of a technological intervention such as DSS because organizations are of sufficient complexity to exhibit many of the dynamics of socio-technical systems.

There are two problems which must be faced by anyone trying to predict behavior. One is the question of whether the cause-effect mode of analysis is applicable in principle to the 'understanding' of human behavior, as it is presumed to be applicable to animal behavior, and the rest of the universe for that matter. The fundamental issue is, to what extent, if at all, human reactions are 'determined' by causal laws – and hence predictable by a positivistic approach – and to what extent they are the result of an 'indeterminate' human will; i.e., the result of conscious and free choices for which the agent (and not some law of nature) is responsible? Even if one believes that the former is true, then there is a second difficulty which needs to be resolved; i.e., measuring the cause-effect relationships. As Hume (1740, 1748) pointed out, we cannot observe cause-effect relationships; all that we can observe is the succession of certain events in time. Hence the causal mode of thinking is only a heuristic device by which we hope to find regularities in the chaos of sense impressions partly concurrent with one another and partly following one another. From this arises the measurement problem of how to select those events which are causally related and not merely sequentially correlated.

## 3.1.1. Causality vs Free Choice

Some very convincing arguments have been proposed that the causal mode of thinking is misleading, or at least insufficient for the analysis of human behavior. For instance, it is a common observation that there appears to be a fundamental difference between ‘explaining’ why a stone would drop from the edge of a cliff and ‘understanding’ why a person would jump from that same cliff. Therefore when it comes to ‘predicting’ whether a stone would drop or a person will jump (commit suicide), the meaning of prediction in the first case has very little to do with the kind of analysis that is needed in predicting the second event. When using the first approach to prediction, we shall employ such terms as ‘causal mode of analysis’ or ‘causal explanation’. In the case where the focus of analysis is on conscious human choice or ‘free’ choice, such phrases as ‘hermeneutic mode of analysis’, ‘hermeneutic understanding’, or simply ‘human understanding’ will be used. To be consistent with this terminology, in the example above we would say that physics can ‘explain’ why a stone falls off a cliff, but a mother might (or might not) ‘understand’ why her son jumped off the cliff.

For the purpose of this paper it is unnecessary to take a stand on this rather controversial issue of whether or not there is a difference between ‘explanation’ and ‘understanding’ (for pertinent summaries of this ongoing debate see: Manninen and Tuomela 1975; and more recently, Apel 1979). It is sufficient to note that there is a ‘causal’ and a hermeneutic mode of analysis. It is our contention that both can be used to predict the consequences of technological interventions in the following ways:

(i) There may be situations where one is appropriate but not the other;

(ii) There may also be cases where both can be used and the results compared for cross-validation.

However, as will be discussed in the next section, there is reason to believe that causal analysis may only be appropriate in very limited circumstances.

Hermeneutic analysis, on the other hand, appears more generally suited to prediction when the subject of study is the human being.

## 3.1.2. The Measuring of Actual Consequences

Given that the mode of causal analysis in principle is said to apply, then the second fundamental issue is that of measurement, or more precisely: What and how should we measure when attempting to attribute cause with effect in the social sciences? The fundamental problem is to devise analytical methods which can separate the effects of multi-causation or 'co-production': How can we be sure that the consequences assumed too be caused by, say, the introduction of a decision support system are in fact caused by it and not something else? Is it truly possible to concretely attribute consequences to a particular cause? In an epistemological sense, the answer is, of course, no. In the natural sciences, however, the adopted scientific paradigm does allow us to deal with such issues effectively (c.f., Popper 1968). Unfortunately this is not necessarily true in the social sciences. Lessnoff (1974) suggests why. He states that the major difference between the physical and social sciences is that the latter deals with people – particularly people's behavior. And it is behavior which is at the center of the problem. People, Lessnoff states, have conscious minds, which means they act differently from the rest of nature. Therefore, the adoption of the physical science paradigm will be problematic (see also Burrell and Morgan 1979).

In the physical sciences, measurement is less of a problem in that various observable features of things are studied (e.g., shapes, sizes, colors). With the notable exception of particle physics, it is not very problematic in the natural sciences to assume that the properties to be measured do exist independently of the observer, and that the measurement process does not create or change them. This is quite different in the social sciences, which are after all concerned with consciously reacting people – those observed are in turn observers. As the reactions of the observed are dependent on the state of mind, the social sciences therefore must somehow deal with the phenomena of the human mind (e.g., intuition, beliefs, desires, purposes, values). Yet how does one measure the ‘phenomena of the human mind’? For instance, whether a proposed system change is a change for the better is to a large extent in the eye of the beholder. How can one predict the favorable or unfavorable reaction which an intervention will ‘cause’ in the minds of all possible subjects affected? Even if one assumes that the subjects’ memory contents are known to some extent there is still one further difficulty: measurement controls are needed to prevent the very act of measuring from influencing the subjects’ attitudes towards the proposed change, as was witnessed in the Hawthorne experiments. Hence the same system change could produce positive or negative human reactions, depending on whether attitude measurements are taken or not.

When transplanting the causal mode of analysis from the physical to the social sciences the usual approach is to assume that mental phenomena can be measured and studied through the observation of overt phenomena, i.e., behavior. In other words, behavior is used as a surrogate for mental phenomena.

In general, social scientists appear to believe in this surrogate, and have therefore developed a strong predilection toward empiricism. Various social scientists have elaborated on the notion of empiricism as is evidenced by the writings of Emil Durkheim. Durkheim (1964, pp. 2–7, 29–30), for example, noted a distinction between ‘ways of thinking’ and ‘ideas’ in human minds. He claimed the former are data for the social scientist, while the latter are not. Science cannot deal with ‘ideas’ at all, but only with ‘ways of thinking’. This is so because the latter manifest themselves through written creeds, written laws, and other forms of observable behavior, such as speech. In modern language one would refer to ‘ways of thinking’ as the patterns or logic of human thought as it is discernable in documented output. In other words, people’s ‘ways of thinking’ are expressed in language (i.e., symbols) which can be studied by the methods of empirical science. In addition, Durkheim made the assumption that symbolic behavior (e.g., speech and writing) can be substituted for observable behavior.

Unfortunately, this view that observable human behavior can be used as a sufficient basis for the explanation of human behavior, and therefore the understanding of the mental phenomena of the mind is fallacious. The description of the physical aspects of behavior does not describe human action, only people doing things. Mere causal analysis fails to analyze what lies behind ‘man as an appearance’ (Kant 1964, p. 127). In order to see an event as a human action, it is necessary to interpret its empirically observable features in terms of mental categories. In essence, it is the purposive (i.e., goal seeking aspect of behavior) not its physical aspects, that constitute the unity of action. It is this purposive aspect of behavior that needs to be understood and measured if mental phenomena of the mind are to be understood. No matter how well the patterns of observable behaviors have been identified, they will fall short of the understanding of human actions. Description, explanation, and understanding are categorically different. A similar point is also made by speech act theory. Searle (1979) argues, for instance, that understanding natural language relies on a sufficient appreciation of the speaker’s intent of purpose. Meaning in language cannot be fully derived from the symbolic context of the text to be interpreted, but must depend on a preunderstanding of the speaker’s purpose – no matter how tentative or incomplete.

The issue of purposive (goal seeking) behavior is also problematic. Some have argued that the goal directedness of an individual can be determined by the results which his actions bring about. This argument, however, is also fallacious. The purpose of an action is indeed the goal to which it is directed, but this cannot be identified with the results it brings about because this would involve two assumptions which are not necessarily true:

(1) The individual believed - correctly - that his movements would have that result;

(2) The individual consciously desired that the result should come about.

The difficulties involved here are self evident.

So it is clear that the issue of attaching consequences – particularly human consequences – to a proposed technological intervention such as DSS is a very tricky business. Consequences embody, in some way or another, the changing of human minds. And as social scientists know and has been described above, its measurement is fraught with problems.

## 3.2. The Issue of Perceiving Actual Consequences

Another problem associated with a consequentialist perspective is that of perceiving actual consequences. That is, consequences have to be perceived by someone, and there is no guarantee that one person will perceive the same consequences as someone else. Which consequences are actually perceived depends on an individual's experiences, environment, learned beliefs, desires, frame of reference, political ideology, or in short; on a person's total 'world view'. From this it is obvious that the perception of actual consequences is not independent of the interests, goals and values which guide human behavior:

"What a person wants and likes influences what he sees; what he sees influences what he wants and likes." (March and Simon 1958, p. 151)

In practice, the perception issue is ‘resolved’ by the choice of a measurement method, but the question of just what criteria should guide the choice of a method which is ‘appropriate’ for systems developed is rarely asked. Whatever tools and techniques are applied condition the perception of the subset of consequences that actually enter the analysts’ focus of awareness.

## 3.3. A Pluralist Conception of Inquiry

It can be noted that the consequentialist position advocated here is largely based on a subjectivist/interpretive epistemology. But, according to Burrell and Morgan (1979), there is a need to consider a second dimension; viz., the nature of behavior. The basic choice available in Table 2 is between the ‘integrationist’ paradigm and the paradigm of conflict.

Whatever paradigm is chosen as a conceptual basis for reasoning about social consequences should be flexible enough to absorb valid insights from its competitors. This we shall term a pluralist conception of inquiry. The critical ‘integrationist’ welcomes both the need for regulative order and the need for change. He also recognizes that change implies conflict and resistance.

"Allowing for various but limited degrees of order and disorder, consensus and dissensus, social integration and disintegration, solidarity and conflict, need satisfaction and frustration, the overall endeavor is to provide an explanation of why the social fabric of society tends to hold together." (Burrell and Morgan 1979, p. 107)

The danger with the integrationist emphasis on understanding ‘the regulated nature of human affairs’ is that it could degenerate into an ideology. It can then be misused for merely rationalizing the status quo as the only one permitted by ‘an order and coherence similar to that found in the natural world’. (Burrell and Morgan 1979, p. 107). However, by fixing our minds firmly on this danger and choosing a conceptual base which can be given dialectically opposed interpretations (c.f., the Hegelian inquiry system in Churchman 1971) we hope to escape the danger. Nevertheless, it remains to be shown that the integrationist paradigm is flexible enough to incorporate all the warranted criticisms of its opponents. Therefore the preference for the integrationist frame of reference at this point is no more than a contingent judgement regarding its usefulness as a heuristic and promising working hypothesis. If one looks at the pertinent literature it is obvious that the integrationist paradigm is the most developed in the study of organizations. It is therefore felt, that some version of integrationism provides the best chance to deal with the issue of selective perception and measurement of consequences in an organizational setting.

## 3.4. Conclusion

In summing up the implications of what has been said about consequence determination, the consequentialist perspective appreciates the difficulties and limitations of addressing the human and social consequences of any new technological intervention such as DSS. It does, however, take the position that some treatment – no matter how difficult – is better than no treatment at all. Moreover, the perspective advocates the best way in approaching the task of consequence determination is through the use of hermeneutic analysis while embracing a pluralist conception of inquiry.

## 4. Elements of a Consequentialist Framework of Analysis

In selecting the key elements or core concepts associated with DSS consequence determination, it is important to note the concern with making

IS/DSS responsive to individual and organizational needs. Yet, as many have pointed out repeatedly, there tends to be severe conflict between the needs of the mature personality and the needs of an efficient organization. Under the ‘radical’ interpretation this conflict turns into total submission of the needs of the individual; under the ‘integrationist’ view it defines a shifting balance.

In rejecting the radical view of the negative effects of social order, yet without denying the importance of this conflict, it follows that IS/DSS cannot be made responsive to human and organizational needs unless there is some consensus about trade-offs between different needs. It is therefore natural to suggest that IS/DSS development must be concerned with its potential of meeting different needs and its effects on consensus formation.

Needs relate to human interests through reason and will formation. The concept of human interests (in terms of both individual and collective interests) appears to be more promising than system requirements to capture the kind of concerns that are usually associated with user ‘needs’. Thus, it can be noted that there are – at this point – four major areas of concern regarding potential DSS impact: (1) individual interests; (2) collective (organizational) interests; (3) consensus formation; and (4) reason. rationality have the same basis and a similar connotation. Since Aristotle it has been held that rationality (commonly seen as a function of the human mind) is that which distinguishes human organized action from organized action in the animal kingdom (e.g., ant 'societies'). In analyzing the concept of rationality, Habermas (1981) suggests that any claims to rationality in human action imply that choices are based on knowledge – no matter how fallible. The only difference between rational and irrational action is that only the former can be defended by relating it to what is known to be true (in the case of factual knowledge), right (in the case of ethical choices) or correc' (in the case of pragmatic rules, i.e. rules of prudence and rules of skill). Irrational action is 'indefensible' in terms of available knowledge and prima facie IS/DSS are concerned with improving the rationality of human action. The more fundamental concept therefore appears to be knowledge; as reason can only be defined in terms of knowledge. Hence, knowledge, instead of reason, will be used as the fourth concept.

However, it is not only individual human action with which IS/DSS are concerned, but social action. The IS/DSS designer must come to grips with the problem of resistance and conflict. Resistance in social action is overcome by the use of power. There is a long-standing tradition debating the potential relationships between power and knowledge. By choosing power as a fifth concept this allows us to focus not only on the rational aspects of social will formation and execution (social action), but also on the psychic side-effects which tends to be beyond the control of the power wielder. Those subjected to power tend to react with feelings of alienation, resistance and in extreme cases, even hate.

From this preliminary discussion emerge 5 core concepts for a predictive framework of DSS consequence analysis: individual interests; collective interests; consensus formation; power; and knowledge.

## 4.1. Need for Broader Sensitization and the Equilibrium Concept

The presence of the connecting line between power and knowledge is to sensitize us to the potential existence of important relationships between these two concepts and hence to the need to inquire how the specific details of this relationship are affected by DSS development in every specific case. The concept of power is relevant from both a conflict and integrationist interpretation. For instance, the latter includes legitimate authority, the former suppression. This sort of analysis can be applied to all parts of Fig. 1. In principle each concept can be given an integrationist or conflict interpretation thereby sensitizing the analyst to a wide range of phenomena. Yet the basic tenet of dynamic equilibrium through continuing change is not given up.

One may look upon the various aspects associated with the five concepts at the corners of Fig. 1 as the parts of a mobile. In spite of their constant motion they are in a dynamic, delicate equilibrium. DSS interventions are one of many ‘disturbances’ which threaten to throw the weight centers and their interconnections off balance. Even though in a mobile it is not true that every part is physically directly connected to every other, it is still true that a disturbance applied to one part has repercussions and counter-repercussions on all the others. This analogy also shows how the consideration of functional dependencies give rise to the fundamental notion of social equilibrium or ‘balance’ – one of the most contested notions of ‘integrationist’ analysis. In this regard, Fig. 1 sensitizes us to the potential conflict between the individual (or ‘subjective’) interests and that of the community (or ‘objective’) interests.

## 4.2. Human Interests

It is taken for granted that ultimately IS/DSS must contribute to the satisfaction of needs. The concern of IS/DSS designers is to ask which needs are to be met and how IS/DSS may, indeed, affect human need satisfaction. Human interests are related to human needs (or desires) if one accepts that the perception of interests is strongly influenced by felt needs. This follows from the definition of the concept of interest proposed by Kant (1964):

"An interest is that in virtue of which reason becomes practical – that is, a cause determining the will. Hence only of a rational being do we say that he takes an interest in something: non-rational beings merely feel sensuous impulses." (p. 128)

While the word ‘cause’ in this quote needs elaboration (c.f., section 3 in this paper), the implications of the insight that human interests are to be distinguished from the ‘sensuous impulses’ of the animal kingdom are of importance to to IS/DSS design. Kant suggests that interests are guided by reason while ‘impulses’ are not. If there is no reason, then there is no interest, only subconscious compulsions (‘blind passion’) or mechanistically programmed instincts. If IS/DSS influence reason, they will thereby influence human interests and human action.

Kant's lesson that human interests can be ‘rationally’ influenced, because they are not beyond reason like animal impulses, implies that system goals are not simply given, but part and parcel of the design process. Kant teaches that the way to deal with conflicts of interest is ‘rationally’ – by examining the different reason behind the different interests. This, indeed, is a very optimistic view of the power of reason.

An important subissue in this context is the potential interest conflict between individual and collective interests. The less radical statement of this problem begins with the observation that at all times there has been a shifting balance between what is defined as the legitimate needs of the individual and those of the collective.

As IS/DSS intervene in this balance (DSS tipping it potentially more towards the individual), future research must fully address the Kantian question how to elicit and deal with the conflicting demands by 'reason'. For instance, we might begin by inquiring which human wills relate to which interests and how various forms of rational or 'distorted' will formation determine the legitimacy of different interests to be served. A general interest is one which is backed by an 'authentic' social consensus that the need related to the interest 'deserves' to be met. The opposite of a general interest is a purely 'subjective' or vested interest. DSS affect the elicitation of interests through the development process, in particular through the formalization of requirements. They can also affect both will formation and the realization of interests through changing the means available for 'rational' reasoning. DSS may affect decision makers' language, and one also might expect it to affect their perception of various interests including their own. Clearly, reasoning and knowledge are intimately related. Therefore, we are also led to ask how DSS affect human knowledge and how human will can be the cause to transform theoretical knowledge into practical knowledge. Practical knowledge is knowledge which can guide human action – for which much of the DSS literature prefers the label information. The need for broadening this to include ‘tacit’ knowledge is also important (see below).

The more fundamental question is whether IS/DS development contributes to social conditions that are ‘good’ or ‘bad’. Fromm (1955) has called this latter social condition ‘insane’ or the ‘pathology or normalcy’ in Western contemporary society (p. 15). Consistent with the above quote by Kant, Fromm draws a distinction between ‘instinctual needs’ and those which are customarily assumed to be uniquely human (p. 32), such as for instance, the Platonian quadruple of justice, wisdom social harmony and strength. If IS/DSS do not contribute to these needs then they might reinforce certain ‘socially patterned defects’ (p. 22). Hence IS/DSS theory needs some debate among those who share the cultural pessimism of social critics like Freud (1953), Marcuse (1964) or Fromm (1955) and those who would be inclined to revitalize Kant’s optimism regarding the possibility for founding practical action on theoretical reason.

Whatever the eventual outcome of such a debate, Fig. 1 suggests that DSS development must be concerned with both the desires of the individual and systemic needs, so-called organizational goals. For instance, DSS development may affect careers and quality of working life aspects, user needs for peer support, need for achievement, need for challenge, etc. (c.f., Klein and Chiarelli 1983). It is fair to say that much of social theory centers on the potential conflict between the needs of the individual and the needs of larger social units: family, organizations, and society (c.f., Nisbet 1982). At the organizational level this sensitizes us to potential conflicts between individual and organizational goals on the one hand and organizational goals and societal goals on the other.

## 4.3. Knowledge and Human Interests

From its earliest time the IS literature in general and the DSS literature in particular has seen a very close relationship between improving information and improving decision making. Typically information is conceived as consisting of predefined outputs which are used to regulate human behavior to comply with given goals. The information output is the result of formally validated procedures applied to coded representations of facts (data). The validation is effected either by formal proof (algorithms) or empirical tests (heuristics). The objectivistic bias of this view is obvious: data (coded facts) are seen as purely descriptive, free from subjective or social distortions due to conflicting interests. Reality exists independent of the concepts used to describe it. Management has unique and consistent goals which can serve as axioms to validate the data processing procedures. Anything that cannot be formalized by appropriate rules of coding or rules of derivation is at worst a source of error and at best a source of confusion and hence not appropriate for a computer-based information system (c.f., the formalistic, descriptive, conceptual, objectivistic and consensus fallacies in Goldkuhl and Lyytinen 1982; see also Bjorn-Andersen and Eason 1980). It is suggested that DSS has much to gain from broadening this view to include the subjective aspects along with the objectivistic meaning of information.

## 4.3.1. From Objective Information to Personal Knowledge

In looking for a concept that would capture the important insight that much of what is known is known ‘intuitively’, we have discovered Polanyi’s (1962) treatment of ‘personal knowledge’. It suggests that knowledge is a much broader concept than information. Personal knowledge includes not only published scientific propositions, but ‘all that is coherently believed to be right and excellent by men within their culture’ (Polanyi, p. 375, emphasis omitted). Hence knowledge is that which is transmitted to us through language and culture. Much of this is ‘tacit’ and resides in what Polanyi calls the ‘ineffable domain’. It is this domain with which DSS must interface if they truly are to support human inquiry. Inquiry is any activity which creates or otherwise affects knowledge (Churchman 1971). Knowledge produces comprehension, understanding, insight and wisdom. When managers act in organizations, they draw on the personal knowledge available to them. Therefore DSS need to be viewed as vehicles of personal knowledge improvement.

The implications here are far reaching and do not seem to have been realized in the current DSS literature. One implication which will be made the cornerstone of what follows is that one must rethink the interface of DSS with human language and thought in that all other vehicles of knowledge improvement go through the medium of (natural) language. By this we do not mean the elusive natural language interface. In considering the relationship between speech and thought Polanyi distinguishes three cases:

“(1) The area where the tacit predominates to the extent that articulation is virtually impossible; we may call this the ineffable domain.

(2) The area where the tacit component is the information conveyed by easily intelligible speech, so that the tacit is co-extensive with the text of which it carries the meaning.

(3) The area in which the tacit and the formal fall apart, since the speaker does not know, or quite know, what he is talking about.

There are two extremely different cases of this, namely

(3a) an ineptitude of speech owing to which articulation encumbers the tacit work of thought;

(3b) symbolic operations that outrun our understanding and thus anticipate novel modes of thought."

(Polanyi 1962, p. 87, all emphasis omitted).

Reinterpreting the domain of DSS along these lines would seem to suggest that what traditionally has been called ‘unstructured’ or ‘wicked’ problems relates to (1) and the semi-structured environment is an instance of (3b); (3a) is of lesser interest here, because it deals with pathological cases (e.g., speech impairment through brain damage).

There is only room to briefly point out one further implication of the hypothesis that DSS really attempt to deal with parts of the ineffable domain of personal knowledge. If this is true then the effect of DSS must be seen in relation to the reasons why people are interested in knowledge. Understanding the human knowledge interests is important for the DSS designer, because without interest in knowledge there is no genuine motivation to use new means of inquiry provided by technology and without motivation to use DSS, DSS will become unsuccessful.

## 4.3.2. Human (Personal) Knowledge Interests

Habermas (1971) distinguishes between three cognitive interests or interests which guide human inquiry (McCarthy 1982, p. 53). It can be noted that all of these interests draw on the ineffable domain, although to differing degrees. The thrust of the orthodox literature on DSS is concerned with the human knowledge interest in improving technical control. The word control here is similar in meaning to that in process control. The ineffable domain is involved in realizing this interest through its tacit knowledge of rules, in particular rules of skill, rules of prudence and categorical rules which determine the ethical acceptability of practical action (c.f., Kant 1964). Not all knowledge related to these rules is consciously known. This is sometimes expressed by pointing to the difference between ‘knowing that’ and ‘knowing how’ (c.f., Ryle 1949, p. 31).

DSS development or use also affects the realization of the other two knowledge interests. From the perspective of the individual, the practical-hermeneutic knowledge interest is concerned with improving the understanding of the social and physical world. This includes improved self-reflection (the Socratic 'know-thyself'), as well as a better understanding of others and the understanding of shared social and cultural bonds. The humanities have been traditionally concerned with advancing this knowledge interest, but one does not have to search very far to see that this interest is also advanced through meaningful social discourse at work. The analyst/user relationship during DSS development would come under this heading – if conducted properly. The knowledge related to the hermeneutic interest need not necessarily be applied in goal-oriented action. But the two are not completely separate. This is obvious from considering what Polanyi (1962, p. 136) calls intrinsic interest. For instance, even people who were not astronomers or physicists were interested in learning something about the theories of Copernicus and Einstein because it shed new light upon the world around them. Yet the knowledge necessary to improve one's knowledge of cosmology may turn out to be useful for navigating the oceans. The same is true about knowledge that relates to one's place at work. By fragmenting access to knowledge and reducing the transmission of meaning to those aspects which can be formalized, IS/DSS can interfere with (or enhance) the 'enlightening' (hermeneutic–interpretative aspects) of knowledge, reducing its intrinsic value and thereby the motivation of inquiry. By paying attention to the relevant aspects DSS can contribute to enriching rather than impoverishing the interpretive interest. But it must be kept in mind that the realization of this interest intimately depends on the use of human language to facilitate 'mutual understanding' in the conduct of life. Hence the replacement of human interaction by machine interaction always poses a threat to the realization of this interest.

By opening (or foreclosing) interesting roads of inquiry and chances for the free exchange of knowledge DSS also affect the ‘emancipatory knowledge interest’. It is related to the human desire for emancipation or personal growth; i.e., the contribution which knowledge can make to ‘free’ people “from seemingly ‘natural’ constraint” (McCarthy 1982, p. 58). The appropriate historical example is the Age of Enlightenment which is said to have emancipated mankind from the domination of superstition and religious dogma. More generally, knowledge is emancipatory if it helps to overcome internal or external ‘compulsions’. Internal compulsions result from lack of insight into personability forces which are partly of a neurotic and partly of a conventional nature: the result of ‘trained incapacities’ which nudge people into submission by preventing them from questioning the seemingly rational basis of conventions or certain personal authorities. If analyst/user interaction is authentic, through sharing their knowledge about the ‘distortions’ of organizational action introduced by unwarranted uses of power, then this can contribute to the mutual emancipation. Fromm’s (1955) example of the concerned teacher who helps the student to overcome his limitations applies with slight modification: each can alternate in the role of teacher and student. In everyday language one might say knowledge is emancipatory if it helps to overcome inhibitions and ‘hang-ups’. This is part of what women complain about when they speak of ‘male domination’. In the jargon of the professional it is expressed by ‘hostage’ taking: it relates to the analyst who perceives and ‘exploits’ the personality limitations of the user. If DSS are to be emancipatory, these dangers must be avoided. In addition there is the issue of reducing ‘external compulsions’ through IS/DSS. This relates to domination by social influence and is addressed more specifically under the heading of power.

## 4.4. DSS and the Dynamics of Social Action

Decision support system are inevitably part of organized social action as their users are embedded in a co-operative social system. Therefore, no analysis of potential effects of DSS or other types of IS can be complete without considering at least some of the effects on human power and social influence. Yet to focus on power alone would be too one-sided. Power is never the only force that 'binds' people into organized action, not even under the most severe circumstances such as in prison or forced labor. The contrast of business organization with these extreme cases where physical coercion dominates, makes apparent the great importance of voluntary action. If people are self-motivated and in agreement about what needs to be done, then the role of power is minimal (though not totally absent). Hence we propose to balance the analysis of power with an analysis of the conditions which lead to supportive relationships under the heading of consensus formation.

## 4.5. DSS and Power

In reflecting upon the difficulties of defining power in any universal way, it seems useful to follow the lead of Etzioni (1968) where he suggests that power is needed to overcome human resistance. If there is no resistance, then there is no need to use power. There are a number of possibilities which exist for organizational actors to remove resistance or 'overcome' resistance if it cannot be removed. We shall speak of power only when means are used to overcome resistance. If resistance is removed by forms of social influence which do not involve overcoming resistance by, for example, non-manipulative persuasion (convincing), then other terms like building support or consensus are used. However, care must be taken to see that persuasion is truly voluntary and not due to exploiting a differential in expert knowledge or other forms of status. The line between using knowledge to convince fairly and manipulatively to 'sway opinion' (like an aggressive salesman) is, indeed, a fine one as will be shown in more detail.

## 4.5.1. Power-Related Effects of DSS

The first and unavoidable effect of all IS including DSS is that they are visible symbols of power.

He who has control over computer resources visibly demonstrates his influence over how money was allocated and hence his importance regardless of his intentions to use this as a symbol of authority (c.f., Markus 1981, p. 210). Hence DSS may be sought as mere status symbols and may be used to manipulate opinion ('my computer analysis has shown that ...').

Another immediate effect of DSS is that they can make the use of power more effective by opening up new avenues of exercising power. To the extent that DSS embody assumptions and premises of managerial ideology which relate to the interests of social control, their effect must be to facilitate hidden persuasion and appeals to values. The effect is hidden because of the ‘loading’ of the DSS development process with assumptions and taking care – by use of power – that these will not be questioned. Markus (1981) found evidence for this effect in her 3PA (Production Planning and Profit Analysis) case.

"Implied in the adage ‘information is power’ is the ability of individuals to influence the outcome of decision processes if they control access to information used to define decision criteria or to evaluate alternatives against these criteria." (Markus 1981, p. 209)

Yet another way in which DSS may affect the prevailing power distribution relates to their use in removing alternative sources of information, as is the case in airline reservation systems. They can be used to provide control over the environment so as to 'channel' the behavior of others, for example, by depriving secretaries of all manual means of filing thereby forcing the use of electronic filling systems.

What is sometimes overlooked is that such measures impinge on human freedom. They cause not only resentment, even alienation, but also may easily reduce the variety and quality of information available. The reason for this is in the negative attitudes that may be produced in the wake of such measures. Nevertheless, by controlling access to information one can control the behavior of people. An extension of this is that new types of evaluation criteria can be introduced by collecting new types of performance data which were previously unavailable (c.f., Markus 1981, p. 210). This produces not only power effects, but also affects the existing bases of consensus formation (see below).

DSS in theory, seem to avoid this because the approach builds on the user's voluntary cooperation. But this needs to be carefully evaluated. Argyris (1971) noted a number of dysfunctional consequences primarily derived from the growth of a new type of rationality which is an inherent characteristic of increasing computerization, be it DSS or otherwise. The consequences identified are concerned with reduction of the space of free movement, psychological failure and double bind, leadership based more on rationalistic authority (competence) than formal (reward) power or personal charisma. Argyris arrived at the pessimistic conclusion that:

'man will not tend to use MIS effectively because of the norms of the existing world and the way he has programmed himself to be more incompetent than competent in dealing with people.' (Argyris 1971, p. 319)

DSS, it might be argued, are more voluntary in nature than MIS and might escape the above problems. Depending on the organizational circumstances surrounding DSS, they might incorporate genuine forms of voluntary use, perhaps through the democratic decentralization of IS/DSS implementation accompanied by measures which insure that DSS will neither reduce the effectiveness of mechanisms for organizational consensus formation nor reduce the motivation for sharing and exchanging information among different parties.

If DSS can be made to contribute to the improvement of organizational consensus formation, this may reduce resistance and thereby reduce the need for relying on power. With more consensus it may also be possible to shift from more intense to less intense forms of power, for instance, by replacing reward power by subtle persuasion. The latter is much less ‘intrusive’ (c.f., Hardy 1981) than the former; which in turn may improve knowledge by stimulating informed and distortion free debate. This is based on the assumption that the less power or alienating forms of power are used, the better are the chances to implement rational forms of reasoning within organizational settings.

## 4.6. DSS and Consensus Formation

Consensus can be looked upon as the opposite of conflict (see Table 2). Existence of consensus implies congruence of perceived interests and the absence of principled resistance (there may still be resistance to action due to inertia). Consensus is facilitated through consistency of shared beliefs among a set of actors. It should be noted, however, that consensus is not synonymous with cooperation – the coordination of organized action. Consensus may exist without action and even if action occurs, consensus alone does not guarantee it will be coordinated. Usually some power is needed to prompt action, although it may only be a recognized position of informal status or someone grasping the initiative. On the other hand, coordinated action can be achieved without consensus through the use of power, although it is likely to be less effective than consensual cooperation.

## 4.6.1. Authentic vs Inauthentic Consensus

From a conflict perspective, consensus is dangerous. First, consensus may create the illusion of having a secure hold on the situation when in fact it is little more than ‘group think’ or blind trust in conventions. Rather, conflict and rejection of the status quo are the engines of progress (c.f., Popper 1968 and Marcuse 1964). Secondly, consensus, particularly in the form of shared traditions and values, can conceal domination:

"Where do the shared values come from? Are they acquired autonomously or imposed on some members of society by others? This question identifies the possibility that consensus may be the product of the use of some form of coercive force." (Burrell and Morgan 1979, p. 14)

As was already noted, through the appeal to consensus, DSS designs can be ‘loaded’ with vested interests and other ‘distorting’ influences which can inhibit much needed reforms. This criticism can be met by distinguishing between authentic and inauthentic consensus.

In brief, authentic consensus is based on the widest possible debate among informed participants of equal status. Care must be taken that this debate is not distorted by power relations. Unnecessary forms of powers should be eliminated and the influence of legitimate forms of power ('authority') must be minimized. DSS can contribute to this through decentralization. Debate which proceeds in an ideal social arena of well-informed equals, is termed a 'rational discourse' (c.f., Habermas 1973). The problems which stand in the way of rational discourse, should be addressed as part of a DSS implementation strategy. If this succeeds, DSS can contribute to both more authentic consensus formation through emancipation and to more effective consensus, because there is reason to believe that rational discourse improves the validity of knowledge (c.f., 'reality testing' effects and 'personal knowledge context' effects of DSS in Klein and Hirschheim 1982).

However, the problems with implementing authentic consensus are great. It is therefore worthwhile to investigate the likely effects of DSS development when consensus formation is inauthentic.

The usual skew of the power distribution due to organizational hierarchy is likely to be increased by DSS, because those with more power have a better chance of acquiring DSS first. Hence, one must be careful in interpreting what is meant by “decentralization”. DSS may simply increase organizational feudalism at the expense of a single center of authority. This is something entirely different from democratic decentralization of decision making which implies a standard of fairness for all. In addition, those who by background or inclination are more skillful computer users will be at an advantage. One of the likely consequences, therefore, is that the gap between the ‘haves’ and ‘have-nots’ and the ‘knowledge rich’ and ‘knowledge poor’ will widen.

"Finally the sophisticated MIS will require of managers a different level of intellectual and conceptual competence." (Argyris 1971,

p. 318)

DSS will simply reinforce this MIS effect in that they carry the process of rationalization one step further.

## 4.6.2. Forms of Consensus Formation

It is apparent that there is a need to pay more attention to the organizational mechanisms that facilitate consensus formation and how DSS may affect them. Only by looking at the various forms of consensus formation and asking in each case if DSS contributes to authentic or inauthentic consensus can progress be made towards the ideal of rational discourse.

Figure 2 is presented as a means of analyzing potential effects of DSS on organizational consensus. It focuses on the processes of consensus formation. In interpreting the table it is important to note that consensus is a relational concept; it exists (as a state) with regard to something – the object or denotation of consensus. Furthermore, consensus tends to have ‘a basis’ or ‘foundation’ upon which it ‘rests’ or is built. If this basis is taken away, consensus may ‘erode’. For example, the consensus that management has the right to change work procedures without union consultation, in a particular organization may be contingent upon the basis that certain unwritten customs remain unaffected (e.g., to take unscheduled overtime or swap overtime against extra days off). If new procedures due to IS/DSS development affect such bases, resistance may be provoked. This resistance is not due to IS/DSS as such, but to the destruction of some existing basis of consensus facilitating change in general.

In translating these concepts to the level of organizational action one must note that organizational consensus partly draws its basis from societal consensus and partly has bases of its own. An example for the former is the historical consensus that married males are to be given priority in job opportunities or the life long tenure of employees in Japanese firms. DSS in general will influence many of these. Whether DSS will strengthen or weaken the foundations of organizational consensus depends very much on the particulars of a specific case, and thus needs to be carefully considered.

## 4.6.3. Consensus-Related Effects of DSS

In turning to the processes of consensus formation as summarized in Fig. 2 one notes that DSS development introduces a new type and quality of interaction among analysts and users. Hence this should increase the opportunities for bottom-up and interwoven forms of consensus formation – albeit only among those involved. DSS may provide a first-time vehicle for drawing certain people into forming consensus. These new opportunities and new objects of consensus formation may be part of the reason why they have become so popular.

On the other hand, to the extent that DSS replace human interaction after they are implemented, the opposite effect may occur (c.f., the above analysis of the fragmentation of organizational knowledge). Hence one might be led to predict at first an improvement followed by a slow long-term deterioration of consensus formation. A remedy for this latter condition is to look for means to increase social interaction; for instance, through distributed audio and video conferencing systems, sharing of data and results, providing electronic mail and grievance boxes, and open agendas.

Interestingly enough, this takes us again back to the rational discourse concept of DSS: free and easy access to the 'arena' of debate and the ability to open up new agendas as new issues emerge are two additional prerequisites for implementing an 'ideal speech situation' (c.f., Habermas 1974; and

Apel 1973). An ideal speech situation is characterized by 'arrangements which motivate and facilitate the initiation of informed debate among all concerned. It is aimed at creating social conditions in which the force of the better argument can prevail, unimpeded by internal (psychological) or external 'compulsions'. Hence to the extent that DSS and other technology can contribute to removing barriers to rational discourse such as limited access, ignorance (through training), cost of communication, emotional inhibition (e.g., by allowing anonymous or written submissions by participants less able to express themselves verbally, under time pressure, etc.), one would expect substantial improvements in consensus formation. Better consensus formation will dampen many of the potential negative effects diagnosed earlier.

## 5. Conclusions

Clearly these are ideas which go beyond the current action agenda of DSS and therefore need to be taken up again in greater detail. Interestingly enough, however, they lead us to conclude that DSS can only unfold their potential benefits if they are somehow tied into an information network linking all organizational influence centers. This runs counter to the early notion of DSS (e.g., Keen and Scott Morton 1978), which saw them more as personalized, stand-alone systems. History seems to have repeated itself: from Blumenthals' (1969) islands of automation we have gone to integrated MIS then on to personalized DSS. Now we are led to argue for moving from decentralized stand-alone DSS to integrated personal and organizational knowledge support systems. If this approach is followed, and any tendency to bureaucratic overcentralization and rationalism counter-acted by a heavy dose of authentic participation in the planning and implementation process of the overall design, then DSS appear to have a bright future.

The challenge, it would appear to us, is to steer DSS development through the proverbial mine field of power and knowledge imbalances, collective vs individual interests incongruencies, and consensus formation difficulties. The lesson to be learned is that DSS growth and development may be hazardous to organizational health.
