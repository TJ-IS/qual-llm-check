# Flexible Management of Computational Models

Levent ORMAN

Cornell University, Johnson Graduate School of Managemen., Ithaca, NY 14853, USA

Computational models consistute a major component of decision support systems, and their effective management is essential to the success of these systems. Many of the concepts that proved useful in data management are shown to be valid also for the management of the computational models. In particular, computational non-redundancy is defined and established as a major goal for the computational model management, analogous to non-redundancy in database management. Many subtle redundancies present in current decision support systems are demonstrated to cause considerable damage to the effectiveness of these systems. A functional architecture is described to detect and eliminate all computational redundancies.

Keywords: Decision Support Systems, Computational Models, Model Management, Non-redundancy, Design Architecture.

## 1. Introduction

Information is an unusual resource whose consumption does not diminish its available quantity. Since the production costs are considerable including the costs of collection, storage, retrieval and processing, there are tremendous advantages to sharing information. Potentially, sharing a piece of information among n applications could reduce the cost per application n-fold without effecting the quantity or the quality of the information received by each. In practice however, designing information systems that facilitate sharing proved to be nontrivial, stimulated a great deal of research, and fueled the expansion of the field into a major field of study [9].

The major difficulties in sharing information result from the elusiveness of the concept of information as compared with other resources, and the lack of understanding of its structure and its components. To share a piece of information, multiple applications must recognize it as relevant and useful. Unfortunately, the information needs of two applications are rarely, if ever, exactly the same, although they may have similarities and contain common components. These common components can be shared if detected and properly identified, but the identification task is far from trivial. It requires extensive analysis and a thorough understanding of the structure and the elementary components of information. The failure to detect the common components in the information requirements of applications usually leads to duplication and separate maintenance of those components by each of the applications that need them. Obviously, failure to share leads to redundancy, and the discovery of the common components in the information requirements of applications is essential to facilitate sharing and to eliminate redundancy.

## 2. Data, Computations and Description

Data and computations are the two major components of information. Most information systems, in their effort to manage the information resource of an organization, contain a number of other components such as requirements, queries, triggers and reports; and in particular they all contain an extensive description of their own contents in one form or another. The description is essential to interpret and correctly use the information provided, and when the system does not provide a complete description, the users are expected to acquire it from other sources. A quick historical survey demonstrates an increasing emphasis on information description along with the increasing recognition of the need to share information.

Early information systems, usually referred to as ‘data processing systems’, were application specific and provided minimal description of their contents. Typical examples are payroll, billing and inventory control systems. Since each system was dedicated to a particular application, the description was geared towards the specialist in that application and was not very extensive. It was usually provided in the form of printed manuals of procedures and standards, and the users were expected to have considerable insight both into the application and the innerworkings of the system that serves the application. Sharing among these systems was also minimal.

The second generation information systems, usually referred to as ‘management information systems’, have recognized the advantages of sharing data, since it was observed that many applications within an organization were using the same operational data, and the duplication of effort to collect, store and retrieve those data was significant. As the number of applications increased to serve all levels of management by reprocessing the same operational data, the amount of duplication also increased and became intolerable. Moreover, the duplication was often less than perfect leading to inter-application inconsistencies. A single centrally controlled database serving all applications of an organization was proposed as a solution, and it is now close to being an industry standard [1]. The major concern with the large, shared databases is the difficulty of describing data objectively, independent of individual applications, to facilitate use by a multitude of diverse applications. Data models and data dictionaries have been used extensively to describe data; and data semantics, the study of the meaning of data independent of the individual applications that use them, became a field of study in its own right [30].

The third generation information systems usually referred to as 'decision support systems' [8,16], recognized the value of sharing computations since it was observed that many applications involved similar computations, with minor differences introduced in personalizing the applications to aid a particular user; and the duplication of effort to design, implement and test these computations was significant. As the number of applications increased to provide the personalized on-line support these systems envisioned, the amount of computational duplication also increased and became intolerable very quickly, since the personalized on-line support required many personalized versions of the same basic application, and each version had to be modified many times in its lifetime to keep abreast with the changes in the user's abilities, attitudes, knowledge and beliefs

[23]. Moreover, the duplication was often less than perfect, leading to inter-application inconsistency and less than efficient computations. A single centrally controlled computational model base serving all applications of an organization was proposed as a solution [10,17,31], but its success has been limited. There are two major obstacles to the success of computational model bases. The first is the lack of a precise definition of computational redundancy which prohibits the identification of all redundant components. The second is the difficulty of describing computations objectively, independent of applications, to facilitate use by a variety of diverse applications. Many attempts were made to overcome these two problems. Higher levels of modularity achieved by standardizing interfaces have improved the ability to detect redundancies [25,32]. Database management systems have been used to control and manage the module interfaces [28]. Systems analysis tools and specification languages have attempted to describe computations in a manner that is appropriate for a variety of users ranging from end users to systems developers [12,13,20, 26,29]. Some system description has been automated and incorporated into the software by using user-system dialogs, parameterized algorithms and menu-style interfaces [11]. None of these techniques have achieved what the data models and database management systems achieved with data, that is, the complete non-redundancy and sharing where existing applications share all components with other applications that may need them, and new applications can utilize all existing components, having to add only those components that are not available in any of the existing applications.

Example 1. Given two simple applications where one needs the total sales value and the second needs the average sales value for the same time period. A first generation information system would typically create two independent systems each with its own data files; a second generation system would recognize the sales data as a common component and create two independent algorithms served by the shared sales data; and a third generation system would recognize that the computation of total sales is also needed in computing the average sales, and hence use the same algorithm to compute totals in both applications. The problem of recognition is immediately obvious from this simple example since the second application may not view the computation of totals as an independent component of its algorithm, and may not be able to use the available algorithm even if an adequate description is available, accessible and intelligable to it.

## 3. Computational Redundancy

A precise definition of computational redundancy is essential to formalizing the intuitive concepts introduced in section 2. The definition of redundancy is crucially dependent on the choice of information unit. Once a unit is selected, redundancy is merely the duplication of the same unit within the system. The critical decision in unit selection involves the size of the unit. The choice of too small a unit will result in highly interdependent units, and will increase the overhead and the maintenance cost; and the choice of too large a unit will fail to detect many sub-unit redundancies. The data models also had to struggle with this compromise. Clearly, if the elementary data-item is chosen as a unit, non-redundancy requires that no data-item be repeated within a system. No existing data model provides non-redundancy at this level. The popular data models [30] employ associations among two or more data-items as units (i.e., records or n-tuples), and seek to eliminate the duplication of those associations [3,24]. The earlier data processing systems used much larger units, typically applications, and individual associations that were common to two distinct applications were not considered redundant.

The early attempts in decision support systems concentrated on algorithms as computational units [6,10,17]. The algorithms have been described in terms of their input-output characteristics to facilitate sharing, and to meet the need to combine them into multi-algorithmic computations [4,5,28]. Input-output characteristics are extremely useful in capturing the semantics of computations. They manage to reduce computational semantics to much simpler data semantics since input and output are data. However, even assuming the existence of an effective semantic data model to capture the complete semantics of data, input-output characteristics are not sufficient to completely describe computations. There may exist in an information system many alternative algorithms to provide the same input-output relationship, each applying under different organizational and environmental conditions, and each providing a different level of reliability, sensitivity and precision. A complete computational description has to contain all of these components [7]. Otherwise, two algorithms that appear redundant in terms of their input-output characteristics may differ in their assumptions, reliability, sensitivity or efficiency; and consequently the algorithms that appear redundant can not simply be eliminated on that basis.

Example 2. Complete description of algorithms is extremely rare in current decision support systems. For example, there are no commercial statistical software packages that can be effectively used without taking a statistics course to learn about the standard algorithms, their assumptions and pitfalls, their reliability and precision. An obvious consequence of this is the proliferation of statistics courses offered to business executives and other end-users who have no intention of becoming statisticians.

Even with complete descriptions, the algorithm is too large a unit of computation to eliminate a significant portion of all redundancies, since it can not detect similarities among algorithms. No matter how similar they are, if two algorithms are not identical, they are not redundant.

Example 3. Two algorithms TOTAL and AVERAGE are given to serve the two applications of example 1. TOTAL and AVERAGE are not identical, and the fact that TOTAL is common to both algorithms can not be detected without breaking AVERAGE into its components TOTAL, COUNT and DIVIDE.

In decision support systems, where the same basic algorithm may have to be personalized many times for a multitude of users with minor differences in taste, or may have to be modified frequently to adopt to the changing style or beliefs of the decision maker, the inability to detect similarities among algorithms is not acceptable. Parameterization and menus to select from a variety of similar algorithms is not sufficient since they require full prior knowledge of all the variants of the algorithm. They may be effective in eliminating redundancy in a given collection of algorithms by combining them into a single non-redundant super-algorithm, but they are hopelessly ineffective in incorporating new variants and modifications into their fixed structure. In other words, their ability to learn and evolve is extremely restricted. The only solution to the dilemma appears to be a smaller unit of computation to provide more flexibility.

## 4. Flexible Management

Smaller units of computation in conjunction with more effective description tools appear to be the only avenue leading to the elimination of large scale redundancy. Each unit would ideally be stored only once, and it would be available for use by a variety of applications. Algorithms that are slight variations of each other would be built by modifying only a minimal number of units. This flexibility is crucial in a shared environment where algorithms are personalized for a variety of users by making minor changes, or in a dynamic environment where the algorithms constantly evolve to accommodate changing requirements. These conditions are typical of the environment of the modern decision support systems.

Although the choice of smaller computational units seems to be obviously desirable, the multipurpose use of these units by a variety of applications would require more objective and more complete description of them to prevent misuse and to avoid extensive user training. The requirement of effective and objective description is a serious obstacle to using minimal computational units. Minimal components such as the individual commands, keywords or statements of procedural programming languages may not be appropriate as computational units if their effect on the system is not easily describable independent of the state of the system. Obviously, the tools available for description seriously restrict the choice of units. By far the most common method of describing computations is to use their input-output characteristics, possibly reinforced by additional predicates to capture their assumptions, reliability, sensitivity and efficiency [7]. This approach reduces the description of computations to much simpler data description since input and output are data, and avoids the more complex procedural semantics [15]. However, although the input-output characteristics are extremely useful in describing algorithms, they break down with smaller components. The failure of input-output characteristics to capture the essential semantics of computations results from the existence of side effects that change the values of global variables and effect the result of other computations in unpredictable ways. Obviously, if computations can effect each other's results by means other than their input and output, the input-output characteristics are not sufficient to describe them. The repetitive changes in the values of global variables is certainly an efficient method to machine execute many computations, but it is not at all useful in describing the purpose or the eventual outcome of those computations. Fortunately, it has been demonstrated that it is possible to express all computations using functional languages with no side effects [2,14,18]. These languages may not be the most appropriate languages for programming today's computers, but their side effect free architecture makes them perfect candidates for identifying and describing small components of computations using input-output characteristics.

## 5. Functional Architecture

A comprehensive functional architecture employs functions as the only building block. All components of information including data, computations, constraints, queries, triggers and reports are built up from primitive functions through functional composition. Even the description of functions are expressed in terms of functions.

A function is a named, set valued mapping from its arguments to its range. The arguments and range may be data sets or other functions. Data sets can also be viewed as functions without arguments leading to a uniformly functional architecture. Each function is described by a function name, and the names of its arguments and range. The complete description also includes a set of integrity constraints and possibly some associated computations.

The functional data models have established the feasibility of expressing all data in terms of functions [27]. The description of these data functions can also be expressed in terms of functions and included in the data model as a metamodel [21]. Three obvious functions of the metamodel map each data function (represented as a database entity) to its name, arguments and range respectively. Such functions mapping functions to functions are essential for a purely functional architecture. There are two other major functions in the metamodel mapping each function to its associated constraints and computations respectively. A data function has associated computations if it is not observed directly but has to be derived from other functions. Constraints and computations are not simple functions but functional expressions.

Functional expressions are used to describe procedures. All procedures are functions from their inputs to their outputs if their side effects are ignored. Functional programming languages have demonstrated the feasibility of programming without side effects by completely specifying procedures in terms of functions operating on lists [14]. Since lists are special functions mapping data sets to consecutive integers, all procedures can also be specified using functions operating on functions. The resulting sequences of functions are called 'expressions' [20]. Expressions can be described recursively within the metamodel by two functions mapping each expression (represented as a database entity) to a left-most function and its arguments which are themselves expressions.

Given expressions, all other components of information can be defined in terms of them using functions defined on expressions. Constraints are functions that map expressions to the set {TRUE, FALSE, i.e., predicates defined on expressions. They are used to capture the conditions that have to be tested to preserve the integrity of the system. Computations have three components: a function to be computed, and an expression that computes it, and a set of constraints that describe the conditions under which the computation is valid. Consequently, the computations can be described within the metamodel by three functions mapping computations (represented as database entities) to functions, expressions and constraints respectively.

Queries and triggers have similar structures. Queries are requests for information. They are used to isolate and retrieve subsets of the database. Each query is characterized by an expression to define the function to be retrieved, and a set of constraints to serve as the selection criteria. Triggers are mechanisms to activate certain procedures under certain conditions. Each trigger is characterized by an expression specifying the procedure, and a set of constraints defining the trigger conditions. Consequently, both queries and triggers are described within the metamodel by two functions mapping them to expressions and constraints respectively.

Reports are two dimensional structures used to facilitate input and output. The major objective is to distribute data items on a two dimensional medium in such a way that captures their relationships geometrically to provide a visual effect and to enhance understanding of their semantics. Reports merely map expressions to locations on a two dimensional medium. Consequently, they have three major components: locations, expressions and constraints. They assign locations to expressions under given conditions. Reports are described within the metamodel as collections of database entities corresponding to components of reports with three functions defined on them mapping components to locations, expressions and constraints respectively. Furthermore, recursion is allowed to create reports within reports for maximum flexibility. Recursion is an integral part of all components in a functional architecture including computations, queries and constraints to achieve a purely functional architecture.

The problem cast in this form requires the simultaneous solution of m equations (m = number of constraints) with n variables (including slack variables) when n - m variables are set to zero. A well known algorithm to solve the linear minimization problem is the SIMPLEX algorithm which avoids checking all extreme points but considers only the extreme points that improve upon the current solution [19]. Table 4 presents the functional specification of the SIMPLEX algorithm where a variable t is used to indicate the time period in the iterative process. The variables that are associated with nonzero values are called basic variables. There is a basic variable $BV(y)$ corresponding to each constraint y. Initially only the slack variables corresponding to each constraint are basic. Eventually, all variables with nonzero values in the optimum solution appear as basic variables. The values of the basic variables are maintained in function $C(y, t)$ which is initially set to $C(y)$ , i.e., the constant terms in the constraints. All nonbasic variables have the value zero. Marginal gain by increasing the value of a variable x by 1 at iteration t is $MG(x, t)$ , and it is given as the constant $A(x)$ , i.e., the coefficients in the objective function. The corresponding marginal cost is given by $MC(x, t)$ and it is computed as the inner product of the value vector $V(x)$ and the coefficients matrix $B(x, y)$ at time t. The opportunity cost $OC(x, t)$ associated with each variable x at time t is the difference between the marginal gain and the marginal cost. The algorithm terminates when all opportunity costs are nonpositive. At each step of the algorithm the variable EV with the highest opportunity cost is chosen to enter the solution. Its value is determined as the highest value attainable without violating the constraints, i.e., the next extreme point. All coefficients are revised accordingly forcing a variable (LV) to leave the solution to accommodate the new variable and the iterative process continues. ## 7. Conclusions

A functional architecture has been proposed to effectively manage the computational models of a decision support system along with its data. Computational non-redundancy has been defined and established as a major goal in managing computational models. Many subtle redundancies have been shown to exist in current decision support systems, and a strategy based on smaller computational units has been proposed to eliminate them. The functional architecture has been shown to provide smaller computational units and an effective methodology to objectively describe them.
