## 1. Decision Support: Progress, Opportunities and Unresolved Issues

The field of Decision Support is now well over ten years old. The first decade saw immense changes in the nature and costs of information technology. It also saw the diffusion of the use of computer-based tools across the entire office landscape. In 1976, Decision Support represented a radical concept of the use of information systems and analytic tools. Now, it is part of the mainstream.

This paper reviews where we stand in terms of both research and practice in the DSS field and highlights the agenda for the next decade. It focusses on four main questions:

(1) What is the ‘news’? Is DSS an idea whose time has passed, now that many of its principles have become commonplace aspects of end user computing?

(2) What is the definition of DSS - and does it matter that after over ten years of sustained research and practice there is still no established definition?

(3) What should be the balance between Decision, Support and System in terms of the knowledge base and focus of effort needed to best exploit the opportunities of DSS?

(4) What are the main issues for making as much or more progress in the second decade as in the first?

## 2. What is the News?

In 1976, the basic ideas of DSS were indeed news:

Here is a perspective on the use of computers and analytic methods that is very different from the traditional assumptions and practice of Data Processing and Management Science. It meshes human judgment and the power of computer technology in ways that can improve the effectiveness of decision makers, without intruding on their autonomy.

We have available a powerful new vehicle for helping decision makers – the computer terminal.

There are better ways of building small-scale systems than the traditional and cumbersome approach of Data Processing. We can deliver systems quickly, evolve them flexibly and make them adaptive and easy to use.

There are better ways of applying the analytic methods of management science than through the normative approach of optimization science, which ignores the need to mesh the decision making process with the needs, habits and preferences of the decision maker, and to respect rather than try to replace judgment.

Ten years later, all this is essentially stale news. The idea that flexible interactive tools can be helpful in nonroutine planning and decision making is a commonplace of management practice. Data Processing has the same tools available to it as has the DSS builder, who now needs to learn about many of the previously disdained skills of DP, such as large-scale data management, project management, test plans and quality assurance, all of which were relatively unimportant in building small model-based DSS.

The news ten years ago about the technology was that the terminal was the mechanism for accessing data, analytic tools and computer power, and that there was a new alternative to batch processing and centralized operations. At the time, technology was the bottleneck to progress, in terms of speed, cost, ease of use, reliability, etc.

The focus has shifted now from having to tame the technology and wait for new developments in hardware and software before many applications could become practical, to a situation where technology is no longer the constraining factor. We now see virtually no limits in hardware, software, communications and storage of information (including documents) for bringing computer power to managers, only limits of imagination and experience.

There are still some technical blockages, such as the lack of data base management software and hardware that can handle high-volumes of transactions cost-efficiently, and problems of incompatibility, standards and integration. Many of the most promising new tools will need several more iterations of development and application before they are ready for widescale use; examples are CD-ROM, non-von Neumann hardware, and many aspects of Artificial Intelligence developments. Here the issue is not if there will be progress, but how soon.

All in all, the message for DSS is clear: technology is not the bottleneck any more.

What is now the news for the next decade? The question is central, not peripheral, since the first and very distinctive promises about the application of technology to decision making have been realized. If the main innovations of DSS were and will be in the area of system development and use, the term is redundant. It makes little difference whether an ad hoc financial model is called ‘personal computing’, ‘end user computing’ or ‘financial modelling’. If the technical base for a DSS is a LISP machine and the application is to build an expert system for production planning, why not just call it an expert system?

For a period during the early 1980's, it looked very likely that there would be no need to retain the term DSS. Some commentators saw it as a subset of office automation, others equated it with end user computing and some just relabelled what they were doing in the area of data base management, management science or computer science and jumped on a bandwagon that had the advantage of having no consensual definition to limit entry and interpretation.

Data Processing signalled a change in its mission and identity by becoming Information Systems and came to terms with personal computers; it now uses the same technical base as DSS builders and increasingly manages the critical data resources that DSS need to draw on.

If there is no news for the next decade, we should honor the vital contribution of the missionaries of Decision Support and write its obituary.

There is news, of course and it implies a reinvigoration of DSS research and a much more aggressive – and risky – mission for practice:

We know how to build systems for managers and can substantially extend their scope from fairly passive tools, that merely answer ‘what-if’ questions and are based on a limited concept of ‘support’ in a narrow range of decision making tasks.

The next step is to provide more active modes of support, partly by drawing on tools for expert systems development, a new means that opens up new ends, but mainly by focussing on supporting decisions that really matter in the organization (Ref 1); that must be the target for DSS research.

DSS is concerned with intellectual as well as computer-related technologies. It is our interest in decision making rather than computers which makes Decision Support distinctive. We need to have a more ambitious view of decision making now that we can afford to pay less attention to the nuts-and-bolts of technology.

New technology opens up immense new opportunities for applying the principles of DSS application, development and use. Telecommunications, optical storage, the development languages of applied Artificial Intelligence and data management software shift the focus from mainly supporting individual decision processes to supporting organizational ones, from viewing 'data' as numbers to building document-based DSS, and from computers to telecommunications.

We have barely begun to exploit these opportunities.

## 3. What is the Definition of DSS?

Right from the start of the DSS movement, and even now, there has been no established definition of DSS. It is not at all unusual for attendees at conferences on the topic to say that they came to find out what DSS is, since people in their company or university are talking about it a lot, and at the end it is still unclear to them.

The definitions in use cover a broad spectrum of what is and is not a DSS. The extremes are roughly marked at one end by a focus on Decision Support and at the other on System:

The application of available and suitable computer-based technology to help improve the effectiveness of managerial decision making in semi-structured tasks (Keen and Scott Morton, Ref 2).

Interactive decision aids for managers (Most software vendors and many Information Systems researchers and practitioners).

The absence of an established definition has had many consequences for the field. One of these has been that the intuitive validity of the mission of DSS attracted individuals from a wide range of backgrounds who saw it as a way of extending the practical application of tools, methods and objectives they believed in. While the definitional problem means that it has been hard to say what is not a DSS, it has also not excluded any contribution or contributor who wanted to see theory turn into practice and technology into application. Basically, DSS is about helping people in organizations do their jobs better.

The more damaging consequences, though, must be acknowledged. They include a growing validity in discussions of DSS, a weakening of its intellectual base, a loss of credibility among competent researchers and professionals who hoped that here was a new source of ideas for their work, and casual use of an intendedly substantive concept by software vendors. At one point, it seemed as if every and any financial planning, personal computing, end user computing or office technology system that might be used by a manager or staff professional was a DSS.

The definitional problem must be solved. The dilemma is that it cannot be ended by trying yet again to produce ‘the’ definition. That has been unsuccessful after ten years of attempts. There has to be some narrowing of the boundaries to make research useful and interesting, to provide clear expectations and objectives for practice, and to ensure intellectual quality control. There has to be enough breadth to ensure a truly multidisciplinary perspective that meshes the managerial, technical, organizational and analytic contributions that – intuitively – explain why even the people who find the lack of a definition irritating so often carry on using the label.

The directions for resolving the issue seem to be:

Recognize the difference between definitions for understanding and definitions for actions. The definition for understanding is aimed at pushing the limits of the possible: extending the scope of DSS and its impact on organizational effectiveness. The definition for action works within the limits of the practical in terms of the target user market, the level of support provided, and the organizational mandate for planning and delivery of services. It defines the role and signals the identity of the DSS group.

Accept that there can be no definition of Decision Support System, only of Decision Support, because the technology that DSS draws on constantly changes, and there is no independent or idiosyncratic technical base for it. As new tools become available and suitable, new types of DSS will be built. The goal is Decision Support, the vehicle is a DSS.

Establish a vision for DSS research which builds on what has been learned about developing systems but that emphasizes what to develop and why. DSS has become too smug and timid. We need to refocus our attention and effort. The agenda for DSS research now and for practice later is to apply intellectual and computer-related technologies to amplify creativity and learning in decisions that really matter. That is a definition for understanding (see Elam et al, Ref 3).

## 3.1. Levels of Support

There is often a gulf between the researcher's and practitioner's view of DSS. In many instances, practitioners cannot in fact use the researcher's. Keen and Scott Morton's perspective highlights the need to think about decision making and how new technologies may increase what can be done as a result of that thinking. Most DSS groups in large firms are expected to justify their existence by delivering computer systems. They use this as the base for defining their role. They cannot even explain to managers what a DSS is except in terms of systems they can see and use.

They need to think through, though, whether they want to be - or must be - limited to this. The following scenario has become too common:

A small group of 'DSS' staff build up their credibility by being very responsive to managers' needs for ad hoc models and reports. They choose one or a small number of software tools that allow them to deliver results quickly. These may include a financial modelling package on a mainframe or personal computer, a flexible data base management system oriented to producing simple reports, and a graphics facility.

They 'cherrypick' – this is Arkush and Gulden's useful and accurate term for looking for quick wins. The cherries are the applications that are easy to handle and where managers are pleased with the outcome. They will by definition be small in scale and scope. For the many teams locked into cherrypicking, a large DSS is a contradiction in terms.

The team soon becomes indispensable and respected for its responsive service, which is very different from what managers have come to expect from a Data Processing unit that has huge development backlogs, tried for years to prevent the introduction of personal computers, and contains technocentric people who seem more interested in computers than the business's needs.

Unfortunately, the team never gets away from reacting to requests and from short-term time scales. As personal computers, information centers and workstations proliferate, cherrypicking is less impressive. By definition, there is no formal long-term strategy; fast, reactive service is the strategy. In addition, whatever its original aims, the group is seen as being the business of building – and maintaining – computer systems.

There are no career paths and no mandate for a solid organizational base. After a few years of building yet more DSS, the leader of the team leaves to do the same thing for another firm. The group is absorbed by Information Systems, which is now fired up to be responsive and to support personal computing and end user development, has good graphics facilities, and provides data base management systems that are oriented to producing simple reports.

Very, very few DSS groups have been able to build a mandate and create a firm organizational base. They drift towards supporting standard personal computing and end user development. They become experts in and often advocates for some particular software package.

It is easy to provide ‘support’ to managers. The question is what level of support? That is a central issue for definitions for action, both in research and practice.

There is a spectrum of support, from Passive to Normative. What happens in the above scenario is that DSS is pushed towards passive support, where the group provides tools with little active concern about how they are used and for what decisions. The equivalent trend in DSS research is a drift from being decision-focussed, with a strong interest in how to improve the effectiveness of decision-making, to a focus on software and how to encourage its use.

In both instances, the distinctive purpose of DSS becomes blurred and weakened. The reasons that it is essential for Decision Support research to build a new vision is that unless it aims at moving DSS towards a more active level of support, it has little to offer that Information Systems and end user computing cannot provide.

## 3.2.Passive Support

Passive support gives ‘managers’ – who are more often than not in reality managers’ planning and administrative staff – tools that they are comfortable with. There is no attempt to change their existing modes of operation. The systems designer or consultant does not bring a normative view of how the decision should be handled. There is no specific target market; the user is largely autonomous and will be the growing stratum of middle-level personnel for whom a computer workstation is becoming as common as a telephone.

Most DSS provide a somewhat more active type and level of support. The goal is still to give people tools they are comfortable with, although there is often the secondary aim of stimulating changes in the decision process. The DSS is the equivalent of a staff assistant that answers ‘what-if’ questions.

The decision domain to support is largely selected – or predetermined – by the suitability of the available software tools. This is why DSS relatively suddenly in the late 1970's lost much of its focus on decisions; with the profusion of financial planning software followed by the profusion of spreadsheet packages, the tools shaped the uses, instead of the reverse applying.

## 3.3. Traditional DSS Support

Traditional DSS provide a computerized staff assistant. The manager's judgment selects alternatives and assesses results. 'What-if?' became the cliche of the DSS field. The assumption was that the ability to generate and analyze more alternatives will improve the effectiveness of the decision process.

There is some practical, conceptual and commonsense evidence to support this assumption. It certainly opened up immense opportunities for using analytic methods. Traditional management science has had many problems in applying its techniques when they require a priori specification of goals, weights and objectives. Optimization science in general – the Normative Support described in the next section of this paper – rests on reducing or even eliminating judgment. It pushes managers towards economic rationality and systematizing of analysis.

There has been a huge gap between the logical idealism of optimization science and managers' willingness or ability to adopt it. Too many of the methods of Management Science were useful but not useable. DSS emphasized useability and by respecting the primacy of managerial judgment reduced the need to specify or even understand objectives and multicriteria trade-offs. This pragmatic attitude accomplished in a few years far more than Management Science had been able to stimulate in several decades.

But it downplayed the problem of quality of judgement. Looking at more alternatives is not a causal force for improving decision making. Why should one expect better decisions by allowing a stupid person to look at more bad options and draw wrong conclusions more quickly? That is obviously an exaggeration of the implications of the 'what-if' assumptions, but it does highlight the fact that traditional DSS has had a weak and informal concept of how to improve the decision process.

## 3.4. Normative Support

Management Science, economics, decision analysis and theoretical research in finance, marketing, production, etc., have had a very clear view of how to improve decision making. From the perspective of believers in the DSS approach, however defined, this has been unrealistic and impractical. From the perspective of those in the field who focus on passive support and view DSS in terms of software, it has been irrelevant – the DSS builders in fact may not know anything about this noncomputer and hence unneeded tradition.

DSS made progress by its pragmatic views on how to work with decision makers, and it built momentum by focussing on computer tools that were useable, rather than on useful but impractical analytic methods. That said, the goals of management science remain valid: to provide tools based on a normative view of how decisions should be made rather than how they are made and extend the bounds of rationality.

Herbert Simon contrasted ‘satisficing’, the search for decisions which are good enough and that do not require effort or involve strain on the decision maker, and optimization, the formal search for the best practical decision (ref 4). He asked ‘Who in his right mind would satisfy when he could just as easily optimize?’ The answer from a DSS perspective is ‘No one. But it is not just as easy. Optimization science is too often unrealistic, impractical, artificial and unused.’

The direction of effort implied by the goals and logic of Normative Support are surely relevant for DSS: to apply the very best of analytic reasoning to complex and important decision making. The argument that it is impractical at a given point in time is true but incomplete; theory has to lead application and practicality must emerge from experience and sustained study.

The emerging field of Expert Systems grew partly because leading researchers in Artificial Intelligence treated practical constrains on computing costs, speeds and efficiency as temporary. They built systems on large mainframe computers, even through this meant response times measured in minutes not seconds and they consumed resources that only Defense Department budgets could see as acceptable. It is because they did so that when low-cost, high performance micros became available there was anything to transfer to them.

DSS has increasingly lost its roots in Management Science and drifted towards the Passive support of end user computing. Some researchers have resisted this trend but the mainstream of DSS has largely accepted Traditional Support as its upper limit.

## 3.5. Extended Decision Support

There is an intermediate level of support between Traditional DSS and Normative that moves in the opposite direction from the historical drift back to the Passive end of the spectrum. It is this Extended Decision Support that the field of DSS research and practice should aim at.

Extended support involves an explicit effort to influence and guide decision making, while respecting the primacy of judgement and focussing very carefully indeed on how managers think, what aspects of their decision process they are likely to be willing to delegate, their expectations and attitudes about the use of analytic tools, etc., etc. Normative support on the whole focusses on how decision makers should behave, ignoring how they actually do. Passive and Traditional DSS support make the reverse trade-off.

Extended DSS has the following broad features:

It looks for areas where the proven skills of DSS builders can be applied in new, emergent or overlooked areas; it does not confine itself to cherry-picking and to decisions that are convenient to support because they are well-suited to the available software tools.

It makes an explicit effort to apply analytic models and methods; decision analysis, multicriteria decision making, and model management are obvious targets of opportunity (Ref 5). Extended DSS will embody a far more prescriptive view of how decisions can be made more effectively than has become the norm among DSS builders.

It exploits the emerging software tools and experience base of Artificial Intelligence to build semi-expert systems. The very idea of expert systems implies the reverse of Passive support.

It reemphasizes the special value of DSS practitioners as being their combination of expertise in understanding decision making and knowing how to take advantage of developments in computer-related fields. Extended support draws on intellectual technologies as well as information technology.

## 3.6. Definitions for Understanding and Action

One reason why DSS needs to distinguish between definitions for understanding and for action relates to the fact that many DSS builders will prefer or be constrained to Traditional support. The idea of Extended Support is to push towards a more active conception and operational capability for DSS; that requires definitions for understanding first and it may be some time before DSS practitioners in many instances can change their mandate. Extended DSS restores many of the early aims of the DSS movement; to have a sigsignificant impact on the quality of decision making and in effect to create a new form of decision consultant.

The substantial differences in focus of attention and even of identity between the Traditional/Passive and Extended/Normative directions of movement for DSS have some obvious practical consequences. The most immediate is simply that many DSS units have no charter to set themselves up as intrusive experts on how managers should be making decisions or which decisions they – the new experts – should be working on. The counter to the arguments presented in this paper is that, like it or not, the term DSS is firmly associated in large firms with computers. That is the reality and it is not practical to reposition DSS to highlight decision consulting.

If that is true, then DSS will continue to become a commodity and a subset of general end user computing. But even then, DSS builders need to recognize that they should try to influence not just passively accept the level of support they provide and to choose their target market accordingly. The default option is to work from an implicit or explicit definition for action which is basically: 'Our role is to provide managers with help in using the software systems we have in place in whatever ways make most sense for themselves.'

There is no clear target market here in the sense of which managers and which decisions the DSS group wants most to influence. The definition for action that reflects Extended support and is fully practical might be: 'Our role is to help senior business managers in critical areas of their planning and decision making where providing expertise and building computer systems can contribute substantively and directly to their primary business goals.'

That definition obviously eliminates some easy markets like the bread-and-butter domain of spreadsheets and ad hoc modelling, for instance. Perhaps it is too strong a statement for a given DSS unit, who might instead define its role as: 'Working with senior Marketing and Corporate Planning managers to identify where we can build them a responsive analysis and modelling capability for competitive and environmental scanning.'

Regardless of the specifics, the general point is that most DSS units do not have a clear target, in terms of which managers and decisions they are actively interested in supporting and what returns to the company they feel they can offer for an investment in expertise and computers. Equally, much of the research on DSS works from the default Traditional or even Passive level of support because that is the practical state-of-the-craft. It may be, but the goal for research should be to open up more options for the craft.

## 4. The Balance Between D, S, and S

Restated, this means that, in Stabell's term, it is time to put the D back in DSS. It does not mean, though, that Extended support can ignore computer technology, the second of the two S's. Instead, it highlights the dilemma that has dominated DSS and that is probably the basic reason for the problem of definition.

DSS pulls together three very different focuses of effort, need for knowledge and criteria for action:

'Decision' relates to the nontechnical functional and analytic aspects of DSS and to criteria for selecting applications.

'Support' focusses on implementation and understanding of the way real people operate and how to help them.

‘System’ directly emphasizes skills of design and development and of technology.

Very few of us can be equally able or are equally interested in D, S and S. Each of the separate elements is a specialist area that attracts people who may have little knowledge of the other two. Examples are the very able management scientists who are unfamiliar with the messy dynamics of implementation – or the messy way many managers handle decisions. The best practitioners of organizational development in the widest sense of the term, in the same way, are often determinedly ignorant of the computer field and analytic disciplines. Few of the best DSS builders feel they need to keep up to date with decision analysis, simulation and forecasting techniques, etc.

The result is that the three components of the term have too easily lost balance. The early work of such people as Scott Morton, Gerrity, Stabell and Little began from a discussion of decision making, moved on to the issue of effective support and ended up with a system. By contrast, ten years later, Bennett's and Sprague and Carlson's books that reflect the mainstream of DSS in 1982 (Ref 6) are both entitled 'Building' DSS and move in the reverse direction: How do we build systems? What does that say about the nature of effective support? There is little about decisions.

There is no simple, stable balance between D, S and S. If there were, the definitional problem would go away. In the mid- to late 1970's, there had to be a strong focus on System to make it possible to provide Decision Support. Without Ness's work, for instance, on middle-out design, progress in the field would have been slowed down by the immense problem of delivering systems, quickly and at a low cost, when all the available tools and techniques were expensive and cumbersome to implement. He provided the training for a number of DSS developers who had an impact on Decision and Support because they were experts in the craft of System.

For DSS to exploit the opportunity of expert systems to provide Extended support, we will need people who know a lot about technology. The same is true for exploiting CD-ROM to provide document-based DSS. While at present it seems that DSS has become asS and needs to put the D back, that may change. There will always be new bottlenecks to progress and many of those will at some stages relate to technology.

## 4.1. Putting the $D$ Back in

The bottleneck is now the D. Anyone who cares about the mission of DSS – helping real people make better decisions through the use of an ever expanding range of technical building blocks – must surely agree that we need more decision research, a body of theory-laden summaries and frameworks that address:

Decisions that really matter: Now that we know how to build DSS, the issue is which tasks justify the investment. Life is not just a spreadsheet. Using our systems building skills on yet more standard and fairly simple ad hoc financial planning applications will not move us forward much.

Organizational rather than individual decision making: Partly because of its deliberate separation from Data Processing at a time when DP was synonymous with bureaucracy and backlogs, DSS highlighted its differences, to a point where it seemed to view DP as a rather idle collection of blue-collar dimwits (Keen and Scott Morton's book radiates this elitist attitude).

It is now clear that DSS needs DP, because the critical resource for supporting organizational rather than individual decision making is data and telecommunications. These go way beyond the capabilities of a small scale group focussed on cherry-picking. The new, responsive IS organization is the manager of the organizational data resource DSS needs to draw on.

Multicriteria decision making: Dilemmas of judgment are inherent in the types of decisions that DSS aims at helping. Whether these are termed ‘unstructured’ or ‘semi-structured’ they are ones where there is no single decision rule that can resolve the trade-offs they involve. Scheduling an oil refinery’s production is a complex task of computation but the objective criterion is clear: minimize cost subject to these constraints. There is a limited need and scope for judgment.

Multicriteria decisions pose dilemmas or even crises of judgement: ethical choices, trade-offs between cost and service, conflicts of preferences, and 'political' problems are obvious examples. The multicriteria problem is at the core of Decision Support. In a sense, DSS finessed the issue in a way that made action practical: 'The user initiates and controls the problem-solving process and sequence, and his or her judgement, personalized objectives, and interpretations guide the choice of solution' (Keen and Scott Morton, page 8). That is a justification for Traditional support, but is it the best we can aim at? Can we not guide decision making by applying analytic techniques that aim at enhancing judgement or reducing the strain involved in resolving dilemmas?

Attending to these issues restores the flavor of Management Science to DSS and reduces the dominance of Information Systems. It also forces us to ask what we mean by ‘supporting’ decision makers. The options are:

Passive support: Give them tools they are comfortable with and do not intrude on their autonomy.

Traditional DSS: Make sure the tools are usable and mesh with the current decision process but look for ways to improve it.

Extended DSS: Look more actively to improve it; provide a consultant rather than just an assistant; find ways of embodying limited forms of 'knowledge' drawing on the tools of AI; use the DSS builder's understanding of the decision domain to help point to and even select alternatives, rather than just evaluate the decision maker's set of choices.

Normative support: Dominate the decision process; provide an explicit methodology for analysis, where the manager's role is mainly to provide inputs and specifications.

It is difficult to see any worthwhile research on the Support component of DSS that does not push towards a more active impact on decision making than that provided by Traditional DSS. It is equally hard to see how a DSS group can sustain a distinctive identity unless it has something to offer the firm other than building software.

## 4.2. DSS Technology; The System Component

It is indeed time to put the D back in DSS and to extend the level of support. At the same time, the System component will remain a critical one. By extending the technological base for DSS, more decisions can be supported in more ways. The range of practical applications for and payoffs from DSS is heavily shaped by changes in the available and suitable technology.

There are three obvious areas where DSS builders should be drawing on emerging tools to extend their work: Document-based systems; Expert systems; Telecommunications and data stores.

## 4.3. Document-Based DSS

The combination of CD-ROM, which transforms the economics of archival storage and retrieval, IBM'S document architecture, DIA/DCA (Document Interchange/Content Architecture), which is increasingly being adopted as a standard by vendors of office technology systems, and videotext, a simple way of organizing and delivering information – all these have immense potential for DSS and have been under used.

For people in the computer field, ‘information’ is equivalent to ‘numbers’ or ‘strings of text’. For anyone who knows nothing about computers, information mainly means documents; many aspects of work center around purchase orders, manuals, contracts, discursive reports, etc., etc. Data base management systems focus on only a small subset of organizational information activities and for many managers, the ability to scan, cross-reference and locate documents may provide far more payoff and involve much simpler systems than those that manipulate numerical data bases.

ihere are some, but not many, examples in the DSS literature of document-based systems. In Europe, there has been much wider and more successful use of videotext, especially for marketing managers to get fast access to product information, news, client contact lists, etc. Several US organizations have recognized that information for competitive scanning is often unstructured and written and build systems where the basic unit is a document: items from newspapers and reports, for instance.

The tradition of extracting text and numbers and then providing tools for structured searches through DBMS or such packages as STAIRS is more limited in this instance than the chance to begin from a broader perspective of classifying and storing whole documents which are the unit of information and mental frames, zero in on interesting items, read and think and then scan deeper or across the information base.

This opens up a new form of Traditional DSS support: it balances computer power and managerial judgment. It may be limited by the generally hierarchical structuring of the information resource implicit in videotext and by our ability to classify documents under categories that facilitate intelligent and efficient scanning, but all in all it seems clear that the entire computer field is shifting to a document-centred world and DSS builders should exploit the opportunities this opens up.

## 4.4. Semi-Expert Systems

The tools of expert systems are an emerging technology that DSS developers recognize as both a key challenge and a key puzzle. The challenge is to find ways of applying PROLOG, LISP, etc., to the domain of managerial tasks. The puzzle is what DSS can add that is in any way different from the mainstream of efforts to build expert systems.

The problem has been compounded by hype. It has been very hard indeed to sort out promise from practice and to distinguish where progress depends on developments outside a technology that is so immediately attractive. For example, an interesting aspect of MYCIN, one of the most widely cited examples of a successful expert system, is that it was built over ten years ago; why was it not widely adopted by real decision makers? This is a ‘behavioral’ not a technical problem and one that DSS has dealt with effectively through its attention to issues of implementation rather than just installation.

There are several practical ways in which the DSS field can exploit the opportunity of expert systems technology, without having to worry about what exactly is an expert system, etc., etc. The first is simply to use the new power tools (this is John Seeley Brown's useful phrase for such development facilities as KEE and the Symbolics LISP machine) to build standard DSS.

The usefulness and useability of a DSS depend heavily on the nature of the user-system dialog. Menus, commands, windows, mice and the like have been used at various stages of the development of interactive systems to make sure that managers can work comfortably with a DSS and have it adapt to them rather than the other way round.

A Symbolics workstation, as an example, permits a further improvement in this area. In particular, being able to see on the screen how the system itself is ‘reasoning’ – how particular rules are triggered, for instance – to think in terms of scenarios with rich verbal explanations provided by the system, and to code information in more meaningful ways than formulae and tables – all these are needed in financial planning models and in fact in most DSS that deal with complex decision tasks.

The second major opportunity is very pragmatic: the DSS approach to systems development is much more likely to move the field of expert systems from expensive pilots to widespread use than most methods of ‘knowledge engineering’ advocated by workers in the Artificial Intelligence field. The distinctive skills of DSS builders, built up over a decade include:

Systematic prototyping and the ability to make rapid progress in getting something up and running without requiring the decision maker to provide explicit information – or even to consciously understand the problem and his or her own decision making process. This is very different from AI knowledge engineering and may help knowledge engineers move ahead in the messy world of management, where tasks are ambiguous or shifting and rules hard to elicit.

'Hybrid' experience and abilities: Hybrids are people who are fluent about technology and literate about the area of applications or the reverse. They are the facilitators, internal consultants and implementers who bridge the world of technology and business. The field of expert systems needs them; it is still mainly an off shoot of technical specializations, such as computer science, cognitive sciences and applied AI. It has to locate the hybrids who can help it translate from design to implementation to use, from decision to decision maker, from the lab to the organization.

Understanding how decisions get made: This is an especial and necessary strength of DSS practitioners, the ability to work directly with managers and to relate to their perspective.

These are strengths the AI field will need to build and draw on. Brown describes three approaches to applying AI (Ref 7). These are the low road, the middle road and the high road. The low road involves showing that an expert system can be built by knocking up a limited system, often for a toy problem. For instance, the designer may put together some rules relevant to a cost accounting activity and then admit that the system is incomplete but argue that if extended...

Brown points out that while this may have been complex to achieve a few years ago, it is now well within the capabilities of a student as a homework exercise.

The middle road is represented by DEC's RCON and by most of the successful applications of AI in business. It involves assembling more and more rules, basically in the form of IF...THEN ...ELSE, to make a system more complete, valid and useful. From a DSS viewpoint, this is an incomplete approach that addresses problems that are by definition rule-based, such as medical diagnosis, but that is less suited to many managerial applications.

From an AI perspective, too, it is also incomplete. Brown argues that the goal should be to understand the decision domain at a level of depth that reduces rather than increases the number of rules. DENDRAL is an effective system because of the ‘elegant’ algorithms that are its base, not just a list of experts’ rules. Brown’s group at Xerox PARC were asked by a visitor how many rules a particular system had. The answer was ‘about 50’. This drew the disdainful response ‘Ours has 550’, and the counter from Brown that the group had worked very hard to really understand the structure of the problem and by doing so could eliminate rules.

The high road focusses on understanding the decision domain – in effect on Decision and Support. The combination of a hybrid team of traditional DSS skills in thinking about how decisions get made, how they can be improved and how to work with managers to evolve a decision aid, plus technical specialists who can exploit the power tools of the expert systems field, will move both disciplines forward faster than either alone.

The target of opportunity for DSS is what may be called semi-expert systems. These relate to tasks or functions where there is plenty of knowledge already available to draw on. Finance, auditing, and production are areas where we can code techniques and expertise from textbooks to build systems which move from the staff assistant, relatively passive mode of interaction, to a more active guided, consultative one – from Traditional to Extended support.

One advantage of the pragmatic DSS approach is that it does not need as complete an understanding of the problem nor need to produce as complete a set of recommendations from the decision aid as does Normative support. The decision maker is included in the process of analysis, stating of opinion, and assessment of results. If DSS too easily drifts to Passive support, the field of applied AI techniques may equally get locked into too Normative an approach to decision making.

## 4.5. Telecommunications

The third technical shift that DSS must exploit and build expertise in is telecommunications. DSS has in general focussed on stand alone technology and stand alone decision making tasks. The technological base has been either a terminal linked to a time-shared computer or a personal computer.

Telecommunications has been only a convenience for accessing data and computer power. The main applications have been to help individual decision makers handle a task that involves their own independent decision choice. The intellectual base DSS researchers have drawn on mainly refers to individual decision making and cognitive psychology. There has been relatively little discussion of group and organization-wide decision making.

As a result, a DSS will tend to support, for instance, individual managers making their individual budget decisions, rather than the organizational consolidation of budgets, sharing of data stores, feedback and control mechanisms, communication for negotiations, and so on.

Stand alone technology for individual decision tasks improves personal productivity. Targely limited to relatively small-scale and ad hoc problems and small-scale systems. Telecommunications plus data stores plus knowledge of organizational design, communication, politics, and implementation change the nature of DSS – and their potential impact. Organizational DSS and improve organizational productivity.

Already, DSS practice is recognizing the importance of data. There is an energy crisis for PC's. What use is a software package that can massage a billion cells of spreadsheet data if there is little to download from the main computer or to type in?

There are plenty of tools that could be used to support such decisions that matter as competitive assessments of intrusions from nontraditional players outside our industry (the dilemma and opportunity of any firm in the financial services or airline field) or monitoring short-term market shifts which indicate long-term structural changes. The tools are of little value without a supply of data.

One of the most effective uses of telecommunications, particularly for the class of DSS targeted at senior managers (Executive Information Systems and Executive Support Systems are the most commission label for these) is to pull from the decentralized, geographically-dispersed business locations operating data that can be used to alert managers to situations that need attention, instead of having to rely on historical reports that tell them the consequences of the situation too late for them to affect it. Such systems reduce information 'float', the gap between something happening and management hearing about it (Ref 8). Case studies of Executive Information Systems show the extent to which simple software tools can provide immense payoffs if but only if they can pull in the needed data.

## 5. The Priorities for the Next Decade

In summary, the balance between D, S, and S needs redressing in the mainstream of research and practice. If we do not focus much more specifically on the Decision component, the drift towards commodity systems building instead of value-added expertise will continue. If we do not look for definitions for understanding and action that open up Extended support, the organizational role for DSS will narrow not broaden. If we neglect the many new developments in technology, we will limit the scope for DSS in terms of both Decision, Support and System.

The DSS field is one of research-with-practice. Practice must be research-flavored or research-influenced if it is to be a source of innovation for D, S and S. Research must be targeted at improving practice if it is to meet the most elementary goal of Decision Support: to help managers.

It will also need scholarship, which is not the same as research. Scholarship searches for knowledge across other and often new fields to create the frameworks to help shape research. For example, the concepts of decision making that have driven the DSS field have mainly come from cognitive psychology; we now need to draw much more from such fields as sociology, political science and organizational theory to enrich our thinking about organizational rather than individual support. That needs scholarship:

Critical insights into a broad range of literature and synthesis of knowledge across relevant fields: political science, AI, Multicriteria decision making (MCDM), financial theory, organizational development and economics are just a few areas where DSS research and practica can gain from sensible, intelligent, and reliable summaries and interpretations.

Intellectual quality control: avoiding simplistic notions disguised as ‘frameworks’ or ‘models’ and misreadings (such as the casual and incorrect interpretations of Minzberg's conclusions about the nature of general managerial work being marked by Brevity, Fragmentation and Variety or assertions about 'heuristic' and 'analytic' decision processes).

The broader purpose of scholarship is to provide paradigmatic frameworks that are theory-laden, but not ‘theories’, to guide research; they must have intuitive appeal plus intellectual coherence. The early progress of DSS dependend quite heavily on borrowings from Simon and the Carnegie School’s work on decision making and from cognitive psychology. They provided a useful intellectual base for the era of individual and traditional support. Obviously, we must extend and enrich that intellectual base; much of it relates to putting the D back in DSS.

This paper ends with a list of priorities for the next decade for scholarship, research and practice. It is obviously a personal and assertive view that can be challenged on many grounds. What surely cannot be challenged, though, is that some such agenda is essential to move DSS ahead and recapture the sense of excitement that attracted those of us who describe ourselves as part of the DSS movement and that, we must acknowledge, is in danger of being lost. There is plenty of excitement for a second decade but not if it is just the tame extension of the first one.

The priority for scholarship must be to bring into the mainstream of DSS the best literature, insights, methods and conclusions of MCDM, organizational science, and perspectives on effective decision making and also to create the synthesizing frameworks for Extended support.

Progress in research needs a driving mission and vision. That proposed by Elam, Henderson, Keen and Konsynski centers on six questions:

(1) What decisions really do matter in an organization and how should we build better environments to help decision makers handle them?

(2) How should we formulate and help solve 'wicked' problems that require representing values, ethics and aesthetics?

(3) How do creative people learn? How should we design our DSS tools to facilitate a real dialog that stimulates learning?

(4) What is the role of modelling and quantitative methods in fostering creative thinking?

(5) What is the technical architecture for evolving DSS in a context of increasingly integrated technologies?

(6) How should we study and measure effectiveness, quality of decision making, learning and change?

These questions reflect a shift in the definition for understanding of Decision Support, from:

The application of available and suitable computer-based technology to help improve the effectiveness of managerial decision making in semi-structured tasks

to:

Applying intellectual and computer-related technologies to help improve creativity and learning for decisions that really matter.

The priorities for practice add up to a lengthy list:

Choose your definition for action, not just accepting a passive role and an identity that stresses building systems.

Support key organizational processes, not just limited ad hoc, small scale and individual ones.

Solve the data energy crisis.

Build an organizational mandate and base:

\- Identify the target users and level of support.

\- Define application priorities in terms of decisions that matter and payoff from investment in decision consulting not just software.

\- Get close to where the data is and to the Information Systems organization, if you are not part of it.

\- Get your business message across; what is special that you have to bring to the firm?

Exploit the new tools of the trade: Document-based technologies; Power tools for development; The telecommunications highway system.

Combine the intellectual technologies and DSS systems building skills to make useful analytic techniques usable tools worth using.

This agenda is ambitious and practical.
