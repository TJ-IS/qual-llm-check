## 1. Introduction

Studies of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) have generally made computer systems available for data input and display as an adjunct to a face-to-face meeting. [Gray (1981), Steeb and Johnston (1981), Huber (1982, 1984), Bui and Jarke (1986), Stefik et al. (1987), Gallupe, DeSanctis and Dickson (1987)]. With this approach, which Jessup (1987) refers to as the “computer-supported conference room”, a “decision room” is usually set up with a large screen for common viewing and one or more individual terminals are used for data input.

However, it has been increasingly recognized that a GDSS could be combined with Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC) to support decision-making by groups dispersed in space and/or in time. For instance, Huber (1984), Bui and Jarke (1986) and DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) explicitly discuss the advantages of incorporating communications components in GDSS. (See the latter paper for a good review of the history, definitions, and prior research on GDSS.)

This paper reports some of the results of the third in a series of controlled experiments exploring the use of CMC structures as a GDSS. Based on a field experiment within a conservative corporation, it compares selected aspects of the process and outcome of group decision-making in “pen-name” vs. “real-name” computer conferences, with some comparisons to a face-to-face condition [See Hiltz, Turoff, and Johnson (1985) for more complete results.]

## 1.1. Computer-Mediated Communication and GDSS

CMC systems use a computer to structure, store, and forward communications among people. In the text-based systems, one communicates by typing into and reading from a computer terminal or micro-computer, using either a typewriter-like printer or a video display. Group members may be spread out, both in space and in time. Two major variations are message systems, which handle discrete items generally sent to one or a few people, and conference structures, which are for extended group discussions on a single topic or task. Software structures can be created to support desirable types of group processes for various types of tasks. Among the structural variations that can be introduced in Computer Conferencing (CC) is the ability to communicate using pen names or anonymity, rather than the automatic signing of each entry with the real name of the author. (For background on the nature and impacts of CMC, see Hiltz and Turoff (1978), Kerr and Hiltz (1982), Hiltz (1984), Rice (1984), Steinfield (1986). See Hiltz and Turoff (1985), for a discussion of structures for CMC).

Early research on the social effects of CMC was aimed at generalizations about the impacts of the new medium. For example, Johansen, Vallee, and Spangler (1979, pp. 180–181) summarize a number of studies with the statement that “computer conferencing promotes equality and flexibility of roles in the communication situation” by enhancing candor of opinions and by helping to bring about greater equality of participation. On the basis of early pilot studies comparing face-to-face and computerized conferences, Hiltz and Turoff (1978) conclude that more opinions tend to be requested and offered in computerized conferences, but that there is also less explicit reaction to the opinions and suggestions of others. However, the democracy bordering on anarchy which characterizes unstructured or “free discussion” CMC makes it difficult for groups to come to agreement on complex issues or problems [Sproull and Kiesler (1986)].

A second generation of research on CMC as it relates to GDSS seeks a better understanding of the conditions under which the general tendencies of the medium are stronger, weaker, or totally absent. For example, current work at the New Jersey Institute of Technology focuses on the development and evaluation of a variety of new capabilities for CMC. The goal is to discover the interactions among task types, communications software, and individual or group attributes that will allow the selection of optimal system designs and implementation strategies to match variations in user group characteristics and types of tasks or applications. The research program involves a combination of field trials and controlled experiments.

## 1.2. The Prior Experiments

The first experiment in this series compared the process and outcome of face-to-face versus computerized conferences for two types of tasks: a priority-setting task with a criterion, and a human relations problem. [Hiltz, Johnson, Aronovitch and Turoff (1980); Hiltz, Johnson, and Turoff (1986)]. Interaction process was coded using Bales Interaction Process Analysis (IPA) [Bales (1950)], and in terms of equality of participation vs. dominance by an emergent leader. Results include:

\- Outcomes - Quality of decision was equally good for the two modes, but there was greater agreement on decisions in FtF.

\- Process and Outcome – Interaction process was related to the different outcomes for the two media. CC tended to produce relatively more of the types of communication which support high-quality decisions, and relatively less of the types which lead to group agreement.

\- Dominance - Lacking non-verbal cues or any suggested procedures for generating a leader, the CC group did not develop any dominant participants for either task; this may have hampered their ability to organize their human resources to solve the problem.

\- Subjective Satisfaction – Though CC was rated as satisfactory for all functions, FtF was consistently rated as more satisfactory. The biggest difference for the subjective satisfaction items was on ability to reach a consensus.

\- Task dependency – Differences between modes in interaction process and outcome are somewhat task dependent.

The second experiment focused on the efficacy of two particular structuring mechanisms for improving consensus in CC without negatively affecting desired quality of decision [Hiltz, Johnson, and Turoff (1982, 1987)]. Twenty-four groups of five professionals and managers used computer conferences to try to reach agreement on the best solution to the complex ranking problem. Two software tools for structuring the conferences were employed in a two-by-two factorial design. Groups with “Designated Leadership” elected one of their members to lead the group in its decision-making discussion. Groups with “statistical feedback” were given periodic tables which displayed the current “group decision” in terms of the mean rankings of items, and the degree of consensus about each of these items. Designated Leadership improved levels of consensus; in the absence of a leader, statistical feedback improved the level of agreement slightly. Statistical feedback as operationalized in the second experiment was detrimental to the ability of a group to achieve “collective intelligence”, defined as a group decision better than the pre-discussion decision of any of its individual members. Characteristics of the individuals and groups were also associated with variations in outcomes.

In this experiment, we wished to explore how the use of pen names might influence communication and decision-making, using realistic tasks within an actual organization. The type of task chosen was the “choice dilemma”, in which participants must decide whether to pursue an opportunity that might result in substantial gains or losses.

## 1.3. Anonymity, Disinhibition, and Deindividuation

Several non computer-based techniques have been developed in an attempt to provide communication structures that will improve group decision-making as compared to unstructured face-to-face discussions. Many of these include written communication as one component, and a mechanism to separate the identity of the participants from the ideas or decisions which they offer, as another component. For example, the voting process in democracies and formal organizations is both anonymous and based on written or electronic ballot forms. In the nominal group technique, group members first separately write ideas on paper; then their ideas are recorded on a blackboard or flip-chart in front of the group, one at a time, and without the identity of the originator listed [Delbecq, Van de Ven, and Gustafson (1975)]. The Delphi technique is usually conducted with successive written questionnaires, with the anonymous judgments of the participants summarized and fed back at the end of each round [Dalkey (1969), Helmer (1970) Linstone and Turoff (1975)]. The first computer conferencing system built was a computerized version of a “policy Delphi” structure [Turoff (1972)]. Both Nominal Group Technique and Delphi structures have been shown to be superior to unstructured face-to-face discussions in some respects [Van de Ven and Delbecq (1974), Hiltz (1975)].

Pen Names - Many of the possible ways of structuring group discussion and decision-making within computer conferences are based on utilizing a form of anonymity. A "pen name", similar to a "handle" on CB radio, is a form of anonymity which marks communications as coming from a particular individual, and thus allows directed responses. On large public networks such as The Source, a substantial proportion of participants choose to be identified only by pen name (e.g., "Superman", "MadamX" or "AppleLover".) For this experiment, identification by full or "real" name vs. pen name was imposed on entire groups, rather than chosen by individuals. The pen names were assigned and neutral, so as not to suggest any particular attributes. On the positive side, use of pen names should encourage participants to be candid and to contribute their ideas and opinions to the group without being afraid of later personal consequences for lack of conformity. On the negative side, perhaps adding pen names to the dynamics of a communication medium that is already "depersonalizing", in many respects, will result in unacceptable levels of "disinhibited" or "deindividuated" behavior.

Disinhibition occurs when social constraints break down, and people engage in “deviant” or anti-social behavior which they would usually inhibit (see for instance, Zimbardo, (1970) and Diener (1979, 1980)]. “Social presence” is decreased in all forms of teleconferencing as compared to face-to-face meetings [see Short, Williams, and Christie (1976)]. Such nonverbal behavior as distance, eye gaze, voice variations, and facial expressions are absent in all forms of computer conferences and therefore cannot be used to attempt to influence or control the behavior of others. Since these are important channels for social control [see Edinger and Patterson (1983)], one might expect an increase in disinhibited behavior in computerized conferences, as compared to face-to-face conferences, particularly with the use of pen names. Differences in status or power within an organization or group may also be used to attempt to influence the behavior of others. And, these would be less likely to become salient in pen-named conferences.

When the social control process breaks down, more disinhibited behavior occurs. In a recently reported series of experiments, it was observed that “people in computer-mediated groups were more uninhibited than they were in face-to-face groups” [Kiesler, Siegel, and McGuire (1984, pp. 1129)]. We would expect more disinhibition in pen-named conferences than in conferences with real names. Among the types of disinhibited behavior we might expect to see more of are “flaming” (name calling, aggressive messages), questioning of the “corporate wisdom”, and perhaps changes related to conformity to group decisions. There may also be effects on leadership or dominance behavior in a discussion, and in the nature of the actual decisions reached.

Deindividuation is a complex and ambiguous concept because its components are sometimes mixed with what we have termed “disinhibition”. The research tradition reaches back to LeBon’s nineteenth century work, The Crowd, in which he argued that under some circumstances, a group of people becomes transformed into a united entity that seems to develop a collective mind. The term “deindividuation” seems to have first been used in 1952 by Festinger and his colleagues [Festinger, Pepitone, and Newcomb (1952, pp. 382)].

Zimbardo (1970) focused on the conditions that produce deindividuation, such as anonymity, and on the nature of the deindividuated state, including loss of self-awareness and loss of self-regulation. He predicts that as a result, deindividuated behavior is likely to be emotional and irrational. It is important to note that we do not incorporate this latter component into our definition. Deindividuated behavior is “going along with the group”, which does not necessarily mean negative, irrational, or abnormal behavior. For example, it may mean going along with a rational argument that other group members agree with, even if you individually feel very emotional about the issue. We will segregate the “emotional, impulsive, irrational” behavior that Zimbardo spoke of into our definition of “disinhibition”.

The various previous studies agree that deindividuation involves a decrease in the amount of self-consciousness and self-monitoring of behavior. When this occurs within the context of a group whose task is to seek consensus, will it result in less likelihood of compromise or conformity in order to reach agreement, or more likelihood? One could argue that there would be less effective pressure from the group. However, Diener et al. (1980) hypothesize that when members' attention is drawn away from a self-conscious focus on themselves and the impression they are making, it is drawn "toward the group as a whole". They speculate that there will be less reliance on one's own standards and more influence by external cues being provided by the group.

We will define “deindividuation” as a decreased reliance by individual group members on their own opinions and values, and increased conformity to group opinions and norms. Computer Conferencing with pen names (“CCPEN”) in which individuals have no recognizable identity, will clearly be more deindividuating than Computer Conferencing with real names as signatures on items (“CCREG”) or Face-to-Face (“FTF”) communication modes. Thus, we predict on the basis of previous work with the concept of deindividuation that participants in CCPEN mode will be more likely to be able to reach agreement on a group decision than those in CCREG or FTF communication modes. We also predict that in a conservative subculture such as that of the host organization for this experiment, group decisions will be most conservative in the CCPEN mode.

## 1.4. Hypotheses

The hypotheses that emerged from our review of the literature on disinhibition and deindividuation, in combination with observations from field studies in which pen names were sometimes used, were as follows:

H1: Pen name conferences, as compared to regular-name computer conferences, will contain more incidents of disinhibition in the form of “flaming” (attacks on individuals or the group, including insults and the use of profanity).

H2: Pen name conferences will contain more examples of disinhibition in the form of comments that may be considered to be disloyal towards or critical of the organization.

H3: (Deindividuation) - There will be more agreement on a final group decision in pen name conferences than in face-to-face conferences or computer conferences in which regular names are used. (This will be operationalized as a smaller standard deviation for final group decision).

H4: (Deindividuation) – Group decisions made in CCPEN mode in a conservative subculture will be more conservative than decisions made in FTF or CCREG modes. (Conversely, in a subculture valuing risk-taking, the decisions would be expected to be more risky.)

We will also look at any possible effects on amount and equality of participation. Arguments could be made that pen names would increase both amount of participation and equality of participation, by lowering inhibitions and the possibility of “loss of face”. On the other hand, one could argue that there may be more unequal participation with pen names. In a group of peers with no designated leader or manager, the usual norm would be for all members to try to “do their part” in helping the group reach a decision. Protected by pen names, those who feel least enthusiastic about the task or the group or the medium itself might not participate as actively, since their lack of participation would not be personally identifiable. To the extent that these negative effects were true, we would expect to find:

H5: There will be fewer comments in pen name conditions, on the average (measured by mean comments); and

H6: There will be more inequality of participation in pen-name conditions

## 2. Method

This was a one factor experiment with three communication modes: Face-to-Face, Computerized Conferencing with regular name signatures on entries, and Computerized Conferencing with pen names. Six groups of five middle-level managers were randomly assigned to each communication mode, which was used by the group for three successive choice-dilemma problems.

## 2.1. Subjects and Setting

The eighteen participating groups were composed of middle-level managers or professionals attending a company-sponsored course during November and December 1981. The Fortune-500 company has a reputation for being conservative and for treating its employees well. Our subjects spent three weeks in residence for the course, having daily coffee breaks and lunch as well as classes together, and tending to go out in small groups together during the evenings. They definitely considered one another peers. They were also a fairly homogeneous population. On the average, they had worked for the company about 15 years (median = 16.0, mean = 14.8) and were middle aged (mean = 42.5, median = 41). About four out of five were male, as would be expected in American corporations. Only 20% had not previously used a computer terminal, and a little over half had previous experience with some other form of computer-mediated communication.

To introduce ourselves and the project and obtain volunteer participants, we gave a presentation to the entire class (about 125 employees) on some of our previous research on computerized conferencing, and distributed sign-up sheets in mailboxes. The experiments were scheduled so as not to conflict with the classes – in the late afternoon, after dinner, and on Saturdays. Volunteers were asked to check off all times when they would be able and willing to participate. We had more volunteers than the 90 required for the experimental design. The groups were assembled by first filling in those time periods for which there were only a few volunteers, and then filling in the rest of the groups from among those who indicated their availability during the scheduled times. The subjects were, thus, not randomly assigned to group, but rather according to the chance of their availability at common times. Some attention was paid trying to distribute the relatively small number of females among the groups as evenly as possible, since sex composition has been significant in previous studies. The subjects were most cooperative – only one, for instance, failed to arrive at the schedule time. They viewed their participation as part of their management training, and played their roles as decision-makers with gusto.

## 2.2. Task

Three “choice dilemma” problems were used for each group. The groups were instructed to reach agreement on the level of risk which they were willing to accept in order to pursue an attractive opportunity for themselves as individuals, their office group, or their company as a whole. Choices were expressed in terms of the minimum chance or level of probability which would be necessary before they would decide to pursue the opportunity: 1 out of 10 (the most risky), 2 out of 10, through 10 out of 10 (the most conservative).

Beginning with Stoner (1961), a number of experiments have presented individual subjects with problems that involve a series of choices entailing various degrees of risk vs. possible payoff. Twelve of these “choice-dilemma” situations are included in the most commonly used instrument for studying “risky shifts”. Usually referred to as the CDQ, Choice Dilemma Questionnaire, it was originally devised by Stoner (1961) and elaborated on by Kogan and Wallach (1964). All of these choice dilemmas involve completely hypothetical situations of giving advice to another party, and generally elicit initially “risky” choices. Dion, Baron, and Miller, in their review (1979), present a critique of the use of the same twelve choice-dilemma situations in the bulk of the research done during the 1960’s.

We devised versions of the choice dilemma that are realistic in the sense that they involve decisions of the type that managers and professionals in the particular organization actually do face. Participants were asked to play their “real” role (employee of the company), making a hypothetical but realistic decision related to their organization. The participants could and did identify with the situations. Though the situations used were “hypothetical”, they were much more realistic and relevant to the participants than the original CDQ items.

The three problems used for each group were supposed to represent three different levels of consequences. The first problem, described as a “practice” problem, was very simple and had not long-term consequences at all. Problem 2, called “The Inside Gamble”, represented a decision for an individual manager, with important repercussions for him or here, but not for the Company. Problem 3 represented a major policy decision with long-term consequences for the entire Company (“The Retail Plunge”).

Since the main focus of this paper is not on the choices made, the full text of one of the problems should be sufficient to demonstrate the general nature of these tasks:

## THE INSIDE GAMBLE

Your are a middle level manager who has in the past and can expect in the future to make average progress in the company – regular, though not spectacular raises and promotions. A senior level manager has gotten permission to form a development team to try to develop a completely new product which may have spectacular success in the marketplace. You would be totally responsible for the management of the development team. If successful, your work with this team would bring you recognition at the highest levels and significantly increase your rate of advance. However, there is another, competing development team in your company working on a competitive product, and several other companies are known to also be making crash efforts. The group might never get a product out the door at all. Should it fail and be disbanded, assignment to an inconsequential position is the best you could expect from the company.

What would have to be the minimum chance of success of the new development group before you would accept the offer to manage it?

## 2.3. Procedure

The organization chosen is one with which we had extensive prior contacts. Working with two sponsoring members of the staff for the course, we were able to devise seven choice-dilemma scenarios which were realistic situations for managers in that organization at that time. The seven problems and the procedures for conducting the face-to-face groups were pre-tested on groups of employees who were enrolled in the session of courses conducted just prior to the time period during which we conducted this study. During the pre-tests, we observed that the managers and professionals took the role-playing situations very seriously, discussing and arguing for as much as an hour before reaching a decision on a choice-dilemma. This meant that we could only give two situations per group in addition to the “practice” problem. We chose the two best problems according to the pre-test ratings of how realistic, relevant, interesting and clear each scenario was.

In all communication conditions, each individual first read the problem, and wrote on a recording form (for F-t-F groups) or entered online (for CC groups) their initial choices. A “private” initial opinion was recorded, as well as a “public” initial choice that was shared with the group. Then the group discussed the situation. They were instructed (in writing and with oral emphasis on the instruction before receiving the problems) that their job was then to assume they were a committee of the Company called together to make the decision or to advise on the decision (as appropriate). They had a twofold task: to arrive at the best possible decision, based on discussion of the pros and cons, and to reach agreement. If they were unable to reach consensus on the degree of riskiness acceptable to all, they could end the discussion without agreement if four out of five so voted.

At the end of the discussion, the participants entered the following information on final choices (on the offline form or in response to online prompting):

\- Their perception of the group decision;

\- Whether the group had reached consensus on the choice, or accomplished a “group decision” which was an average of different final choices;

\- Whether they actually agreed with the group decision;

\- If they did not agree, their own final individual choice.

For the two situations described to the groups as their “real” problems (as opposed to the practice problem), the experimenters withdrew from the field of interaction. In the face-to-face discussion, the experimenter retired to a small table in a corner of the room with her/his back to the group following distribution of each problem, and waited to be notified by the group that they were ready for a new problem or procedure. A tape recorder was placed in the middle of the table around which each group sat during the discussion. The group was told that the experimenter would not answer any questions. In the computerized conference, the doors to each office were closed after the completion of the practice problem, and the participants were told to summon the experimenter only if they became disconnected. In both conditions, a 40 minute time reminder was delivered (orally or online), but the group was permitted to continue the discussion as long as they liked.

Following the two “real” problems, participants individually completed a post-experimental set of questionnaires giving background information on themselves, their reactions to the problem-situations, and their subjective satisfaction with the discussion process and outcome. They were then debriefed.

## 2.4. Manipulation of the Mode of Communication

Software was written to construct a simple four-command subset of the host system, EIES (the Electronic Information Exchange System). Participants in CC conditions could use the command “enter” to make a comment in the group conference, “choose” to make a choice of a number for the decision choice problems, “look” to see the new comments of others, and “end” to vote to end the discussion. Instant notifications of choice shifts or votes to end were delivered to the others. Comments were automatically entered with either the full name of the participant or an assigned pen name, according to the condition set when initiating the group discussion. Once initiated, the CC conditions proceeded automatically through the programmed steps without intervention by the experimenters. For instance, the program informed participants when all had agreed on a choice or when four out of five had voted to end the discussion; and then proceeded to deliver the next instruction.

The groups using CC were required to conduct their discussions in “real time”, or synchronously. Though participants in computerized conferences are sometimes online at the same time, it is much more usual for participation to be asynchronous. Generally, participants sign on any time it is convenient, and spend as much or as little time as they wish. A “real” choice dilemma might be discussed during a period ranging from several days to several weeks. Some of the participants might spend a total of five minutes on the discussion, whereas others might spend five hours or more, depending on their interest in the situation, whether or not they were travelling during that period, convenience of terminal access, and other factors which could not be controlled. A “controlled” experiment, in which asynchronous conferencing is used, is thus a contradiction in terms. In order to make sure that only mode of communication was varied among the groups and that observations of participant behavior could be collected for the entire discussion, synchronous CC was used.

For six of the groups, all entries were headed with the full name of the participant (as well as time of entry, and a unique “conference comment” number, that could be referenced as a shorthand way of indicating that a new entry was in response to a previous one). This is the normal or “regular” way in which entries in EIES appear. In the six groups in the pen name condition, the pen names were assigned (we used “one” “two” “three” “four” and “five” for half of the groups; and five colors for the other half. People seemed to prefer the colors). The items entered were identified only by the pen name and the conference entry number. The subjects were instructed not to give away any hints about who they “really” were, and amazingly, none of them did. Usually, EIES conferees can choose whether to make a particular entry appear with their regular name or with a pen name. For the experiment, the CC REG groups were not aware of this option and were unable to use it, whereas the CC PEN groups had pen names imposed on them for all entries.

## 2.5. Dependent Variable Measures

Disinhibition was measured through a content coding of the conference transcripts. Three types of “disinhibited” comments were noted for this analysis. An “insult” is name-calling or putting down of another group member or of the group as a whole. “Profanity” includes “four letter” and other words which might be considered obscene or sacreligious, including abbreviations of such words. “Disloyalty” to the company includes criticism of the company or intimations that one might leave and work elsewhere. The unit of analysis was the conference comment, the equivalent of a “turn” in a face-to-face discussion. The identification and recording of such comments was done independently by two student assistants with any differences resolved through a re-coding by the first author. Every comment that could possibly have been interpreted as falling into the above types of disinhibited behavior was included.

Counting of the number of conference entries and number of lines of text entered by each participant was done automatically by the software developed for the experiment. Following measurement procedures used in our previous experiments, inequality of participation is measured by the difference between the expected proportion of comments or lines by each participant if there were complete equality (20% in groups of five), compared to the observed distribution, as follows: $I = \left(1/N \text{ Sum } (Ei - Oi)\right)/1/2(1 - 1/N)$ ,

where $Ei =$ expected cumulative proportion of comments if all contributed equally; $Oi =$ observed cumulative proportion starting with the least active member; $N =$ size of group.

This index varies from 0 for total equality to 1.0 for complete inequality (a monologue).

## 3. Results

## 3.1. Occurrence of Disinhibited Behavior

There is not much difference between the pen name and real name conferences in the likelihood of disinhibited behavior. There were no such comments in two out of the six pen name conferences, and in three out of the six regular conferences. However, once a participant does engage in disinhibited behavior, it appears that it is likely to be followed by more such comments when pen names are used. In the regular conferences where disinhibited comments occurred, there were two in two of the conferences and one in the third. In the pen name conferences, there were 2, 3, 5, and 7 such comments. The sample is small, but a greater tendency for a “bandwagon” effect of insults and profanity does appear to occur for pen name conferences.

Overall, the surprising finding is that there is a very low level of disinhibited behavior in any of the computer conferences. Not a single occurrence of disinhibited behavior in about half of all the computer conferences was observed. This runs contrary to a popular expectation that pen-name conferences and computer conferences, in general, may provide an interaction space with all the seriousness and social control of a mardi-gras. On the contrary, when the participants are part of a social organization or community, and when they have ongoing expectations of common group identity and shared activities to accomplish, the mode of communication does not necessarily produce high levels of disinhibited behavior.

Looking at the type of disinhibited comment, it is noteworthy that comments that could be interpreted as disloyal to the company are much more likely to occur in the pen name condition: five out of the six such comments. Whether this is “good” or “bad” depends upon whether one wants to encourage criticism of the company. If one wants only loyalty and praise for the organization, it is obviously safer to enforce the use of signatures on all entries. If one wants to know what people are “really” thinking about the company, pen names are more likely to make people feel safe in expressing such opinions.

## 3.2. Deindividuation

Our conceptualization of deindividuation is the extent to which the individual members seem to lose their identity or individuality and get “caught up in” the group. One indicator is the amount of agreement with the final group choice on each problem, as measured by the standard deviation.

As it turned out, this indicator was not sensitive enough to capture differences among modes in this particular corporate setting. As one of the subjects put it during a de-briefing, “If we are given a job, in this company, we get it done. Our task was to reach agreement. Therefore, we were determined to reach agreement”. Surprisingly to us, given our first experiment with adhoc groups, almost all of the groups reached complete agreement on a final group choice, for all problems and in all conditions. Out of the 56 group decisions, there were only four on which there was a single person not agreeing on what was the final group choice. Three of these occurred in face-to-face groups; one in CC with regular names, and none in CC with pen names.

## 3.3. Mode and Conservatism

Choices made by people in the pen name condition are more conservative, on the average, than choices in other communication modes. This is true both of initial public choices and final public choices.

For final group choices, the greater conservatism in computer conferencing pen mode is statistically significant. An analysis of variance for mean initial public choices is included in table 2; no one of these differences in conservatism is significantly different, taken alone. However, even though these are not independent observations, there is very little chance that all three replications could have shown this great a difference in the level of conservatism in initial public choices by chance.

Something is going on here in terms of the communication mode affecting the tendency towards risky or conservative choices. Remember that these initial choices were not made independently of mode. In the face-to-face group, they had to be announced orally by each individual (even though they were recorded for us in hand written form). In the CCREG mode, the participants had already completed a short preliminary discussion in this mode, and they knew that their choices would be delivered to others in writing with their names attached. In the CCPEN mode, they knew that their initial choices would be delivered in writing by computer, but that their choices would not be identified. The latter mode tended to make them more conservative.

As a result of the differences in initial choices by mode and of differences in choice shifts by mode, there were significant differences in the nature of the final group choices (as identified and reported by the individuals). The CCPEN groups had a consistent and significant tendency to make more conservative choices than groups interacting in other modes.

We believe that the process which explains the finding of more conservative choices in CCPEN is “deindividuation”. In the pen name condition, where individuals are not identified, their individual values and opinions are less important than the dominant group values and opinions. In this conservative subculture, the surrounding organizational values are conservative. Thus, in CCPEN condition, deindividuation produces more conservative choices. In an organization which encourages risk-taking, we would expect pen-name conditions to result in riskier group decisions.

## 4. Summary and Discussion

It is commonly assumed that the use of pen names in a computerized conference will result in behavior characterized by a normless abandonment of standards of behavior for polite and constructive interaction. From this point of view, using pen names might be fun, like going to a Mardi Gras wearing a mask and costume, but it is not an activity that a serious business organization would consider using. If this were a true set of suppositions, it would preclude the adaptation of decision support processes that have proven valuable in other modes to GDSS within computer-mediated communication systems.

We compared aspects of the group interaction process in computerized conferences using real names and those using assigned pen names. Our experiment was conducted using decision-making tasks for peer groups of managers within a large corporation with a well developed and conservative “corporate culture”. No statistically significant differences were observed for various types of behavior that could be considered to illustrate “disinhibition”. There was relatively little disinhibited behavior in either type of computerized conference. We examined insults, profanity, and expressed criticism or disloyalty towards the Company as categories of disinhibited behavior. In about half of the computer conferences, there was not a single incident of disinhibited behavior of these types. There do appear to be slight qualitative differences. If one person makes a disinhibited comment, then it appears slightly more likely to be followed by others when pen names are used. And, it was only with pen names that criticism of the Company was at all likely to occur.

Almost all groups agreed completely on a final group decision for each of the problems. There were no instances of disagreement on the final group choice for the 18 pen-name discussions; one out of 18 for the regular name discussions; three out of 18 for the face-to-face discussions. Thus, the findings are in the direction predicted by the “deindividuation” hypothesis: pen name conferences show more agreement with the group.

However, the differences are not statistically significant.

One hypothesis derived from the concept of deindividuation for this study within a conservative subculture was that the pen name condition will produce more conservative results, because participants are more likely to go along with the conservative company values when they lose their individuality through the use of pen names. We did observe that the group decisions made in the CCPEN mode are significantly more conservative than those made in the other modes of communication.

Other aspects of “disinhibition” or “deindividuation” might be related to the absolute and relative amount of participation. Whether measured by number of comments (turns) or number of lines entered, we find a consistent but statistically insignificant tendency towards more participation in pen name conferences, and no difference in equality of participation. Thus, hypotheses based on negative suppositions about the effects of pen names on participation patterns were definitely not supported. However, once again, the differences are too small to be statistically significant in terms of supporting the opposite hypothesis that pen names will lead to both greater participation and more equal participation.

Certainly, the use of pen names will tend to result in disinhibited behavior when there are no shared norms and no social relationships among participants. On Compuserve, a large public network that permits the use of pen names or “handles”, there are many instances of disinhibited behavior occurring in this mode of communication. Participants steal one another’s handles and use the opportunity to commit malicious verbal mischief, such as sending an obscene message under another person’s handle. They act in irresponsible ways when hiding behind a pen name, such as mis-representing their age, sex, or characteristics. Our point is that whether or not pen names will result in disinhibited behavior is dependent upon the social context in which they are used.

## 4.1. Comparison with Other Experiments

Our results are completely different than those for the only other comparable experiments, by Kiesler and her colleagues. The differences may be due to using different types of subjects and groups, or differences in the CMC software. Another possible explanation is differences in procedures in terms of the exact nature of the choice shift problems used or in the content coding, but we do not think that these latter differences are significant.

Kiesler, Seigel and McGuire (1984) used the original Stoner choice dilemma problems on three person groups of Carnegie Mellon students, employing communication modes similar to those in our study: face-to-face, anonymous computer conferences and non-anonymous computer conferences. They found more uninhibited remarks in computer conferences than in face-to-face conferences, and more in anonymous conferences than non-anonymous conferences (ibid., figure 4, page 1129).

The Carnegie-Mellon software, called “Converse”, differed considerably from the EIES structures used in our experiments. It divided a screen into three parts, in each of which the messages being produced by each participant scrolled independently. The EIES conference modes encouraged each participant to concentrate on what he he or she was thinking and composing for the discussion as long as necessary, by blocking out receipt of all communications except one line notifications of choice shifts during composition, then delivering the full text of other entries that had been completed in the interim. Our experiment also used hard copy terminals rather than video display units, so participants could refer back to any part of the proceedings. The EIES version of CMC probably encourages more lengthy and thoughtful participation. It could also be that the hard copy record produced for the participants gave them a greater sense of accountability.

Though differences in software may be influential, we suspect that the most important source of differences in findings is that the corporate culture within which our groups communicated was simply stronger in its effects than the tendencies toward depersonalization generated by the medium. Kiesler et al. were using students who did not know each other in their initial experiment and completely hypothetical tasks. We were using groups of managers, who were role playing a choice dilemma, which was realistic for their organization. Take our managers and professionals out of the corporate context, give them a screen on which comments seem to live only momentarily and then scroll off into oblivion, and they, too, would probably act disinhibited.

## 5. Conclusion

In cohesive task-oriented managerial groups, the use of pen names rather than real names in computerized conferences will not dramatically alter the interaction process. Pen names may make people feel a little bit freer to criticize the organization or to attack one another's positions on the issues being discussed. They may result in a slightly greater tendency toward "deindividuation" in the form of going along with the group. They may encourage greater participation and greater equality of participation. However, these are very slight tendencies. The amount and type of interaction is primarily determined by other factors related to the organizational setting, interpersonal relationships among group members, and the nature of the group task.

Our findings indicate that decision-making structures which rely upon anonymity can be used as part of GDSS within computer-mediated communication systems. This is the major practical implication of the findings. Methodologically, it should be noted that corporate management training programs may be fruitful “strategic research sites” in which to obtain subjects for experiments with GDSS.

In terms of theoretical implications, our findings suggest that a clear distinction should be made between the concepts of disinhibition and deindividuation. In much previous work, they are grouped together as if they are different aspects of the same thing. In a crowd, where there is no strong subculture to provide norms, both would tend to occur together. Within the confines of a managerial group in a conservative corporate culture, however, they are quite distinct. Disinhibition is not likely to occur very much in this context, because the norms are too strong. Deindividuation, which is not necessarily anti-social, can take the form of greater than usual conformity to the norms of the group, despite one's personal opinions.

The results of these experiments confirm qualitative impressions gained from over a decade of experience with the EIES system. In EIES, any member can decide to send a private message or enter a conference comment either anonymously or with the use of a pen name. Abuses of this capability are relatively rare. While the majority of EIES users have been paying subscribers from academia, industry, and government, a good number of students have also been allowed access to the system. The presence of an etiquette guide in the manual and the social norms obvious in the public conferences appear to have limited potential abuse considerably among the students.

For paying users, we have found that the use of pen names and anonymity is largely constructive in nature, and is an infrequent occurrence in normal conferences. Occasionally, specific conferences are set up where the use of pen names is established as the norm. Examples of this have been the exploration of very sensitive issues, and therapy-like discussions. Situations supporting common use of pen names and anonymity include:

\- Sanctioning a new member in a conference who is drifting off the subject or writing long winded comments.

\- Expressing half thought-out ideas to which the writer is not ready to commit his or her name.

\- Describing a relevant situation in one's own company, where discussants do not want to embarrass their organization by identifying themselves.

\- Avoiding identification of work going on in one's organization in a comment that hints at potential future developments, or critiques of a current technology.

As constructive inputs to discussions, we have seen examples of pen-named contributions ranging from a complete description of the errors made by various levels of management that resulted in a half million dollar purchase of the wrong computer system, to a comprehensive description of what drives a person to a suicide attempt.

It is our view that the use of pen-names should be an available option in any Group Decision Support System using Computer Mediated Communication. The reasons for this are similar to the justifications for the use of anonymity in the Delphi process [Linstone and Turoff (1975)].

\- Preventing the status of a person from influencing the consideration of the merit of an idea or concept.

\- Allowing high status individuals to release “trial balloons” involving uncertain ideas or information, and avoid being embarrassed or feeling committed to it if the idea does not prove out.
- Decreasing the chance that several disagreements or arguments will result in subsequent interpersonal strain among participants.

In most Group Decision Support Systems that have been designed on EIES [Bahgat (1986), Johnson-Lenz (1980, 1981), Turoff and Hiltz (1982, 1987)] implementations of various forms of voting have always been designed to protect the identity of the voter. The difference between a numeric vote on disagreement and an expression of a qualitative reason for disagreement are conceptually alike with respect to the use of pen names and anonymity. Pen names or anonymity for opinions may be especially helpful for exploring alternative decisions on sensitive issues, such as the need to cut staff or projects during a budget reduction crisis.

Perhaps the most important issue in this area is what a decision maker wants from the group trying to investigate and analyze the factors leading to a decision. General Lincoln, the head of the U.S. Office of Emergency Preparedness during the late 60's and early 70's, explained his encouragement of the use of the Delphi process in that office by saying: "Even if I know what the decision has to be before the analysis is done, what I need to know is the worst arguments against that decision, before the decision goes public". While this is an informal quote made to one of the authors, it does express that appreciation for dissent in situations involving equivocality that leads decision makers to appreciate the constructive use of pen name and Delphi-like procedures.

The most interesting result of this research is that within a true corporate setting, the “flaming” phenomenon did not occur. The homogeneity of the experimental groups was reflected in their behavior. Despite very different communication modes, their behavior varied very little. These findings serve to confirm the idea that the social context of group interaction (in the case, conservatism and homogeneity) have a greater effect on the process and results of that interaction than any technological intervention. Variations in the structure of computer-mediated communication can encourage or discourage forms of interaction, but the primary shaping of this interaction is the social, rather than the technological, context.

This experiment raises more questions than it answers. Certainly we need a great deal more experimentation which will enable us to untangle variables which are confounded or untreated in this study: conservative vs. risky organizational climates, different types of decision tasks, and different forms of decision support processes and tools which incorporate pen names or other forms of anonymity. We hope to locate organizational settings which would provide the large number of experimental groups necessary to provide adequate statistical power for such an experimental design in the near future.
