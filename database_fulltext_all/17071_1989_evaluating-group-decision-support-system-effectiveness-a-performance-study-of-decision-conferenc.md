# Evaluating Group Decision Support System Effectiveness: A Performance Study of Decision Conferencing

Anne T. McCARTT and John ROHRBAUGH
Rockefeller College of Public Affairs and Policy, The State University of New York at Albany, NY 12222, USA

Effectiveness of decision making with decision conferences was explored by examining groups using the Decision Techtronics Group. Quantitative data were gathered through a self-administered survey of participants in 14 conferences. Perceived conference effectiveness was assessed with respect to a global outcome measure and 8 scales measuring the decision process effectiveness, based on the Competing Values Approach to organizational analysis. Differences in perceived conference success were related to (1) the proportion of participants who believed the conference resulted in a decision, and (2) the level of benefits derived from: full support of the structure or preference technology; the opportunity for full, extended discussion; development of an action plan; and expected resolution of the problem by the conference end.

Keywords: Group Decision Support Systems; GDSS; Evaluation of GDSS; Decision Conference; Decision Techtronics Group (DTG); Group Decision Processes.

Introduction

Group Decision Support Systems

The study of decision making is of central importance not only to organizational theory but also to organizational practice. Although an effective decision-making process cannot guarantee rewarding outcomes (particularly in a less than beneficent environment), examples abound in which the results of inadequate decision processes embarrass and even destroy the most resilient of organizations. During the past decade, many types of Decision Support Systems (DSS) have been developed to meet the unique needs of individual managers and executives. Now attention is being directed to the design of DSS technologies for use by groups of managers and executive teams [Huber (1984a, 1984b)].

Any application of information technology to support the work of groups may be considered a Group Decision Support System (GDSS). One common form of GDSS is the use of interactive computer-based sets of procedures to assist groups of decision makers in using information to analyze collectively the possible implications and contingencies of alternative courses of action. Such GDSS aim to improve group decision making by removing common barriers to communication, providing techniques for better defining and structuring organizational problems, and systematically directing the pattern, timing, or content of discussion.

DeSanctis and Gallupe (1987) suggested three distinct levels of group intervention with GDSS. Level 1 GDSS attempt to reduce common barriers to communication through technological enhancement of the usual interaction pattern. Such features as automated input of anonymous ideas and concerns, electronic voting, and a large viewing screen can be found in most “electronic boar rooms" or "computer-supported conference rooms". Level 2 GDSS provide structure technologies for cognitive mapping (e.g., COPE) or system dynamics (e.g., STELLA) and preference technologies for decision modeling (e.g., HIVIEW) or judgment analysis (e.g., POLICY-PC) with which a group of managers or an executive team can work collaboratively [see Humphreys and Wisudha (1987)]. Level 3 GDSS are characterized by some form of computer-mediated communication with electronic enforcement of a variety of possible rules that substantially alter the pattern, timing, or content of the interaction process of the group. Level 3 GDSS include, for example, so-called "computerized conference" or "decision network" system designs.

## Decision Conferencing

Decision conferencing is perhaps the most widely applied type of Level 2 GDSS [Phillips (1984, 1985, 1986)]. Decision conferencing employs a portable, single-user computer system to support groups of managers and executive teams working face-to-face on a wide variety of organizational problems. Unlike Level 3 GDSS, verbal and nonverbal communication in decision conferences is not restricted by electronic networking but, rather, takes a completely connected, “each to all” pattern enhanced by the presence of a group facilitator.

Decision conferencing typically refers to two-day working meetings that include every person with a substantial stake in solving a pressing organizational problem. In a meeting room apart from daily interruptions and distractions, the group is offered an opportunity to derive a shared understanding of the problem and to create a clear plan of action. A distinguishing feature of decision conferencing is the on-the-spot development of a computer based model that incorporates the differing perspectives of participants. The group can examine the implications of the decision model, modify it, and test the effects of different assumptions, thereby ruling out ineffective strategies and focusing quickly on primary issues of major impact.

During a decision conference, the group is assisted by at least three people from outside the organization: the facilitator, the analyst, and the correspondent. The facilitator works directly with the group to help structure and focus their discussion, think creatively about the problem, and address the full range of issues involved. The analyst provides the group's computer modeling support, while the correspondent tracks the group's discussion so that thorough documentation of the process is available at all times.

The facilitator begins the conference by assuring that the participants are well-acquainted with each other, the “rules” of the working meeting, and the schedule that the session will follow. With guidance from the facilitator, a specialist in the techniques of group dynamics and information technology, the first task of the group is to formulate the nature of the problem and identify key issues of concern. Once the problem has been well-stated, the facilitator then selects from a variety of structure and preference technologies to provide the most useful organizing framework for the ensuing group discussion.

As the participants begin to elaborate the details of the model, a simple, though not simplistic, representation of the group's collective thinking about the problem emerges on the white boards in front of the group. Both available data and subjective judgments are combined in the model so that it reflects an integration of explicit information and expressed priorities. The analyst supports the group by recording these assessments on the computer. At the end of the first day when the initial version of the model is complete, the computer is used to project the implications of the group's judgments onto a large screen so all participants can review the results.

Rarely does the group accept these initial results. As the conference continues on the second day, participants suggest modifications, and different judgments are tested until the group is satisfied that the model captures their best thinking about the problem. When this process of change stabilizes, the model has served its purpose, and the group turns to summarizing the key conclusions. The final act of decision conferencing is to convert the proposed solution into a plan of action, backed by a printed summary of the session produced by the correspondent who has maintained a record of the entire process.

## The Decision Techtronics Group

One of the more active centers of decision conferencing is the Decision Techtronics Group (DTG) of the State University of New York (SUNY). Since its inception in 1982, DTG has hosted approximately 60 decision conferences for private and public sector organizations located throughout the United States. A majority of these conferences have been conducted for agencies and departments of the State of New York. DTG is recognized particularly for its innovative work in decision support for senior executive teams in government, as well as its application of a diverse range of structure and preference technologies in the decision conferencing setting [Quinn, Rohrbaugh, and McGrath (1985), Milter and Rohrbaugh (1985), Milter (1986a), Milter and Rohrbaugh (1988)].

The problems tackled with decision conferencing through DTG have been diverse. In Georgia a decision conference for 30 community leaders was convened to develop a \$100 million, 10-year strategic plan for their city. A national professional organization based in Washington, D.C., used a decision conference to support the work of a 10-member task force charged with the review of alternative revisions in its dues structure. Three AT&T executives in New Jersey arranged a small decision conference to plan the consolidation of regional offices. A Texas decision conference of 15 child welfare experts prioritized the urgency of response to individual abuse cases. A state regulatory agency in New York scheduled two decision conferences for senior executives to identify their common database needs across divisions as preparatory planning for their first computer-based information system.

DTG's decision conferencing has been reported in the Wall Street Journal, Dun's Business Month, and Datamation. The American Management Association's Personnel devoted a feature article to DTG conferences. Meyer and Boone (1987) estimated one-year returns on investment between 400% to 2800% to DTG clients, concluding “...we expect to see the rise of ‘decision centers’ with the right tools and trained support staffs, such as the DTG at SUNY” (p. 259). Is this level of enthusiasm for decision conferencing (or other forms of GDSS) justified? To date little research has been conducted to determine how the process of decision making is affected by the introduction of alternative information technologies to the traditional meetings of managers and executive teams. In fact, no agreement exists on a broad conceptual framework that would guide such assessments of the processes by which critically important organizational choices are made.

Evaluating the Effectiveness of Group Decision Processes

The common wisdom is that good decisions result in good outcomes, poor decisions result in poor outcomes; therefore, outcomes are taken as useful reflections of the quality of the decision made. In fact, there is little doubt that the value of a decision depends on the confluence of subsequent events: a revenue forecast is accurate only if actual revenues match the projection; the worth of an investment depends wholly on the return it produces. Nevertheless, such widely accepted tautologies (i.e., that good decisions are good or that bad decisions are bad) beg the much more fundamental and important question of whether ineffective decision processes sometimes result in good outcomes. It is quite possible for a most unreasonable method of information integration to be linked over time with a windfall, while in another instance for a most reasonable method of collective choice subsequently to fall far wide of the mark.

Rohrbaugh (1987) has argued that the decision process of a group, unlike the decision itself (made as a result of such a process), cannot be evaluated readily on the basis of observed outcomes except in the most carefully controlled social experiments. Such research designs (i.e., ones that assess the effectiveness of a particular form of decision process based on outcome data) must be able to rule out not only the possibility that alternative group interventions at work in the same environment could produce equally satisfactory outcomes but also the possibility that alternative decisions could do as well or better than the actual choice made by the group. Virtually all “real world” applications of GDSS do not provide enough baselines of comparison (i.e., tests of alternative interventions and alternative decisions under parallel conditions) to satisfy a thoughtful skeptic.

Linking the “goodness” of outcomes to particular decision processes is extraordinarily difficult, especially if the intention is to identify a set of interventions that will improve the effectiveness of a variety of managerial groups or executive teams. The research design at a minimum would need to involve multiple teams working on multiple tasks in multiple ways and producing multiple decision rules (not just the one preferred rule) that could be tested over multiple periods of time. Even if such a program of research were to provide empirical support (on the basis of outcomes) for the relative superiority of a particular decision process in a specific organizational setting, the preferred use of the same decision-making process could not be generalized reasonably to other circumstances.

Therefore, any assessment of the effectiveness of a group decision process requires directing primary attention to the process itself, not to subsequent outcomes. Of course, it is difficult to argue for such pertinence of group processes (instead of outcomes) in traditional organizational settings that are managed entirely on the basis of short-term results. As unnatural to some observers as it may seem to distinguish conceptually between good group processes and good outcomes, however, it is even more irregular to propose that an assessment of the decision-making effectiveness of managers or executives must focus exclusively on the nature of their interaction. Few standards exist to assess the deliberations of groups in the absence of outcome data; most analyses of group discussion has been devoted to nonevaluative content coding of individual remarks. What is required is a larger conceptual framework with criteria by which to judge the effectiveness of group interaction in decision making. The Competing Values Approach described in the following section provides such a framework.

The Competing Values Approach to Organizational Analysis

The theoretical framework to be used in this paper is the Competing Values Approach (CVA) to organizational analysis [Quinn and Rohrbaugh (1983), Lewin and Minton (1986)]. According to the CVA framework, there are 4 middle range models of organizational analysis:

\- an internal process model focuses on information management and coordination as the means by which stability and equilibrium can be developed as primary organizational objectives;

\- a rational goal model focuses on planning and setting objectives as the means by which productivity and efficiency can be improved as primary organizational objectives;

\- an open systems model focuses on flexibility and readiness as the means by which resource acquisition and growth can be increased as primary organizational objectives; and

\- a human relations model focuses on cohesion and morale as the means by which the value of human resources can be made greater as a primary organizational objective.

The CVA framework makes clear the parallel relation between these 4 models of organizational analysis and the 4 functional prerequisites of any system of action identified by Parsons (1959): the integrative function, the goal attainment function, the adaptive function, and the pattern maintenance (and tension management) function. When the CVA framework was applied to the process of group decision making as a particular system of action [Milter (1986b), Rohrbaugh (1987)], 4 parallel perspectives concerning the effectiveness of decision processes emerged:

\- an empirical perspective (parallel to the internal process model) emphasizes the importance of information in a decision process, encourages the development of reliable databases to provide decision support, and stresses the need for documentation and full accountability;

\- a rational perspective (parallel to the rational goal model) favors logic and clear thinking over empiricism, attends primarily to organizational goals and objectives, and tends toward methods that can efficiently assist decision makers with their reasoning;

\- a political perspective (parallel to the open systems model) values adaptability and flexibility in a creative decision process, is attuned to shifts in the problem environment, and is concerned about finding solutions that maintain or enhance the standing of the decision maker(s); and

\- a consensual perspective (parallel to the human relations model) expects a fully participatory decision process, advocates open expression of individual attitudes and beliefs, and prizes collective agreement on a mutually satisfactory solution.

## Method of Study

## Overview of the Study

To provide an evaluation of one type of group decision support system, a cross-section of decision conferences hosted by DTG between 1982 and 1985 were selected for follow-up study. The purpose of the research was to investigate participants' perceptions of the effectiveness of the original decision process and the long-term (i.e., 3 to 5-year) consequences of the conferences through the use of a mailed survey of conference participants. The questionnaires also contained a series of items that inquired about the perceived value of certain aspects of the interventions in group decision making, some common to most forms of GDSS and some unique to decision conferencing. The target conferences were divided into three groups based upon the level of apparent success of each intervention. Using the conference rather than the individual participant as the unit of analysis, tests of differences between groups were conducted to explore possible explanations for observed variability in the perceived level of conference effectiveness.

## Participants in the Study

A total of 14 decision conferences hosted by DTG between 1982 and 1985 were selected for study. The cross-section of conferences included were chosen to represent the various types of client organizations that had formally contracted for the services of DTG during this period. The clients included 10 departments of state government (9 within New York State), two not-for-profit social service agencies, one professional organization, and one policy-making task force. Conferences ranged in size from 10 to 22 and averaged 13 participants.

In the fall of 1986, questionnaires were sent to all participants in the 14 conferences for whom addresses remained available. Of the 166 questionnaires that were distributed, 114 were completed, resulting in a response rate of 69 percent. The typical conference participant was 44 years of age, college educated, and employed by the client organization for 12 years, now at a senior management level. Approximately 40 percent of the conference participants were women.

## Measures Used for the Study

Process measures. The largest number of items on the questionnaire pertained to the perceived effectiveness of the decision process at the conference. These items provided the basis for constructing 8 scales, one for each criterion associated with the CVA framework. The reliability of these scales, ranging from 0.60 to 0.74, as well as the items contained therein, are provided in the Appendix. Furthermore, discriminant validity was relatively high; a check of scale intercorrelations revealed that on average about 20 percent of the measurement variance was shared between pairs of scales. Individual scores on each scale were averaged in each client organization to prepare for subsequent analyses.

Outcome measures. Participants were asked to what extent they believed their organization had derived benefit from decision conferencing in the form of improved information management, stability, planning, efficiency, adaptability, external support, morale, or human resources. Furthermore, participants were asked to what extent they believed their own on-the-job performance had derived benefit from decision conferencing in the form of improved expertise, dependability, goal orientation, decisiveness, creativity, political astuteness, empathy, or diplomacy. Because only moderate discriminant validity was found in these 16 items (i.e., the average correlation between items was about 0.60), a single summative scale was constructed to measure a generalized perception of conference benefit. Individual scores on this scale were averaged in each client organization to prepare for subsequent analyses.

Conference characteristic measures. In a separate section of the questionnaire, a series of characteristics, some common to most forms of GDSS and some unique to decision conferencing, were listed. Items included the presence of an outside facilitator, more formal structuring of group discussion, location of the conference away from office distractions, building of a computer-based decision model, opportunity for open and extended discussion, construction of an action plan to be implemented, expectation that the problem would need to be resolved by conference end, and computer output on the implications of the group's choices. Since half of the correlations between these items were below 0.40 and about 90 percent below 0.50, each question was treated as a reasonably unique measure. Individual scores on each of the 8 questions were averaged in each client organization to prepare for subsequent analyses.

## Three Groupings of Client Organizations

For each of the 14 decision conferences selected, 8 scale scores were generated to measure participants' perceptions of decision process effectiveness. From these measures of the 8 criteria from the CVA framework, as well as the global measure of perceived outcomes, three groupings of client organizations emerged. For 5 organizations, decision conferencing had been a very effective intervention, judged consistently and positively both in terms of process and outcomes. For 5 organizations, the intervention clearly had been problematic, evaluated with sharply lower ratings on process and outcome items. Participants from the remaining 4 organizations provided responses that were intermediate.

Two characteristics of decision conferencing (in fact, two characteristics of many group process interventions) are shown in table 2 to have been viewed as considerably beneficial conditions for participants: the location of the conference away from office distractions and the presence of an outside group facilitator (grand means of 5.14 and 4.82, respectively). The other 5 characteristics of decision conferencing appeared to play a significant role in explaining decision process effectiveness. In particular, these characteristics included: (1) providing an opportunity for open and extended discussion, (2) building a computer-based decision model, (3) reviewing computer output on implications of alternative choices, (4) constructing an action plan to be implemented, and (5) firmly believing that the problem would be resolved by the end of the conference. The analyses of variance reported in table 2 provide empirical evidence to suggest that decision conferences were not successful when these 5 benefits had not been fully realized. It should be noted that the greatest distinction between the three sets of client organizations could be made with respect to the perceived usefulness of their collective work supported by the analyst's use of a personal computer, that is, the group's examination, refinement, and testing of the decision model projected on the large viewing screen ( $F = 10.90$ ).

## Discussion and Conclusion

The research described above is the first effort to evaluate formally the effectiveness of decision conferencing. Although the study is limited in scope to groups of managers and executive teams from 14 client organizations supported by DTG during a 30-month period, the conferences selected must be considered as a representative cross-section of a much larger set of applications extending over more than 5 years.

The development of the CVA framework for organizational analysis provides a useful conceptual representation of the multiple perspectives on effective decision processes. Furthermore, the ongoing work to construct a valid and reliable instrument based on the CVA framework is beginning to yield practical results. The measurement of the performance of decision-making groups with respect to 8 criteria of effectiveness provides the opportunity to set reasonable standards not only for decision processes in traditional group settings but also for the variety of interventions now being afforded by an information technology as GDSS.

It is instructive, of course, to reveal that even a single type of GDSS intervention such as decision conferencing (admitting the variety of facilitators, problems, structure and preference technologies, physical locations, and client organizations) can produce such dramatically different profiles of decision process effectiveness as shown in fig. 2. It is more interesting, however, to understand certain consistencies in the results obtained from this study. For example, participants in decision conferences clearly view the intervention (no matter how successful) as providing a fully participatory and goal-centered process.

Perhaps the most important finding reported here pertains to characteristics of interventions in a decision process that apparently are associated with the belief that the performance of a group of managers or executive team had been enhanced. Clearly, an isolated setting, an external process consultant, and more formal structuring of group discussion were viewed by participants as beneficial. These aspects of decision conferences, however, were not linked to more effective decision making. Although these three characteristics are common to many forms of group process interventions and are widely valued, there is no empirical evidence in the present study that they explain much of the observed variability in conference success.

The most effective decision conferences in the study were those in which participants found real benefit in the full support of structure or preference technology and in the opportunity for open and extended discussion about the decision models that had been built. Important factors, as well, were the need to construct an action plan to be implemented and the expectation to resolve the target problem by the end of two days.

Decision conferences fail when these benefits are not provided. For example, an executive team may squander so much time initially building a decision model that only a brief opportunity remains to use it to full advantage. A group of managers may convene to discuss a problem but feel little pressure to reach consensus or construct a plan of action. An inappropriate devotion to the use of information technology may substantially reduce the opportunity for open and extended discussion that is so essential for building collective commitment and support for successful implementation of solutions [Rohrbaugh (1981), Mahler (1987)].

On the positive side, decision conferences (indeed, many interventions in decision processes) succeed when a group is prepared to take decisive action, to thoroughly discuss alternative options, and to attend to the practical task of making concrete, short-term plans. The most distinctly advantageous aspect of decision conferences, however, may derive from their unique integration of advances in information technology with more traditional forms of group interaction.

The process was very flexible in dealing with the problem.

The process was too structured. (R)

An effort was made to find a solution that would not in any way damage the standing of our work unit.

Group members were encouraged to raise questions and express personal concerns even when divergent.

Conflict was dealt with constructively.
