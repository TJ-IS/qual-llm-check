# Group decision support systems: decision process, time and space

Guest editor: Myron E. Hatcher

California State University, Fresno, CA 93740, USA

## Introduction

The following seven articles resulted from presentations at the Vancouver ORSA/TIMS National Conference. The presentations served as outlines for the final papers. Of the original twelve presentations, seven papers are published here and two have been published in other journals. Although many referees were involved in the process a special thanks goes to Dr. George K. Easton of San Diego State University. Dr. Easton reviewed more than his share of papers and assisted the guest editor in resolving challenging issues.

The papers are discussed from a time, space, and decision process dimension. These are the future issues in GDSS research that will demand attention. Time is the internal dimension of people that allows them to know their own existence. Space is the dimension that individuals use to view the outside world. $^{1}$ Decision process is the cultural framework that guides the process. Decision process could be viewed as the manifolds from Kant philosophy. This includes decision philosophy, values, culture, etc. Indications are discussed in the papers for overcoming time and space limitations and how the decision process can be refined and improved for better decision making.

Time has many attributes that hinder decision making. Information gathering and storage takes time. Retrieval of information is principally limited by time. In GDSS another time limitation is decision makers working on a problem at different time. This problem is often overlooked with the focus on War Rooms. In a War Room setting, everyone is in one location at the same time. Perhaps one of the most important future contributions of GDSS will be in this area of “intertemporal” group decision support.

Space is more one dimensional in its challenge. Decision makers who are not in the same room or area have limited information exchange. This limitation results from all the senses not being used for communication. The senses normally used in face to face communication are vision, hearing, touch, and smell. This limitation, which is a bandwidth limitation, can be a help or a hinderance depending on the exact nature of the decision problem. It can obstruct the exchange of essential information or it can focus the attention of decision makers and produce a quick resolution.

Decision process is mainly determined by the culture of the decision makers. What is evidence? What is argument? What is a decision? When is a decision reached? The majority of GDSS' experimental research focuses on the decision process. Oddly enough, little attention is paid to the main value of decision support. This is the ability for each decision maker to interpret data and not require data to be scaled for the decision process.

Part of the decision process is reaching a decision. Holsapple, Watabe, and Whinston applied the Nemawashi decision process in GDSS. Nemawashi comes from traditional Japanese culture and focuses on consensus among the decision makers. Information can enter the decision process from any avenue and the emphasis is on reaching total agreement among decision makers. A key role in a Nemawashi decision process is that of coordinator. This person tries to achieve consensus, prepares documents and circulates the documents for consent. Holsapple et al. discuss a flexible mathematical model that considers decision makers preference and influences.

Dyer and Forman approach consensus using the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP). A variety of methods for combining individual decision makers opinions are presented using AHP. AHP produces a ratio scale that reflects the decision maker(s) value system for combining the decision attributes. Problems with common objectives to retributive conflict are viewed from the GDSS viewpoint. Having a ratio scale allows unit differences between attributes to be defined and alternatives to be finely differentiated.

Wolfe's paper provides a good implementation contrast with Martz's paper. Wolfe presents an inexpensive GDSS implementation. With the literature reporting on large scale and expensive GDSS it is often easy to forget that many future implementations will be small scale and inexpensive. Wolfe also provides a theoretical justification for the concept of Nemawashi. An architecture is developed for providing the coordinator with technology for obtaining consensus among a group.

Martz, Vogel, and Nunamaker investigate values and attitudes of decision makers and problem structure of a GDSS implementation. Specifically, work group infrastructure is investigated. Work group infrastructure includes impact on technology, work group performance, group and task characteristics and the organization in general. Detailed analyses of this level are indications of future implementation research efforts.

Easton and Vogel's research looks at the decision process specifically from the stakeholder model. Timing of information is considered in the design with interactive versus stand-alone GDSS. The stakeholder model is but one of many models that can be used as part of GDSS. Interactive versus stand-alone is only one of many designs. Both Martz and Easton present excellent implementation research studies. Similar research designs can be used to study other hypotheses as they evolve.

Reisman, Johnson and Mayes portray videodisc as a method of partially overcoming the time barrier. Decision makers can leave messages that include both voice and image for other decision makers. This eliminates the need for all decision makers to be involved at the same point in time. This GDSS also provided tools for idea collection, idea ranking, report generation and consensus realization. Consensus is reached with the nominal group decision making procedure.

Hatcher describes a geographically distributed GDSS that eliminates the space barrier. Decision makers can discuss a problem without being in the same room or location. GDSS's tools that enhance dispersed decision making are outlined. This working system provides both audio, visual, and document sharing.
