# Electronic Meeting Systems

# Results from the field \*

William Benjamin Martz, Jr., Douglas R. Vogel and Jay F. Nunamaker, Jr.

Management Information Systems Department, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85719, USA

The findings of an exploratory field study concerning the effects of implementing an Electronic Meeting System (EMS) at an IBM manufacturing site are presented. A comparison of an electronic work group infrastructure with its corresponding manual practice is the primary basis of the study. The first section summarizes the University of Arizona's background in the EMS field and goes on to describe the particular EMS used in this study. The next section details the experimental background, the research model, and the data collection methods used. The effects of the implementation were measured by their impacts on the technology, work group performance, group and task characteristics, and the organization. A summary of the contributions made to the knowledge accrual process in terms of insights concerning the implementation and future study of EMS concludes this study.

Keywords: Electronic Meeting Systems, Field study, Implementation, Work group productivity

Overview

The Management Information Systems department at The University of Arizona has been developing an electronic meeting system (EMS) for several years. By 1985, Arizona had established a dedicated workgroup facility – PLEX-Center – and had coded network-based software to enhance the effectiveness of meetings. Since 1985, an experience base has been developed during use of the prototype EMS system in actual group meetings. Evaluation of those group meetings has indicated that the system raised both the participants' level of satisfaction with the meeting process and the quality of outcomes of the electronic meetings [2].

The EMS knowledge accrual process [17] has been uneven. The underlying theory can be traced back through Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) [5] to Decision Support Systems (DSS) [28] to Systems Analysis and Design Techniques (SADT) [4]. However, while experience was accumulating in research settings [6] [2] [16], [19], empirical results from application of the technology in field settings to provide a balance were lacking.

The problems were many. When they began the study at IBM, Arizona researchers could only speculate that the electronic system they had created would perform in the field as it had at their PLEXCenter laboratory. It was unknown whether or not the reactions they had observed were influenced by the obtrusive data collection process or by groups' having been removed from their daily business settings and brought to Tucson for their meetings. No hard results using consistent measures of meeting performance in business settings existed to demonstrate cost justification for installing electronic work group systems and facilities. Finally, no comparisons with former research on group work could be demonstrated. It therefore was not known precisely what factors might have influenced the experimental work groups' perceptions of satisfaction.

The purpose of this paper is to present the results of an exploratory field study that was designed to address the questions raised by the characteristics of experiential results at PLEX-Center. The research was conducted as part of a project funded by International Business Machines Corporation, one of whose planning groups, composed of the site managers from IBM's System Integration Division, was among the first to have used Arizona's EMS. After this group had used the system and facilities in Tucson for their annual strategic planning session, IBM decided to build a work group facility of its own and to install the software system at one of its sites. A joint research contract was signed, the site was chosen, and implementation was begun [22]. These findings are from that implementation.

## 1. Electronic Meeting System History

The basic component of this research is an Electronic Meeting System (EMS), which by definition provides an information systems infrastructure to support work gross and the activities they use in their meetings [14].

A survey of systems analysis and design techniques (SADT) provides a history of methodologies that have been shown to be usable for structuring exchange of work group information in the information systems field. Meetings held by analysis and design groups exhibit the classic activities, processes, and debilitating factors of work groups. These groups need a high level of information integration and are composed of members with varying backgrounds. Users, programmers, systems analysts, and members of management are typically represented, with each specialty bringing its own expertise, its own information on means and outcome preferences, and its own reasons for attending the meeting. These groups operate to reduce the unfamiliarity and uncertainty of the problem domain concerning a desired information system and pass on a well-defined system definition statement to the programming department. Only recently has the use of the techniques begun to include emphasis of the structure of the meeting process. PLEXSYS is one example of an analysis and design technique that incorporates meeting activities by which groups can arrive at a well-defined problem.

Originally, PLEXSYS was “designed as an analyst’s and user’s workbench to facilitate the development of information systems” [4], [11], [12], [16], [21], [32] that includes the following principles: the requirement of a memory for the development process, no predefined procedural invocation schedule, “translation of a very high level descriptive statement into executable code for target hardware/software configuration,” multiple forms of specification for various cognitive styles, and continuous feedback. Under the PLEXSYS research program at The University of Arizona, the early work concentrated on structuring information and encoding it into an electronic memory. [13] [29]

The concepts of computer augmented support for groups have received increasing emphasis in the Management Information Systems literature. Arizona's EMS system addresses the structuring of the meeting process as well as the structuring of information. Arizona's system evolved more quickly than other SADTs in that a set of electronic tools has been developed to help facilitate the broad range of work group activities necessary for systems analysis and design. Under the direction of Dr. J.F. Nunamaker, Jr., there has been a significant change in emphasis. Group oriented electronic tools have been developed to structure the meeting process in such a way as to help group members exchange information. This approach posits that the information generated by a group is more than the simple summation of the information provided by the individuals participating in the group.

## 1.1. EMS Tools

The EMS software has been developed with the facilitation of group processes in mind. The system positions individual problem solving as a subset of group problem solving. By design, the software facilitates both individual and group problem solving. It allows the participants flexibility to use individual problem-solving techniques within the context of a group but it also recognizes that groups must operate as groups and provides appropriate process modules for the group problem-solving process. The four primary tools used in the study are detailed below.

Electronic Brainstorming. This tool is designed to assist the activity of idea generation by a group. Brainstorming reputedly originated in meetings held by Walt Disney at his studios in the 1920s [9]. The process was popularized by Osborn in the 1950s and gained wide acceptance due to its flexibility and ease of use. In his book Applied Imagination, Osborn [24] cites basic rules for the participants in a brainstorming session – number, combination and rearrangement. Whiting [31] suggests additional guidelines for using the technique and provides a transcription of a typical session in his book.

The Electronic Brainstorming (EBS) process moves this popular technique into the automated EMS' environment by using available microcomputer networking technology as the medium for the expression of ideas. The code of this software tool uses a callable text editor to allow each participant to read previous comments from an electronic file and then append his or her new comment to that same file. Once a comment is completed, the software interchanges the files and obtains another for the participant to read and comment upon. The participant studies the new file and uses it to stimulate further comment. The software manages this process for all participants, trading and exchanging the files back and forth between participants during the session.

EBS has enhanced the original process and added several advantageous characteristics. Quietly entering comments at a computer keyboard brings a higher level of anonymity to the process than the original verbal execution. A higher level of anonymity will amplify “free-wheeling” and at the same time reduce the effects of a judgmental comment, since the judgment will not become public until after the session. While pet phrases and expressions may disclose a commentator’s identity, use of the word-processor eliminates use of handwriting, again heightening member anonymity and reducing the psychological stress $[9]$ associated with sharing one’s thoughts verbally. Each electronic file can be envisioned as a separate conversation. Thus, at any point in time, each group member is participating in his or her own discussion. The resulting parallel conversations represent a major advancement of Osborn’s quantity rule $[21]$ .

The electronic format also enables participants to channel energy toward the combination and rearrangement rule. Since the software allows the participants to read and review previous comments before making their own, a better individual synthesis of the comments should be accomplished. Additionally, because all comments during the session are recorded and stored in electronic form, no comments are lost. The software does not miss comments nor does it filter them in any way. All comments remain unedited and even their context is retained by maintaining the absolute order in which the comments are entered. An additional advantage is that the electronic files are easily merged, stored and printed.

Issue Analysis. The Issue Analysis (IA) tool is a collection of three problem-solving modules which allow the participants in a group to identify, edit, and rank a list of categories or focus items for a planning session. This tool works in the analysis process phase or the idea structuring mode of problem solving, where large quantities of information are refined into a set of alternatives. In his book, The Systems Approach [3], Churchman suggests such a list of alternatives be viewed as one of the basic components for planning.

Using the first module of Issue Analysis, each individual reviews the contents of a set of files from which each participant identifies a list of one-line categories he or she considers pertinent to the group's problem domain. The software's second module moves the group problem-solving process to a group analysis phase by combining the individual lists and displaying the merged list for review by the whole group. The final module of Issue Analysis functions as the group synthesis process, using the rank ordering option of the voting procedures module.

Voting Procedures. The voting module allows the facilitator to initiate a ballot for anonymous voting by the individual participants. The tool includes a selection of five different voting methods; binary, yes, no; five-point Likert; 1 to 10 scaling; multiple choice; forced-order listing: [1] [8] [25] [30]. The phrasing of the voting topic remains critical and the facilitator is responsible for matching the wording in the topic to the appropriate voting method. Raw group statistics are reported and saved for reference (to support the system's anonymity, individual votes during a session are currently not saved).

Proposal Formulation. Proposal Formulation and its companion tools, Session Dictionary and Survey, exemplify a class of tools which help bring a group to agreement on nomenclature and wording for the planning problem domain $[15]$ , $[18]$ and $[23]$ . Each variation of the process allows the participant to edit his or her own version of a topic. The topic, which may be a broad policy statement or single word, can be determined in several ways. First, it may be predefined, as in the case of a “strawman” policy statement which the group is required to review and edit. An alternative way to determine the topic is “on the fly,” when some statement or issue that needs to be addressed is raised during a session.

## 2. Research Model

The research model for this study contains the six major constructs related to work group meetings: group, task, environment, infrastructure, actual meeting process, and outcomes. These are depicted in the somewhat temporal order. Figure 1 simply shows the elements of four constructs interacting with each other, implied by the "pitch-forked" arrow, to produce an actual meeting process, which in turn produces outcomes. Each construct offers its own perspective for research on work groups.

Work Group Characteristics. The work group construct deals with the characteristics of the group and draws its variables mainly from the area of group dynamics. The vast literature in this area has proposed and researched numerous potential variables. Shaw identifies as many as nine different theories which are being “adopted for the analysis of groups” [26]. He summarizes the basic variables and characteristics used in studies involving those theories. The characteristics listed below have been used in other research and were adopted in the UA model to help classify groups initially.

\- Group size has major consequences for group work. Although it is clear that the more people there are in a working group the more channels there are available for interaction, the effect of this variable on group performance has not been conclusively shown.

\- Groups demonstrate cohesiveness in at least two areas. One measures how well the group “sticks” together. In this study, the focus of group cohesiveness centers on the “friendliness of the group members to one another.” Just as cohesiveness can be measured at a social level, it also can be measured at an informational level. Group information – that available to the group – may be private or public. Private group information is known by only a single individual (or small subgroup), while public group information is known by all members (or a considerable majority). The more the group’s information tends to be private, the higher the level of overall unfamiliarity the group has with the total information.

\- Group structure deals with the internal organization of the group – its configuration and makeup. To capture the concept of structure for this study, variables concerning homogeneity, managerial height, and group width were measured. Homogeneity measures the ability of a group to be easily divided into subgroups whose members support the same basic positions and attitudes toward the problem domain. Managerial height captures the number of managerial levels represented by the members of the group. As managerial height measures the “tallness” of a group (in a managerial level perspective), group width measures the “breadth” or scope of groups by evaluating the number of different business functions or departments represented by the group members.

\- Group history plays a major role in classifying work groups. Group age, ongoing status and formality all make up attributes of a group's history. Different groups have different life expectancies – they are formed and disbanded over time. Task force groups disband quickly after making recommendations while other groups meet over a period of years. Group newness measures the length of time a group has existed. Whether or not a group is expected to be ongoing affects the interaction and interaction between group members. Members of ongoing groups have a major concern for the maintenance, survival, and effectiveness of the group. Finally, some groups are built formally by management, assigned specific tasks and charged to follow certain guidelines. Others are informal, formed simply because of a mutual need of the members. A variable called formality will operate to measure this concept.

Work Group Task Characteristics. Characteristics of the work group's task construct emphasize the problem domain assigned to the group. As was the case in the work group construct, research concerning task variables has been extensive [10] [26]. The study at IBM pulls the notion of some of those variables from that body of research.

\- Task size is one major characteristic concerning a task. Whether a task is large or small is relative to the environment in which the work group operates, as measured by the number of man-hours anticipated necessary to complete the task.

\- A measure of the difficulty of a task can be obtained by measuring task complexity, task clarity, and adequate knowledge of the task on the part of the group. A simple measure of task complexity is the number of interrelated activities required to complete the task. The greater the number of tasks, the greater the complexity and more difficult a problem domain. How clearly and concisely the group's manager states a task provides a measure of task clarity. As the knowledge domain of a work group expands over the problem domain, the greater the likelihood of a good viable solution grows and task difficulty consequently is reduced. The final component of difficulty, task knowledge, is measured by determining how likely it is that the individuals in a group had/have adequate knowledge to accomplish the task.

\- An important characteristic of a task is the cooperation requirements it forces upon its group members. A task may have a large number of interrelated activities but require little interaction between members of the group – as in a production line where the interface is the product. Therefore the degree to which the integrated action of all group members is required to complete the assigned task is measured and referred to as the task variable – cooperative requirements.

\- One categorization of task types noted by Ivancevich et al. [19] evaluates tasks as production oriented, evaluative, or problem solving. A production oriented task is one which requires the generation and discussion of ideas.

Evaluative tasks require the group to evaluate a set of alternative issues. Finally, if the group needs to determine a course of action, the task is one of problem solving. Instead of making this a mutually exclusive list, the study asked for evaluations of the group tasks in all three categories.

Organizational Environment Characteristics. This construct works to classify the overall environment in which the work group operates. The organizational environment construct includes such variables as organization size, managerial hierarchy, primary business of the organization, organizational strategy, economic well-being of the firm, organizational policies and procedures, and even the position of the company in the industry. In this study, this construct was limited to an implementation at a single site, so these characteristics remained fairly constant.

Work Group Infrastructure. The EMS tools represent an electronic work group infrastructure that contains the salient variable of interest for this research. According to Webster's dictionary, an infrastructure is an underlying base or structure. For our study, the underlying base refers to the environment in which groups hold their meetings. It can be either manual or electronic.

Meeting Process. Meeting process mapping is a term used to mean the documentation and mapping of a current group's planning activities to an appropriate set of EMS tools. Documentation of the site's current planning process is undertaken by reviewing such physical documents as training manuals, transcribed minutes of actual planning meetings, and final reports of previous plans. Interviews with planning leaders or facilitators, questioning group members and observations of planning sessions, provide additional sources of documentation about the group planning process. Outcome Characteristics. The outcome construct relies almost exclusively on the research question to identify its underlying variables. For the IBM study this category measured characteristics and perspectives concerned with performance over multiple levels, including meeting, project, and site.

\- It seems reasonable that any positive effects on meetings, such as lower costs or better meeting processes, would accumulate over time to a project level. The property project length represents the estimated elapsed time between the start and end dates of the project, measured in hours.

\- Projects, as described, will usually have multiple work group meetings. The number of meetings necessary to complete a project provided a second project level variable.

\- The length of a meeting provides one meeting variable to measure. This property represents how long the members of a group are actually together in the meeting process.

\- In this research, attempts were made to capture the administrative costs of meetings, which included activities such as taking minutes, final report writing, and distributing information.

\- Man-hour dollar costs are probably the largest single cost identified and associated with group meetings and therefore represent the most likely place to make a great impact. The length of a meeting and number of people in a group meeting can be multiplied to obtain the man-hours billed to a meeting. The organization's hourly load factor was multiplied by the man-hours to produce a dollar cost figure for a meeting.

## 3. The study

## 3.1. Target Group Selection

The site under study was a manufacturing plant in the System Integration Division (formerly Federal Systems Division) of IBM, which maintains several such sites throughout the country.

The one used in this study is located at Owego, New York in a rural area of the northeastern United States. The plant is large enough to function as an entity having its own security department, running its own backup power plant, and operating its own water treatment plant. It employs around 5,000 people and constitutes the major employer and economic base for the local community.

In the past, employees at this site conducted the major portion of circuit board manufacturing for the parent company. This meant that long production runs of standard generic circuit boards for the electronics industry were commonplace. Now, because the circuit boards the plant produces are limited-production, small-lot, and special-order, management in charge of this site has rented office space outside the plant for research and accounting personnel and found alternative business functions in which to specialize, including government sponsored projects, robotics, local area network testing, and IS systems development.

Within the subject organization, a quality circle technique has site-wide usage as a group problem identification and solving activity. High level managers were brought to the site EMS facility and asked to use the software to brainstorm on the question, “What subject areas would these [EMS] tools be appropriate for within your business area?” The managers then ranked the areas and assigned the problems to group leaders for solutions.

Group members were selected based upon their likely contribution to solving the task. Group membership was not restricted and was as likely to include production floor workers as management executives. The groups participating in these sessions brought with them myriad problem domains, ranging from requirements analysis to vendor negotiations to site project planning. In summary, the groups used in this study were functional work groups formed and assigned to address an identified task as a part of their job assignment.

## 3.2. Data Collection

The data were gathered from three sources: experiences, group leaders, and meeting facilitators. Data were gathered during the installation and implementation to record the impact of the implementation on the technology. After the EMS system was installed at the site, a case profile for performance data was constructed from five of the first groups which used the systems. Then, over the next 6 months, a set of performance data along with group and task characteristics were collected concerning 59 sessions which used the EMS system.

In general, group leaders were asked to plan the group activities necessary to complete the assigned project prior to the group's first meeting using the work group software. Activities included determining the number of people in the group, number of meetings, calendar time needed to complete the task, and anticipated administration costs. After the group's exposure to the system, the leaders were asked to re-plan the group activities. Additional impressions and responses were solicited in a free-form manner. Any differences between the results of the activities were attributed to exposure to the tools and their impact on the work group productivity as perceived by the group leader. After the meeting, the facilitators filled out a questionnaire (detailed in the Appendix) concerning the characteristics of the group and task identified earlier. The productivity measures and characteristics were reported using a unique session number. The case profile in Table 1 is a template for the data recorded. Comments and suggestions made by the group members and leaders were also documented. The questionnaire data were filed for 59 groups and coordinated with the case profile data obtained from the group leaders to produce the information presented next.

## 4. Discussion of effects

The effects of the implementation can be defined by its impact on the technology, the performance of the work groups, the change in group and task characteristics, and any observable impacts on the organization. Each of these impacts is discussed below.

## 4.1. Impact on Technology

There was no reason to expect that the EMS technology would affect the meeting process without the meeting process having a corresponding effect on the technology. In fact, a software engineering perspective acknowledges this complementary effect by incorporating the change process into the lifecycle. During the course of the initial-visit-to-installation time period, several changes were made to the EMS software. Some resulted from suggestions from the site team, others from the users at the site, and still others from ongoing experiments at Arizona. The changes made are indicators of the impact the implementation had on the technology. These are summarized in the subsections below.

Menus. The facilitator menu system originally provided with the EMS software was oriented first toward the initialization of the tools and second to the data accumulation process. This design required the facilitator to move back and forth between the screens, regardless of which tool was operating.

Changing the menus to a tool orientation was suggested. Trade-offs between effective use and code redundancy were considered and the suggestion implemented so that under the opening submenu each tool had its own sub-menu, from which all processes for that tool were available. This led to redundant calls to system functions but seems to have been cognitively cleaner and more effective for the facilitators.

Screens. Cosmetic changes were made to the screens. Displaying only the most frequently used function keys and using the borders to display titles and instructions were two such modifications. These save screen space, an important consideration for interactive programs. Cognitive processing enhancements were brought into the system by standardizing the background color of text windows and restructuring the help screens. The standardization of the text windows produced a major enhancement of the system. For example, whenever a participant viewed a black background, he or she knew that the programs expected input. On the other hand, blue backgrounds defined “read only” windows, with no input expected.

The “help screens” also were given another possible cognitive enhancement. To help a user who needed an answer to a process-oriented question like, “How do I accomplish [process]?” the help screen set-up was altered to display the processes as the left-side index to the right-side function keys, because it was felt such an arrangement would result in quicker responses. Remote Processing. Users who were “computer literate,” not new users, proved to be the ones who created the most trouble with the system.

Originally, the participants' computers had been set up to return to the Disk Operating System (DOS) command prompt, making it possible for the computer literate to explore the directory and subdirectory configurations, run alternate programs, and ruin the integrity of the system file structure. As a response to this problem, a general purpose “opening” screen program was written to lock the keyboard and place the machine's processor into an infinite loop, removing the participant's access to the underlying system. The facilitator's command to start a tool was required to break the loop and install the correct program into the participant's machine processor. Upon exiting from a tool, the participant's screen was programmed to return to the “opening” screen. Tool start-up, data accumulation, and every other computer operation for a meeting thus could be handled electronically, freeing the participants to concentrate on the problem.

New Programs. Two new programs were constructed in response to suggestions made during the process mapping of the initial visit. One, a log-in program, prompted the user to supply an identification number as had been requested by the site team. In the past, there had been little formal record keeping of who attended a particular meeting. The simple expedient of requiring a log-in “tracked” participation in a group and recorded attendance for the final meeting report.

One of the site's popular group activities had been a technique called the 4M method or “fishbone.” Using this technique, a group decomposes a problem into four main subproblem areas: Manpower, Machines, Money, and Methods. Each list of subproblems assigned to each area is ranked and reviewed. The subproblem designated most important on each of these four ranked lists becomes the problem for the next iteration of the technique. Again, the new problem is decomposed, 4M lists generated, and the most important subproblem established. Iteration of this process continues until the subproblem identified is at a level where direct action can be taken to solve it. This technique explores the problem domain in a structured fashion, which ultimately enables a group to explore the feasibility of a particular action and produce a plan of action.

The usefulness of this technique was apparent, as was its applicability to electronic processing. An EMS software tool that brings up identical screens on all participant and facilitator monitors and contains six windows labeled Problem, Machine, Money, Methods, Manpower, and Notebook was created and implemented at the site. Using it, each participant could select one of the windows in which to edit. After saving his or her comment in that window, the user could return to the menu and choose another topic area for comment. All participants entered their input simultaneously.

Summary of the Impact on Technology. Insights concerning the implementation's impact on technology that resulted from experiences gained during the implementation at the subject site prompted changes to the system. Orienting the menus to the tools provided a more manageable system for the facilitator. On-site facilitators found this format simple and easy to work with. Minimizing the need for on-screen help and reducing the number of function keys to those required helped keep the screen uncluttered. Standardized background and foreground colors were found to reinforce visually the recognition of the current mode (edit or read-only) of the tool. Help screens were determined to be more useful if process-indexed than if key-indexed. Numerous advantages were gained from the remote processing of the tool models. The amount of “hacking” possible for computer literate persons was limited and facilitator control of the sessions was enhanced. Finally, the recovery of the system from user mistakes and hardware failures was made easier.

## 4.2. Impact on Performance

This section extends the data and furthers the analysis reported in an earlier paper $[22]$ . First, additional data points have been included raising the total from 29 to 64. Second, detailed case discussions of the various types of meetings from which the data was received are reported. Actual user comments and feedback are included. Finally, the complete data set generates a foundation for the analysis of group and task impacts.

This section describes the software implementation's impact on performance as determined by measuring the difference between the group leader estimates pre-system and those generated after the use of the system. While the cases reported here are somewhat disguised to protect the content – or the detailed information generated in the sessions – the raw data are accurately reported. Five cases are reported in detail to demonstrate the variance in group problem domains to which the EMS software was applied and to suggest the atmosphere in which these activities took place; Table 2 summarizes these five. Case 1 – Systems Requirements Case: No other company sites are located near the site under study. In all, more than 1000 persons classified as systems programmers work at the three sites. At the subject site, an existing work group had been trying to define system programming adequately, with the further intention of automating the systems programming process. In the past, very little progress had been made toward the goal of an adequate definition because no meeting technique proved adequate to consolidate the activities of the groups from all three sites. The site team conducted its preliminary marketing awareness presentation and facilitated a meeting of the study site work group. The work group attended a two-hour session and generated a document of more than 250 EBS comments which was stored and made accessible through the Issue Analysis file menu. A group with members from all three sites was then called in for a session and finally succeeded in producing the definition.

Case 2 - Systems Programmer Definition Case: One of the subtasks of the systems programmer exchange case was to reach agreement on a working definition of a job description for a systems programmer. A subgroup from the systems requirement main group, detailed above, was assigned this task. Group members were selected from three sites and a meeting was set up for this specific purpose.

Case 3 – Vendor Negotiations Case: The site had employed an outside vendor to document the site's computer systems. For several months, site staff and the vendor had been exchanging accusations regarding responsibility for the completion of the tasks. The site claimed no product had been delivered, while the vendor claimed the site needed to establish firm guidelines and procedures for the product before the documentation could be accomplished. Little had been done. The site team recommended that “vendor people” and “site people” join together in a single work group which would be given the task of “negotiating an algorithm for documentation” and selecting the EMS tools needed for that task. After a four-hour planning session, all work group members were satisfied that the project would move forward.

Case 4 – Departmental Planning Case: Each year, the departments at the subject site must go through a series of planning meetings to establish goals and objectives for the next year. The previous year's goals and objectives are reviewed and the current status of the department's mission presented as a preface to the meeting. This year, the Information Systems department conducted its sessions using the site's decision room and the EMS software.

Case 5 – Policy and Procedure Review Case: The Information Systems department at the site has a detailed process for I/S planning which included procedures and guidelines which must be followed by anyone requesting an information system. Because the justification underlying organizational policies may disappear, over the years it is important for organizations to update or even drop policies and procedures if no valid underlying justification for them can be found. The department decided to review its policies and procedures, making use of the decision laboratory and the EMS software for its meeting.

Summary of Initial Cases: All group leaders reported savings in man-hours with the major portion of the decrease being a reduction in meeting hours as opposed to administrative hours (Table 2). In addition, fewer actual meetings were reported to be needed. In all five cases, the group leaders anticipated the need for fewer face-to-face meetings after the use of the software.

Again in Table 2, the group leaders reported a reduction in the length of time necessary to accomplish the project once the EMS software was employed through the decision lab. Estimates of the reduction in project calendar time ranged from 51% to 83%. Elimination of meetings deemed non-essential resulted in a total savings of 478 hours. As an example, using a \$50.00 per Person per hour overhead charge the total estimated dollar savings in these five cases was \$23,900. In all cases, both the number of physical meetings and the man-hour costs of the project were reduced. Four of the five group leaders also reported a reduction in their administrative costs. In one case the number of people attending the meeting actually increased, although it should be pointed out that a second group leader found it significant to be able to incorporate work from another group as part of his own meeting.

In light of the above, it is significant that total meeting hours decreased even though two important factors in determining meeting hours (group size and the length of the meeting) had a tendency to increase. Summary of All Cases. Over a period of 11 months, the site supplied data from 59 additional meetings, analysis of which produced the overall performance results presented in Table 3.

The research case results provide strong support for believing that the use of automated planning tools will improve the performance of work groups. First, fifty-eight (58) of the additional cases reported a reduction in the time estimated for completing the projects; only one did not, and it reported no change. A matched-pairs test comparing pre and post man-hour estimates proved significant at the 0.0001 level. Results show that man-hour costs fell an average of 51% in all the cases reported and those reporting calendar time estimates showed an average reduction of 89%. Together, these data suggest that the value of an hour of meeting time using the automated tools increased for these groups. Comparison of the estimated and the revised case parameters shows the value of an automated work-group hour to be equal to 2.32 manual work group hours. Whether this was because the groups focused more precisely on solving the problem or actually accomplished more work has yet to be determined.

## 4.3. Impacts on Group and Task

Grounded theory suggests a continued exploration of collected data $[7]$ . The data collected in this study can provide insight into the impact of the EMS implementation on task and group characteristics. The data were categorized into three sets of projects; 20, 20, and 19, segmented by time (five cases had incomplete data). Analysis by time phase provided insights into how the characteristics of groups and tasks (those tasks assigned to the software) changed over time. In other words, it helped in answering are there significant differences between the characteristics of groups using the EMS software over time.

Changes in nine of the fifteen characteristics used in measuring the group and task categories were significant across time. These changes presented in Table 4 must be interpreted carefully. There were only 59 cases, rigorous experimental control was not applied, and the data collection occurred during an implementation period which was filled with experimental complications. Changes represent only the differences in the characteristics of the groups and tasks assigned to use the EMS tools not differences in the same group brought on by exposure to the EMS tools. Reasons for making the assignments may be as complex as the site's “learning” about how the decision room works with different type groups and tasks or as simple as fulfilling a request for use of the lab. Either way, changes exist and they should be examined for insights concerning the implementation.

Both Unfamiliarity and Cohesion varied significantly across the three phases. The structure of the groups assigned to the decision room changed – both as to number of functions covered and as to an increase in the number of management levels represented.

Task. Of the three properties measuring task type – production, problem solving, and evaluative – only one, evaluative, changed significantly over time. The decrease in the rating on this property means that the tasks given later groups required a higher degree of issue evaluation than those assigned to early groups. Task clarity was the only other task property of significance and its rankings showed a significant increase over time.

To summarize the implementation's impact on tasks: the “later” tasks assigned to the groups using the decision room tended to be more evaluative in nature and to be more clearly and concisely stated than the tasks of the “early” groups.

It may be that in an effort to reduce the classical dysfunctions of manual meetings, electronic meetings systems may generate a whole new set of problems. 2. Higher levels of management have used the system and publicly championed its use.

4. IBM is distributing the technology internally to sites in the United States and Canada and making plans to distribute world wide.

## 5. Future Research

Any future research should try to sustain the software engineering approach to EMS system design. It is from implementation studies that observations concerning the practical, real-world uses of these systems can be added to the knowledge domain concerning electronic meeting systems. In these environments, observations can be made about the functioning of the system and changes suggested to enhance it. The experiential data generate new ideas for controlled experiments which in turn produce new generations of software systems.

In conclusion, the results of this field study help confirm what is already suspected – electronic environments for group meetings are different from manual environments. The results from the field also are consistent with the premise that electronic meeting systems can increase the level of performance of group work. The insights and results of this study also suggest many future directions in EMS research.

One of the most important research areas will be to study groups with large tasks and information sharing requirements, over time. This suggests that a new unit of analysis – the project – be added to those previously used: individual, meeting, and organization. If a project is defined as a collection of meetings (which may or may not be held by the same group) over a period of time (longer than a seminar) which demand a high level of information coordination, then the study of this unit of analysis may measure more fully the impact of EMS systems. Certainly, projects will inherit the savings (performance, effectiveness, efficiency) seen in individual meetings, but will they be simply cumulative or multiplicative?

If one accepts that the general purpose of a meeting is the efficient and effective sharing of information between group members, then the results of this study demonstrate the electronic environment to be a more appropriate channel (or infrastructure) for group communication than the manual one. Future research must use larger group-oriented tasks which require higher levels of information sharing to test if the electronic infrastructure is truly a better information sharing environment.

It may be that the electronic process is not particularly sensitive to group size, since it appears that adding members in the electronic environment did not seem to increase the dysfunctions associated with increasing the size of a group in a manual environment. This implies that the electronic environment can handle a much more intense level of group member participation than the manual one. Future studies should determine how to capitalize on this participation to help groups in ways not available within manual environments.

Finally, an EMS provides a new environment for an organization to integrate into its existing structure. This change may have a significant impact on group member behavior. Some observations reported that this environment may alter the role, status and “power” of group members. Studies should be undertaken to observe and monitor the impact of these environments on group roles.

All of these conjectures need further study. Conjecture: The electronic environment may increase the number of people who can effectively participate in a group meeting.

Conjecture: The electronic environment allows work groups to deal with larger tasks more effectively by increasing the number of people who can participate in a meeting.

Conjecture: Task size may be the greatest predictor of performance in an electronic environment.

Conjecture: Group size (up to 10 member groups) has no significant impact on work group performance in an electronic environment.

Conjecture: An electronic meeting environment supports groups whose members represent different functions and more management levels better than traditional environment.

Conjecture: Electronic meetings involve more intense participation than traditional meetings.

Conjecture: Group work software builds new “power” positions in groups and organizations.

## 6. Summary

In summary, an EMS is defined as an information systems infrastructure for work group meetings. Previously, the accumulation of knowledge concerning EMS emphasized theory, laboratory findings, and observations. This field study adds to the accumulation by describing the effects of an EMS field implementation in terms of its impact on the technology, work group performance, the characteristics of groups and tasks, and the organization.

The results of this study are directly applicable to real business-world groups, having been derived from groups functioning in their own environments not from laboratory groups. Impacts on the technology were experienced as a result of the changes required to basic assumptions concerning the menuing system, the screens, the basic start-stop operations of the software and by the new processes which the meeting process began to demand. The implementation's impact upon the performance of work groups is revealed in the man-hour data from fifty-nine groups, which showed an average reduction of $51\%$ while calendar time fell an average of $89\%$ for those groups reporting calendar times. Nine of the fifteen variables characterizing the groups and tasks assigned to use the EMS system changed significantly over the time span measured.

The concerns raised by facilitators and users at the site and the organization's acceptance based upon its “propensity to use” the software are examples of organizational impacts reported.

The insights and results of this study provide impetus for many future directions in EMS research. The paper proposes the continued use of the software engineering or design approach for accumulating applicable real-world results to complement the results from the laboratory. This study also suggests a new unit of analysis – the project – to evaluate the performance of group work oriented systems. Finally, the study contributes a set of conjectures concerning the future evaluation of electronic meeting systems based upon findings drawn from the real world.
