# Interactive versus stand-alone group decision support systems for stakeholder identification and assumption surfacing in small groups

Annette C. Easton

Information and Decision Systems Department, College of Business, San Diego State University, San Diego, CA 92182, USA

Douglas R. Vogel and Jay F. Nunamaker, Jr.

Management Information Systems Department, College of Business, University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721, USA

As we begin to see evidence that Group Decision Support Systems can be beneficial, it becomes increasingly important to investigate the effect of different GDSS designs on decision making processes. This study investigated the effect of a GDSS based on the Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing model for strategic planning impact analysis. A controlled laboratory experiment compared the results of 4-person groups that had either Stand-Alone GDSS support (one personal computer per group) or Interactive GDSS support (4 networked personal computers per group). Measures were taken on decision outcomes (quality, time and satisfaction with the outcomes) and decision process variables (quantity of unique alternatives, distribution of individual participation, and satisfaction with the process). We found that the interactive GDSS groups generated a higher number of unique ideas and had more even participation rates. However they were less satisfied with both the process and the outcomes. We also found no significant difference between the groups in terms of decision quality and decision time.

Keywords: Group decision support systems, Group process and outcomes, stakeholder analysis, Computer supported meetings, Computer supported groups, Computer supported collaborative work, Computer supported work, Strategic planning.

## 1. Introduction

As groups today spend an increasing amount of meeting time sorting, analyzing, regrouping, and distributing data, the need for methods to better process that data has become more evident. Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) increasingly are used to assist workers involved in group meetings. GDSS aim to improve the process of group decision making by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for structuring decision analysis, and systematically directing the pattern, timing, or content of the discussion $[5]$ .

It is anticipated that the natural decision process of a group will be more affected as the sophistication of the GDSS technology increases. This research is a follow-up study to an earlier investigation that compared the effect of three processes (a group decision support system, a structured manual process, and an unstructured manual process) on group performance and attitude $[6,7]$ .

This follow-up research paper addresses the following question:

How will the use of an Interactive Group Decision Support System, compared with a

Stand-Alone Group Decision Support System, affect group performance and attitude in decision making for strategic planning impact analysis?

The broad categories evaluated were the effect of the type of technological support on the decision process, and the effect on the decision outcomes. A lab study compared groups with interactive computer support and groups with standalone computer support. Data were collected through questionnaires, decision materials and videotape analysis.

The following section presents a review of experimental GDSS literature applicable to this study. The remaining sections, in order, discuss the GDSS model, the research methodology, the results, their meaning, and our conclusions.

There are many contradictions in the results of these prior studies. Many factors could have caused the conflicting results noticed in this earlier research. The GDSS Decision Rooms used have varied architectural designs, room configurations, display capabilities and system software $[4]$ . Additionally differences in the group composition, the task, and the structure of the process could have caused any of the differences. A question that follows these studies is: Are the benefits, if they exist, derived from the decision process being structured, or from the structured process being computerized?

In an attempt to answer this question an earlier GDSS study $[7]$ was conducted to investigate the effects of three processes; group decision support systems, structured manual support, and no support, on decision process and decision outcome variables. This earlier study found that the use of the Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing structure, whether automated or not, led to higher quality decisions, the generation of more unique ideas, and a more even distribution of participation. The use of this structure also increased the amount of time to complete the task. The only differences found between the automated and manual structure treatments were that the automated groups were more satisfied with the process and the outcomes. For a more detailed development of this prior research see Easton $[7]$ . With these results, we decided to investigate the effect of a different design of the GDSS; an interactive version.

This study is a first attempt in investigating two different designs of a GDSS. The research used groups of four persons to conduct an impact analysis of a policy statement. The GDSS used is based upon the Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing Model (SAST) [15,18]. SAST has been used by its developers and has proven useful in planning by groups. Although this previous work was with non-computerized usage, it provides a history for use of this model. The task, analyzing the consequences of a policy statement, is also realistic. Organizations must be concerned about the consequences of a policy under consideration. The realism of the task addresses a criticism of some laboratory studies, the use of a trivial task.

## 3. SAST model and GDSS design

Both of the GDSSs tested in this study are based around the SAST process. SAST is a participative planning process designed to help identify the stakeholders and their assumptions regarding an organization. It has been found to be helpful in uncovering the critical assumptions that underlie policies, plans, and strategies. It also helps managers make better judgments concerning the reasonableness of their assumptions $[15]$ .

The Stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (SIAS) GDSS electronically accomplishes tasks similar to those performed in SAST. SIAS was originally developed as a standalone tool that can be operated by a group member, or a facilitator. In this stand-alone mode, the group has one terminal into which the input is entered. Suggestions of ideas, discussions and voting occur in a traditional face-to-face setting. The SIAS software has four main phases; (1) Identify Stakeholders, (2) Surface Assumptions, (3) Rate Assumptions, and (4) Graph Assumptions.

During the Identify Stakeholders phase the group generates their list of stakeholders who will be affected by or who will affect the policy statement.

The Surface Assumption phase allows the group to identify the assumptions that the stakeholders hold about the policy statement. The software prompts the user to proceed through the list of stakeholders in an orderly manner. The software also provides the capability to adjust the stakeholder list during this phase.

The Rate Assumptions phase is the third task the group performs. The group rates each assumption according to its importance to the stakeholder and its importance to the policy statement. The rating scale is from zero to ten, with ten being the most important. The ratings of assumptions are not mutually exclusive, more than one assumption can have the same rating. Additionally, each assumption is classified as either supporting the policy or resisting the policy.

The final phase is Graph Assumptions. A graph displays the assumptions according to their ratings of importance to the stakeholder and importance to the policy. While viewing this graph, the group identifies the most important assumptions that need attention to ensure that the policy will not fail. Assumptions with high importance to the stakeholder and high importance to the policy are likely to become bedrock assumptions. Assumptions with high importance to one rating and low importance to the other should be carefully reviewed as they may represent potential problems. In addition, these assumptions may be used as bargaining chips. Assumptions with low importance to both ratings are likely to be disregarded. Changes in the ratings or text are performed now.

The outcome of SIAS is a list of the stakeholders and their assumptions deemed important concerning the policy decision being considered for implementation. Reports generated by supporting software include a list of all stakeholders and assumptions, a list of stakeholders and assumptions by quadrant, and a list of supporting/resisting stakeholders and assumptions.

After the development of SIAS as a standalone tool, an interactive version of the software was created. Interactive Stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (I-SIAS) is a networked version of SIAS, with each member using a personal computer. The basic functionality of I-SIAS is similar to SIAS. However differences do exist. I-SIAS alters the communication medium by providing simultaneous and anonymous input of ideas. Each member is able to enter their ideas anonymously. The software continuously collects and displays the individual input on a large screen at the front of the room. An additional difference with this tool is a change in the process. In SIAS when an individual suggested an idea, discussion occurred immediately to clarify the meaning, or sometimes to question the validity of the suggestion. In I-SIAS a separate face-to-face communication phase for consolidation of the list (to remove duplicates) and discussion of the ideas was needed. This occurred after input for both the Identify Stakeholder and Surface Assumption phases had ceased. Additionally, in I-SIAS each person casts an individual vote for each item. The software then averages the votes, arriving at one final group vote. In SIAS, the group vote was obtained by a round robin polling of the group members. Since all communication is oral, members may be influenced by another member's vote.

## 4. Research methodology

## 4.1. Research design

The design upon which this study was based involved a random assignment of groups to one of two experimental situations: interactive group decision support or stand-alone group decision support. The subjects were assigned randomly to groups. There were six groups in each treatment, resulting in a sample size of 12 groups. The group size was four members. The research participants were junior and senior business students from the University of Arizona. The average age was 22.35 years. 71% of the subjects were male and 29% were female. The participants received extra course credit for participation in the experiment. While some of the group members had worked together previously, others had not. This simulates a practical situation where group members may have worked together previously.

![](/api/attachments/H4REEFB3/fulltext/images/6bc4cd16898f6b8dbe06354f805926d1e535c2c7ce9bae7c651234ac7f9eed56.jpg)  
Fig. 3. SIAS graph results.

The decision task was constant across both treatments. The task selected for this research was to perform an impact analysis of a policy statement. Groups were required to analyze the impact of having a policy requiring undergraduate business students to have individual access to a personal computer for admission to a business college. The groups were instructed to derive a concise list of the most critical issues that the policy makers should consider in order to ensure success upon implementation of this policy.

## 4.2. Research variables

The nature of technological support was the only independent variable. This variable was manipulated by providing either stand-alone GDSS support or Interactive GDSS support.

The dependent variables measured the process and the outcomes of the decision making session. The decision process was investigated in terms of quantity of unique alternatives, distribution of individual participation, and process satisfaction. The specific outcomes of the decision investigated were decision quality, decision time, and decision satisfaction. The prior research related to the importance of these variables is discussed with the results in Section 6.

Quantity of Unique Alternatives was determined by counting the number of stakeholders and assumptions generated by each group.

Distribution of Individual Participation was obtained by analyzing audio-video recordings of each experimental session to count the number of comments presented by each individual member of a group. The percentage of participation was then calculated by dividing an individual's total comments by the group's total comments. If all members participated equally, their percentages would all equal 25. To find the amount of variability in each group from this mean value, each individual's percentage was subtracted from 25. The absolute value of this deviation was then used as a measure of distribution of participation. Process satisfaction was recorded on a self-report, post-session questionnaire given to each group member. The questionnaire had been previously developed and tested [9] and has been used in GDSS related research at the University of Minnesota [22,23].

Decision quality was measured for each group's solution based on the number of stakeholders and assumptions that they identified that were also on a predetermined solution list. The higher the quality number, the more completely the group covered the list of valid stakeholders and assumptions; indicating a higher quality solution. Decision time was the length of time it took each group to complete the task. The maximum time allowed was 90 minutes.

Decision Satisfaction was recorded on the same self-report questionnaire used to measure process satisfaction.

## 4.4. Procedures

The meetings for all the sessions were held at the University of Arizona's MIS Department PlexCenter. All groups followed the same basic guidelines for the experiment. The subjects filled out a pre-session questionnaire, were read instructions by the facilitator, and were given five minutes to read a copy of the policy statement. The groups then began the experiment. When they had completed their concise list of stakeholders and assumptions they notified the facilitator. They filled out a post-session questionnaire and were debriefed. All the sessions were recorded on audio-video tape.

In both treatments the facilitator was present to aid the participants in the use of the tool and to guide the groups through the decision making process. The facilitator did not provide advice to assist the group in reaching a decision.

## 5. Results

Our data were analyzed using the SPSS-PC + ONEWAY analysis of variance (ANOVA). The statistical results are presented in terms of the decision process variables and decision outcome variables. Results were considered significant at the 0.05 level. Section 5.3 describes the additional observations of the studies. The interpretation of these findings will be discussed in Section 6.

## 5.1. Decision process variables

Quantity of Unique Alternatives - The I-SIAS groups identified more alternatives than the SIAS groups (p = 0.030).

Distribution of Individual Participation - The I-SIAS groups had a more equal measure of participation compared with the SIAS groups (p = 0.001).

User Satisfaction with Decision Process - The I-SIAS groups were less satisfied than the SIAS groups (p = 0.004).

## 5.2. Decision outcome variables

Decision Quality - There was no significant difference between the I-SIAS and SIAS groups.

Decision Time - There was no significant difference between the I-SIAS and SIAS groups.

User Satisfaction with Decision Outcome - The I-SIAS groups were less satisfied than the SIAS groups (p = 0.051).

## 5.3. Observations

Several observations were made on the behavior of the groups during the experiments based on review of text and videotapes.

In the Interactive-SIAS process a phase for consolidation of ideas was added after the Stakeholder Identification Phase and again after the Assumption Surfacing phase. This was necessary to remove duplicate ideas, and to clarify vague ideas. This proved to be a difficult task. It appeared that once an idea was in writing, the group resisted removing it from the list. In the Stand-alone SIAS the ideas were discussed and consolidated at the time of their suggestion since it was a face-to-face input of ideas. Members in this treatment appeared to be more likely to agree that an idea was the same as one already listed, or to agree that the idea was not relevant than groups in the Interactive-SIAS treatment.

The second observation dealt with the Voting Phase. The Interactive-SIAS groups had approximately 60 items on which they had to vote. Each vote consisted of three key strokes: one for the importance to stakeholder vote; one for the importance to the policy vote; and one for the supporting or resisting assumption vote. It appeared that this led to much frustration. Members spent a considerable amount of time in this phase, and there was a time difference among members in completing this phase. This led to several members of the group sitting with nothing to do while the rest of the group finished their voting. This could have led to the decrease in satisfaction with the process that the Interactive-SIAS groups expressed.

## 6. Discussion

## 6.1. Summary of results

The groups supported by the Interactive-SIAS generated a higher number of unique ideas and had a more even distribution of participation. However, they were less satisfied with both the decision process and the decision outcomes. The observational analysis revealed that the groups exposed to the I-SIAS treatment were less likely to consolidate their ideas and more frustrated with the voting phase.

## 6.2. Decision process

In our previous study we found that the groups supported by structure identified significantly more unique alternatives than groups not supported by structure. This is consistent with previous research $[14,19,20,21]$ . However we found no difference between the structured treatments. We hypothesized that a different type of GDSS that allows for simultaneous input of ideas or anonymous input from all participants may result in the GDSS treatments identifying significantly more ideas than the manual, support groups. This study did find that the groups using the Interactive SIAS did generate more ideas than the groups using SIAS.

In our previous study we also found that the groups supported by structure had more equal participation rates. Previous research $[8,14]$ suggests that structure does provide a framework that encourages all members to participate. We found no differences between the structured treatments. We hypothesized that a GDSS that significantly alters the communication medium from that which occurred in the structured manual treatments would result in differences in this measure. This study did find that the I-SIAS groups had more equal participation rates than the SIAS groups.

In our previous study we found no differences in user satisfaction with the decision process between the structured and unstructured treatments. Prior research $[20,21]$ found that groups supported by structure tend to be generally more satisfied than groups not supported by structure. This may be attributed to increased member participation afforded by a structured methodology. However, structured groups have been found to be less satisfied than the unstructured groups $[8,22]$ . Perhaps the type of structure being used has an effect on satisfaction. Some methodologies may be easier to understand and use, and may as a result be perceived as more or less satisfying. However, we found the groups supported by computer structure were more satisfied than the groups supported by the manual structure. This contradicts an earlier study that found that the GDSS groups were less satisfied than the manual supported groups $[22]$ . The differences between the studies may be because the computer provided an easier mechanism to record and analyze the data than the use of flip charts.

In this study we hypothesized that the I-SIAS groups would be more satisfied than the SIAS groups. This was not true. The I-SIAS groups were less satisfied than the SIAS groups. As noted in the observations (Section 5.3) the voting phase of I-SIAS created much frustration.

## 6.3. Decision outcome

In our earlier study we found that the structured groups produced a decision of a higher quality than the unstructured groups. This result is consistent with previous researchers $[3,12]$ . However, differences in the source of structure, manual or computerized, did not result in differences in quality. We hypothesized that this result may be affected in a GDSS that significantly alters the structured support from that which occurs in a comparable manual setting. This study found no differences between the I-SIAS and SIAS groups.

In our previous study we also found the decision time to be longer in the structured groups than in the unstructured groups. Previous research $[17]$ suggests that as member participation increases with structured approaches, decision times will also increase. This is consistent with prior GDSS research $[20,23]$ that found that the GDSS structure groups took longer than unstructured groups. There was no difference between the structured treatments. We hypothesized that the I-SIAS groups would take longer to complete the task as the I-SIAS is designed to increase member participation. However we found no differences between the I-SIAS and SIAS groups.

Our earlier study also found no differences in satisfaction with the decision outcomes between the structured and unstructured treatments. Reasoning similar to that applied for satisfaction with the decision process can also be applied here. Groups supported by structure are often more satisfied than unsupported groups $[20,21]$ . We did find the computer supported groups more satisfied than the manual supported groups. Earlier research $[22]$ found no difference in computer structure compared with manual structure. We hypothesized that the I-SIAS groups would be more satisfied than the SIAS groups. Our results found the I-SIAS groups less satisfied than the SIAS groups. With no differences in the quality of the decision, these results could be attributed to the problems noted earlier with the voting phase and also the decrease in face-to-face discussions.

## 6.4. Practical significance of the research

The differences that we found have implications not only for the use of an automated SAST methodology, but also for the design and use of GDSS in general.

The use of the SIAS tool for strategic planning impact analysis looks promising. Groups using the interactive version exhibited more equal participation and generated more ideas. Where these capabilities are desirable, the I-SIAS tool should prove beneficial.

Perhaps a more interesting finding is the importance of the design and use of the GDSS. The original SIAS was able to mimic closely the unautomated process. However after using the I-SIAS in this study we realized that certain changes in the process needed to be made in using a networked version. Since the technology allows for many more ideas to be captured, changes had to be made in the process to better manage these ideas. The facilitator or group leader must take an active role in getting the group to consolidate the list. This tended to be a problem because the ideas are submitted anonymously in the generation phases. During consolidation, members must own up to generating any idea if another member has a question about it. This problem must be dealt with if we are trying to use anonymity as a means to encourage submission of ideas. Another change that must occur with the process is in the voting phase. As more ideas are generated, more need to be voted on. Perhaps a better job in the consolidation phase will alleviate this problem. If not, this process also needs to be looked at to determine the best way to capture this information.

## 6.5. Strengths and limitations of the research

This study has several strengths that can be viewed as contributions of the research. The first is that the study measured key variables in a rigorous experimental setting. As this study was a follow-up, the combined experiments represent a comprehensive evaluation of the use of the SIAS process for an impact analysis.

The second major strength is the selection of the task and setting. This experiment used a “real” task, recurring more often in organizations. The task was appropriate for use with students as the decision makers since they had both a knowledge of and an interest in the solution. A third strength is in looking at a single task facilitated by two different approaches to GDSS. Additionally, the decision room used in the experiments is used by actual decision making groups. These strengths appear to outweigh the possible limitations of the study.

The use of a laboratory experiment in this research is the source of the first limitation; low external validity. Conclusions are limited to small groups, performing an impact analysis of a policy statement, using the SIAS methodology. While the methodology has been used successfully with “real” decision making groups, caution must be used in extending the results to different populations, settings, and situations.

## 6.6. Future research

Future research into the success of group decision support systems is necessary. This research has several possible extensions resulting from variations in controlled variables. One of the extensions involves varying the planning task to determine if the results are affected by the task. Perhaps a more complicated planning problem may result in different answers. Group size is another variable that should be manipulated. With a larger group some of the benefits or difficulties in the SIAS methodology may become more apparent. The usage of the GDSS is another factor that can be modified. For example, changes in the consolidation and voting phases may produce different results. Finally, research should be conducted using groups of actual decision makers.

## 7. Conclusions

This study adds to the expanding body of experimental research regarding the effectiveness of group decision support systems. The findings of our study provide mixed results about the benefits of an interactive GDSS. We found that the I-SIAS structure led to more equitable participation among members, and the generation of more unique alternatives, compared with the groups using the stand-alone SIAS. However, they were less satisfied and there was no difference in decision quality or time. These results, however, hold only for this particular task and for the SIAS GDSS.

We also found that as we begin to automate tasks, it will become very important to determine exactly how the process will function in an automated fashion. Designers and users of GDSS must learn the best way to create and use this new technology in order to reap the most benefit possible.
