# Group Decision Program A videodisc-based Group Decision Support System

Sorel Reisman, Thomas W. Johnson and Bronston T. Mayes

School of Business Administration and Economics, California State University, Fullerton, CA 92634, USA

In recent years group decision support systems have been developed to improve the process of group decision-making and improve its outcomes. This paper describes Group Decision Program (GDP), an interactive videodisc-based GDSS. GDP is a topic-independent system designed to provide a computer-based, structured process to direct the pattern, timing, and content used in group decision-making. Based on the structure of Nominal Group Technique (NGT), it is designed around a multi-phase process that includes an optional tutorial, idea collection, idea ranking, and report generation.

Keywords: Group decision support system, Interactive video-disc, Nominal group technique.

Decision-making groups are an integral part of virtually every organization. DeSanctis and Gallupe [5] have defined a decision-making group as two or more people who are jointly responsible for detecting a problem, generating and evaluating potential solutions, or formulating implementation plans.

Groups are usually delegated responsibility for matters considered too significant for any single individual. Ideally, they are able to use the capabilities brought to the group by each member as well as to generate greater enthusiasm and commitment by means of involvement. However, this potential is often unrealized because of limitations inherent in the structure and process of group decision-making. In recent years Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) have been developed to help groups overcome these limitations.

The purpose of this paper is to briefly review existing GDSS efforts and the lessons learned from experience with these systems. The paper will then describe Group Decision Program (GDP), a GDSS based on interactive videodisc technology.

## Emergence of Group Decision Support Systems

According to DeSanctis and Gallupe [5], group decision support systems are designed to improve the process of group decision-making by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for structuring decision analysis, and systematically directing the pattern, timing, or content of discussion. Improving the process of group decision making should result in improved outcomes of group efforts, including decision quality and timeliness, satisfaction with the decision, cost or ease of implementation, and the group's willingness to work together in the future.

Group decision support systems may incorporate a variety of features including electronic messaging, decision modeling methods (such as decision trees, risk analysis, forecasting methods, and multi-attribute utility functions), and structured group methods (such as Nominal Group or Delphi Techniques). A number of empirical investigations into the use of GDSS have been reported [1], [6], [7], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [16], [18].

## GDSS Efforts to Alter Group Process

Of particular interest for our purposes are those GDSS efforts which employ structured group methods. Although they vary considerably in their complexity, these systems explicitly alter the structure of interpersonal exchange in the group's decision-making process.

The pioneering effort to develop a GDSS employing structured group methods was reported by Turoff [17] who developed a computerized conferencing system based on a Delphi communication structure. Later, Turoff and Hiltz [18] experimentally evaluated a simple GDSS that provided “computer feedback” for participants in a computerized problem solving conference. The feedback consisted of a statistical aggregation and display of the numerical inputs generated by group members in the process of trying to reach agreement on the solution of their problem.

Lewis [11] has discussed the development and testing of a GDSS designed to support three main phases of group problem solving; (1) problem definition and exploration, (2) generation of alternative solutions, and (3) choice/synthesis of best solution. The GDSS consisted of three program modules managed by a program executive. Program modules included (1) Nominal Group Technique (NGT) which incorporated the steps of Nominal Group Technique, (2) Cross-Impact Analysis designed to explore interactions between the suggested solutions and any obstacles that had been identified, and (3) Rating, a routine to allow group members to exercise their judgement concerning the relative merits of the various solutions. More recently, Keleman et al. [12] have discussed a demonstration project using an updated version of this system.

In an ambitious effort, Nunamaker et al. [13] have reported on the design, implementation, and evaluation of a GDSS for support of idea generation and analysis. The GDSS included four automated tools; (1) Electronic Brainstorming (group version), (2) Electronic Brainstorming (individual version), (3) Idea Structuring and Analysis, and (4) a callable voting program for interactive idea prioritization.

Burns et al. [2] have developed a message-based model of a Distributed Decision Making System (DDM) that resulted from a survey of group decision making within organizations. The prototype implementation of the model was used to obtain experience with distributed decision support. The system was employed in several ways, including support for a computerized decision making conference using the Nominal Group Technique.

## Results of Experience

Results of experiences with some of these systems have indicated great potential $[12]$ . Based on their experiments, Turoff and Hiltz $[18]$ concluded that computerized conferences can effectively support group communication and decision-making, particularly when the conferences are supported with tools suitable to the problem at hand. Lewis $[11]$ found evidence that use of a GDSS can improve both the process and final product of group problem solving, and that the use of such systems will be accepted by participants. Finally, Nunamaker et al. $[13]$ found that the benefits of Electronic Brainstorming clearly outweighed any problems associated with it. They reported that participants expressed high levels of satisfaction with the outcomes of planning sessions in which the models and the process were used to achieve those outcomes. Participants rated the computer as an important tool for idea generation, and those who had used a manual brainstorming model rated the computer brainstorming process as “much better” than manual brainstorming.

Despite these encouraging results, some potential drawbacks have been recognized. Nunamaker et al. [13] identified three factors which they believed inhibited the effectiveness of their GDSS; (1) limitations stemming from the size of the computer screen, (2) the keyboard as an unfamiliar interface for some executives, and (3) limitations on the efficiency of the network in handling multiple, simultaneous file transfers.

A demonstration project designed to evaluate a GDSS developed by Lewis [11] has identified three elements essential for the success of a GDSS; (1) a high quality user interface, (2) adequate user training, and (3) organizational integration. According to the report, failure to deal effectively with any of these three areas may diminish substantially the effectiveness of a GDSS and decrease the likelihood of decision implementation or adoption in an organization.

Burns et al. [2] used a prototype of a Distributed Decision Making (DDM) system to verify the appropriateness of their principles guiding its development. One of their primary assertions is that a decision support system intended to serve broadly based constituents in an organization must be designed to accommodate the political realities of organization life. To this end, Burns et al. [2] concluded that such a DDM system must meet the following requirements:

1. Each “node” (participant) in the decision-making system must be provided with classical DSS tools. These tools should be amenable to evolutionary development to permit alteration and growth as familiarity with the DDM grows in the organization.

2. Communication among decision-making participants must be provided. The communication mechanism must allow users to disconnect from the DDM for periods of time.

3. Mechanisms must be in place to allow disagreements among participants to be exposed, explained, and resolved.

4. Conflict arising during decision making should be resolved among the decision participants rather than referring the conflict upward in the organization's hierarchy. This feature should be available independent of the formal structure of the organization.

## Limitations of Existing Efforts

According to DeSanctis and Gallupe [5], GDSS environments for smaller size groups may range from Decision Rooms for face-to-face groups, to

Local Area Decision Networks for dispersed groups. In the Decision Room members are in a single physical location and meet for a fixed block of time to address a problem or set of problems. The GDSS's developed by Lewis [11] and Nunamaker et al. [13] both fall into this category.

In the Local Area Decision Network several system configurations are possible. Such networks enable communication among locally or remotely connected user workstations. The GDSS developed by Turoff and Hiltz [18] falls into this category. Although there are major differences in the nature of participants' interactions when different kinds of GDSS's are used, these interactions share one important characteristic. Whether participants interact remotely via some kind of electronic network, or whether they are physically present in a Decision Room, in order to participate they must all be present at the same time. This results in significant limitations.

In real organizational settings, the opportunity to convene a decision making group at a particular time and for a prolonged period is usually very difficult. Often, when face-to-face group decision-making sessions are planned, the meetings are conducted in off-site environments in order to remove participants from the disturbances of day-to-day business demands. Justification for such meetings is usually based on the claim that the participants must be completely involved in the task at hand and should not be distracted by office routines.

Regardless of whether face-to-face or remote, electronically networked sessions are used, it is usually very difficult to coordinate the calendars of participants so that the group members can all be available simultaneously. This is particularly true when the individuals are from the ranks of senior management or dispersed across functional boundaries where short-term and long-term differences in planning horizons are evident.

Burns et al. [2] recommended that the decision support system allow participants to disconnect from the system as needed; this would accommodate scheduling variances among participants. Such time-bound related limitations have also been recognized by Jarvenpaa et al. [9] They have suggested that a GDSS “may have its greatest potential value in supporting groups distributed in space or time [p 663].” Despite these limitations, a review of information technology to support meetings by Dennis et al. [4] has revealed very few examples of GDSS's that are not time bound. In addition to the time-bound constraints of these configurations, there are other problems associated with the use of these systems. GDSS use with a Local Area Network (LAN) or a Decision room is facility bound. That is, to use either of these configurations/environments requires a facility in which there must be a significant investment in hardware, software, space, and personnel. For example, Decision Rooms which usually contain a number of networked workstations, a large screen projector, and other electronic paraphernalia are often special facilities that serve few other purposes beyond that of a site for use of a GDSS. On the other hand, while LAN's do not require a unique environment, they are usually part of a computer system requiring extensive cabling and communication devices as well as the support of highly specialized personnel.

The expenses associated with the use of these methodologies are an additional consideration. Expenses may include; (1) the cost of renting a facility (conventional hotel room or specialized Decision Room); (2) the cost of transporting participants to and from a facility; and (3) the availability, cost, and bother of selecting or hiring (either on a full time or consulting basis) a facilitator cognizant of an appropriate decision making process.

## Purpose and Objectives of Our System

Group Decision Program (GDP) is a GDSS designed to address some of the foregoing limitations. It can be used to enable a project team to conceptualize and evaluate solutions to an assigned task. GDP is a topic independent system that provides a computer-based, structured process to direct the pattern, timing, and content used in group decision-making. Based on the structure of Nominal Group Technique [19], it is designed around a multi-phase process that includes an optional tutorial, idea collection, as well as modules for idea ranking, and report generation. Consistent with the recommendations of Burns et al. [2] we have provided mechanisms to allow participants to disconnect from the system at their discretion and to discuss/resolve differences of opinion or values that arise during the decision process. These features are independent of the actual structure of the organization.

GDP was designed as a Level III interactive videodisc-based GDSS. (For a description of levels of videodisc interactivity, see Schwartz [15]). GDP was implemented on an IBM InfoWindow, an interactive videodisc system comprised of an IBM AT or PS/2 computer with 640KB of RAM, EGA graphics, a touch sensitive display, keyboard, and mouse. The computer controls an attached Pioneer or Sony videodisc player. The system output consists of stereo audio, and a combination of color computer text and graphics together with full motion video.

## Description of the System

The GDP procedure is a multi-step process consisting of the following activities:

Stage A: Task Assignment – by manager

Task preparation – prepare Situation Diskette
Team meeting – verbally assign task

Stage B: Group Decision Process - by project team

Phase 0-Introduction and tutorial

Phase 1-solution entry

Phase 2-Group discussion and solution clarification

Phase 3-Solution rating

Phase 4-Solution ranking and report generation

Stage A. During the Task Assignment stage the manager defines a task together with a completion deadline, and assigns the task to a project team of up to about six team members. During this stage, a module of the system guides the manager through the process of preparing a Task Definition Memorandum that GDP stores on a “Situation Diskette” that is later presented to the group in a Team Meeting. At this meeting, the group is briefed and instructed to convene at a videodisc workstation and use the Situation Diskette with the GDP system to address the assigned task.

Stage B - Phase 0. At their convenience (but with the deadline in mind), the group assembles at the workstation to begin using GDP. When the system is first loaded, a Phase Selection Menu (Figure 1) is displayed. In this session the group must select the menu option, “Introduction & Tutorial.” This selection initiates a computer-controlled interactive video sequence that begins with a narrator who welcomes the group and explains the process that will be used to address the assigned task. The narrator's dialogue is independent of any specific task. However, the dialogue has been scripted as if both the narrator and the project team were cognizant of that task.

Every group member must complete Phase 1 before the group can proceed to Phase 2. As each member completes Phase 1, entered solutions are saved on the Situation Diskette. The last team member to complete Phase 2 is instructed by the facilitator to use the appropriate GDP options to print a list of all team members' solutions, to distribute the list to each team member, and to reconvene the group at the workstation to begin Phase 2.

Stage B – Phase 2. In “Phase Two 2 – Group Discussion & Solution Clarification,” GDP displays to the convened team, each solution entered by each team member. The narrator advises the group that the purpose of the session is to ensure that everyone completely understands every solution. GDP provides text editing tools that allow the group to alter a solution’s wording to reflect better its intent, to combine solutions that can be combined, or to delete redundant solutions. The group is warned not to judge or discuss the merits of any solution.

When the group has clarified and modified all the solutions, the narrator explains that a “Final List” must be printed and distributed to each team member (Figure 5). When this phase is complete, each team member, working alone and away from the workstation, must examine every solution on this list and rate its “desirability” on a scale of 1 to 10. When any member has completed the rating, he/she should return to the workstation and select “Phase Three-Rating Solutions Alone” from the Phase Selection Menu.

Stage B - Phase 3. In Phase 3, with guidance from the narrator, GDP presents each team member with the Descriptive Label of each previously recorded solution. The team member must then enter a rating for each. These ratings are saved on the Situation Diskette. Figure 6 illustrates some consecutive screens that are presented for this phase.

When the last team member has completed this phase, he/she is instructed to select “Rate & Print The Solution Ranking By, All Team Members” from the “Printing Options” menu. This selection causes all the team members’ ratings to be averaged and displayed/printed in a final report.

Stage B - Phase 4. GDP prints all the solutions ranked from highest to lowest mean rating. Each solution that is printed contains the Descriptive Label, the Solution Description, the mean, maximum, and minimum rating, and the response frequency from all team members.

## System Benefits

GDP was designed to eliminate many of the problems identified by designers and users of other GDSS systems. Consequently, features of GDP resolve both inherent limitations of existing systems and also address practical issues regarding the use of GDSS's in the workplace.

User interface. GDP consists of a set of modules that are logically integrated into an overall GDSS. Although the modules are physically independent, they are all driven by one master option menu.

One of the more significant innovations in GDP is the use of interactive videodisc technology to provide a user friendly consistency to the GDSS interface. This is unlike remotely networked GDSS's that are devoid of the effect of a human facilitator. The presence of the videodisc-based facilitator provides some of the benefits of a traditional facilitator-directed session using NGT.

When GDP is invoked, users view a videodisc-based facilitator who provides instruction and guidance regarding each step in the process. Users respond to verbal or displayed textual prompts by using the keyboard, or by pressing a finger to a designated touch-sensitive area of the workstation screen surface.

GDP menus have all been designed to employ “window” characteristics that have become commonplace in personal computer applications. Selection of any option from the main window (the Phase Selection Menu) invokes one of GDP’s main modules or sub-modules. Every one of these has an identical “look and feel.” When the facilitator directs users to enter data to the system, all displayed text and all data entry takes place within an input or an output window. Every module that requires user input, does so in an identical manner. Consistent and identical data entry and data editing features are available for every module, and the location of every data entry window and every user prompt is uniform among the modules.

In addition to the uniformity exhibited by the user interface, GDP's modules are integrated at the data file level. First use of any module requires previous use of the prior module. This is necessary for GDP to create new data files and to append data to existing ones.

Keyboard limitations. As Nunamaker et al. [13] have noted, some users of their GDSS complained about the need to use a keyboard to enter their comments/ideas into the computer. Because automated and generalized speech recognition is unlikely to become a practical and cost effective technology for many years, computer applications' reliance on keyboard data entry will continue to be the norm for the foreseeable future. Nevertheless, GDP has been designed to compensate somewhat for these realities.

GDP allows users to interact with the system with a mouse, keyboard, or touch sensitive screen. For example, menu option selection is very simple, allowing the user to employ cursor control keys, to key in a menu item number, or to use a mouse for option selection.

Data entry sessions require minimal keyboard use if that is preferred. For example, when a user is expected to key a list of solutions into GDP, the provision exists to enter a brief Descriptive Label (maximum of 52 characters) and a lengthier Solution Description (maximum of 201 characters). The system uses the former to display efficiently as many options as possible on the space-limited computer screen. The latter is included in all of GDP's printouts, and provides the elaboration that team members require when they use GDP's output lists.

GDP provides full text editing features for its text input windows. For the user's convenience, a summary of those features is always displayed with the text input windows. These features include; (1) automatic wordwrap when the user types past the end of a line, (2) text insert and overstrike, (3) the ability to move the cursor forward/backward by a character, a word or a line, (4) the ability to delete a character word or line, and (5) a function to allow reformatting of all text in a window.

Each GDP session consists of a mixture of interactive video together with a data entry session. Video sessions that require a response expect the user to make decisions by touching the video screen.

One of the more serious complaints about keyboard dependent GDSS's is the stress induced on participants because they must either type data faster than their skill level permits, or from frustration that arises from waiting for slower typists to finish their input. GDP completely eliminates these sources of irritation by first requiring participants to prepare their data away from a workstation. Whenever any individual has completed that task, that person needs only to get the Situation Diskette and use GDP alone at a workstation at his/her own pace without worrying about others who might be waiting for that person's data entry session to be completed.

The sequential, offline, and individual aspects of GDP usage can also result in improved efficiency of time utilization for team members. In traditional GDSS sessions, the entire group must wait for the slowest member. GDP allows efficient team members to use the system at their own pace, freeing up time that they would otherwise spend waiting for their colleagues to complete tasks that are inherent in the structure of group sessions.

Screen size and printed output. Nunamaker et al. [13] have noted the limitations of idea presentation on computer screens whose capacity is generally limited to 80 columns and 25 lines of text. Even the use of a large screen video projector cannot overcome this limitation. GDP resolves this problem in a number of ways.

GDP is essentially an interactive computer application designed around procedures that are implicit in modern personal computer display technologies. However, Nominal Group Technique requires that participants have a “world view” of the problem being solved and the ideas/solutions under discussion by other team members. Limitations of today’s display technology preclude the ability to display on one screen as much information as might be available from viewing a number of flip charts that have been attached to a wall. Creative solutions to this problem are called for, and compromises are inevitable.

In GDP modules, where it is essential to present an idea to the workstation user, screen use is optimized by the display of both the brief and the lengthy description where possible, or only the brief description where necessary. In either case, users are in possession of complete printed reports of the “world view” as it has been collected by GDP up to the time of that phase.

These reports are generated as part of the process in which GDP is used. At the completion of each phase, when a group's total solution set has been entered, rated, etc., the GDP videodisc-based facilitator instructs the last user to print the appropriate report. The reports are intended for use in the offline paper-and-pencil activities necessary to use GDP. They are also intended to be used as supplemental material whenever a workstation session is taking place. A list of these reports is shown in Figure 7.

Training. Keleman et al. [12] have noted that participants in the use of a GDSS must receive training in the process of group decision making as well as in the use of the GDSS itself. GDP addresses this requirement by providing the previously described tutorial. This highly interactive tutorial is completely optional and can be skipped by groups already familiar with these methodologies or who may have previously used GDP.

Organizational integration. Keleman et al. [12] have noted the importance of integrating the use of a GDSS into an organization's conducting off-site nominal group sessions (whether or not a GDSS is used). Use of GDSS in networked environments presents similar as well as additional problems.

GDP was designed with a perspective on the manner in which managers in corporations are truly able to participate in the use of a GDSS. For personnel whose job descriptions do not focus on computer use, most users of computer-based applications use a terminal or personal computer when there is an essential need for computer-based data. GDP capitalizes on this reality while still demanding a compromise from participants whose time is deemed to be too valuable to attend too many “time wasting” meetings.

To use GDP, the manager must convene the group and present the members with a task and its constraints. This is a normal business activity, and one also required for GDP. Under normal business conditions the group would meet soon after, discuss the problem, and determine a preliminary strategy for solving it. This is akin to using the first module of GDP.

Usually, the group would schedule meetings over an agreed-upon time period, hold some, cancel many more, and ultimately arrive at some resolution just prior to the assigned deadline. GDP allows team members to work alone and to prepare ideas/solutions that can be entered into GDP whenever team members are able. The only constraint is the agreed-upon time by which all members must have entered their solutions.

When all solutions have been entered, GDP demands that members meet together only one more time – to review and clarify all ideas that have been entered to GDP. During the execution of this module, GDP solicits the date by which all members will have rated all the solutions. Sometime prior to that date, each member must, whenever it is convenient, return to the workstation and enter his/her ratings. The last person to enter the ratings is instructed to print the final ranked solution set for distribution.

## Conclusion

Group Decision Program overcomes important limitations of previous GDSS efforts. It is neither “time-bound” nor “facility-bound.” In addition, it is designed to incorporate lessons learned from previous experiences implementing GDSS. This does not mean however, that GDP is intended to replace other GDSS approaches. As DeSanctis and Gallupe [5] have observed, different environmental settings and group tasks call for different types of GDSS’s. Consequently, GDP should be viewed as another alternative, one which has specific advantages for certain circumstances. Furthermore, as Burns et al. [2] postulated, GDSS’s need to incorporate features that make them compatible with the political realities of organizations.

GDP was designed with these notions in mind. It is flexible in that its use is not constrained by either location or time. It provides a communication link that is independent of the structure of the organization. It explicitly solicits sharing of participants' disparate ideas. It permits the participants to temporarily disconnect from the system, and prompts them to resolve conflicts at the local level rather than to overburden the administrative hierarchy. We believe GDP is a significant improvement over other GDSS's.
