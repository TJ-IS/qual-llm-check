# Analysis of future development opportunities for OIS methods and tools

John Hawgood, Antoinette Kieback, Edda Pulst, and Joachim Niemeier

c / o Fraunhofer-Institut für Arbeitswirtschaft und Organisation, Stuttgart, Germany

Over the last two decades, formal methods and tools have been introduced into the process of planning, developing, implementing, and managing applications of information technology (IT). For the practical usage existing methods and tools often are seen as incomplete, poorly integrated, often ineffective, and typically insensitive to the application environment. How to make the most of what is available today? Methods and tools to support the newer and more integrated forms of IT applications are still at an early stage of development and are not in common use. A guidance on the selection of methods and tools is needed which shows both what can be achieved now and where developments will come in the future. To a considerable extent the solution of behavioural and organizational design problems are seen as critical. But are methods and tools available for this objective? How to bring together approaches which have so far been pursued along separate paths? What is the most suitable product supporting different environments? The selection and evaluation of appropriate methods and tools for a specific project in a company requires a considerable effort and is itself a difficult and complex task. Method and tool vendors are confused about the development priorities. More and more they raise questions about market potentials and the pay back period for their investments. The purposes of HECTOR project (Harmonized European Concepts and Tools for Organizational Information Systems, ESPRIT project no. 2082) have been to determine what is needed in the marketplace, where opportunities lie to better meet these needs, and to present a framework within which these issues could be studied. The term Organizational Information System (OIS) is used to describe any socio-technical system which supports the work of an organization by facilitating the collection, flow, and analysis of information within or between organizations. The HECTOR market analysis has shown that a good initial choice of methods and tools is very important presupposed for the success of a project. A wrong initial choice often leads to the failure of a project or at least to higher costs. A decision support tool, supported by a comprehensive database of methods and tools would improve the process of selection and evaluation in organizations acquiring these products.

## 2. A framework for investigation of development opportunities

The investigation of development opportunities was done by confronting the HECTOR framework of reference $[5]$ with market data. The HECTOR framework of reference is essentially a conceptual schema for a database of methods and tools: it includes hierarchies of static entities for describing OIS, their environments and methods and tools for improving them, and a process model intended to comprise all activities involved in improving OIS, from business strategy to the post-evaluation of OIS improvements which have been made.

The market data was obtained from the HECTOR market survey (case studies, vendor surveys and postal surveys in 7 European countries) and from the existing knowledge of the team and other experts consulted, also from published sources and from vendors' literature.

## 2.1. Types of development opportunities

By “development opportunities” we mean both filling of the gaps in the OIS method/tool market that could or should be filled – if it is worth filling – by development of new methods or tools, and opportunities for improvement of existing methods or tools. Such improvement opportunities might appear as shortcomings or deficiencies in individual methods or tools that could or should be ameliorated, or as incompatibilities between existing methods or tools that could or should be corrected.

## 2.1.1. Gaps

Gaps may appear in form of absolute gaps, relative gaps, and in the form of incomplete coverage of the life cycle. If there are no methods or tools at all which cover a particular part of the life cycle as defined in the process model of the HECTOR Framework of Reference, we call this an absolute gap. For example, we found no methods or tools (except checklists) intended to help with the choice of OIS methods and tools despite market analysis revealed a great need. A development opportunity might also be detected by finding only very few methods or tools addressing an important life cycle phase; we call this a “relative gap”. For example we found very few methods or tools addressing the wider aspects of benefit and impact analysis – most evaluation methods concentrate on financial costs and returns. We also use the word “gap” about incomplete coverage by a particular method or tool, if it does not cover a part of the life cycle that seems relevant to its stated scope. For example, existing organizational engineering tools are oriented towards individual working places and information flows among those working places – we have not found any oriented towards the overall organizational structure and business processes.

## 2.1.2. Improvement opportunities

Improvement opportunities may relate to technical or performance deficiencies of individual methods or tools, or to incompatibilities or inconsistencies between different methods or tools.

## 2.1.2.1. Types of deficiencies

We identify three main types of deficiencies, “technical”, “performance” and “dissemination”, with some subtypes as listed below.

## 2.1.2.1.1. Technical deficiencies

Technical deficiencies are functions absent from the specification of methods or tools. That is, they are functions the method or tool developer did not try to include, but which some of the market survey respondents felt should be included. For example, market analysis in almost all European countries revealed a lack of support for team work in the analysis and design process. Other examples are missing integrated computer support and missing user guidance.

## 2.1.2.1.2. Performance deficiencies

Performance deficiencies of methods or tools are qualitative or quantitative shortcomings in performance of methods or tools perceived by respondents to HECTOR market surveys or by other HECTOR information sources. We identify five subtypes of performance deficiency.

## - Implementation deficiencies

Implementation deficiencies are related to the particular hardware/software environment on which a tool is mounted (of course it is not relevant for a method). Examples: ease of handling, response time.

## - Input Deficiencies

These are deficiencies related to input requirements. Methods studied eg. mostly collect data by paper-based questionnaires, whereas it would have been possible to arrange for direct input onto screen-based forms.

## - Output Deficiencies

These are deficiencies in the types of output provided by the method or tool. Market analysis revealed that eg. the representation technique in communication analysis is still conventional business graphics, i.e. pie, bar and line charts. Sometimes method output does not meet the decision needs particular for organizational issues. The limited adaptability of methods and tools to the organizational problem situation and specific features of the project imposes a standardized form to be imposed on the organizational reality.

## - Economic deficiencies

This category relates to poor cost/efficiency or cost/effectiveness of projects, ascribed to shortcomings of a particular method or tool. For example, there is frequently an immense burden of data collection, and a large diversion of effort by method and tool users, associated with use of certain methods and tools.

## - Flexibility deficiencies

Market survey respondents often mentioned that they wished methods and tools to be more easily adaptable to the users' circumstances, for example in that they do not allow for continual benefit/result checking with respect to the milestones set. A reason can be seen in the insufficient provision for preparation of a tool for each individual project and the limited degree of tailoring of methods and tools for individual organizations.

## 2.1.2.1.3. Dissemination deficiencies

Dissemination deficiencies refer to difficulties caused by users' inability to obtain or unwillingness to use methods or tools which do already exist; they could be clustered into national, sectoral, inter-organizational and educational subtypes. What are possible reasons for dissemination deficiencies? We describe these reasons in terms of dissemination barriers:

## - Know-how or know-what barrier

From a macro-economic point of view the market is incomplete. Methods and tools are offered in the market but are not used in an organization or on a more global level in a country. An impermeability of the market in combination with a strong segmentation (e.g. a CASE market and in parallel an organizational engineering market) are underlying reasons. This point has been shown as very important in relation to country specific differences.

## - Awareness barrier

This barrier relates mainly to ideological processes in an organization. Organizational theory defines ideologies as logically integrated clusters of beliefs, values, rituals, and symbols. “We don’t need methods and tools” is a typical perceptual filter that strongly distorts attempts at rational analysis. This can also be explained by the high rates of homemade methods in some countries. Missing user participation seems also in most cases to be based on the awareness barrier.

## - Cultural barrier

This barrier relates to consequences of national cultural barriers. In organization sociology there is a measure called “uncertainty avoidance”. This dimension determines the cultural needs for structure. According to the national level of uncertainty avoidance people may feel definitely uncomfortable with systems of rigid rules and prefer flexible, ad hoc structures which leave much to improvisation and negotiation, or vice versa.

\- Methods and tools specific dissemination barrier

Tools on the market in one country are not available in another. Case studies revealed that some German tools are not offered elsewhere. Vendors however realized the need and Siemens Nixdorf Informationssysteme, for example, is now beginning to spread MOSAIK to other countries. On the other hand, DEC can sell “Top Mapping” anywhere, as its symbols can be understood quite independently of native language.

## 2.1.2.2. Incompatibilities

Incompatibilities between methods or tools may either be conceptual or technical.

## 2.1.2.2.1. Conceptual incompatibility

This arises mainly between methods, but may also affect tools, as a tool must have capabilities to understand and to interpret the results from other tools. It occurs when there are differences of philosophy, theoretical background, object classes addressed and/or semantic differences (same classes but using different terminology). Conceptual incompatibility between tools eg. would arise from lack of interrelation between social and organizational tools in the same life cycle phase (for example in some organizational engineering methods between communication and process analysis). Conceptual incompatibility between tools would also arise if one tool was not based on object-orientation while this was required by the new generation of applications.

## 2.1.2.2.2 Technical incompatibilities

This arises mainly between tools; tools from different vendors, tools related to different life cycle phases and parallel tools in the same phase. We identify two categories of technical incompatibility, arising from communication factors and from data format factors. That is, tools must be able to pass signals physically from one to the other, and they must also “understand” the signals they receive. Technical integration needs can be seen on different levels: user interface, tools, database, operation system, hardware:

## - Communication incompatibilities

This may either arise from different hardware/software environments for tools which should be used together, or from lack of communication between tool and OIS (lack of automatic data collection). Tools must have the capability to communicate. This means that a tool often has to have access to the results of other tools and also to produce results for other tools. The communication of results between tools has to be arranged via a technical communication path, e.g. a common database. An example is lack of provision for simultaneous engineering – parallel methods and/or tools, which need to communicate properly to take account of each others' results, cannot do so.

## - Data incompatibilities

This is caused by different data formats or data base structures. Case studies showed that often a great deal of data is wasted that could be passed from one phase to another (call for tender, implementation, planning, training, maintenance, evaluation). There is often no data dictionary/data base supporting cross checking within a phase and reusability between phases. Frequently a strong link to transfer data between a project management tool and other tools is missing.

## 2.2. Method and tool market categories

In order to group and prioritize development opportunities three market categories have been identified with groups of customers having similar needs.

## - Existing markets

Users' need can already be met with available products though possibly no information is available in a particular company. “Innovations” at the vendor side in this kind of market are usually modest product improvements, product line extensions, respositioning or repackaging.

## - Latent markets

These markets are made up of needs that customers know they have but which are not met by available products. Innovation by vendors can here be driven by customers.

## - Incipient markets

Unanticipated markets can be created through innovation. Customers are unaware of needs until a product or a solution appears. Asking customers to come up needs for innovative products is generally not useful.

## 2.3. A European perspective on development opportunities

The HECTOR investigation of the European survey was therefore guided by the juxtaposition of the development opportunities and kind of target market. HECTOR market analysis results show in the existing market, that many organizations have turned to outside help to provide the necessary resources and skills. Often an individual consultant, experienced in a particular method or tools, act as a “facilitator” in an OIS improvement project. A problem often quoted in HECTOR market analysis was a users’ lack of understanding and difficulty in working with the outputs of the method and tools used by the external specialists. Satisfactory OIS improvement is found to be dependent above all other factors on end-users and management understanding and participating fully in the OIS project. But methods and tools have been designed by IT specialists for other IT specialists to use.

In the latent market none of the widely known products is available in a form designed to help organizations with the introduction of OIS improvements which depend on the newer technologies and their integration. There is a large difficulty of understanding which OIS methods and tools are the most appropriate for a particular type of organization or OIS improvement project. Methods and tools are needed which proved more support for defining and updating OIS requirements in the context of an organization's strategic objectives.

The incipient market reveals the need for experience knowledge bases as well as the need for

European dissemination of new national methods and tools.

## 3. Selecting methods and tools for information system improvement projects

Two software prototypes have been available at the end of the HECTOR project: SESAM and OISEAU [4]. SESAM (Systematics for Elaboration, Selection and Assessment of Methods and Tools) is a hypertext database of methods and tools. After the termination of the HECTOR project a smaller project group works on the extension of the database. OISEAU (Organizational Information System Enhancement Advice Unit) is a prototype decision support system to facilitate the choice of methods and tools. It uses SESAM as a database and several features of the HECTOR framework of reference [5] as parts of its user interface.

## 3.1. The SESAM database

SESAM is a tool designed to gather, characterize and review current methods and tools. Methods are specific or generic methods/approaches covering one or more parts of the process of planning and controlling, building and introducing changes to organizational information systems (OIS). A special effort was made to include methods and tools in the investigation suitable for information system improvement projects which can not be found in existing inventories of available methods and tools. Some surveys on methods and tools are available on a commercial basis. The most serious limitations of these surveys can be seen in the narrowed focus on purely technical tools and the absent foundation of underlying description structure. A methods and tools database could not appear as a paper document because in a dynamic market the information is obsolete in the moment when “the ink is dry”.

SESAM is implemented as a database using a hypertext system (SuperCard). The philosophy of SuperCard is to represent all screens by cards, with mouse sensitive buttons and graphical backgrounds. Each entry in the database is represented logically by an object and physically by cards. The structure of those cards is described in a mastercard containing all the related information (attributes of the object), physically seen as fields on that card. Each entry is therefore an instance of a mastercard.

## 3.2. The OISEAU decision support tool

OISEAU (Organizational Information Systems Enhancement Advice Unit) is a decision support tool to facilitate the selection of appropriate methods and tools. It is a software tool designed to help users to plan their information system improvement/development project around the critical success factors for their business, bearing in mind also non-technical factors like their organizational structure and the applications required.

In daily practice the search for an appropriate method and/or tool is very time consuming and complex, depending on the scope of the questions to be answered:

\- Does it cover the life cycle phases that are important for system success? Does it cover the relevant subsystems (e.g. organizational design, if appropriate)?

\- Is it flexible? Can it be adapted to our organization's needs? Will it accommodate possible changes in system development such as switching to prototyping after a preliminary analysis has been conducted?

\- Is the language (graphical and verbal) easy to learn and easy to use? Does it permit to describe all of the details of a system, needed to know, to understand it sufficiently so that it can be improved?

\- If the method provides only partial coverage of the situation, is it compatible with the other methods that will be used alongside it?

\- Are there tools supporting the methodology or method? Are those tools compatible with each other? Does the expected benefit from their use (higher productivity, better quality results) exceed the various costs (cash outlay, time required to learn to use them, user acceptance)?

This list of questions can only give a taste of the complexity of the decision situation. Fluctuating and conflicting requirements cause additional problems.

## 3.2.1. The OISEAU cycle

Three categories of data relevant to the operation of OISEAU (see Figure 6):

\- One covers the description of a project's environment, which relates the organizational project environment to methods and tools.

\- Another category details all of the available methods and tools. This is SESAM.

\- The third contains data on the performance of methods and tools in defined environments obtained from completed projects.

![](/api/attachments/BSX5TSSX/fulltext/images/9705d54402a0f730f8f0a9953ac58409918cf904bbeab9cbfb08679759bbbedc.jpg)

OISEAU now match the current project environment with those in its historical database (the database containing how well various methods and tools have worked in different environments in past projects). It examines similar environments to identify those methods and tools which have performed well, and then deliver a list of methods and tools that would be acceptable in ranked order. A choice would then be made by the user of specific method(s) and tools to be used, related to the current project development phase. To continue the cycle, the project would be launched using the method(s) and tools chosen. After its completion an evaluation would be conducted, especially with respect to methods and tools as far as OISEAU is concerned. Their performance, along with the original environmental data and the methods and tools chosen, would be stored in the past projects database, to be used the next time a project is in the formulation stage.

## 3.2.2. The OISEAU demonstration prototype

The available demonstration prototype actually covers that part of OISEAU, that describes the OIS improvement project, but just with the most important structures of the HECTOR framework of reference, and ends up with a list of methods and tools found in the SESAM database. The prototype allows a selection of appropriate methods and tools on basis of a maximum of 10 project relevant criteria out of 70. Our ongoing analyses have identified over 300 tools specifically designed to support one or more parts of the process of planning and controlling, building and introducing changes to organizational information systems. About 250 methods and tools have then been described in the SESAM database, whereas 150 have been classified in such a detail that they are usable for the OISEAU prototype. At present OISEAU exists as a demonstration prototype, developed object oriented using SuperCard on a Macintosh computer. This user friendly interface is used to illustrate the OISEAU functional model guiding the user through OISEAU, and visualize the HECTOR framework of reference for inserting project data.

The functional model of the OISEAU demonstration prototype is represented in the following picture. Circles are representing data structures and the SESAM data base is represented by a drum. Rectangles represents processes. OISEAU consists of four parts implicitly shown in Figure 7:

\- Definition of the OIS project characteristics.

In the actual implementation there is a lack of data on the performance of methods and tools in various project environments. What is implemented, however, is a list of keywords connected to each method and tool in the database that indicates the possession of the attributes represented by those keywords. Thus the user can describe the various aspects of methods and tools that are important to a project, and the system will scan the database to see which approach meets the stated requirements/constraints. This is done by means of a predefined check list shown on the screen of the computer, so that all the user has to do is to mark those attributes he seeks. At this point only yes/no measures are available.

## 3.2.3. Discussion and conclusions

It is worth to mention that with 70 available criteria even the prototype can handle more criteria in contrast to the number of criteria applied in a “normal” human decision making process. For the final choice the decision maker can investigate the preselected methods and tools in more depth, e.g. via personal contact to a vendor. These factors only can complement the preselected decision information produced using OISEAU, but never substitute them.

The application of OISEAU in test case scenarios has led to valid results. Using OISEAU the possible solution space is widened. At the same time the decision making process is more efficient: A broader spectrum of possibilities can be checked. The effect of altering the constraints produces shorter and longer lists of acceptable methods and tools. This provides insights in the robustness of solutions.

The overall quality of OISEAU strongly depends from the quality of the SESAM database. Therefore, quality assurance mechanisms for SESAM are needed. Starting from the prototype, OISEAU now needs to be refined. The most important steps to be taken are enhancing the conceptual model of SESAM, to cover performance data, and gathering a sufficiently rich database on the performance of methods and tools in various environments so that OISEAU can operate as proposed. The available yes/no measures are also in some distance from the intended long range objectives. The result list must be ranked according to the best choice and indicating, why one method/tool is better than another one.
