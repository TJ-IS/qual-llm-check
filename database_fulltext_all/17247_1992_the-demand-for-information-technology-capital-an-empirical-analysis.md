# The demand for information technology capital

# An empirical analysis \*

Vijay Gurbaxani

University of California, Irvine, CA, USA

Information technology (IT) capital currently represents seven percent of total capital stock in the U.S. and accounts for a rapidly growing share of capital expenditures, making it one of the most important innovations of recent times. Given the significant role of IT as a factor of production in every sector of the economy, it is important to analyze these growth trends. The adoption and subsequent diffusion of IT is naturally driven by the nature of the underlying demand. This paper studies the demand for IT capital for the period 1970–1987 at both the aggregate economy level and the industry sector level. In particular, the effects of innovation diffusion, price trends and sector output on the demand for computing are examined through an empirical analysis of data on IT investment at these levels. The relationships between the aggregate and the industry demand functions as well as inter-industry differences are investigated. The results provide: i) A theoretical understanding of the nature of demand for IT capital; ii) measurement of the level and growth of computerization in the economy; and iii) predictive ability.

Keywords: Information technology investments, Demand for computing, Diffusion of technology, Information technology costs.

## 1. Introduction

The U.S. economy is becoming increasingly information intensive, as is evidenced by the growing levels of computerization in every sector of the economy. The stock of information technology (IT) capital currently accounts for roughly seven percent of total U.S. capital stock and continues to increase as IT consumes an ever larger share of new capital investment [3]. Firms in information intensive sectors of the economy spend over four percent of their revenues on information systems and IT industries account for approximately two percent of the GNP. This widespread diffusion has made IT one of the most important innovations in the twentieth century. As the adoption and use of IT as a productive capital input continues to grow rapidly, contributing significantly to the transformation of the economy into an information economy, it is im-

The only formal analysis of the level of computerization in the economy appeared in 1967. Chow [6] was interested in measuring the extent to which the use of electronic computers had grown in the U.S. since 1955, and in explaining this growth. He suggested that in order to understand the tremendous growth that had occurred, it would be necessary to consider the natural growth which would have occurred even in the absence of technological process, as well as the growth which was induced by technological change. Surprisingly, there have been no subsequent empirical studies, to the best of our knowledge, which have focussed on the adoption and diffusion of IT capital. $^{1}$

This paper is motivated by the desire to understand the nature of the growth in the use of IT in the ensuing period. Our main objectives are to develop a theoretical understanding of the nature of demand for IT capital and to measure the level and growth of computerization in the economy. Accordingly, we develop and estimate economic models of the demand for IT. In particular, we focus on the role of three major factors that influence the demand for IT: Price, innovation diffusion and sector output. While the increasing use of IT is commonly described in terms of the diffusion of innovation, it has also been suggested that the technology has matured and that the recent growth is primarily the result of the declining costs of computing $[9]$ . In fact, even as early as 1955–1965, almost half the growth could be explained by the price effect $[6]$ . Another important factor that may influence the demand for computing is the rate of growth of output. Clearly, the demand for IT should be positively correlated with the scale of the industry. That is, as output grows, the quantity of IT demanded should increase. While Chow $[6]$ acknowledged the importance of output on the demand for IT, he was unable to measure its effect.

In this paper, we analyze the demand for IT in the period 1970–1987 at both the economy-wide and industry sector levels. This allows us to determine the relative computerization of each of the industry sectors and the corresponding growth rates. Moreover, a two-tiered analysis permits an examination of inter-industry differences and provides insight into the relationship between the aggregate and industry demand functions. It may also allow us to obtain insights that are unavailable from an examination of aggregate behavior.

The results of this study have significant implications for user organizations and the IT industry. An understanding of the role of innovation diffusion, price trends, and output in the demand for IT is of value to IS managers in their planning decisions. Further, IS managers are in need of norms on trends in IT investment in their own industries to assist them in evaluating and justifying their own spending $[13]$ . The models are also of obvious interest to the IT industry in forecasting the demand for their products and services.

The outline of this paper is as follows. A brief review of the literature is presented in Section 2. The theory and the resulting models are presented in Section 3. In Section 4, we provide a description of the data and perform some preliminary analysis. Section 5 contains the formal statistical analysis. Our concluding remarks are in Section 6.

## 2. Previous literature

The theory and models developed in this paper are based on the existing literature on computing growth. We present a brief review of the relevant earlier empirical research.

Chow [6] proposed a model where the aggregate stock of electronic computing in the United States, in the absence of technological change, was assumed to follow an S-shaped diffusion pattern and attain an equilibrium level. The equilibrium level was hypothesized to be a log-linear function of the costs of computing and the level of output. As technological progress continued to occur, resulting in lower prices for computing, and as output grew, the equilibrium level was continuously being increased. Two versions of the model, one including and one omitting the output variable, were tested using both the Logistic and Gompertz curves. With output excluded as an independent variable, the Gompertz curve model resulted in a good fit with the data. The corresponding price elasticity of demand for computing was estimated as 1.44. When output was included, the results were unsatisfactory due to multicollinearity in the estimation and Chow reported being unable to measure the output effect. He also noted that the estimates resulting from a model which excluded the output variable were likely to overstate the price effect, since the growth due to the output effect would be attributed to price. Thus, the 'true' price elasticity of demand would be somewhat lower than 1.44. Nonetheless, Chow's study made an important contribution to our understanding of the growth of computing.

Gurbaxani and Mendelson [8,9,11] examined the growth in spending by the aggregate of information systems departments in the United States. $^{2}$ The analyses are based on a theoretical model [8] which relates information systems spending to the cost and value of information services. They showed that the nature of production of information services in organizations is time-dependent and results in distinct patterns of growth in two different periods. The first period is a transient period, when diffusion and learning effects dominate as users gain experience with the technology and its uses. The resulting pattern of growth of information systems expenditures in this period is S-shaped, as might be predicted by innovation diffusion theory. However, as the technology and users mature, a steady-state is reached which is characterized by an exponential growth trend, or a constant annual percentage increase in these expenditures. In the mature stage [11], organizations make increasing investments in computing technologies because the trends in the price-performance of hardware technology have made it cost effective for organizations to computerize a progressively larger number of tasks. These underlying trends in the patterns of growth are modified by general business conditions, with the rate of growth of investment being positively correlated with the growth rate of the economy.

Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9] suggest that a comprehensive model which can describe information systems spending growth from its inception, would require the integration of the diffusion effects with the price effects. They propose and test such a model using data on information systems spending for the time period 1960–1987. They found that for the Logistic and Gompertz representations of S-curve behavior, the predictions of the integrative approach were consistent with the data. Since the focus of this model was the integration of diffusion and price effects, the model did not consider the effect of the growth in output. There are currently no estimates of the output elasticity of demand for IT. It should also be noted that since Gurbaxani and Mendelson do not use a price series but instead model the price decline as a time trend—exponential decline at a constant rate—they do not estimate the price elasticity of demand for IT.

## 3. A model of information technology growth

Since the objective of this study is the analysis of the demand for IT capital at the industry and aggregate levels, we extend the Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9] model to focus explicitly on IT investment and to account for output effects.

Sector output can affect the demand for IT in several ways. First, it is a measure of the scale of the industry. As an industry grows or shrinks, the demand for IT should vary accordingly. Importantly, the output elasticity of demand for IT provides valuable insight into the economies of scope and scale in the use of IT. Note that we do not mean economies of scale in the costs of IT, but rather the ability of the technology to support increases in output with a less than corresponding increase in the quantity of IT input. An estimate of output elasticity that is less than unity would suggest the presence of such economies while an estimate greater than unity would suggest the absence of such economies. A second aspect of this variable is in its measurement of general business conditions. While in most industry sectors, the underlying trend is usually a steady increase in output, the actual value of output fluctuates around the trend line. For example, in recessionary or expansionary periods, the rate of growth can vary considerably from the trend line. Since capital investments are often affected by general business conditions, one would therefore expect that IT investments would be similarly affected. It is therefore important to consider the influence of output on the demand for IT.

A related issue to be considered in the operationalization of the model is the timing of the impact of the trends in output on IT investment. Current output may influence the current demand for IT capital as well as the demand in future periods. The timing of the impact will depend on industry-specific factors such as the nature of the capital planning process and the ability to adjust production processes to incorporate new IT investment. It is therefore likely that the effects of output on demand may occur with lags that vary across the different industry sectors. In this paper, we assume that the effect of output occurs in a single period which may either be the current or a subsequent period.

Our model, consistent with that of Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9], assumes that the underlying process of growth of IT investment is S-shaped, that the rate of growth is affected by the costs of computing and is also influenced by the trends in sector output. The model predicts that, for a fixed output level, if the costs of computing were to remain constant, the demand for IT would grow according to an S-curve. However, the demand for IT should continue to grow even when the technology has matured as prices fall and as output grows. We begin by considering the case where the demand for computing is stable over time (when there are no diffusion effects), which corresponds to the steady-state growth period. When output is held constant, the demand curve represents a downward-sloping relation between the period-T quantity, $q_{T}$ , and the period-T price, $P_{T}$ . That is, $q_{T}$ is a decreasing function of $P_{T}$ . Holding price constant, the demand curve is increasing in output. Thus, $q_{T}$ is an increasing function of the period-T output, $X_{T}$ . The alternative assumption that the quantity series is dependent on a lagged output series can be incorporated in the model through a simple change in notation. Assuming a demand curve that is constant-elasticity in both of its inputs, this relation has the functional form

$$
q _ {T} = D P _ {T} ^ {- \alpha} X _ {T} ^ {\beta},\tag{1}
$$

where D is a scale constant, $\alpha$ is the price elasticity of demand and $\beta$ is the output elasticity of demand. If the demand curve shifts over time, the constant D has to be replaced by a function of time, $D_{T}=D\phi(T)$ . Then, we obtain

$$
q _ {T} = D \phi (T) P _ {T} ^ {- \alpha} X _ {T} ^ {\beta},\tag{2}
$$

where the function $\phi(T)$ describes the pattern of demand shift over time.

We suggest that this shift in demand is closely related to the innovation diffusion process. Most studies of the diffusion of innovation demonstrate that new products follow well-defined patterns of adoption. These patterns are described by diffusion models which represent the level and spread of the innovation among a given set of its prospective adopters. The adoption process is typically characterized by a bell-shaped curve which depicts the density function of the time taken by different segments of the population to adopt the innovation. The resulting time pattern of the cumulative number of adopters is S-shaped, as has been found in numerous studies (see for example, [1], [5], [16] and [20]). In the information systems context, Nolan [19] based the stage hypothesis on an S-shaped budget curve, and Brancheau and Wetherbe [2] have applied innovation diffusion models successfully to describe the adoption of spreadsheet software. Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9] suggest that the function $\phi(T)$ will be S-shaped and hypothesize that the Gompertz and Logistic forms, which have been widely used in the diffusion and new product planning literature [16], are reasonable representations for the function $\phi(T)$ .

Equation (2) implies that the behavior of the quantity series, $q_{T}$ , over time depends not only on the S-shaped multiplier $\phi(T)$ but also on the behavior of the price series $P_{T}$ and the output series $X_{T}$ . Note that when price and output are held constant, equation (2) reduces to an S-curve $q_{T}=D\phi(T)$ , consistent with the effects of innovation diffusion. On the other hand, consider the case when $\phi(T)$ becomes flat (for large T). For this case, holding output constant, equation (2) states that the quantities of IT acquired in each period grow exponentially, reflecting the effects of the exponential decline in prices.

To analyze the model further, we examine the behavior of the first derivative with respect to time of the logarithmic transform of equation (2), which can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log q _ {T} = \log D + \log \phi (T) - \alpha \log P _ {T} \\ + \beta \log X _ {T}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Differentiating with respect to time results in the following equation

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} T} \log q _ {T} & = \frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} T} \log \phi (T) - \alpha \frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} T} \log P _ {T} \\ & + \beta \frac {\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} T} \log X _ {T}. \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

The discrete time analog of this equation can be written as

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \log q _ {T} - \log q _ {T - 1} & = \log \phi (T) - \log \phi (T - 1) \\ & - \alpha (\log P _ {T} - \log P _ {T - 1}) \\ & + \beta (\log X _ {T} - \log X _ {T - 1}). \end{array} \tag {4}\tag{4a}
$$

The above equation expresses the percentage growth in the quantity of IT demanded in any period as a sum of the percentage growth that can be attributed to each of the three factors, diffusion, price and output effects. Thus, the model facilitates a comparison of the relative impacts of each of the three factors. We believe that the magnitude of the impacts of these factors will depend in part, upon the stage of diffusion of IT. While a variety of classifications of the lifecycle of information technology can be developed, our model suggests three stages of growth: (i) an initial learning stage where learning effects and other factors related to the diffusion of technological innovation dominate; (ii) an intermediate stage where price effects have a considerable impact on the pattern of diffusion but where innovation diffusion is still significant; and (iii) a maturity stage where most of the growth can be explained by the price effects alone. Note that price and diffusion effects are directly related to the production and use of IT, while output effects may be considered to be largely exogenous. Thus, while the above categorization is based on the relative importance of price and diffusion, the actual rate of growth in all of these stages will also be influenced by the output effects.

The first term on the right-hand side in equation (4) represents the percentage rate of growth in the quantity of IT demanded due to diffusion effects. A property of S-curves is that this term is positive and decreases over time. In the case of the Gompertz curve, this term is a decreasing linear function of $\log q_{T}$ , while in the case of the Logistic curve, it is a decreasing linear function of $q_{T}$ itself. The second term is the percentage growth due to price effects. If prices decrease exponentially at a constant rate, then this term is constant and positive. In actuality, while the trend in prices is an exponential decline [17], the percentage rate of decline is not the same in each period. Thus, while the percentage growth in the quantity of IT demanded due to price effects will be positive, it will not be constant but fluctuate around the trend line. Finally, the general trend in output is consistent with a constant annual percentage increase, which would imply that the third term is constant and positive. As in the case of the price decline, the observed values of output also fluctuate around the trend line. This model predicts that the influence of diffusion declines steadily over time while the effects of price and output fluctuate around their respective trend lines.

The above model is fairly general and can be applied to both the industry level analysis as well as to the aggregate economy-wide analysis. In the next section, we validate the model by testing its predictions using data on the investments in IT at the industry and aggregate levels. The estimates of the relative impacts of price and diffusion will also provide us with a means to categorize the various industries into one of the three stages of growth. Moreover, the estimates of the output effect will provide useful insights into the impact of scale and general business conditions on the demand for IT.

An important component of the analysis is an exploration of inter-industry differences in the nature of demand for IT. Consider for example, the relationship between the information intensity of an industry and the price elasticity of demand for IT. By information intensity, we mean the input share of information-related activities relative to production activities. On the one hand, we might argue that information intensive industries ‘require’ IT and therefore predict that the demand would be relatively inelastic. On the other hand, these industries are typically characterized by higher ratios of information workers to production workers. Since IT capital is likely to be more easily substitutable for information workers than for production workers, we could predict that the demand for IT would be more price elastic in information intensive industries [12]. Correspondingly, as the costs of IT fall, these industries will invest proportionately more in IT. Another interesting issue is whether the heaviest users of IT are also experiencing the highest rates of growth in their consumption of IT, or whether they are approaching saturation and are therefore witnessing a slowing rate of growth in consumption. The analysis will also facilitate a comparison between the rates of growth at the industry levels and at the aggregate level. Since the overall rate of growth of GNP has remained relatively steady, in contrast with individual industry sectors which have witnessed varying rates of growth, one would expect to find considerable differences in the consumption patterns of different industries that may be masked by an analysis at the economy-wide level.

## 4. The data

The data necessary for this study are the annual IT capital investment at the industry and aggregate levels, the annual output of each industry and a price index for information technology. These data are available from various government agencies. A description of the data follows.

## 4.1. Data sources and measurement

The Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), a division of the U.S. Commerce Department, collects annual data on current dollar investment in capital assets by industry sector. $^{3}$ The asset category which measures investments in IT is called office, computing and accounting machinery. This category covers electronic computers, computer storage devices, computer terminals, computer peripheral equipment, calculating and accounting machines, and office machines. In 1987, the percentage share of computer-related categories exceeded 90 percent. In this paper therefore, we use the terms IT and office, computing and accounting machinery synonymously. Similar data are also available at the aggregate economy-wide level.

In order for the data to be comparable between years, the current dollar flows are converted to constant dollar flows. The methodology used by the BEA is to deflate the current dollar investment figures by a price index for office, computing and accounting machinery to arrive at a constant dollar estimate for industry investment in any given year. The price index is computed using hedonic regression techniques (see Cartwright [4] for details). $^{4}$ Briefly, the methodology used to compute the index is as follows. For each year, and for every product class, a regression equation is estimated that develops the relationship between price and the attributes of the technology. One year is picked as the base year, in this case, 1982. In years other than the base year, this relationship is used to estimate the imputed price for computers in the base year. This method provides a price index for every technology class. The deflator is constructed using a weighted average of the different types of equipment, where the weights are determined by the shares of business purchases. The price deflators are available for the years 1970–1987. We therefore choose to examine the time period 1970–1987. Using these deflators, the constant dollar investment flows in office, computing and accounting machinery are computed. The corresponding measure of the ‘quantity’ of IT is in effective units of 1982 dollars. That is, one unit of IT is that amount which one dollar would have purchased in 1982. The constant dollar stocks of IT capital are computed from the constant dollar flows by aggregating the investments over time and subtracting the retirements. It is assumed (as suggested by the BEA) that the retirement pattern of investments in IT follows the modified Winfrey S-3 distribution, which is a bell-shaped distribution centered on the average life (see NBER [18] for details of the method).

Thus, data are available for the stocks and flows of IT in constant 1982 dollars by industry sector. $^{5}$ Further, as discussed above, a relative price index for IT is available for the years 1970–1987. To compute a constant dollar price index, the relative price index is divided by the corresponding GNP implicit price deflator. Finally, annual output data by sector are also available from the BEA. These data measure a sector's share of GNP in constant 1982 dollars. We therefore analyze the data for the eight industry sectors. These industries are; Manufacturing; Finance, Insurance and Real estate; Services; Agriculture; Mining; Transportation and Utilities; Trade $^{6}$ ; and Construction. We also compute the sum of all of these sectors as corresponding to the aggregate U.S. economy-wide (excluding government) level of investment.

Turning to our model, we analyze the pattern of behavior of the first differences of the logarithm of the per-period quantity of IT demanded, as specified in equation (4), and denoted here by the term $Dq_{T}$ . As discussed above, this term is expressed as a sum of the percentage growth in IT quantities ascribed to diffusion, price and output effects. Figures 3a–3c present a graphical depiction of the behavior of $Dq_{T}$ for the eight industry sectors and the economy. It is apparent from these figures that $Dq_{T}$ has remained basically flat in the period of analysis in the case of all sectors. Recall that the price effect and the output effect are predicted to fluctuate around their trend lines, while the diffusion effects are predicted to decline steadily over time. If diffusion effects were significant in the period under analysis, the graphs would display a decreasing trend until the point where diffusion effects were no longer significant beyond which the graphs would be basically flat. Thus, figs. 3a–3c suggest the hypothesis that diffusion effects do not play a measurable role in influencing the demand for IT capital in the period of analysis. Indeed, even the least computer intensive sectors display the same pattern of behavior. This graphical result provides preliminary evidence that all industry sectors are in the maturity stage, where the growth in IT investments is due only to price effects and to growth in sector output. We test the above hypothesis through formal statistical analysis in the next section.

## 5. Empirical analysis

In this section, we first confirm the hypothesis that the effects of diffusion are insignificant in the period of analysis for all industry sectors. When diffusion effects are no longer significant in any sector, then all industry sectors can be considered to be mature users. We then provide a rigorous empirical analysis of the pattern of demand for IT capital in the maturity stage.

## 5.1. The role of diffusion

The graphical analysis has indicated the strong likelihood that all industry sectors are in the maturity stage. In this section, we formally show that the hypothesis that diffusion effects are insignificant in the period of analysis is consistent with the data.

Consider the model specified in equation (3), which is reproduced here for convenience.

$$
\begin{array}{c} \log q _ {T} = \log D + \log \phi (T) - \alpha \log P _ {T} \\ + \beta \log X _ {T}. \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

This model can be operationalized by specifying the functional form for the S-curve, $\phi(T)$ . The Gompertz and Logistic curves are the most widely used S-curves in the literature. The Gompertz curve is specified as

$$
\log \phi (T) = \log K + b ^ {T} \log A,\tag{5}
$$

and the Logistic curve is specified as

$$
\log \phi (T) = - \log \left(K + A b ^ {T}\right).\tag{6}
$$

To derive the corresponding testable models, we substitute equations (5) and (6) in equation (3). This leads to a testable specification for the Gompertz curve. For the Logistic curve, the testable equation is derived by taking the antilogs of both sides of the equality. Note that the resulting specifications are non-linear and we therefore use non-linear least squares to estimate the parameters of the models.

It is often the case with economic data on prices and output that the two series are highly correlated. In the current dataset, the price series for IT is correlated with the industry output series, leading to a multicollinearity problem in the estimation. Multicollinearity is usually indicated through large standard errors of the coefficients and coefficient values that fluctuate considerably (for example, with small changes in the period of analysis). The choice of the solution to multicollinearity depends on the objective of the analysis. In the case where all parameters are not of interest, the appropriate solution is to drop one of the collinear variables $[15]$ . Since our objective in this phase of the analysis is to estimate the coefficients of the diffusion parameters and their significance, it is appropriate to drop a collinear variable. We choose to drop the output variable from the current analysis since we know from previous research $[6]$ that price effects are likely to be much more significant than output effects. However, since output is positively correlated with IT investment, this is likely to result in overestimates of the effects of price and diffusion. Since the aim of the current analysis is only to verify that diffusion effects are insignificant in the period of analysis, demonstrating the absence of these effects in spite of the presence of an upward bias in their estimates is obviously sufficient for our purposes.

It is important to note that earlier studies using similar methods were able to estimate the effects of diffusion. Chow [6] found that diffusion effects explained over half of the growth in the ten year period 1955–1965, and Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9] concluded that diffusion effects played a measurable role until the early seventies, but were subsequently insignificant. Our results, consistent with those of Gurbaxani and Mendelson [9], imply that users have entered the maturity stage. While individual technologies may display the effects of innovation diffusion, our results support the proposition that in general, the developments in IT, though rapid, have been incremental rather than resulting in significant demand shifts.

## 5.2. Steady-state analysis

In the absence of diffusion effects, the model specified in equation (3) reduces to

$$
q _ {T} = \log D - \alpha \log P _ {T} + \beta \log X _ {T}.\tag{7}
$$

Ideally, we should estimate the above model. However, as discussed above, the price series and the output series are correlated for all industry sectors resulting in multicollinearity in the estimation. While several solutions have been proposed in the literature (see $[15]$ for a description of the various methods), two of these are appropriate here. We incorporate both of these methods in our analysis.

First, we estimate a model that excludes output as an independent variable. That is, we only consider the effects of price, since as discussed earlier, price is expected to be the dominant factor. We call this model the price effects model. Since this model will ascribe part of the growth that is due to the output effect to the price effect, this will result in an over-estimate of the price elasticity of demand. Indeed, Chow's [6] analysis also excludes output considerations. Thus, these results provide an upper bound on the price elasticity of demand for IT capital.

Next, we estimate a model that is derived from equation (3) by taking first differences. This model, which we call the first differences model can also be derived by considering the discrete time version of equation (4). Taking first differences results in series of estimation variables that are not as highly correlated as the original variables, and multicollinearity is less of a problem. However, if the error terms implicit in equation (3) are serially uncorrelated, then the error terms in the discrete (or first differences) version of the model specified in equation (4) will not be independent. In our case, however, the error terms in equation (3) are not independent, and the estimations use the Cochrane–Orcutt autoregressive technique to eliminate first-order serial correlation. Moreover, the residuals that result from our estimations of the discrete time version of equation (4) will be seen to be serially uncorrelated.

Now consider the first differences regression model specified in equation (4a). This model includes the first difference of the logarithms of both price and output as explanatory variables. Thus, the independent variables are the annual constant dollar percentage changes in prices and in sector output. While equation (4a) assumes that the effects of industry output occur in the same period, the alternative model which makes the assumption that output effects occur in a subsequent period is also tested. The results of the estimation are presented in table 5.

In the price effects model, the analysis attempts to derive the relationship between the overall pattern of growth of IT investments and the price series. In the first differences model, the model attempts to explain the fluctuations around the pattern of growth, measured by the variation in the percentage growth of IT investment, by examining the per-period percentage change in price and output. In the case of such models, it is therefore expected that the variance explained by the model will decrease substantially. Indeed, the values of $R^{2}$ range from a high of 0.54 to a low of 0.08. In all cases, the Durbin–Watson statistic is consistent with the hypothesis of no serial correlation among the residuals.

The best estimations occur in the cases of the Economy, Finance and the Trade sectors. The quality of the fit for these sectors is good and both coefficients are significant at the 95% level. In all three cases, the price elasticity of demand is lower than the corresponding estimate in the price effects model. The same pattern is observed in Manufacturing, though the output term is significant only at the 85% level. In the case of the Construction sector, both coefficients have the correct sign, and are significant at the 90% level.

The estimates of the price effect are generally consistent with our expectations. In most cases, these estimates are lower than the estimates derived from the price effects model. With the significant exception of the Finance sector, where the price elasticity was estimated at 1.16, the estimate of the price elasticity of demand is slightly less than unity in the case of most sectors. At the aggregate economy-wide level, where the estimated price elasticity is 0.9, the results imply that the increased investment corresponding only to the price decline will lead to slowly decreasing expenditures on IT. Taken in conjunction with the trend of increasing output however, the overall pattern of growth is that of a steady increase in real expenditures. Similar patterns of behavior will be observed in most of the other sectors as well with the quickest rate of growth of IT investment in the Finance sector.

The coefficient of the output term is significant at the 95% level in the Services sector and only marginally below that in the Transportation and Mining sectors. The coefficient of the price term is statistically insignificant in all three cases. Interestingly, an examination of the underlying data reveals that investments in IT are indeed very sensitive to changes in output. In the case of the Mining sector, output has decreased in the last several years along with investments in IT. In contrast, the Services and Transportation sectors are two of the fastest growing sectors making it likely that the effects of price are masked by the rapid changes in output. These results suggest the importance of including output as an explanatory variable in models of the growth of computing.

In all sectors other than Manufacturing, Construction and Mining, the best results were obtained when output effects were assumed to occur in the same period. The best estimations for these three sectors were obtained when a one period lag for the output effects is incorporated in the model. Note that these three sectors are 'heavy industries' and therefore very capital intensive. These sectors are characterized by highly formalized capital planning processes that were developed to assist in the introduction of expensive durable inputs. Moreover, the period of adjustment to incorporate new IT capital into a production process is likely to be of longer duration in industries with large stocks of fixed capital inputs.

We have been able to estimate the output effects at the 95% significance level in four cases, Economy, Finance, Trade and Services, and at only a marginally lower level in Transportation and Mining. The magnitude of the estimated coefficients indicate the absence of economies of scale in the use of IT. Specifically, the quantity of IT demanded (in 1982 prices) per unit increase in sector output (in 1982 prices) is greater than unity. These results demonstrate that output effects have a measurable influence on the demand for IT capital and must be taken into consideration when estimating the demand for IT. In particular, the estimates of the price elasticity of demand are lower when output effects are considered.

The rate of growth of output however, has been considerably slower than the rate of the price decline of IT. Specifically, price has declined at an average annual rate of 20%, while the output series have grown at annual rates that range from 2–4%. This implies that the coefficient of the output term would have to be at least five times the coefficient of the price term in the fastest growing industry sectors and ten times the coefficient of the price term in the slowest sectors for the percentage growth attributable to each factor to be equal. The estimations show that in most cases, the coefficient of output is between two and three times the magnitude of the corresponding price coefficient. Our results therefore demonstrate that in the period of analysis, price effects account for the largest share of the growth in the use of IT. For example, in the case of the Economy, roughly 75% of the growth can be attributed to price effects, while 25% of the growth is attributable to the growth in output. In the Finance sector, 97% of the growth is due to price effects, while only 3% is due to output effects and in the Trade Sector, price accounts for 66% of the growth with the balance due to increases in output.

## 6. Concluding remarks

Since IT now accounts for the largest share of new capital investment, it is extremely important to understand and quantify the growth in the use of the technology. The aim of this paper has been to enhance our understanding of the nature of demand for IT and its impact on the pattern of diffusion of IT. Our results provide: (i) a theoretical understanding of the nature of demand for IT capital; (ii) measurement of the level and growth of computerization in the economy; and (iii) predictive ability.

Through a series of theoretical and empirical models, the research has demonstrated the role of individual factors on the demand for IT at the industry and aggregate levels. These factors include the diffusion of technological innovation, price trends and industry output. The accompanying empirical component has provided a rigorous validation of the theoretical models. The results have shown that the inclusion of output as a variable in the estimation affects the estimates of price elasticity and must be included in models of IT growth. This paper has been the first to estimate the output elasticity of demand for IT. Further, the empirical analysis has provided a means of calibrating the models and quantifying the level and rate of computerization in the U.S. at the aggregate and the industry levels.

The models and their associated estimates should be of considerable value to user organizations as well as the IT industry. The models can be used by the industry as tools in forecasting the future demand for IT as well as in analyzing the impact of alternative hypotheses about future trends in price-performance and output growth. While the analysis was performed at the industry sector level, implications for organizations can also be drawn since the underlying behavioral phenomena at the aggregate and organizational levels are similar. Accordingly, information systems managers can utilize the models to plan their IT investments in light of the cost trends and the rate of growth of the size of their organizations. The measurement of the level of computerization and the associated growth rates also provide a set of industry-specific norms, which were previously unavailable, to information systems managers who need such data to assist them in evaluating and justifying their own investment trends $[13]$ .

The models also provide an enhanced ability to predict the future steady-state growth in the use of IT, an issue of concern to users and suppliers alike. The notion of a mature IT industry and its resulting implications have received considerable attention. A recent cover story in Business Week $[14]$ examined precisely this issue focusing on whether revenues could continue to grow in an industry characterized by such rapidly improving price-performance trends. Since IT industries have been accounting for an increasing share of GNP, along with growing employment, the recent downturn in the fortunes of this industry have had significant negative economic impact. We therefore believe it to be of considerable importance to understand the nature of maturity in this industry.

The study has shown that all sectors of the economy have achieved a steady-state pattern of consumption of IT resources, with no significant effects of innovation diffusion. The demand for IT is driven primarily by the price declines with a measurable share also being attributable to growth in output. The results show that if current trends persist, expenditures on IT will continue to increase due to the cumulative effects of price and output growth. Since the increased demand for IT due to decreasing prices results in slowly decreasing real revenues at the aggregate level, the growth of the IT industry will depend in considerable degree on economic growth. Our results also serve to differentiate the IT industry from other industries. Even as the industry matures, revenues continue to grow in contrast with industries where S-curve behavior prevails.

The analysis has also allowed us to examine inter-industry differences. While the magnitude of the price elasticity of demand at the aggregate level appears to have stayed relatively stable over time, the aggregate behavior masks the wide variations in the behaviors of the individual sectors. The price elasticity of demand varies considerably across the different industry sectors reflecting their different information requirements. We found that sectors which are widely considered to be information intensive display higher values of the price elasticity of demand. This perhaps reflects the differing degree of difficulty with which IT can be introduced into the respective sectors. In particular, since IT is more easily substitutable for information workers than for production workers, industry sectors with a higher share of information workers may be more price elastic. This result may also be counter-intuitive since the competing hypothesis that information intensive industry sectors ‘require’ IT and will therefore have a relatively inelastic demand is also plausible. Similarly, we also found that the demand for IT in capital-intensive industries adjusts more slowly to changes in output. Industry sectors with the highest levels of IT were also seen to be the fastest growing sectors in their ongoing investments in IT, suggesting that they had not achieved saturation in their use of the technology.

Two directions for future work appear promising in their ability to explain the patterns of IT investment. The first is to develop more sophisticated models for the current level of aggregation.

Such work could involve testing more general models that relax the assumption of constant elasticities by allowing for the price and output elasticities to vary over time. Alternatively, the models presented here could also be extended by incorporating additional industry-specific variables. It should be pointed out however, that it is often quite difficult to obtain consistent data that are available for the same period and level of analysis. The second direction is to pursue similar analyses at more disaggregate industry levels. Since considerable differences may exist in the characteristics of industries even within any given sector, more specialized models may provide better explanations of IT investment.
