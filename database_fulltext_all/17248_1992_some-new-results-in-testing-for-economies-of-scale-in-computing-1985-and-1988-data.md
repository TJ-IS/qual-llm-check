# Some new results in testing for economies of scale in computing

Terry Barron

University of Rochester, Rochester, NY 14627, USA

This paper presents and evaluates a new specification of models used to test for the presence of economies of scale in computer processing power. An analysis of data for systems available in 1985 (which has been analyzed previously in the literature) is consistent with this specification. Analyses of a new set of data for systems available in 1988 are also performed using the same method. These results are also consistent with the specification, and in addition imply statistically significant diseconomies of scale. A discussion of some managerial implications of the results is also presented, as well as suggestions for future research.

Keywords: Economics of computing, Economies of scale, Grosch's law; Management of computing and information systems

## Introduction

Testing for the existence of economies of scale in computing capacity has been of persistent interest at least since the 1950's because this cost behavior is a major factor in such fundamental decisions as how to price computing resources, what combination of hardware will yield the minimum total cost for supplying a given capacity, and whether to have a centralized or decentralized computing facility. For example, suppose we are interested in finding the minimum cost way of supplying a capacity of 10 MIPS (millions of instructions per second) and are considering combinations of ten 1 MIPS systems, two 5 MIPS systems and one 10 MIPS system. If there are economies of scale, then the cost per MIPS falls with increasing capacity and the single 10 MIPS system will be cheapest. Diseconomies of scale imply that cost per MIPS rises with increasing capacity, making ten 1 MIPS systems cheapest. If there are constant returns to scale then the cost per MIPS does not vary with system capacity, so each alternative will yield the same total cost. Clearly, information on the behavior of capacity costs is central to this important decision.

The issue of determining minimum cost hardware configurations is distinct from, but related to, the broader question of centralization or decentralization of computing resources. Clearly a cluster of networked VAXes, say, could reside at a single centralized site, so that an organization might have centralization as a policy and yet still be faced with choosing between a single mainframe and several cooperating minicomputers in order to minimize capacity costs. However, if there are constant returns or diseconomies of scale, then it makes sense to explore the larger question of how systems should be distributed within an organization. It is clear that hardware capacity cost behavior is only one of several important factors in such a decision. For example, distributing the hardware may imply communication costs, agency costs, maintenance and operations costs, or general organizational turmoil that more than offset any benefits stemming from reduced capacity costs. In fact, in most issues of publications such as Computerworld and Data-mation one can find stories of organizations, some of which are centralizing hardware resources and some of which are decentralizing. Such behavior is consistent with recent empirical evidence which shows either constant returns to scale (e.g. (10)) or weak diseconomies of scale (this paper, for the 1988 data) combined with the various other above-mentioned costs that rational organizational designers would consider. The weak relationship between capacity and cost per MIPS thus implies that for most organizations the sum of the other costs is highly significant and likely to predominate in the centralization/decentralization decision.

The two most influential recent papers dealing with economies of scale issues are those by Mendelson, [10], and Kang, [8]. Mendelson analyzes data from 1981 and 1985, while Kang's data covers the period 1981–1985. Both of them conclude that there were constant returns to scale in this period. This cost behavior is dramatically different from results using data from the 1960's and early 1970's which indicated the existence of very significant economies of scale. Since technology moves quickly, the analysis of more recent data seems prudent. In particular, the extremely rapid advance in power of microchip CPU's such as Intel's 80386 now makes small single-chip multi-user systems quite feasible and may imply significant price pressure on the low end of the minicomputer market. As a result, one of the objectives of the present paper is to examine capacity costs in a more recent set of data.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section 2 lays out the theory behind the model used in the estimations, while Sections 3 and 4 employ the model to reanalyze 1985 data and then perform extensive analyses of a 1988 data set which appears here for the first time. Section 5, on managerial implications, discusses the estimation of relative system costs using results for the 1988 data as well as possible impacts resulting from using mis-specified models. The final section contains conclusions and a discussion of important issues for future research.

## 2. Theory

The most widely used model (see, e.g., [4] and [10]) to determine the nature of computer capacity cost behavior expresses the relationship between price, $P_{i}$ , and performance of the ith system, most often measured as MIPS $_{i}$ (millions of instructions per second) as

$$
P _ {i} = b _ {0} \left(\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}\right) ^ {b _ {1}} \eta_ {i},
$$

where $\eta$ is an error term. For purposes of estimation, including correcting heteroscedasticity of the error terms, this model is transformed to the additive form

$$
\log \left(P _ {i} / \mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}\right) = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \log \left(\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}\right) + \epsilon_ {i}.\tag{1}
$$

Thus $a_{0}=\log(b_{0})$ and $a_{1}=b_{1}-1$ . The coefficient $a_{1}$ is known as ‘Grosch’s coefficient’; its sign and significance are used to test for economies of scale. If $\hat{a}_{1}$ , the estimator of $a_{1}$ , is positive and significant, diseconomies of scale are present; if $\hat{a}_{1}$ is negative and significant then there are economies of scale; and if $\hat{a}_{1}$ is not significantly different from zero then there are constant returns to scale.

Various researchers have made efforts at improving the specification in (1) by adding potentially relevant variables. The major reason for worrying about omitted variables is that their omission can bias the estimators of the coefficients of the variables that are actually included in the model [9, pp. 392–395]. This is a potentially important issue here since the sign and significance of Grosch's coefficient determine the presence or absence of economies or diseconomies of scale. Further, the magnitude of Grosch's coefficient can be used to estimate the relative costs of different ways of supplying a given amount of capacity, so significant bias can lead to costly wrong decisions.

Among recent papers, in [10] Mendelson, analyzing data for systems available in 1981 and 1985, found a significant relationship between the year a system was introduced (i.e. the age of its technology) and its price per MIPS, and also that there was a premium in price per MIPS for systems which were IBM compatible. These findings led to the addition of year of introduction and an indicator variable for IBM compatibility to equation (1).

In [8], Kang derives a more elaborate model from basic microeconomic production theory, consisting of two simultaneous equations. One of the equations is (1) with the addition of variables for the amount of memory and number of channels, as well as incorporating variables for year of introduction, IBM compatibility, and indicator variables for seven manufacturers. Using pooled cross-sectional and time series data for 1981–1985, he also found that the IBM compatibility and year of introduction variables were significant. The nature of his estimation procedure however required the enforcement of certain constraints on the coefficients of the MIPS, number of channels and memory variables which made it impossible to determine their individual significance. He also does not report on the significance of the manufacturer variables.

Kang's estimations are somewhat undercut by the quality of the available data. First, he restricts his sample to mainframes and superminis (332 and 27 models, respectively; p. 589) because he requires that lease price data be available. (There is no intrinsic requirement in his modeling that lease prices be used. In fact, he also supplies results using list prices for the systems in his sample, but again it is the same sample, restricted by the availability of lease price data.) As a result, there is a significant chance of introducing the kind of selection bias demonstrated in [10]—low-cost systems are the least likely to be profitable for lessors, thereby excluding low price/MIPS systems from the sample. Second, the Computerworld data is inherently quite noisy with regard to technical attributes and the precise correspondence between system features and the prices reported. For example, the number of channels is often reported as a range, often quite large—32–64, 64–128, and 4–176 are not uncommon examples. Two difficulties then arise: (1) How to reduce the range to a single number in a way that is consistent across systems, and (2) how many channels a system with the reported price has. It is quite frequent in the Computerworld data that (2) is not reported. Similar problems are common with the memory figures.

The difficulties with the Computerworld technical characteristics data makes the practical inclusion of such variables in models problematic. This is not to be taken lightly since the Computerworld data are the most easily accessible, being published yearly in a timely fashion, making it highly attractive for managerial decisions which must be made on the basis of currently available data for currently available systems. Thus an improved specification appropriate for the Computerworld data is of interest; one highly pragmatic benefit of manufacturer variables is that they can be determined noiselessly from that data. However, the broader importance of such variables seems underexplored. In addition to this pragmatic benefit, there are at least four scenarios that could cause such indicator variables to have significant explanatory power.

Scenario 1. System costs vs. system prices. In this paper, as in [4] and [10], prices from Computerworld's Hardware Roundup [2,5,6,12] are used. There is reason to believe that these prices are not market clearing prices but ask prices. That is, a buyer could buy a system if he offered the indicated price, but he could almost certainly buy it for less than that. For example, a recent Data-mation survey [11] provided some information on prevailing average discounts from mainframe list prices for several computer manufacturers as reported by actual purchasers of the systems, see table 1. These discounts clearly vary both across manufacturer at a given time and across time for a given manufacturer.

Further, the price paid for the system represents only a portion of the cost as seen by the buyer. The buyer wants to provide a stream of computing capacity across time, and the cost of doing so is affected by the periodic operational costs (e.g. maintenance, operations personnel, etc.) and the residual value of the system at the time of replacement as well as the initial outlay. Thus a representative buyer sees the cost of system i as $C_{i} \equiv \alpha_{i} P_{i} - R_{i} + O_{i}$ , where P is the list price, $\alpha$ is the fraction of the list price the buyer pays, R is the present value of the (expected) residual value of the system, and O is the present value of the operational costs. Therefore market forces from the demand side will act to equilibrate $C_{i}$ and $C_{j}$ , not necessarily $P_{i}$ and $P_{j}$ , when systems i and j are close substitutes. The effect of this would be to introduce a significant amount of noise into the observed relationship between P/MIPS and MIPS.

The argument in the preceding paragraph implies that $P_{i}$ should be replaced in this model by $C_{i} = \alpha_{i} P_{i} - R_{i} + M_{i}$ . Define $\beta_{i}$ such that $R_{i} = \beta_{i} P_{i}$ and $\gamma_{i}$ such that $M_{i} = \gamma_{i} P_{i}$ . This yields

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \log (P _ {i} / \mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) & = a _ {1} + a _ {1} \log (\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) \\ & - \log (\alpha_ {i} - \beta_ {i} + \gamma_ {i}) + \epsilon_ {i}, \end{array}
$$

as the correct specification of the relationship between $\log(P_{i}/\mathrm{MIPS}_{i})$ and $\log(\mathrm{MIPS}_{i})$ . Thus in the models that have been previously estimated, the error terms have included both the 'true' error term $\epsilon_{i}$ , and the term $-\log(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}+\gamma_{i})$ .

If for a given manufacturer, say j, the values of $\log(\alpha_{i(j)} - \beta_{i(j)} + \gamma_{i(j)})$ are similar for all those systems $i(j)$ in his product line, there will be a common value $k_{j}$ for each manufacturer which gives rise to a model of the form

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log (P _ {i} / \mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \log (\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) \\ \qquad + \sum_ {j} k _ {j (i)} d _ {j (i)} + \epsilon_ {i}, \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

where $d_{j(i)}$ is an indicator variable equal to 1 for those systems i which are manufactured by j and 0 for all other systems. In effect this model says that the machines of all manufacturers share a common value of Grosch's constant, $a_{1}$ , but the intercept may differ across manufacturers. (Note that the possibility of the manufacturer coefficients varying across time makes pooling of different years' data, as Kang did in [8], questionable. His formulation implicitly assumes those coefficients have the same values in all years even though he has a variable for year of data collection.)

Scenario 2. Different manufacturers exploit different market segments. This is rather similar to the specification in [4] which asserts the possibility of equation (1) having different intercepts for different classes of machines (i.e. minis, mainframes, etc.) However, rather than using system price for classification purposes as was done in [4], an approach which was shown in [10] to produce spurious results, our specification involves classification by manufacturer, which also leads to equation (2) as the model specification.

Scenario 3. Technical attributes of systems other than MIPS are priced and a given manufacturer's systems tend to have similar technology. In this case indicator variables for manufacturers would act as surrogates for any consistent technological differences across manufacturers, again giving rise to equation (2) as a specification.

Scenario 4: Manufacturers supply data on systems which are similarly equipped in their own product lines and such configurations may differ across manufacturers. There is some evidence for this from the Computerworld data. In their tables, the 'Base Price' column sometimes includes notes on the features of the system for which the price is stated. Some manufacturers include an operating system or other software in these prices, some include disk or tape drives, etc. Thus there is likely to be considerable variation across manufacturers in the way they report data, implying different intercepts for different manufacturers and leading to specification (2).

The interpretation of the resulting estimates $\hat{k}_{j}$ is somewhat tricky. It is impossible to have an indicator variable for each manufacturer and also estimate the intercept term $a_{0}$ since the resulting matrix will be perfectly collinear; with $a_{0}$ in the model we must omit the indicator variable for (at least) one manufacturer. As a result, $\hat{a}_{0}$ will be the sum of the estimate of $a_{0}$ and the estimate of $k_{j}$ for the omitted manufacturer(s). Hence the resulting $\hat{k}_{j}$ 's must be interpreted as being incremental to $\hat{a}_{0}$ and not as direct estimates of the corresponding $k_{j}$ 's. In this paper we use IBM as the 'base' manufacturer, the one having no indicator variable, so that coefficients for other manufacturers will then be significant or insignificant relative to IBM.

Since the year of system introduction has previously been found to be a significant explanatory variable (i.e. more recently introduced systems have a lower price per MIPS on average), we included it in the estimations below. Thus our basic model has the form

$$
\begin{array}{r l} \log (P _ {i} / \mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) & = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \log (\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) + a _ {2} \text {year} _ {i} \\ & + \sum_ {j} k _ {j (i)} d _ {j (i)} + \epsilon_ {i}, \end{array} \tag {3}
$$

where ‘Year’ has values 86, 87, etc. We examine the results of estimating (3) in the data analysis sections below.

Note that (3) does not include an explicit variable for IBM compatibility even though this was found to be significant in earlier research. The reason is that those manufacturers considered to be IBM compatible (certainly IBM and Amdahl; some consider NAS to be, but they are apparently not plug-compatible in the way Amdahls are reputed to be) each has its own manufacturer variable in (3). An IBM compatibility variable is the same as the sum of the variables for those manufacturers, a specification which is implicitly the enforcement of a hypothesis that all the IBM-compatible-manufacturer variables have the same coefficient (see the section below which analyzes the 1988 data for more details.) Therefore the model of [10] which has variables for log(MIPS), Year and IBM compatibility is a reduced form of (3), which means that an F-test can be used to discriminate between (3) and this three-variable model.

It is important to note that the interpretation of equation (3) is different under Scenario 1 than under Scenarios 2, 3 and 4. Under Scenario 1 there is a single relationship between MIPS and system cost; the manufacturer coefficients serve as multipliers to the list prices to determine an estimate of the system cost. Under Scenarios 2, 3 and 4 (assuming Scenario 1 is incorrect) there will be a distinct relationship between price and MIPS for each manufacturer.

The remaining objectives of this paper are threefold. First, note that a necessary condition for the correctness of any of the preceding scenarios is that at least some of the indicator variables in (3) be statistically significant. Since testing those scenarios individually and distinguishing among them (note however that they are not necessarily mutually exclusive) will require significant additional data collection. There is little point in expending such effort if estimations of (3) yield insignificant results for the bulk of the manufacturer variables. Second, improving regression models by adding relevant but previously omitted variables will improve the reliability of and confidence in research results on economies of scale in computing capacity. Since previous research has not carefully explored the relevance of manufacturer indicator variables, and there are plausible hypotheses implying their inclusion, we are also interested in estimating equation (3) for this reason. Third, if (3) turns out to be an improved specification, we are interested in using it to analyze the behavior of capacity costs in more recent data than has been analyzed previously.

## 4. Analysis of the 1988 price-performance data

In this section we analyze data for a sample of systems available in 1988 in order to test model (3) in another period and, perhaps more importantly, to see whether conditions facing those making computer acquisition decisions have changed across time.

The 1988 sample comes primarily from [5] and [12]. Unfortunately, a relatively large number of the systems, notably for Honeywell-Bull, National Advanced Systems and Unisys, were missing data for either MIPS or price or both. However, a substantial part of the missing MIPS data for the Honeywell and Unisys systems was available from [2], while most of their systems' prices were listed in the 1988 data. In no case was a 1987 price used. If no 1988 price was available the system was excluded from the sample. However, this means that the systems still having missing data will be the newest systems in these manufacturers' lines, probably implying that any estimates of the average Price/MIPS for their product lines will be biased upward due to the year-of-introduction effect. The data for the National Advanced Systems products lacked both MIPS and price, and it was impossible to locate satisfactory substitute data. The resulting sample consisted of 226 systems.

The systems included in Computerworld's 1988 data [5,12] were divided by them into several categories: Special purpose, large- and medium-scale systems, small systems, workstations, and personal computers. Because of questions about the comparability of MIPS figures, discussed at length in [10], personal computers and workstations were excluded from the sample, as were the special-purpose systems. There is still a question as to the extent to which the MIPS ratings of machines in the three remaining classes are compatible with one another. Since several manufacturers have product lines that extend across the large- and medium-scale classes with apparent similarities such as sharing the same operating systems, there is reason to believe that these systems can be treated as a group, as has been done in earlier research. On the other hand, many of the small systems are based on either the Intel 80386 or one of the Motorola 68000-series CPU's, are made by manufacturers who do not have broad product lines, and can handle only a small number of users. As a result, there is reason to be cautious in interpreting the MIPS ratings for some of these machines since they may in fact basically be personal computers.

The obvious approach to this problem is to analyze the large and medium systems separately from the small systems, using Computerworld's classification. However, their scheme is based in part on system price, so using their partition of the sample could result in a selection bias similar to that documented in [10], leading to the omission of legitimate low-price, hence low-price-per-MIPS, systems. Our approach is to calculate the ratio: Typical number of users/MIPS, for each system, and eliminate those for which this ratio is very low (The Computerworld data includes figures for maximum and typical numbers of users.) Since the basic problem in comparing reported MIPS numbers for personal computers and larger systems revolves around MIPS being too large for the former systems, the value of Number of users/MIPS for them will be quite small. Further, since we can calculate values for this ratio for the medium and large systems, which we know belong in the sample, we have a possible way of defining 'too-small' systems. In particular, the observed minimum value for this ratio among the medium and large systems is 3.9; there are fifteen systems in the sample having Number of users/MIPS < 3.9.

In an effort to bolster the robustness of the results, in the analyses that follow we employ three subsamples: (1) the entire set of data (226 systems); (2) the set that results from classifying by Number of users/MIPS (211 systems), and (3) only the systems classified by Computerworld as large or medium (145 systems.) The presence of the systems in subsample (3) would appear to be relatively non-controversial based upon previously published research, so diseconomies of scale among these systems may be the most persuasive evidence, especially since selection bias would tend to cause underestimation of diseconomies from this sample. It turns out that in all three cases mild but statistically significant diseconomies of scale are present, being least evident for sample (3) as would be expected from the effects of any selection bias introduced by Computerworld's classification scheme. This is also consistent with downward pressure on capacity costs being driven by the low end.

(1) Is the specification given by equation (3) superior to the three-variable model of [10] (i.e. log(MIPS), Year and IBM Compatibility as the independent variables) for the 1988 data?

(2) What is the best estimate of Grosch's coefficient for the 1988 data?

Turning to the first of these questions, note that for each subsample the three-variable model is a restriction of equation (3). Specifically, in the 1988 data IBM and Amdahl are the IBM-compatible manufacturers. Let $d_{1}$ and $d_{2}$ be the corresponding indicator variables, so that $d_{3},\ldots,d_{n}$ are the non-IBM-compatible firm variables. Comparing the full model (3) and the three-variable model then reduces to a test of the following hypothesis about (3) (recall that the $k_{j}$ are the coefficients of the indicator variables)

$$
H _ {0}: k _ {1} = k _ {2} \text {   and   } k _ {3} = k _ {4} = \dots = k _ {n}.\tag{4}
$$

This can obviously be restated as

$$
H _ {0}: k _ {1} - k _ {2} = 0 \quad \text { and } \quad k _ {3} - k _ {4} = 0 \quad \text { and }
$$

$$
k _ {4} - k _ {5} = 0 \text {   and   } \dots \text {   and   } k _ {n - 1} - k _ {n} = 0,\tag{5}
$$

which is the imposition of n-2 linear equality restrictions on (3). The appropriate test for $H_{0}$ is an F-test; the statistic for it can be written as (see [1], pp. 83–84)

$$
\mathrm{F} _ {n - 2, N - m - 1} = \frac {\mathrm{R} _ {(3)} ^ {2} - \mathrm{R} _ {H _ {0}} ^ {2}}{\left(1 - \mathrm{R} _ {(3)} ^ {2}\right)} \frac {N - m - 1}{n - 2},
$$

where $\mathbf{R}_{(3)}^{2}$ is the $R^{2}$ from the full model (3); $R_{H_{0}}^{2}$ is the $R^{2}$ from the model which results from substituting the restrictions of $H_{0}$ into (3); N is the sample size; and m is the total number of variables in (3).

Substituting $H_0$ into (3) and gathering terms gives

$$
\begin{array}{l} \log (P _ {i} / \mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) = a _ {0} + a _ {1} \log (\mathrm{MIPS} _ {i}) + a _ {2} \text {year} _ {i} \\ \qquad + k _ {1} \sum_ {j = 1} ^ {2} d _ {j (i)} + k _ {3} \sum_ {j = 3} ^ {n} d _ {j (i)} + \epsilon_ {i}. \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

Thus $d_{I(i)} \equiv \sum_{j=1}^{2} d_{j(i)}$ and $d_{\text{Non}(i)} \equiv \sum_{j=3}^{n} d_{j(i)}$ are indicator variables for the IBM-compatible systems and non-IBM-compatible systems, respectively. Note that exactly one of $d_{I(i)}$ and $d_{\text{Non}(i)}$ equals 1 for each system i. As a result, (6) cannot be estimated because of the presence of $a_{0}$ and the two indicators. The most natural procedure here is to eliminate $d_{\mathrm{Non}(i)}$ , since the result is the three-variable model of [10]. This implies that the estimate of $a_{0}$ will include the effects of $k_{3}$ , and that $k_{I}$ is then interpreted as incremental to their sum. (It would of course be equivalent to suppress the estimation of $a_{0}$ in (4) and retain both $d_{I}$ and $d_{Non}$ , or to suppress $d_{I}$ ; they all give the same value for $R_{H_{0}}^{2}$ .) ## 5. Managerial implications

Suppose we are trying to find the minimum cost way to supply a given processing capacity, say 10 MIPS. A variety of combinations could be used: Ten 1 MIPS systems, two 5 MIPS systems, one 10 MIPS system, etc. What are the relative total costs of these configurations? And, how badly would we err by using the results from the three-variable model in tables 10–12 rather than those from tables 13–15? It is important to remember that the models estimated here represent only the capacity component of total system costs that vary with MIPS and therefore are relevant to broad questions of organizational design and industry-wide trends. Clearly the total costs of any particular configuration to a particular organization will depend on several other factors as well. For example, in a given organization ten 1 MIPS systems may imply larger agency costs, maintenance and operations costs, etc., when compared to having one 10 MIPS system. The sum of these costs could more than offset any capacity cost advantage resulting from the multi-system configuration. This is particularly true since the diseconomies of scale are quite mild. It is easy to show that if $M_{1}$ and $M_{2}$ are the capacities of systems 1 and 2, then the estimated ratio of costs of supplying a given level of MIPS is $C_{1}/C_{2} = (M_{1}/M_{2})^{a_{1}}$ , where $a_{1}$ is Grosch's coefficient. Figs. 6 and 7 use the convention that $M_{1} \geq M_{2}$ ; the X-axis then corresponds to the ratio of system capacities and the Y-axis to the ratio of the resulting total costs. Figures 6 and 7 each show estimated costs using the three-variable and full models for the Users/MIPS and large-and-medium-systems samples, respectively. (Recall that in the latter case the three-variable model implied constant returns to scale.) In fig. 6, the lower curve results from Grosch's coefficient as estimated in table 14, while the upper one comes from the three-variable model in table 11. In fig. 7, the lower curve results from the three-variable model in table 12, while the upper one comes from Grosch's coefficient as estimated in table 15.

Returning to the above-mentioned capacity decision, supplying 10 MIPS capacity with one 10 MIPS system versus ten 1 MIPS systems gives a ratio $M_1 / M_2 = 10$ which implies, using the lower curve in fig. 6, that the total cost of the single 10 MIPS system would be about $150\%$ of that of ten 1 MIPS systems. Similarly, if the comparison were between one 50 MIPS system and ten 5 MIPS systems the ratio is still 10, so the single system's cost is again about $150\%$ of that of the multiple system configuration. If we were to use the upper curve in fig. 6 rather than the lower curve we conclude that the small system configurations have a somewhat greater cost advantage. Thus for alternatives having $M_1 / M_2 = 10$ we would conclude that the larger system would cost about $170\%$ of the smaller system as a way of supplying a given level of MIPS. This of course could lead to erroneous configuration decisions when combined with other relevant costs to decide on a minimum cost system.

Fig. 7 can be used in a similar fashion, but in this case the three-variable model understates rather than overstates the smaller systems' cost advantage. The constant returns implied by the three-variable model leads to the estimated relative cost being 100% regardless of the configuration, while the upper curve says that the larger capacity system will cost about 125% of the smaller when the MIPS ratio is 10. Clearly the error gets worse as the MIPS ratio rises.

## 6. Concluding remarks

This paper has examined one possible source of omitted variables in tests for the presence of economies of scale in computing power. Our results are consistent with the hypothesis of significant omitted variables in previous estimations. F-tests comparing the model having a full complement of manufacturer variables with a model having log(MIPS), year of introduction and an IBM compatibility variable as used in [10] show the latter model to be markedly inferior.

Tests for economies of scale yield quite different results for the two years' samples. The 1985 data continues to display the previously reported ([8] and [10]) constant returns to scale once the new variables are added, while the 1988 data shows statistically significant diseconomies of scale. These are present in all three of the sub-samples considered: The full data set, a set selected on the basis of number of users/MIPS, and a set of large- and medium-scale systems only. They are however least for the latter sample. Also, for the 1988 data set, omission of the manufacturer variables results in larger estimates for Grosch's coefficient whose use would lead to overestimations of the cost advantages of using small systems. The summary statistics for the 1988 data show a continuation, and perhaps even an acceleration, in the decrease in the real cost of capacity, falling an average of nearly 29% per year over the period 1985–1988.

The fact that the findings here for the 1988 data are unlike those seen for any previously analyzed data set requires some further discussion. First of all, it should be noted that although the diseconomies found are statistically significant, they are not very large in absolute terms, and therefore the degree to which they are economically significant is unclear since hardware configuration decisions also involve a number of other costs. Second, the 1988 data are just one sample; it is certainly possible that it represents an unusual year. For example, as was noted earlier, IBM was pricing its AS/400 line quite aggressively, and it is not yet clear whether this represents equilibrium pricing for this series or a short-term attempt to gain market share. In any case, their strategy may have caused other, directly competing manufacturers to respond similarly, thereby resulting in temporarily lower prices in this segment. (However, dropping the AS/400 models from the three 1988 subsamples does not materially change any of the results.) Third, the actual costs required to substitute several small systems for a large system may be understated by examining the computer prices without also considering network costs. This would tend to bias downward the price per MIPS for low MIPS systems and could explain the observed results. (Also see the related comments about future research possibilities below.) On the other hand, it is clear that microcomputers are squeezing the low end of the minicomputer range and offering very impressive price per MIPS performance. If this is the explanation, then analysis of 1989 and 1990 data should display similar results.

Even though many papers on the behavior of capacity costs have appeared over the years, there is a remarkable amount of potentially worthwhile work yet to be done. Broadly, such work can be broken into two categories: (1) Improved theoretical formulations leading to more appropriate and robust models for empirical work; and (2) improved data sets.

Kang's paper [8], although itself not carefully done, represents a step in the right direction with regard to improved theoretical formulations. One could well add to his technical attributes. For example: Is there a premium or discount for systems running Unix? For systems which are field-upgradable?

Even more interesting and important is better modeling of how system capacity fits into the broader organizational picture, and how sensitive decisions involving organizational structure and organization of computing resources are to the behavior of capacity costs. Clearly there is considerable room here for theoretical modeling and subsequent empirical testing which could relate observed organizational changes to the behavior of capacity costs.

A highly significant issue which has yet to be addressed satisfactorily is the extent to which microcomputers are substitutable for their larger brethren, and whether there is an appropriate measure of capacity that can be used across the spectrum of machine sizes. (One potentially interesting measure is SQL benchmarking since the advent of client-server architectures has made networks of micros potentially substitutable for larger systems in this important application.) The issue is made more interesting and complicated by the fact, mentioned above, that in most cases substitution of micros for minis or mainframes implies a significant investment in a network, so it is inappropriate to consider the micros in isolation from the supporting communication system and its attendant costs. Thus satisfactory modeling is required, including the determination of appropriate units of measurement followed by the gathering of appropriate data.

The testing of Scenario 1 described in Section 2, that is, the significance of costs borne by the user rather than list prices, is also a worthwhile project. This will require a significant data-gathering effort to arrive at reliable figures for discounts from list prices, expected residual values and maintenance and other operations costs.

With regard to improved data, one important issue noted earlier is the collection of more reliable observations on the technical variables required by models such as Kang's. It is obviously extremely important to be sure that each system's reported price corresponds to the actual technical attributes of a system so priced. The estimation of any models incorporating measurements of such variables drawn from the Computerworld data is not conclusive evidence for or against the usefulness of such variables in the models. Also, the gathering and analysis of more recent data than the 1988 data reported on here is clearly important. The 1989 Computerworld data is available, and as this is being written the 1990 data should soon be available.
