# A model of competing interorganizational systems and its application to airline reservation systems

William G. Chismar and Johannes Meier
University of Hawaii, Honolulu, HI, USA

Interorganizational systems (IOS) are automated information systems that cross company boundaries. This article presents a descriptive model of competition between providers of interorganizational systems in a mature market. The model emphasizes the competitive role of switching costs in conjunction with user benefit functions which increase with the number of users. Also introduced is a notion of competitive position which aids in describing competitive risks and opportunities in a market. The case of airline computerized reservation systems (CRS) is used throughout the paper to demonstrate the model.

## 1. Introduction

An interorganizational system (IOS) is an automated information system that is shared by two or more companies [5]. The sharing of resources between companies is made possible by a computer and communication infrastructure. The resources to be shared can be applications, such as reservation systems or programs for supply ordering. Bakos has stressed this aspect by defining IOS as “systems based on information technology that cross organizational boundaries and whose purpose is the exchange of information-based products or services” [1, p. 44]. Other labels for the same concept include Barrett and Konsynski’s [2, p. 94] acronym ‘IS\*’, inter-organizational information sharing system, and Choudhury’s [6, p. 44] acronym ‘IOIS’, interorganizational information system.

Most of the literature on IOS consists of ease studies that are used to stress the importance of IOS for gaining competitive advantage. However, the continuous repetition of the same case studies points at a need to reexamine the claims of competitive advantage. Difficulties in sustaining a competitive advantage with information systems are being realized $[7,6]$ increasingly. Also, as IOS becomes more widely used, competitive issues of the introduction of a new system become less important than issues of competition in markets with established IOS.

In this paper we move from the introduction of an IOS to competition in a market where multiple IOS are established. Rather than focusing on first mover advantages that seem to explain many of the early success stories of IOS, we concentrate on the identification of competitive options in a multiple system market. The existing literature which moves from the case studies toward the building of models of IOS highlights several economic factors: Switching costs, economies of scale (both included in most studies of IOS), efficiency of operations (e.g., [1]), network externalities [13,14], and the vertical integration of firms (e.g., [8]). Kleindorfer and Knieps [15] developed a model for investigating the viability of contracts for sharing sunk capital investments in light of different bargaining powers between the firms. Considering the first four factors, we introduce a descriptive model that provides the basis for a more rigorous treatment of competing IOS. We demonstrate the model with the computerized airline reservation systems (CRS) which, for some time, have had almost complete market penetration.

The paper proceeds as follows. An overview of CRS in Section 2 sets the stage for our analysis and Section 3 presents the model. As interorganizational systems cross organizational boundaries, it is important to distinguish between the perspective of the users of the systems and that of the providers of the systems. In Section 3.1 we introduce a representation of the user's perspective on competing IOS that centers on a decision rule of users concerning switching between the competing systems. Then, in Section 3.2, we present the competitive options of the providers of the IOS. The set of generic competitive moves provides a good framework for explaining some aspects of the competitive moves observed in the CRS case. Section 4 extends the model to include heterogeneous users and discusses the application to the airline CRS case. Finally, the conclusion points at further extensions and research challenges.

## 2. Setting the stage: Overview of CRS

Beginning in 1975 United Airlines and American Airlines started offering travel agencies direct access to their reservation systems. This marked the transition of CRS from solely internal information systems to IOS. Deregulation of the airline industry in 1978 had the direct effect of “heightened competition (…which) made the airline industry very information-intensive, and the conduit for much of that information was the reservations systems” [9, p. 366]. By 1988 there were five major providers of reservation system services. (Listed with date of introduction and the 1986 and 1988 market-shares (%), measured by the number of agencies using the system as their primary system. Complied from [23] and various issues of Travel Weekly.):

(1) American Airlines' Sabre (1975, 35.1–31.8);

(2) United Airlines' Apollo (1975, 26.4–24.7);

(3) Texas Air's System One (1981, 17.3–18.2) $^{1}$ ;

(4) TWA's and Northwest's Pars (1976, 12.9–17.1);

(5) Delta Airlines' Datas II (1982, 8.3–8.2) $^{2}$ .

Also, by 1988, 95% of all travel agencies in the U.S. subscribed to one of the CRS [19] and less than 10% subscribed to multiple systems.

In addition to the CRS vendors and the travel agencies, a third set of participants in the CRS is made up of companies which provide information for and receive bookings through the CRS. These participants include airlines that do not own a CRS, hotels, car rental agencies, and tour organizers. With CRS becoming the dominant method of distribution of tickets and reservations, these travel suppliers must be listed on a CRS and pay a fee to the vendor for each booking made via the CRS. $^{3}$ Because travel agents typically use only one system, the suppliers must participate in systems with significant penetration of the market or risk being unable to reach a certain fraction of the customer base; in essence, they are captive buyers of CRS services. The U.S. Justice Department and some airlines have argued that because of the necessity to be on the CRS, booking fees are not disciplined by competition and should be reduced or eliminated by government regulation [18]. (So far the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) has rejected these arguments and refused to regulate a ‘zero fee’.)

Thus, the providers of CRS compete in two distinct markets: the market to get travel agents to adopt the CRS, and the market to get travel suppliers to participate in the CRS. However, important interactions exist between these markets. To begin, since they represent two sources of income for the CRS provider, the provider has options of tradeoffs between the markets. A provider could, for example, use the income from one market to subsidize competition ill the other market. Much of the U.S. DOT's investigations into the CRS market has focused on unfair competition through cross subsidization. With respect to the users of the system, the value of the CRS to the travel suppliers increases as the size of the network increases, i.e., the more agencies on the network, the greater the value to the providers. Similarly, more travel suppliers on the network means more information and greater value to the travel agencies.

The market to get suppliers onto a CRS represents a major source of income and a very critical issue for new, startup CRS providers. However, because of the captive nature of the market, it is less important to established CRS providers than getting travel agents onto their CRS. In this paper we concentrate on the travel agent market.

The providers of CRS receive benefits from four primary sources: Booking fees paid by the participants; user fees paid by the travel agents; the value of the system to internal operations; and, possibly, increases in ticket sales due to the 'halo effect' [23]. The halo effect is the "systematic tendency [of travel agents] to book a disproportionately large share of revenues on the vendor airline" [23, p. 116]. The source and importance of the halo effect, and even the existence of the halo effect, have been debated in the government reviews of the CRS industry [23]. Much of the debate has been concerned with determining if the source of these benefits may be bias in the display of information, most of which has been ruled by the DOT to be illegal. On the cost side, the providers face high fixed investment to start up the CRS, periodic maintenance costs, and variable costs related to the number of users on the CRS.

The users of the system, the travel agents, receive benefits from three primary sources: Improvements in internal operations; increase sales from improved service to their customers; and incentive payments from the providers of the CRS. The introduction of a CRS into a travel agency almost totally eliminates the need to handle paper except for the issuing of tickets. In fact, given the complex fare structures after deregulation, CRS access constitutes the only efficient means of obtaining up-to-date travel information. An indicator for the importance of CRS in this context is the huge daily transaction volume. For example, Sabre processes about 60 million messages per day [21]. A large part of the improved service to their customers arises from options available on the CRS which are directly related to size of the network in terms of the number of travel providers on the system.

On the cost side, the users pay fixed fees for using the CRS and incurs expenses to train employees to use the CRS. In addition, if they decide to switch to a different CRS, the users face significant costs. Among the costs for an agency switching to another reservation system are:

\- Paying the former system provider for the cost of removing the equipment and for the lost equipment rental fees;

\- Opportunity costs in the form of liquidation damages: Agencies may be “responsible for the estimated value of the offline booking fees from carriers whose passengers they would have booked.” [3, p. 40];

\- Costs for lawsuits brought by the carrier whose contract is being broken;

\- Learning costs: “For an agent to use a new system, he or she would need to stop work and undergo a complete retraining program.” [20, p. 173].

Many of these switching costs result from bilateral bargaining between the agency and the CRS provider and incorporated into their contract. The CRS requires very large sunk capital investments in terms of computer and telecommunications equipment. Through the bargaining process, the provider attempts to displace some of the risk associated with these costs to the agency through guaranteed income. Such negotiations to distribute the sunk costs are common to many interorganizational systems. (See [15] for the discussion and model of this process.) In the CRS case, many parameters of the contracts are regulated by the U.S. DOT, with specific formulas for calculating such terms as liquidation damages; most formulas are a function of the volume of business of the agency.

Although some travel agencies initially acquired more than one system, the airlines, “through severe financial penalties or pressure” [11, p. 5], managed to impose contracts forbidding multiple systems. The push for exclusive use of one CRS in a travel agency was in line with the quest for competitive advantage through display bias (the preferential displaying of the provider’s flights over those of other participating airlines) and halo effect by the carriers that owned the CRS. In fact, although the Department of Transportation officially banned display biases in 1984 (D.O.T. Rule 225), more sophisticated forms of bias can still be observed today [4]. Similarly, if one does not control the system’s operations, the right to equal access of information on a CRS by participating carriers is hard to enforce. The CRS providers had also pushed travel agencies to sign long-term contracts for the respective CRS use. However, in 1984 the Department of Transportation imposed constraints on the contracts (D.O.T. Rule 225) that limited the maximum length of a contract to 5 years and forbade exclusivity clauses.

At present, the majority of all travel agencies still subscribe to only one system. The financial burden of subscription to two or more parallel CRS can be justified only by very large travel agencies. In addition, agents using multiple systems must contend with format and process differences across the systems, yielding additional search costs in terms of effort and time to complete tasks. And, finally, the CRS-airlines can still exert considerable pressure on travel agencies.

## 3. A model of competing IOS

In this section we introduce a new model of two competing IOS. We restrict our analysis to a single type of IOS structure, that of an IOS without an intermediary [1]. Thus, there are two types of organizations involved, the providers and the users of the IOS. $^{4}$ Next, because we are interested in looking at competition in a mature IOS market, we assume that the IOS are established systems, each with an existing set of users, and that no new users enter the market. It is the switching of user across existing IOS that is of interest, rather than the introduction of new IOS. Also, we begin with an assumption of homogeneous users who are sufficiently small and therefore not able to unilaterally change the market. Although the homogeneity assumption is very restricting, it aids in demonstrating the basic concepts and ideas necessary for the heterogeneous model presented in Section 4. Also, we assume that there is no collusion by the users, and that the users have information only about themselves and not about the intentions of the other users. Finally, we assume that benefits of the IOS to the users increase with the number of users on the system.

Let the established IOS be represented by $S_{1}$ and $S_{2}$ , and $n_{i}$ represent the number of users on IOS $S_{i}$ in a market segmentation. A market segmentation p is defined by $p = (n_{1}, n_{2})$ , i.e., the number of users on each of the systems. The assumption of no new users requires that $n_{1} + n_{2} = n$ for all p.

## 3.1. The users' perspective

The benefits that a user on system $S_{i}$ derives is the sum of the efficiency benefits, that are independent of the network size, and benefits which depend on the current number of users, $n_{i}$ , on $S_{i}$ . This benefit function, $b_{i}(p)$ (or equivalently, $b_{i}(n_{i})$ ), is strictly monotonically increasing in $n_{i}$ because of the network externalities and, possibly, the distribution of scale economy benefits to the users.

Users tend not to be able to observe these benefit functions. Rather, they can only compare their own current benefits with the current benefits of users on another system. Thus, we define the following two-stage decision rule for users: In the first period, users compare the relative benefits of each system while taking account of the switching costs, $sw_{i}$ , for switching to IOS $S_{i}$ . Based on this comparison, switching may occur in the second period. The assumptions of homogeneous users implies that, if switching of users from one system to another occurs, it encompasses all users on the respective system.

Formally, switching from $S_{1}$ to $S_{2}$ occurs only if

$$
b _ {1} (p) \leqslant b _ {2} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2}.\tag{1}
$$

Similarly, switching from $S_{2}$ to $S_{1}$ occurs if

$$
b _ {2} (p) \leqslant b _ {1} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {1}.\tag{2}
$$

Note that the switch to another system entails a change to another segmentation, leading to an additional increase in the user's benefits.

Because of the strict monotonicity of the benefit functions, there is at most one segmentation, $p_{1}^{\prime}$ , where $b_{2}(p_{1}^{\prime}) = b_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}) - \mathrm{sw}_{1}$ . Similarly, there is at most one segmentation, $p_{2}^{\prime}$ , where $b_{1} = b_{2}(p_{2}^{\prime}) - \mathrm{sw}_{2}$ . Thus, we can define three regions of market segmentation and assume, without loss of generality, that each of the regions exists.

$$
\begin{array}{c} \alpha := \big \{p \mid b _ {1} (p) \leqslant b _ {2} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2} \big \}, \\ \beta := \big \{p \mid b _ {2} (p) \leqslant b _ {1} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {1} \big \}, \\ \gamma := \big \{p \mid b _ {1} (p) > b _ {2} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2} \text { and } \\ b _ {2} (p) > b _ {1} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {1} \big \}. \end{array}
$$

According to the user's decision rule, all users on system $S_{1}$ in an initial market segmentation in region $\alpha$ will switch to system $S_{2}$ . Given an initial market segmentation in region $\beta$ , users will switch from $S_{2}$ to $S_{1}$ . Finally and most interestingly, an initial market segmentation in $\gamma$ results in no switching at all. Figure 1 illustrates these conclusions in the case of linear benefit functions.

It is interesting to note that the switching costs allow nonoptimal market segmentation in terms of aggregate user benefits to persist. While all users would experience increased benefits if everyone switched to one system, no user will unilaterally switch. Further, the model shows that as switching costs increase, the set of segmentations where no switching occurs increases. It should be noted again that these results depend on the imperfect information of users in the model.

## 3.2. The provider's perspective

In the previous section, we presented a model of IOS and looked at the behavior of users. The model can also provide a framework for an investigation of the options for competitive moves by providers of IOS. From the model, two options for competitive moves can be identified: A system provider can change the level of benefits associated with an IOS and he can influence the switching costs between systems. In the latter option we can further distinguish between switching costs from a system and switching costs to a system. This gives us three generic moves:

(1) Increasing the benefits of an IOS to its users;
(2) Increasing switching costs from own IOS;

(3) Decreasing switching costs to own system.

The effects of these moves can be studied straightforwardly in the model. For all moves we assume that the initial segmentation, $p_{0}$ , is within $\gamma$ ; otherwise there is no need for a competitive move, as users will switch to one or the other system anyway.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that the benefits to users of IOS $S_{2}$ are increased. This leads to a new benefit function $\tilde{b}_{2}$ , which is also assumed to be strictly monotonically increasing. Figure 2 illustrates this move in the linear case; $p_{i}^{\prime}$ and $\tilde{p}_{i}^{\prime}$ denote the segmentation where switching to $S_{i}$ occurs before and after the move, respectively. The move to increase the benefits of users of $S_{2}$ can be characterized by a decrease in the distance of the current segmentation to the segmentation where switching to $S_{2}$ occurs and an increase in the distance to the segmentation where switching to $S_{1}$ takes place.

Similarly, an increase of switching costs from $S_{2}$ results in an increased distance from the current segmentation to the segmentation where switching to the competitor occurs. This also leads to an increased size of $\gamma$ , the set of segmentation where no switching occurs. Conversely, the final move of decreasing the switching costs to $S_{2}$ results in a decreased size of $\gamma$ , as the distance from the current segmentation to the switching segmentation to $S_{2}$ is decreased.

The above discussion suggests that a clear competitive danger (or opportunity) exists for a provider when the current market segmentation is close to the boundaries of $\gamma$ . Under such conditions, only a relatively small change in switching costs or benefits is necessary to cause users to switch to a new system. Thus, we propose to use the distances from the current segmentation to each switching segmentations as indicators of the competitive position of the IOS. Also, the size of $\gamma$ can serve as an indicator of the ‘stability' of the market: A larger $\gamma$ indicates a greater number of segmentations where no switching occurs, which implies a more stable the market.

## CRS Example.

We use the model to investigate some aspects of competition in the CRS market. In particular, we focus on the market for travel agencies. The market share data at the national level for the CRS would infer that the CRS market has been static, that all the competitive moves by the providers have had little effect. However, one need only look at the data from a geographic regional level to get a very different, dynamic picture. Table 1 shows the number of large and medium air traffic hubs (metropolitan airports) at which CRS providers lost or gained more than 10% market share between 1983 and 1986. (Source: [23])

Support for a user benefit function which increases with the number of users on the CRS comes from two sources. The first comes from the passing through of gains from economies of scale: As the CRS system grows in the number of users, the provider increases the benefits to the users. While this practice of passing some of the benefits through need not be true, a provider in a dominant position may even reduce the level of benefits, evidence exists to support the practice. As more travel agencies join a CRS in a region, that CRS vendor tends to allocate more resources to the system. For example, the vendor will assign full time support personnel to the region rather than having a toll free, 800 number for support. These added resources represent increased benefits to the agencies.

The second source of support for an increasing benefit function comes from the dual market for CRS of travel agencies and suppliers of travel services (airlines, car rental companies, hotels, etc.). The value of the CRS to the travel suppliers increases as the size of the network increases, i.e., the more agencies on the network, the greater the value to the suppliers. Similarly, more travel suppliers on the network means more information and greater value to the travel agencies. Thus, while the model does not explicitly show the interaction, an increase in the number of agencies on the system indirectly increases the benefits to agencies on the system. $^{5}$

The model assumes a starting point of a mature market with users on each of the systems. In the CRS case, this point occurred around the time of the deregulation of the airline industry in 1978. Up to that time, the airlines nominally competed in the CRS market. But, deregulation created a competitive environment that revealed the potential market power of the CRS and drove all but the largest carriers from the CRS marketplace [9, p. 362].

## Competitive move: increasing user benefits

The increase of user benefits as a competitive move has a long history in the competition of CRS providers. Beginning in 1978, American and United both added features, such as, invoice/itinerary print-outs, that were aimed at increasing the productivity of travel agencies. The introduction of graphics terminals and a decentralized system architecture is just another instantiation of this type of move. It is interesting to note that a simultaneous increase of user benefits by both competitors does not change the competitive position of the IOS. Thus, it is not surprising that the CRS providers are careful to duplicate benefit increases of competitors.

Another way to increase travel agent's benefits is to provide additional information on-other carriers. American Airlines was the first to introduce co-host agreements with other carriers that gave those carriers preferential treatment of their schedules on Sabre in 1978. Such co-host agreements, especially with carriers that did not compete directly with American, allowed American to increase Sabre's presence in the market and increase the travel agent market share [9, p. 361]. Interestingly enough, United immediately introduced its own co-host option to counter this increase in benefits by American.

User benefits can also be increased by improving the timeliness of the available information on the CRS. The idea is to provide up-to-date flight data of all participating airlines, not only the vendor's airline. System One was the first to introduce this feature by directly linking to other airline's databases. Without direct access links, CRS providers can only store the flight schedules information for all airlines as compiled in the Official Airline Guide (OAG) on a weekly basis $^{6}$ and the airlines' fare information as collected by the Airline Tariff Publishing Company (ATP) on a daily basis [20]. From the travelers' and the travel agencies' standpoint, there is clearly an urgent need for more current and reliable information in view of the scope of the changes due to deregulation and CRS. "The consensus among agencies is that the more direct the access to the internal system of the particular carrier, the more reliable the information" [20, p. 161]. The need for up-to-date information can be illustrated by the huge number of daily fare changes due to yield management. For example, on a single day in 1987 Delta used its CRS to make 79000 fare changes, "adjusting them up and down as tickets were sold," while "American adjusted 106000 fares the same day" [16, p. 68]. System One's network of direct access links is currently most useful for travel agencies with fast access to the databases of 23 carriers [3]. Thus, direct access is "probably System One's strongest selling point for agencies using rival [systems]" estimates Nadine Godwink, automation editor of Travel Weekly [3, p. 41]. The success of these moves can be seen in System One's increased market share of $18\%$ of the travel agency market in 1987, up from $15\%$ in 1986 according to Travel Weekly magazine.

## Competitive move: changing switching costs

More recent applications offered by the CRS providers include personalized services. A good example is “the ability to build individual or corporate customer profiles that can be automatically merged with the Passenger Name Record whenever new bookings are made” [3, p. 39]. Agencies clearly benefit from increased functionality through personalization of service. As these new services require personalized data files, they are hard to transfer to a competitor’s system. This brings us to a discussion of the strategies involving switching costs.

In line with our model's suggestions, there have been reports on large bonuses paid to switch reservation systems. In the addendum of [22], Frontier Airlines refers to the business press to show that United has engaged in different types of sponsoring ranging from cash bonuses, the dropping of user fees, to installation givebacks. A 1985 congressional hearing contains testimony by Northwest that, for example, United offered an agent in Northwest's territory for a switch from Sabre to Apollo the following incentives: \$500 000 in cash, a 10% 'override' (on top of the regular commission) for sales on United and five years' free use of Apollo including telephone line charges [11, p. 6].

Particularly elucidating are observations of the competitive moves of the latecomers to the market, especially Texas Air. Texas Air tries to lower an agency's switching costs to its system by defending agencies that switch to System One in lawsuits. The expected revenue increases from the switching travel agencies are “seen to more than compensate for even vast sums in damages, according to System One.” [11, p. 8]

## 4. Extending the model to heterogeneous users

We now relax the assumption of homogeneous users by allowing differences in switching costs. We incorporate heterogeneous users into our model by grouping all users into sets of homogeneous users. Thus, users in each group face the same switching costs, but the switching costs are different across groups. The grouping of users according to switching costs has to be viewed for each system separately. In other words, users in a group that face the same switching costs towards system $S_{1}$ , need not all face the same switching costs towards system $S_{2}$ . This construction is a natural extension of the basic model with the basic model being subsumed in the extreme case of all users in the same group for each system. The other extreme is given by the case where all users face different switching costs towards the systems and thus the group size is one. We continue with the assumption that users have information only about themselves, not about the other users, and that they cannot collude.

The following construction ranks groups of users according to their switching costs towards one system. We shall apply the construction explicitly to users of system $S_{1}$ that face switching costs to system $S_{2}$ . The construction is analogous for users of system $S_{2}$ .

Let $sw_{2}^{i}, i = 1, \ldots, k$ , be the different switching costs faced by users of system $S_{1}$ . The $sw_{2}^{i}$ are assumed to be ordered to increase with i. Let the actual and potential users of system $S_{1}$ be grouped into groups $g^{i}, i = 1, \ldots, k$ , with the switching costs of users in group $g^{i}$ being $sw_{2}^{i}$ . Each user in the market is in one and only one group with respect to one system, i.e.,

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {k} \mid g ^ {i} \mid = n.\tag{3}
$$

Let $p_0$ be the current segmentation. We assume that users in the groups $g^1 \ldots g^{j-1}$ are on system $S_2$ and users in the groups $g^j, \ldots, g^k$ are on system $S_1$ . Thus,

$$
p _ {0} = \left(\sum_ {i = j} ^ {k} | g ^ {i} |, \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {j - 1} | g ^ {i} |\right).\tag{4}
$$

The $\gamma$ region in our initial model, where no switching occurs, must now be extended to allow for a region for each group of users: $\gamma^{i}$ , j =

$1,\ldots,k$ . The left boundary of the $\gamma^{j}$ intervals are determined by the following equations

$$
b _ {2} (p) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2} ^ {j} = b _ {1} (p) \quad j = 1, \dots , k.\tag{5}
$$

These k points correspond to our previous definition of $p_{2}^{\prime}$ . We shall now label the respective segmentations that are defined as switching segmentations for the different $sw_{2}^{i}$ as $p_{2}^{i}$ . As we are only interested in switching to $S_{2}$ we may neglect the right boundary of the interval $\gamma^{j}$ and just label it $p_{1}^{\prime}$ . Let $\gamma^{i} = [p_{2}^{i}, p_{1}^{\prime}]$ . The increasing switching costs $sw_{2}^{j}, \ldots, sw_{2}^{k}$ now lead to an ascending chain of intervals, analogous to the $\gamma$ region in the model with homogeneous users and representing the regions where users will not switch systems without incentives

$$
\gamma^ {j} \subset \gamma^ {j + 1} \subset \dots \subset \gamma^ {k}.\tag{6}
$$

As in the previous model, we assume that the market begins in equilibrium and providers are interested in how the users on system $S_{1}$ react to a competitive move by the provider of system $S_{2}$ . As an initial investment, that gets a group of users to switch also, changes the returns for all users, we have to take account of higher order effects of the initial competitive move. Thus, one needs to identify conditions when such a 'bandwagon' effect gets started and when it will stop.

Our previous analysis of generic competitive moves gives system provider $S_{2}$ two options to target the group of users of S, with the lowest switching costs. If users of $g^{j}$ would switch to $S_{2}$ without switching costs, the provider of $S_{2}$ can decrease the switching costs $sw_{2}^{j}$ of users in group $g^{j}$ sufficiently to induce switching. The second option is to increase the benefits of system $S_{2}$ globally to the point where users in $g^{j}$ will switch.

Let us start by looking at the first option of reducing the switching costs of users in $g^{j}$ . The first-order effect of this move is the switching of users in $g^{j}$ to $S_{2}$ if condition (7) holds, where $\tilde{w}_{2}^{j}$ denotes the reduced switching costs of users in group $g^{j}$ .

$$
b _ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {j} | g ^ {i} |\right) - \mathrm{s} \tilde {\mathrm{w}} _ {2} ^ {j} \geqslant b _ {1} \left(\sum_ {i = j} ^ {k} | g ^ {i} |\right).\tag{7}
$$

As a result of this competitive move, there are now $\sum_{i=1}^{j}|g^{i}|$ users on system $S_{2}$ and $\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}|g^{i}|$ users on system $S_{1}$ . In other words, we now face a new market segmentation, $p_{0}^{j}$ , due to the competitive move, where

$$
p _ {0} ^ {j} = \left(\sum_ {i = j + 1} ^ {k} | g ^ {i} |, \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {j} | g ^ {i} |\right).\tag{8}
$$

A second-order effect now occurs if the new market segmentation and the ensuing different network externality values for the users induce the next group of users, $g^{j+1}$ , to switch. This is the case if the following condition holds

$$
b _ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {j + 1} | g ^ {i} |\right) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2} ^ {j + 1} \geqslant b _ {1} \left(\sum_ {i = j + 1} ^ {k} | g ^ {i} |\right).\tag{9}
$$

Naturally, a second-order effect may cause in turn higher order effects. This type of switching behavior corresponds to a bandwagon gathering steam.

The bandwagon only comes to a stop before all users have switched, if there is one hurdle imposed by the switching costs of one group $g^{i}$ , $i \leqslant k$ , that cannot be compensated by the increase in benefits due to network externalities. Formally, the bandwagon will stop if there exists a switching cost $sw_{2}^{l}$ such that

$$
b _ {2} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {j} | g ^ {i} |\right) - \mathrm{sw} _ {2} ^ {j + 1} <   b _ {1} \left(\sum_ {i = j + 1} ^ {k} | g ^ {i} |\right).\tag{10}
$$

Similar to decreasing switching costs, one can start the bandwagon by increasing the benefits of users of $S_{2}$ .

## CRS Example.

Primarily due to the great differences in contracts with the CRS providers, the switching costs faced by travel agents vary greatly among travel agents. Taking competitive factors, such as the presence of competing CRS, and user requests for capabilities and technology into account, the providers negotiate specific contracts with individual users. The contract between the user and the provider represents the most significant factor determining the user's switching cost. [23] Other factors include the size of the agency, the need to retrain employees and to modify procedures.

Almost all contracts contain liquidation damage clauses which require the user who switch systems before the expiration of their contract to pay the provider damages equal to the value of the users own future subscription fees, plus the booking revenues the provider would have received from the participating airline, plus, in some cases, the value of lost equipment. (The assumption is made that equipment at a user's location is specific to that user and, therefore, has zero value when that user leaves the CRS.) The formulas for calculating the lost revenues vary from provider to provider, but normally estimate the number of bookings per month the user makes and multiplies that estimate by the average booking fee. In addition, most contract contain a rollover clause under which the contract is automatically renewed for 5 years with the addition of any new equipment, such as, a terminal, pc, or printer. $^{8}$

Our model, which assumes that there is no collusion by the users and that the users have information only about themselves, suggests that providers would be targeting users with the lowest switching costs, which would be those agencies with the least amount of time remaining on their contract. The evidence from the 1984–1987 period seems to support this strategy, along with the logical response: A wave of “contract renewal programs...[to reduce]...the pool of likely conversion targets by starting new, five-year contract terms for a large number of agents.” [23, p. 131].

Again, the providers seem to be concentrating on geographic regions, within which halo effects and the market presence of provider airlines have reciprocative, additive effects. These selective campaigns primarily involve the payment of bonuses for converting, the granting of commission overrides, and the paying of switching costs. Thus, the model would predict, in the absence of competitive counter actions, a bandwagoning effect within regions. Indeed, some providers were quite successful in gaining large market shares in the 1983–1986 time period: For example, System One in Miami jumped from 16 to 45%; Apollo in Orlando moved from 10 to 35%; and Pars in St. Louis moved from 59 to 77% [23]. Although further data is needed to isolate the specific reasons for such large jumps, bandwagoning effects seem quite possible.

In the absence of a hurdle imposed by some group of users (as specified by equation (10) above), our model suggests the eventual dominance of a single CRS within a geographic region. The above discussion of heterogeneous users points toward the possible convergence of CRS market dominance and market dominance of that provider's airline. Current market data does not support or refute this possibility. However, the U.S. Department of Transportation has mentioned that such a development would be of concern because of the obvious limiting effects on competition in the airline industry.

## 5. Conclusion

In this paper we introduced a model for illustrating competitive moves between providers of interorganizational systems ill a mature market and applied the model to the airline computer reservation systems. The model emphasizes the role of switching costs in conjunction with user benefit functions, which increase with the number of users, in determining competitive options. It introduces a notion of competitive position, which aids in describing competitive risks and opportunities in a market. Generic options for competitive moves for IOS providers are derived. An extension of the model from uniform users to heterogeneous users results in the identification of bandwagon effects which determine some dynamics of switching. Further extensions of the model to incorporate more than two competitors and new users entering the market are discussed in $[17]$ .

The model is a static model and, as such, lacks the power to model the dynamics of a competitive environment. Our goal in this paper was to provide foundation for more sophisticated models that could be used to empirically test competitive strategies. Extending our approach to a dynamic setting, where time-lags and sequential moves by IOS providers are modeled, seems to be a promising direction for further research that aims at a predictive theory of competition among IOS providers.

In the discussions of airline CRS, we tried to show that contrary to how it might appear by looking at national market share figures, the CRS market place remains quite dynamic and a fruitful area for research. We highlighted several issues for further research; two of the more interesting elements are the time dependent switching costs and the possible convergence of airline market dominance and CRS dominance in geographic regions.
