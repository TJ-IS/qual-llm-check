# Towards a theory of managerial problem solving

Gerald F. Smith

Information and Decision Sciences Department, Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN 55455, USA

Managerial problem solving, action-oriented thought in organizational contexts, is a key target of DSS intervention and has been extensively researched from a number of perspectives. Nonetheless, this domain is poorly understood, practice remaining largely beyond the range of effective scientific prescription. Existing theoretical work is reviewed in light of the nature of domain phenomena. A new theory building strategy, focusing on the conscious representational and reasoning activities of managers, is proposed. Related research needs and opportunities are identified.

Keywords: Managerial problems, Managerial problem solving, Decision making

## 1. Introduction

It is an article of faith among researchers on decision support systems and other intellectual tools that to be effective, interventions must be compatible with the problem solving activities of their human users. One version of this assumption treats interventions as part of a man-machine system designed to effect an appropriate division of labor between human and technological components $[56]$ . Thus, research on decision aids and other intellectual tools presumes an understanding of the cognitive activities of their human users.

While much managerial thinking serves a general “sense making” purpose, other thinking, commonly termed “decision making” or “problem solving,” is action-oriented. One thinks in order to determine how best to act. Consider Fig. 1, a synopsis of a problem situation recently faced by a large corporation. While highly individuated, the case is not atypical. Assuredly a great deal of thinking – by the manager of R&D, the subsidiary CEO, and others – was generated by this situation. Ultimately actions were taken and consequences ensued. Thinking affects actions which affect outcomes. Prevailing assumptions about rationality [69] imply that good thinking increases the likelihood of achieving desired outcomes.

A science of management should have relevance to phenomena like “The R&D Problem.” Theories of managerial thinking should provide deep accounts of the cognitive performances of involved parties. Prescriptive theories must be able to assess these performances and suggest solution strategies appropriate to the situation. Interventionists should be able to provide intellectual tools that respond to problem demands and yet are considerate of human capabilities. Arguably, existing theories of managerial thinking achieve none of these objectives. We only partially understand what differentiates good from bad problem solving, and our prescriptions and interventions are often inoperable or irrelevant.

The purpose of this paper is to promote and provide direction for the development of a theory of managerial problem solving. The next section characterizes managerial problems and problem solving, noting the difficulties these pose to theory building. Existing research on managerial problem solving is surveyed and classified into four streams. After discussing the contributions and limitations of these approaches, a promising strategy for theory building is identified. Research needs and opportunities are defined, providing impetus and starting points for progressive research in this domain.

## 2. Managerial problems and problem solving

A theory of managerial problem solving must consider both problem and problem solver, the nature of the task as well as the individual's activities and capacities. The theory must also account for the organizational context within which problems transpire and individuals seek their solution.

## 2.1. Nature of managerial problems

While all problems are partially subjective, conceptual, entities involving a relationship of dissonance between reality and preferences [2] [40] [73], managerial problems are especially indefinite. As in “The R&D Problem,” multiple parties are commonly involved, each having their own beliefs and objectives. People may not even agree that a problem exists. Undesirable conditions tend to be interconnected, there being no grounds for identifying one or a cluster of conditions as the “true” problem. As Dery and Mock [22] noted, an undesirable state can be conceptualized in many ways, multiple definitions adding further to problem indefiniteness.

Managerial problems display infinite variety. Being intrinsically difficult [2], problems include the conditions that make solutions hard to find. In managerial domains, no neat boundary demarcates problem from context (contributing further to problem indefiniteness), since apparent background conditions can bear decisively on solutions. In “The R&D Problem,” the personal history of the subsidiary’s CEO might enable or block R&D efforts at project renewal. The problem, fully defined, must take that history into account. Details can be critical, differentiating problems without bound.

Thirdly, most managerial problems lack a clear-cut solution or stopping rule, qualities that Rittel and Webber [60] ascribed to “wicked” problems. Solutions are provisional and often cannot be validated as correct, even after implementation. Unable to evaluate solutions by their outcomes, researchers are forced to assess the processes by which solutions are derived. This evaluation is far more difficult and not much more conclusive.

## 2.2. Managerial problem solving

While much, if not most, managerial problem solving transpires as a group process within an organizational context, all such activity depends, at bottom, on the contributions of individuals. Narrowly construed, problem solving is action-oriented thought, an individual cognitive process [71] [73]. Reflecting the psychological nature of its phenomena, the managerial problem solving domain has no firm conceptualization of relevant processes and capacities. For instance, the process by which one becomes aware of a problem is variously termed problem “finding” [57], “sensing” [39], “recognition” [14], and “identification” [72]. Some of these differences are purely terminological, reflecting relatively arbitrary choices among the many natural language terms that suggest discovery. In other cases, however, differences result from alternative ways of splitting processes into components. For instance, Cowan's account of problem recognition [14] consolidates activities that could, in principle, be split between problem identification and definition [71]. These discrepancies reflect the lack of an authoritative scientific lexicon of cognitive capacities and processes. Rather than a fundamental anatomy of mind, psychologists describe thought with a loose network of concepts derived from the folk psychology of everyday affairs [77]. Many of these concepts are poorly defined and important conceptual relationships have not been specified.

Conceptual difficulties result from the nature of psychological processes. Many are automated and leave no conscious trace $[49]$ $[62]$ , making them resistant to empirical investigation. It is difficult to distinguish differences in kinds of processes from differences in degree. Memorial recall, for instance, shades into inference and also into creativity. Attempts to decompose processes into ever smaller components run into seemingly irreducible capacities that must be “black boxed,” accepted as unexplained primitives. While black boxing may be necessary, it can thwart prescription.

Cognitive science's signal claim is that thought is representational, involving mental contents depicted in some language. This is expressed by the concept of “intentionality,” not to be confused with the idea of purposefulness [29]. Relatedly, research on managerial cognition argues that managers interpret or ascribe meaning to situations [18]. Problems are represented internally, variable mental definitions yielding variations in problem solving responses. Associative processes affect definitions, as when activated schemata “type” a situation [14]. The variety of perspectives afforded by managerial problems suggests that several schemata may be applicable to a single situation. But any practicable mental typology risks overlooking critical details. Thus, the intrinsic variety of managerial problems argues for the importance of problem definition [22].

Another characteristic of managerial problem solving is its reliance on experiential knowledge recalled from memory. Whereas psychologists traditionally study performance in puzzle-like tasks not involving general knowledge, in managerial problem solving the existence, evocation, and application of such information is of the essence.

Perkins, Allen, and Hafner [53] investigated the reasoning people do in response to practical problems, identifying various common failings. Most reflect inadequate access to memorial knowledge: People know much that is relevant, but fail to generate it, mistakenly adopting the first plausible argument they construct. The importance of experiential knowledge results from the lack of scientific knowledge concerning managerial phenomena: the behaviors of humans, organizations, and competitive markets. Managers must rely on individual experience and general folklore, which is not codified in books and is only weakly organized in memory. As a result, knowledge evocation is difficult and fallible.

Finally, managerial problem solving often lacks clear procedural structure. While Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret [45] found some order at the organizational level, and although elements of individual thinking are intelligible, the thinking process rarely displays the coherence and direction epitomized in a mathematical proof. Solution efforts follow heuristic strategies that are not well understood. This is partially explained by the thought-action linkage that Weick [92] noted: One's next thinking step is conditioned on the outcomes of ongoing activities. The absence of procedural structure is also due to the nature of managerial problems. Since they are typically indefinite, one-of-a-kind, and lack a clear-cut solution, such problems do not lend themselves to proceduralization.

## 2.3. Organizational context

Many of the problems managers face result directly from the nature of organizations. For instance, problems often arise because the legitimate activities of one organizational member create difficulties for another (e.g., Operations reduces inventory to hold down carrying costs, causing Marketing to complain about increased stockouts). If the nature of organizations leads to certain kinds of problems, it also affects how issues are addressed. For one thing, organizations implicitly constrain alternatives, adding to the criteria that a successful solution must satisfy. This occurs because organizations necessarily involve structures – policies, procedures, roles, and systems – which, while changeable, are relatively stable. A proposed solution must suit the organization as well as the problem: It must be reasonably consistent with established structures, so that its implementation does not threaten organizational consequences that are worse than the initial problem itself. For instance, while “The R&D Problem” could be solved by top management fiat, this option might be ruled out as in conflict with an established policy of decentralized decision-making.

Secondly, organizations affect individual cognition. This derives from their status as interpretation systems perpetuating a set of shared beliefs and values $[18]$ . Walsh and Fahey $[89]$ noted that organizations provide arenas for negotiating the assumptions on which problem solving depends. In “The R&D Problem,” if the corporate memory includes instances of information leakage to competitors by outside R&D providers, the subsidiary CEO may well want to change his plans.

The organizational context also alters the problem by expanding the set of relevant objectives. Beyond being right for the organization, a solution must take the interests of involved individuals and subunits into account. This gives rise to the widely acknowledged politicality of managerial problem solving $[23]$ $[35]$ $[42]$ and the opportunistic, “garbage can,” quality cited by Cohen, March, and Olsen $[13]$ . As in any group process, individuals need to communicate with and persuade others. Because right answers can rarely be found and many parties are stakeholders to outcomes, persuasion involves appeals to self-interest as well as reason. This creates the prevalence of bargaining and coalition building.

## 3. Existing theoretical perspectives

As noted by Garfield [29, p. 11], scientific domains are individuated “on pragmatic rather than ontological principles.” Clusters of research spring up, staking out overlapping territories, being differentiated more by assumptions and strategies than by the empirical targets of investigation. The same applies to research within a domain. Particularly in the behavioral and social sciences, whose phenomena themselves are conceptual products [30], a topic can be conceptualized in many ways, each providing the basis for a stream of research. Though neither mutually exclusive nor exhaustive, four major theoretical perspectives can be discerned in research on managerial problem solving. These are demarcated along the following lines:

Decision theory. A manager's action-oriented thought can be conceived of as decision making, a choice among alternatives. This conceptualization lends itself to expectancy-value theorizing, notably via Von Neumann and Morgenstern's [87] expected utility theory. As a result, this is the most overtly normative of the four theoretical perspectives.

Organizational decision making. Managerial problem solving can also be studied as a group or organization-level phenomenon, the latter being prominent in the literature. The implicit assumption is that organizational factors are decisive in explaining problem solving outcomes.

Individual traits. Working at the individual level of analysis, some theorists propose trait or dispositional accounts of managerial problem solving. Thus, it is argued that highly stable, innate characteristics of managers strongly affect their problem solving performances.

Cognitive processing. Finally, managerial problem solving can be viewed as a cognitive process that individuals perform to achieve their goals. Research in this vein is concerned with delineating the mental activities and capacities that people employ during problem solving.

The remainder of this section discusses each of these theoretical perspectives in turn, assessing its contributions and limitations.

## 3.1. Decision theory

This is the most fully developed perspective, encompassing by far the largest volume of research. Action-oriented thought can be viewed as progressing through a decision tree, decomposing decision tasks into predictive and evaluative judgments. The latter express one's preferences over alternatives; the former yield subjective probabilities that quantify uncertainties endemic to decision problems. Scientific research on decision making began with the axiomatizing efforts of

Von Neumann and Morgenstern [87], who showed that choices under uncertainty could be described, and prescribed for, as maximizations of subjective expected utility. Subsequent formal decision theoretic research has explored alternative axiomatic structures [28]. Formal decision theory has spawned an applied decision making technology: decision analysis. Raiffa [59] offered a classic presentation of the technique; von Winterfeldt and Edwards [88] related it to behavioral research on decision making. This latter, termed “behavioral decision theory,” encompasses a variety of psychological research, primarily on judgment [1] [26]. An influential substream of behavioral decision theory is heuristics and biases research [38].

The contributions of the decision theory perspective reflect its three modes of development. First, it has brought formal theoretical methods to bear on a domain that might seem resistant to such. Second, in decision analysis it has produced a scientifically grounded technology that can be usefully applied to certain practical problems. Finally, behavioral decision theorists have achieved insights into the nature of human judgment and the factors affecting decision making under uncertainty.

The limitations of the approach mirror its strengths. Consider that formal deductive theories are constructed on a base of assumed primitives. The postulated nature of primitives concerning human thought – judgment, preference, uncertainty – must be consistent with empirical psychology. It is possible for deductive theorists to beg crucial questions through convenient assumptions about primitives. This has happened in formal decision theory. As argued by Pettit [55] and Tversky [82], expected utility theory has little explanatory power since it cannot prespecify the values attached to decision consequences. Unable to say what preferences humans are maximizing, decision theoretic explanations of choice behavior are often unsatisfying. Likewise, many decision analysts assume that subjective probabilities express some measurable internal state of uncertainty or encompass all of one's knowledge concerning a proposition. These assumptions are not defensible [74].

Other limitations derive from the basic decision making conceptualization itself. Many managerial problems cannot be usefully regarded as decision problems. Though “The R&D Problem” can be conceived as encompassing various choices, it also involves such activities as determining the reasons for the CEO's opposition and designing a project that this executive would endorse; these activities do not fit naturally into a decision tree. On the psychological side, while judgment is an important cognitive process, problem solving also involves perception, memory, creativity, and reasoning. These abilities have been largely overlooked by behavioral decision theory research.

## 3.2. Organizational decision making

This approach adopts the organizational level of analysis, arguing that managerial problem solving involves the organization in two ways. First, organizations include structuring devices (e.g., procedures for authorizing capital expenditures) that direct and constrain managerial problem solving. Second, organizations shape individual cognition and add a sociopolitical dimension to managerial thinking.

Studies of organizational decision making began with the Carnegie School's [17] [43] refutation of economic notions of managerial rationality. These theorists argued that managers and organizations are boundedly rational and act despite uncertainty. Subsequent theoretical research proposed incrementalist accounts in which organizations adapt their way towards preferred states [9] [58]; a “garbage can” model which claims that solutions look for problems [13]; and an enactment view that sees action as driving thought [92]. Much empirical research has been conducted, ranging from individual case studies to large-scale field investigations [3] [8] [45] [50] [66].

The contributions of this perspective reflect its appreciation of the organization's role in managerial problem solving. First, it has shown how individual problem solving is directed and constrained by the organization. Mintzberg, Raisinghani, and Theoret's [45] field study amply evidences the ways in which policies, information systems, authorization routines, and other procedures structure organizational decision processes. Second, this research highlights social and collective aspects of managerial problem solving. Because complex problems affect many parts of the organization, various parties are consulted or directly included in problem solving. Rather than being solitary thinkers, managers solve problems through group discussions, during which meanings and assumptions are negotiated [89] and informal debate is used to develop consensus [65]. Finally, the politicality of managerial problem solving is made apparent by this perspective [35]. Different parties have stakes in alternative courses of action, and problem complexity creates uncertainty, enabling agents to promote their own agendas as part of the solution.

One weakness of this research derives from the weak conceptualization of cognitive processes discussed earlier. Organization-level theorists have largely adopted this individual-level language, augmenting it with new constructs. This practice creates terminological confusion. Not all theorists are as careful as Daft and Weick [18] in explicating how organizational learning and sense-making relate to and differ from their individual counterparts. For instance, Nutt's [50] study of organizational decision making centered on alternative generation. Yet few of the processes he identified were organizational procedures, most being heuristic practices employed by managers. Lacking principled ways of differentiating individual from organizational activities, theorists fail to explain either.

Another limitation derives from the nature of managerial problem solving itself. Though problem solving can be conceptualized as a group or organizational activity, these higher levels ultimately depend, more or less strongly, on underlying individual contributions. It is an empirical question which level of analysis contributes most to the explanation of problem solving performance in organizational contexts. However, two points deserve consideration. First, organizations commonly direct individual activities by establishing procedures for individuals to follow. The more a task allows procedural structuring, the greater the organization's influence on its performance. Many organizational tasks are proceduralized, but managerial problems are just those that cannot be structured in this way. Scarce managerial attention should be and generally is reserved for those issues that are sufficiently important and complex to require it [37] [75]. Thus, the uniqueness and complexity of managerial problems immunizes them against proceduralization, an important means by which organizations determine individual behavior.

Second, while it seems likely that groups (e.g., executive committees, task forces) are the prevailing mode of problem solving in organizations, this does not establish that performance is best explained by group-level variables. A methodological individualist [91] would argue that only individuals can define problems, determine causes, design options, and evaluate alternatives, the collective's performance being largely determined by member activities. Again, it is difficult to differentiate individual from group factors in an empirical context, much less to assess their relative importance. But, as an example, Hickson et al's [35] study of strategic decision making concluded that organizational variables were less important than problem characteristics in determining decision processes and outcomes.

## 3.3. Individual traits

Another way of explaining managerial problem solving is in terms of personal traits or dispositions, relatively stable characteristics that vary across individuals and affect thinking outcomes. Research on individual differences has a long history in psychology, but has been overshadowed by, first, the behaviorist and, later, the cognitivist paradigms. Cronbach [15] described dispositional research as a separate discipline in scientific psychology. Trait theories represent one side of the nature-nurture debate, proposing that behavior derives largely from innate aspects of one's character. Another formulation, the person-situation controversy [54], concerns the extent to which individual responses are adapted to task and situational demands. Of direct interest is the way in which problem solving is shaped by individual dispositions. In addition to personality variables, trait theorists have posited the explanatory significance of such cognitive characteristics as intelligence [76], creativity [79], and cognitive style [34] [61].

Individual difference research has been controversial, primarily for conceptual and methodological reasons. The major contribution of this perspective may be that it has kept scientists alert to the potential existence and importance of dispositional factors. Recent research suggests that genetic differences influence behavior in more ways than is often assumed [4].

But criticisms of the approach are legion. Huber [36] assessed the contributions of cognitive style research to the study of MIS and DSS design, arguing that findings were weak and inconclusive, and that further research was unlikely to be productive. Evaluating dispositional research on individual behavior in organizations, Davis-Blake and Pfeffer [20] claimed that: (1) Dispositional effects are mild compared to the strong influence of organizational settings; and (2) Rather than being dominated by traits, individual behavior is adaptive to situational demands. These authors criticized trait theorists for not explaining the nature and origins of dispositions, and for inadequately defining and measuring their constructs.

Davis-Blake and Pfeffer's argument that “measures of dispositions should be conceptually and empirically distinct from measures of the effects of dispositions” [20, p. 395] recalls Cummins's [16] view of scientific explanation. Cummins contrasted transition and property theories: The former explain change; the latter account for an entity's properties in terms of its parts. Dispositional explanations, the goal of property theories, must say how attributes of system parts explain system dispositions; and “Attributions of analyzing properties should be justifiable independently of the analysis that features them” [16, p. 26]. In this light, trait accounts of problem solving can be faulted for not explaining how dispositions like cognitive style affect problem solving performance. A disposition-performance relationship is merely posited; researchers then search for empirical correlations to evidence the claim. The inadequacy is apparent in discussions of left brain-right brain thinking (e.g., [85]). Even granting that hemispheric differences are physiologically real, they cannot be treated as psychologically significant – say, as explanations of problem solving style – absent a scientific account of the brain-thinking relationship. Science is far from having such an account.

## 3.4. Cognitive processing

This stream, which includes parts of the problem formulation and behavioral decision theory literatures, explains managerial problem solving in terms of individual cognitive activities. It has largely been shaped by information processing theory [48] [68] [83]. Another important influence, evident in Schwenk's [64] review of strategic decision making, is heuristics and biases research [38].

Most work in this vein adopts what Mohr calls a process theory approach, presenting “a series of occurrences as a sequence over time so as to explain how some phenomenon comes about $[46, p. 9]$ . Phase models decompose managerial problem solving into sequences of seemingly necessary functions. Simon’s $[70]$ intelligence-design-choice trichotomy is the most influential; Cowan’s $[14]$ model of problem recognition shows the degree to which such descriptions can be refined; Lang, Dietrich, and White $[41]$ provide a general review.

Interestingly, much cognitive process research has a variance-theoretic flavor. Variance theories attempt to explain the variance in some dependent variable in terms of a set of independent variables $[46]$ . Dominant in the behavioral and social sciences $[51]$ , the approach implies that managerial problem solving can be understood as the product of interactions among individual, organizational, and problem-related factors. Thus, Volkema $[84]$ speculated about factors – the capabilities and experiences of planners, complexities of the problem, and the planning environment – that might affect the problem formulation process. Payne $[52]$ used process tracing methods to explore the effects of task variables on decision making behavior. The cognitive processing perspective has made progress towards a useful account of managerial problem solving. To some degree, however, the approach currently offers more promise than performance. It is a promise in which virtually all behavioral and social sciences have invested; cognitivism is a pervasive influence throughout these disciplines. And there have been returns, as in the design of teaching methods, human-computer interfaces, and expert systems $[6]$ .

For the cognitivist promise to be realized in the study of managerial problem solving, several significant difficulties must be addressed. First, there are reasons to be skeptical of the variance theoretic approach. Due to the variety and complexity of domain phenomena, the number of interacting variables is huge, many lying beyond the pale of experimental control. Randomization is futile because “A tenth-order cause may sponsor a first-order effect” [90, p. 14]. The problem solving impact of any small set of independent variables is slight compared to the effects of all others. And the key source of performance variance – the task or problem being solved - has not been adequately characterized.

Process accounts also face difficulties, especially due to the lack of a scientific lexicon of psychological processes and capacities. Researchers are starting to bridge the gap between low level psychological terms like memory, perception, and inference, and the broader, functional language – problem formulation, alternative generation, design – in which accounts of managerial problem solving are commonly expressed. Yet there continues to be a great deal of conceptual confusion in the domain, as in the psychology on which it depends. A second limitation is the existence of mental “black boxes,” stubborn islands of cognitive indeterminacy. Managerial problem solving hinges on one’s ability to recognize the significance of certain stimuli, to recall experiential knowledge, and to generate creative alternatives. Psychology has not explained these abilities. While applied scientists can work around such barriers, they do so at great loss in explanatory and prescriptive power. For instance, the prominent role of a black box like “schemata” in current research on managerial thinking [14] [64] seriously limits prescription.

## 4. Directions for future research

Each of the four theoretical perspectives just discussed will continue to add to our understanding of managerial problem solving. Arguably the cognitive processing approach has the most to offer. It is proposed, however, that these four research streams, as currently conceived, cannot individually or collectively provide a workably complete scientific explanation of managerial problem solving. Another kind of research is required, also rooted in cognitivism. It will herein be called the reasoned action perspective.

Almost from their inception, the behavioral and social sciences have recognized that human behavior could be understood in terms of individual beliefs and motivations $[7]$ $[31]$ . Lay people, and scientists in their daily lives, explain human behavior in this way: “He quit his job because he wanted more autonomy.” It was long felt that scientific explanations of behavior could not take this form, simply because reasons are not causes. However, Davidson $[19]$ made a compelling argument against this position, and the subsequent philosophical consensus has regarded reasons and motives as legitimate forms of scientific explanation [44] [80].

The challenge now is to effectively employ these constructs in scientific theories of behavior. Much contemporary research, loosely termed “action theory,” has been attempting to do so, with unclear results $[10]$ $[32]$ $[86]$ . Early efforts focused primarily on individual motives or values. As Mohr $[46]$ explained, these failed in either of two ways: Theories were not powerful enough to identify the particular motives responsible for particular behaviors; and accounts couched in terms of general motivations were explanatorily empty. Recent developments, notably Fishbein and Ajzen’s $[27]$ theory of reasoned action, give more attention to individual beliefs as determinants of behavioral intentions and subsequent action. Even here, however, there is no adequate account of how beliefs are generated and applied in the production of intentions.

Managerial problem solving is a somewhat special class of behaviors. The individual or collective is faced with an unsatisfactory state of affairs and is motivated to change it. Thus, motivation is transparent: One wants to solve the problem. Much more at issue are one's beliefs about the situation and the ways in which these are applied to draw conclusions. As noted earlier, managerial problems are marked by the relative absence of useful scientific knowledge, and the need to generate and apply elements of one's vast store of common-sense, experiential knowledge to determine a course of action. The activation and application of these beliefs is directed by two other aspects of problem solving: the individual's problem definition or mental representation of the situation; and the strategy or general problem solving approach that is adopted. In summary, managerial problem solving can be understood as reasoned action that works from a mental representation of a problem situation to a more-or-less well-defined goal state. Of the problem solving strategies that might be employed, most depend for their effectiveness on the solver's evocation of memorial knowledge, used in reasoning towards conclusions about how best to achieve objectives.

Several directions for future research on managerial problem solving are implied by this perspective and by the overall account presented heretofore. The importance of problem representations, and the fact that they are often expressed in natural language, creates a research opportunity. A problematic situation can be defined in many ways – unsatisfied goals, unknown causes, barriers and difficulties – and from many perspectives, personal and metaphoric [71]. Research must determine how managers represent such situations. Are definitions sensitive to theoretical problem structures? Are other categorical types frequently employed? To what characteristics of problems do managers excessively or inadequately attend? How, and to what extent, are concrete particulars translated into abstract renderings of the situation? The notion of perspective, often employed by theorists [63], has not been developed. Research could suggest the origins and identities of different perspectives for particular problems. Organizational studies can investigate how stakeholders' definitions of problems interact over time.

Problem solving strategies have received a great deal of attention from artificial intelligence researchers, beginning with Newell and Simon's [48] focus on means-ends analysis. Clancey [12], for instance, described a “heuristic classification” strategy, a generic approach in which one identifies a problem as being of a certain type, and solves it by applying knowledge keyed to that type. Chandrasekaran [11] identified other general strategies, including that of reasoning about the consequences of actions. Classic management science has relied on formal modeling as a problem solving strategy. Researchers must specify and characterize the strategies that managers use during their problem solving. They must develop principled means of relating strategies to classes of problems, so that appropriate strategies can be prescribed and selected. Given an appreciation of strategies, interventionists will be able to develop strategy-specific decision aids.

Reasoning and inferential activity, conscious mental processes that are often verbalized, afford empirical investigation and prescription. Theorists must overcome two inadequate views of practical reasoning – that it is like deductive logic, and that it is judgmental [74] – in order to appreciate the “divergent rationality” of everyday affairs [67]. The elements of managerial reasoning must be identified, perhaps as suggested by Toulmin [81]. Argument types can be distinguished, based on the warrant used to justify movement from data to conclusion [25]. Managerial reasoning can be analyzed to determine if classic fallacies of informal logic (e.g., hasty generalization) are prevalent and what other kinds of weaknesses recur. Since all inference appeals to assumed structure – causes, similarities, past cases – theorists must identify and assess the structural assumptions invoked in managerial reasoning.

The quality of conceptualization in this domain must be improved. A limited set of mental processes and capacities can be distilled from psychology. Abstract concepts like “goal” and “strategy” are specifiable through the analysis of exemplars, critical cases, and necessary and sufficient conditions. Agre’s [2] analysis of “problem” is instructive. Functional concepts denoting mental or organizational activities must be defined reductively in terms of those activities. A definition of “problem formulation” should specify the cognitive/organizational states or processes encompassed by the concept. Terms must be employed more carefully, consistent with their definitions. Rather than sterile operationalism, the domain requires more disciplined conceptualization.

Managerial problems comprise the least studied part of this domain. Theorists must be able to characterize problems in ways pertinent to their solution. Most generally, this involves specifying features that solvers should include in problem definitions. More narrowly, one can try to identify attributes or structures that support classification and the identification of solution strategies. That problems can be characterized in many ways is apparent from the many nonoverlapping taxonomies already proposed [5] [47] [78]. Since problems are intrinsically diverse, as varied as reality in relation to human desires, it is unlikely that a comprehensive but prescriptively useful taxonomy can be devised. Theorists should strive for partial categorizations that support solution efforts. One approach is to decompose managerial problems into simpler subproblems – diagnose causes, design alternatives, etc. – addressed by prescriptive research [73]. Alternatively, theorists can look for structures, not necessarily part of a taxonomic scheme, that characterize common problem situations. The queuing, inventory, and other generic problems addressed by management science could be augmented with problem types drawn from empirical research.

Empirical research on problem representation and practical inference has methodological prerequisites. Researchers must be able to deal with “contentful” data. Motivated by variance theories, existing data analysis techniques usually categorize items of data, enabling counts and computations of correlation. While these are useful results, researchers must be able to assess the content and quality, not just the frequency, of process components. Which analogies were effective, which were not? What aspects of the situation were included in good problem definitions but ignored in others? Researchers must identify and deal with the problem’s ascribed content, studying how the solver processes meanings to reach a conclusion. Methodologies developed for discourse analysis [21] [33] should be helpful, as also the abstract knowledge structures specified by AI research on natural language processing [24].

Empirical research also requires a means of assessing problem solver performance. Scientists must be able to evaluate process components – representational adequacy, the plausibility of inferences – as well as proposed solutions. When problem solvers agree that performance was not effective, error analysis can specify flaws. This approach can be applied to the study of problem identification, if managers admit in retrospect that they ignored situations that should have been identified as problems. When solution quality is of interest, it may be possible to use tasks having solutions that are demonstrably correct or that have been confirmed by a panel of experts. Care must be taken, however, that tasks do not lose realism and that experts are expert. Arguably managers are the only authorities in this domain. It may be necessary to derive ratings of solution alternatives through group processes that alternate voting and arguing within a panel of informed practitioners.

Managerial problem solving theory attempts to specify the nature of action-oriented thought in organizational contexts. While the domain encompasses a sizable volume of existing research, its progress has been slow and often misdirected. To facilitate future advances, this paper has compared past theoretical efforts with the intrinsic needs and resources of the domain. Problem solving must be understood as goal-directed activity that works from a mental representation of a situation to reasoned conclusions and responses.

Adoption of this perspective promises greater explanatory success, and the development of interventions that can more effectively support problem solving performance.
