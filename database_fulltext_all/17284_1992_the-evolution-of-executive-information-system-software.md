# The evolution of executive information system software

R. Kelly Rainer, Jr.
and Charles A. Snyder
Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA

Hugh J. Watson

University of Georgia, Athens, GA 30602, USA

Executive information systems (EIS) may be developed from three categories of software: Custom-built software; full-featured vendor software specifically designed for EIS; or a combination of custom-built and general purpose vendor software. Based on data gathered from a survey of 50 companies with operational EIS, this study compares and contrasts the three types of EIS software. Twelve of the firms developed their EIS using custom-built software, 16 used full featured EIS vendor-supplied software, and 22 used a combination of custom-built software and general purpose vendor software. Study results suggest an evolution in EIS software from custom-built software toward the other two types of EIS software.

Keywords: Executive information systems (EIS), Executive support systems (ESS)

## 1. Introduction

An increasing number of companies are implementing executive information systems (EIS). International Data Corporation, a market research firm, predicts that the U.S. market for EIS is growing at a compound annual rate of nearly 40 percent and that expenditures for EIS software development, including the purchase of software, custom consulting, and in-house software development, will grow to \$350 million in 1992 [1].

EIS software has evolved over time. A few pioneering firms created their EIS in the late 1970's [7]. Developing an EIS at that time was an impressive feat. It required both a vision of what might be done to bring computer support to executives and the skill to create such systems. An EIS is a computer based information system, tailored to meet the information needs of executives. It provides internal and external information in a variety of formats meaningful to the executive user. Executive information systems typically:

(1) extract, filter, compress, and track critical data;

(2) provide on-line status access, trend analysis, exception reporting, and “drill-down”;

(3) are user-friendly and require minimal or no training;

(4) are used directly by executives without intermediaries. [2,5,8,9,12,13,15,18].

The terms, EIS and ESS, both appear in the literature. According to Rockart and De Long [13], an executive support system (ESS) usually refers to a system with a broader set of capabilities than an EIS, including:

(1) support for electronic communications (e.g., e-mail, computer conferencing, and word processing);

(2) data analysis capabilities (e.g., spreadsheets, query languages, and decision support systems);

(3) organizing tools (e.g., electronic calendars, automated rolodex, and tickler files).

The distinctions between an EIS and an ESS are not especially important in this study other than to recognize that an ESS increases system requirements.

Firms can use three types of software to develop EIS: Custom-built software, full-featured vendor-EIS software, or customized generic vendor software. Firms employing custom-built software perform all software development in-house. Firms using vendor-EIS software buy full-featured EIS software requiring minimal customization. Firms using the hybrid-EIS software buy software with limited EIS features and must customize it when developing their EIS.

## 2. The research methodology

This study relied upon a multi-part questionnaire mailed to a large sample of geographically dispersed firms. The first part of the questionnaire defined an EIS because a precise definition is not universally accepted. Further, respondents were provided a consistent definition on which to base their responses. The survey defined an EIS as a computerized system with the following characteristics:

(1) EIS are used directly by top managers without the assistance of intermediaries;

(2) EIS provide easy online access to current information about the status and plans of the organization;

(3) EIS are designed with management's critical success factors (CSFs) in mind;

(4) EIS use state-of-the-art graphics, communications, and data storage and retrieval methods.

The second part of the questionnaire gathered demographic data. The third section focused on the development, operation, and support of the EIS in the organization.

The survey was targeted at companies that already had EIS. Thus, the sample is a convenience, rather than a random sample. The survey sample was chosen from three groups. The first group were companies whose representatives attended either the 1987 or 1988 Decision Support Systems (DSS-87 or DSS-88) conferences. The authors reasoned that these attendees would be likely to come from organizations with an EIS. One hundred and eighty-five questionnaires were sent to this group. The second group, all of whom received questionnaires, consisted of the one hundred firms identified by a Computerworld [4] survey as having invested the most effectively in information systems. The third group consisted of nineteen firms which did not appear in the first two groups, but were known by the authors to have an EIS. Because 18 firms appeared in more than one group, a total of 286 questionnaires were mailed.

## 2.1. The sample

A total of 112 (39.1%) usable responses were received. Fifty companies reported that they had an EIS, and 62 firms reported that they did not have an EIS.

Of the 50 firms with an EIS, 12 indicated that they developed their EIS using predominantly custom-built software. They were therefore placed in the “custom-EIS” category. Sixteen firms reported that they developed their EIS using either Pilot’s Command Center or Comshare’s Commander EIS. They are categorized as having a “vendor EIS.” Both vendor products are full capability EIS software. The capabilities present in full-featured vendor-EIS software are listed in table 2. While only two vendors were represented, these two companies accounted for 85% of all operational vendor EIS at the time of this study. [1]

The remaining 22 firms indicated that they used a mixed approach, i.e., a combination of general purpose vendor software and custom-built software. These firms are categorized as “hybrid EIS” firms. The 12 “custom-EIS” firms, the 16 “vendor-EIS” firms, and the 22 “hybrid-EIS” firms are the sample sizes on which the percentages in this article are based. The sample sizes for the three groups are small due, in large part, to the small number of EIS that were operational at the time of the study.

## 3.2. Comparison of EIS software

The survey findings support the fact that EIS are a recent development. The average age of all 50 EIS in the survey is 24.8 months. EIS developed with custom software have a greater mean age (39.17 months) than EIS developed with hybrid (21.35 months) or vendor software (15.69 months). This age difference helps to explain the finding that respondents from custom-EIS firms have more experience with EIS than respondents from hybrid-EIS firms and vendor-EIS firms.

Prior research states that executives typically initiate EIS development $[2,3,6,7,13]$ . The survey results support this contention. Seventy percent of the EIS in this study were initiated by an executive.

An interesting finding is that more custom-EIS firms (83%) have executives show initiated EIS development than hybrid-EIS firms (73%) or vendor-EIS firms (56%). By way of contrast, when the I/S department (i.e., either the I/S manager or an I/S staff member) initiates development, it is more likely to use vendor-EIS software. Forty-four percent of the vendor-EIS firms, but only 27% of the hybrid-EIS firms and 17% of the custom-EIS firms, had the I/S department initiating development.

There are several possible explanations for these differences. The older systems tended to use custom-EIS software because it was the only option available at the time. Many of the firms have continued to use custom software because of the time, cost, and effort of converting to commercial software. In many instances the custom-EIS software is serving the organization well.

The driving force behind many of the older EIS was an executive who was dissatisfied with the timeliness, availability, or relevance of existing information. This executive wanted a system even if it involved custom building the software. Far fewer I/S departments had the vision, confidence of management, and risk-taking propensity to push for an EIS based on custom-built software.

As vendor-EIS software has emerged and the number of EIS success stories has grown, more I/S departments are advocating the development of an EIS. They recognize the opportunity that EIS holds for an organization, and on a personal level, for favorable recognition of I/S within the organization. In addition, I/S departments already face large application development backlogs and may not have the resources to custom build EIS software and still address existing backlogs.

Organizations developing an EIS usually designate a group to be specifically responsible for the EIS implementation $[7,13]$ . All respondents in the three categories indicate that they have EIS development teams. The average size of vendor-EIS teams, 2.94 people, is smaller than the average size of hybrid-EIS teams, 4.18 people, and custom-EIS teams, 8.04 people.

Conventional wisdom suggests that vendor-EIS teams would tend to be smaller than custom-EIS and hybrid-EIS teams because the wide range of vendor-EIS capabilities (e.g., application shells, templates for screen design, facilities for screen maintenance, and support for rapid prototyping) eliminates many tasks for system developers. The survey data support this contention.

Data on actual EIS development and operational costs were gathered in five categories: Hardware, software, personnel, training, and total. Clearly, both types of EIS are costly. Larger firms are better able to commit large amounts of resources to develop an EIS, which largely explains the billion dollar average size of firms in this survey.

The three types of firms spend different amounts of money on the categories of development costs (see table 7). The data suggest that, in every category, vendor-EIS software is more costly than the other two EIS software options. Note that all three categories of EIS spend the least amounts on training, which is to be expected, as minimal or no training is a requirement for these systems [3,10,14,15].

Though not specifically addressed in the questionnaire, a partial explanation for the differences in hardware and software development costs may lie in the nature of the types of EIS software. Custom-EIS developers may tend to use the hardware and software that are already available in their firms. The other two types of EIS software, though, may require the purchase of additional hardware, but will require the purchase of software.

Several related considerations impact personnel costs. Because of the tools available with full-featured vendor software, screen creation, update, and maintenance are usually easier than with custom-EIS software. There is a price to pay, however. There is normally a labor intensive vendor-EIS software process that must be followed. Vendor-EIS software typically requires two weeks of training before it can be effectively used by the support staff. This training is expected because vendor-EIS software is unfamiliar to the client's support staff. Custom-EIS software, however, does not require such a labor-intensive course, because the support staff is already familiar with it. Survey results support this contention (see table 7.)

The data suggest that the three types of EIS software require differing amounts on all categories of annual operating costs (see table 8). The average annual operating expense for custom EIS is higher than that of hybrid EIS and vendor-EIS. Also, the annual training cost for custom-EIS seems to be higher than that of the other EIS software options, implying that vendor-EIS may be easier to use than the other two. Finally, the lower annual software cost for vendor-EIS suggests two things: first, vendor-EIS have the capabilities to easily modify an EIS and second, modifications to custom-EIS or hybrid-EIS may involve the purchase or development of additional software.

Distinguishing between development costs and annual operating costs is important. At first inspection, cost-conscious firms may decide that custom-EIS are more cost effective than hybrid-EIS or vendor-EIS, based on the finding that the latter two types of EIS software seem to cost more to develop than custom-EIS. However, the annual operating cost data suggest that custom-EIS are more expensive to operate. In particular, the higher development costs for hybrid or vendor-EIS may be recovered in approximately two years through lower operating costs.

EIS support/builder teams usually produce initial versions of an EIS quickly $[2,7,10,13,14]$ . The average time to develop the initial version of a vendor-EIS, 3.03 months, is shorter than the average time to develop the initial version of a hybrid-EIS, 4.43 months, and a custom-EIS, 6.05 months. An explanation for this finding is that vendor-EIS are easier and simpler to develop than the other two types of EIS software because there are tools within vendor-EIS software that contribute to this ease and simplicity (e.g., screen design templates and application shells).

The number of EIS users and the number of screens available tend to increase over time $[7,13,16]$ . This survey found (see table 9) that all three types of EIS software have a similar number of users initially, and that the numbers of users tend to grow at similar rates.

## 4. Summary of findings

This study supports the contention that companies first developed executive information systems using custom-built software. Vendor firms that produced full-featured EIS software entered the market more recently. Therefore, in the early 1980's, custom EIS predominated, but EIS software evolved in two directions. The first direction is toward full-featured vendor-EIS software. The second direction is toward a combination of custom-built software and general purpose software.

The choice of direction appears to be at least partially dictated by the expense of EIS to develop and operate. In a recent study, respondents listed cost considerations as the fourth most important factor (out of seventeen) affecting successful EIS development [11]. EIS developers, therefore, while being sensitive to the cost of these systems, may choose the lower cost hybrid approach or the high cost, full-featured vendor software approach.

One possible explanation for the cost difference between custom-EIS, hybrid-EIs, and vendor-EIS is that custom-EIS and hybrid-EIS may have more hidden costs than vendor-EIS. That is, custom-EIS or hybrid-EIS developers may be able to hide some costs under other development projects, while vendor-EIS developers must pay the vendor for the EIS software and development. This vendor payment would be difficult to hide.

Table 12 summarizes the study results and provides insights into the two directions of EIs software evolution. If EIS developers select full-featured vendor software, they can expect high development costs. However, fewer members are necessary on the EIS development team, development time is very short, operating expenses are relatively low, versatile interfaces are available, screen generation and maintenance are easy, and all capabilities are offered.

If EIS developers select hybrid software, the development costs are relatively low, comparing favorably with custom EIS software. The number of development team members, operating costs, development time, response time, and interfaces offered compare favorably with full-featured vendor-EIS software. However, the capabilities offered by hybrid-EIS are far less inclusive than vendor-EIS software. EIS developers simply face a trade-off if they employ hybrid software. They can incur relatively low cost, but will have fewer capabilities.

## 5. Conclusions

The study results suggest that most EIS will be developed in the future with hybrid software or vendor software. EIS developers can use hybrid software if two conditions are present: (1) Their firms place cost restrictions on the EIS development process; and/or (2) their firms already have well-established reporting and analysis systems in place. In the first place, developers must honor cost restrictions. Secondly, if the firm's executives consider reporting and analysis systems to be satisfactory, developers need only add an attractive and easy to use front end to these systems.

EIS developers can use full-featured vendor software if cost considerations are less important and if all possible EIS capabilities are needed by the firm's executives. In addition, the costs of vendor software will inevitably decrease over time with the proliferation of EIS vendors. For example, IBM (Executive Decisions in the mid 1980's) and Channel Computing (Forest and Trees in 1989) have entered the EIS market. Competition among EIS vendors will typically result in a decrease in the cost of full-featured vendor-EIS products, resulting in added attractiveness for vendor EIS.
