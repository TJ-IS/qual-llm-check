# Machine learning methods for intelligent decision support

# An introduction

Michael J. Shaw

University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Champaign, IL, USA

This special issue focuses on research work developing machine learning methods for enhancing intelligent decision support systems. For many years, researchers in the IDSS area have developed various methods to handle the problem solving requirements of IDSSs. A typical architecture for an IDSS consists of a problem-solving unit, an array of databases, knowledge bases, model banks, and a user interface. An IDSS supports decision-making tasks by coordinating activities such as information gathering, data retrieval, selection of appropriate heuristics, rules, or models, making inferences, interacting with the user, deriving solution strategies, and suggesting solutions or possible candidates for the solution. These decision-support activities can be aided by machine learning capabilities.

Machine learning generally refers to the class of computational methods for deriving insightful knowledge (including heuristics, strategies, or structure) from data, observations, or past solutions. Machine learning methods evolve from theories and techniques developed in artificial intelligence, pattern recognition, classification, and cognitive psychology. These methods have greatly contributed to generating new insights into how the problem-solving functions of IDSSs can be enhanced. They appear to have the potential to address two critical issues of IDSS research: How an IDSS can acquire and refine problem-solving knowledge and solution strategies; and how to equip an IDSS with the capability to adapt and evolve, thus progressively becoming more effective in handling the problem-solving activities necessary for supporting decision tasks. In fact, we may want to take a more unified approach and treat learning as part of the functions of problem solving activities in an IDSS [10].

The focus of this special issue is more than just on surveying existing machine learning methods. More importantly, it provides a forum focusing on the study of machine learning in the context of intelligent decision support with the specific goal of improving the performance of IDSSs. To that end, the decision tasks under study become an integral part of the IDSS design. A variety of tasks are covered by the papers in this special issue, ranging from IDSS architecture, organizational decision making, auditing, negotiation and conflict resolution, man-machine interface, model management, real-time process monitoring, financial analysis, to manufacturing scheduling. Among these applications, some patterns on the potential contributions of machine learning to intelligent decision support emerge. They include the following:

\- automating the knowledge acquisition process in developing IDSSs for a given task domain;

\- establishing decision models and knowledge structures representative of past observations;

\- building and refining standard solution procedures, i.e., cases, for handling recurring decision scenarios;

\- forming strategies for adaptive decision making; and

\- establishing organizational memory and group facilitating mechanisms to enhance group decision making.

The articles in this special issue cover a wide range of perspectives on machine learning methods for intelligent decision support. In the first paper Holsapple et al. add machine learning as a new component to the classic DSS framework which consists of a problem-processing unit, a language unit, and a knowledge unit. This component enables refinement of the DSS's knowledge base, thus making the DSS adaptive. They apply the framework to the problem of manufacturing scheduling and use a genetic algorithm to achieve the machine learning functions. The second paper, by Elofson and Konsynski, looks at the issue of organizational learning. They develop so-called machine apprentices to support organizational learning. Such a method should be valuable in developing organization-wide support systems for the effective adaptation of an enterprise. It certainly points to some interesting directions for group decision support systems (GDSSs) or computer-based collaboration technology.

When the decision-support tasks involve a course of problem-solving actions, the learning system needs to be able to handle more complex knowledge structures. An example of such a machine learning method is the case-based learning approach. The third paper, by Sycara, articulates how case-based learning can be used to support conflict-resolution tasks in the labor negotiation situation. In the system she developed, called PERSUADER, cases are used as the basic organizational unit for capturing knowledge related to preferences, strategies, and past histories of the participants. These cases can be used to facilitate the conflict resolution process in negotiation. In the fourth paper, Liang applies case-based learning to the model management function in a decision-support setting. He describes the process for analogical model formulation, which includes feature mapping, transformation, and evaluation.

A number of empirical studies applying similarity-based inductive learning to various types of business analysis have attracted considerable interest in recent years. Interesting results were generated that showed great potential of the techniques for analyzing business data. On the other hand, this class of methods is non-parametric, thus making it difficult to support any evaluation of the results. The fifth paper, by Tsai and Koehler, addresses this issue. They provide several methods for measuring the accuracy of a learned concept. Tam describes in the sixth paper a conceptual clustering approach, called HiC (an acronym for Hierarchical Constructor), for automatically generating concept hierarchies. HiC is also capable of incremental acquisition and update of structured knowledge, and making inference with partial information.

The support of real-time decision making raises a number of issues about incorporating machine learning. In the seventh paper, Lewis employs an assessment of decision heuristics used by pilots in avoiding intruding aircraft and by operators of a simulated process plant to address some of these issues. Chaturvedi et al., by contrast, use flexible manufacturing systems scheduling as the domain for real-time decision making in the eighth paper. The performance of the resulting learning-augmented scheduling system shows again the efficacy of machine learning as a method for accomplishing adaptive DSSs.

Similarity-based inductive learning methods are functionally similar to statistical classification methods such as discriminant, probit, or logit analysis. In the ninth paper, Hansen et al. use an auditing application to compare two inductive learning methods, ID3 and NEWQ, with logistic regression. Such empirical comparisons point to research needs for more understanding of the performance characteristics of the learning mechanisms so that improved methods can be developed.

By all accounts, research in machine learning is an ongoing process although the progress is being made rapidly. This special issue represents one of the first focused efforts of our research community to explore the potential of machine learning in intelligent decision support. Just as the developments in data-base management and expert systems technologies have influenced IDSS research in the past decade, so will research developments in machine learning likely impact the next generation of IDSSs. In contrast with the past emphasis of IDSS research on data and knowledge, machine learning provides a new dimension on the strategy level. Given machine learning capabilities, an IDSS can determine the appropriate domain knowledge to use, identify more useful pieces of knowledge so that they can be stored in forms appropriate for future application, and incorporate mechanisms for systematic refinement of the performance. This shift of focus from data to knowledge to strategies suggests a healthy evolutionary path for achieving intelligent decision support.

In this process of incorporating machine learning as an integral component in the IDSS framework, researchers in our community need to work together to identify a research agenda most relevant to IDSS research. That is precisely the goal of this special issue. The following list suggests some critical topics that need to be addressed in future research.

1. Improvements over existing learning methods
One of the most often used inductive learning methods is the tree induction method by Quinlan [5]. Some interesting features associated with this method make it popular in various application domains. Yet this method employs a greedy algorithm that decides on the branching attribute guided by a heuristic representing the maximal amount of information gained. The method can get stuck in a local solution, may generate trees with an excessive number of nodes, and is limited to the initial set of attributes included in the training examples. Learning schemes need to be developed that can improve on these shortcomings. For example, local solutions can be avoided by coupling the learning method with other learning methods, such as genetic algorithms, which are characterized by their ability to avoid converging to local optima. Tree induction may incorporate some pruning algorithms to cut down the size of the tree; the resulting inductive algorithm also improves on the predictive accuracy. Finally, the learning result may be improved by constructing new attributes in the learning process; in some instances, it can be shown that such a constructive induction technique can achieve more concise trees with improved stability and predictive accuracy. These qualities, namely, conciseness, stabilities, and predictive accuracy, are especially important for IDSS environments because its performance demands on response time, accuracy, and the ability to handle uncertainties prevalent in most decision-support tasks.

2. Understanding the computational properties of inductive learning One of the criticisms of machine learning algorithms is that the quality of the algorithms and of the solutions generated are difficult to evaluate. They are often greedy algorithms, directed by hill-climbing heuristics to search in large instance spaces [6]. On the other hand, emerging methods such as genetic algorithms or artificial neural networks, use learning mechanisms that start with randomly generated initial solutions and do not guarantee convergence or stability of solutions generated. Some of them also take a long computational time. We need to better understand the inherent complexity of learning methods and to be able to evaluate the quality of the solutions generated. This issue becomes even more critical in most IDSS domains because they usually are associated with high volumes of data. For example, in the development of an IDSS for commercial loan evaluation, often a large amount of financial data needs to be deciphered to make loan-granting decisions. The cost of getting training examples are thus very high. Besides, some classes may have much lower number of instances than other classes in the training set, creating unbalanced sample sizes between the classes. Some guidelines are needed to indicate the sample size required for learning reliable decision rules and the quality of the rules learned from the given training set.

3. Using machine learning to improve the user interface of intelligent decision support systems In the related area of developing intelligent tutoring systems, researchers have incorporated what they call the user model in order to characterize the level of the user, i.e., the student, through observing the student's answers to a series of questions. The system then decides on which level of difficulty to present the tutorial material. The student model would be constantly adjusted, based on the way the student is performing. An IDSS can use a similar user model to capture, say, the preference of the user. This user model would then be used to restrict the solutions recommended to the user. Also, the user model can be used to prevent mistakes frequently made by a particular user.

The learning unit of the IDSS can learn by observing the prior performance of a user (by tracing the user's responses in a problem-solving session, for instance) and learn from the errors and problematic decisions made previously. For example, a loan officer may have the tendency to overemphasize the potential growth of the applying companies while overlooking their debt levels, as reflected by the decision rules induced from the prior decisions he made. The IDSS can then suggest the overlooked decision rules for the particular loan officer during the decision-support process to compensate for the user's bias.

4. Developing adaptive IDSS using machine learning mechanisms A system is adaptive if it is capable of self improvement in a changing environment. In the IDSS framework, the decision support system would need to observe the quality of the solutions generated and make changes on the content of the knowledge base if necessary to maintain the required level of performance. To adapt, this refinement process must be initiated by the IDSS itself. Scheduling FMSs is a good example of such a decision-support environment. Dynamic scheduling in an FMS is frequently achieved through applying the best scheduling heuristics for the particular setting. But because the settings in an FMS are highly dynamic and changeable, the effective scheduling heuristics must be updated constantly. The need to change heuristics can be triggered by deteriorated performance of the scheduler, which in turn would activate the decision module for selecting a better set of heuristics [8]. Since the heuristics may be generated by learning from simulation results, the (making decisions) → (observing the results) → (learning new rules) cycle results in a learning process similar to the learning from experimentation process [4].

A related issue is that after an IDSS is developed, the knowledge base of the system needs to be validated and, if necessary, refined. An example is the “learning by doing” approach used in $[1]$ . The refinement of rules may be triggered by detection of unsatisfactory solutions; when the performance falls below a given level, the learning process takes over. The questionable rules are identified and the problematic portions of those rules must be modified. These questionable rules can be the basis for generating more experiments, observations and test cases so that additional conditions can be checked and rules can be refined [3].

5. Knowledge chunking, learning decision strategies, and case-based decision support The problems faced by IDSSs are inherently unstructured and complex. It is usually difficult to find a standard solution procedure for every problem; instead, an IDSS needs to solve each problem differently, taking into account the characteristics of the particular problem at hand. Such custom-made solutions demand costly searches on the part of the IDSS, which can be made more efficient through learning the solution procedures. Case-based reasoning, explanation-based learning [2], and the learning of macro-operators all can achieve this goal. These learning methods work in a way similar to how MBA students learn business strategy through case studies. This class of learning mechanisms is capable of deriving a generalized description (i.e., a template) for representing the solution process in handling a problem. Such a template serves as a solution menu which can be applied directly when faced with similar problems in the future, thus saving the redundant search time for deriving the individual steps. The generalized solution procedure is called a schema in explanation-based learning and a case in case-based learning. It is also referred to as the macro-operator, as in operator-based planning. However, more research is needed in these fields regarding the decision on whether to generalize a solution process and store it as a fixed case or schema, i.e., the utility problem. Otherwise, the benefit of having new cases would be diminished by the additional storage and search cost. Another development in this area related to IDSS is the learning of contingent plans, i.e., instead of applying a case with a priori instantiated parameters, the content of the case is not determined until execution-time, thus accounting for unexpected events. Crisis management decision support is a good example for such a task domain. The task of learning to support dynamic manufacturing planning/scheduling is another.

6. Using machine learning to enhance group decision support systems (GDSSs) Most of the research progress related to machine learning to date has focused on single-agent learning. The learning mechanisms involved in group decision making is an interesting area yet to be explored. The participants in a group process can learn from each other by directly transferring useful knowledge, by adopting each other's strategies that prove to be effective, or by learning what to look for in the search space to identify the solution [9]. The traditional group process, such as brainstorming, has already been able to incorporate some group learning mechanisms. But acquiring a more fundamental understanding on group learning mechanisms is still uncharted territory. Potentially, machine learning mechanisms for groups can be used to facilitate group decision support processes and to improve the quality of group solutions.
